# 017 — Events, processes, and occurrent ontology

## One-line thesis

A salient non-reductive metaphysics should adopt **disciplined occurrent realism**: events and processes are real concrete occurrents, not mere shadows of object-talk; but they must be distinguished from facts and propositions, and individuated with enough discipline to avoid redescription explosion or process imperialism.

## Why this matters

The archive already says that substances and processes both matter, that time is load-bearing, and that causation cannot be reduced to a thin pattern summary. But it still lacks one dedicated place to say **what happenings are**. Without that, too much of the archive's talk about change, action, history, and causation depends on an underdescribed category.

One mistake is **event erasure**: everything important is forced back into the ontology of objects having properties, so happenings become derivative bookkeeping. The opposite mistake is **occurrent inflation**: every true sentence, every redescribable phase, or every causal gloss is treated as a distinct event. A tighter archive needs a middle path. It should allow that events and processes are genuine inhabitants of reality while insisting that their individuation, relation to continuants, and explanatory roles be kept under control.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish continuants from occurrents without choosing one as universal victor

The archive should keep both relatively stable bearers and happenings in view. Some realities are best modeled as continuants or continuant-like individuals; others are best modeled as occurrents—things that happen, unfold, erupt, continue, or terminate. A non-reductive metaphysics should not force all occurrents back into object-language, but it also should not pretend that objects are merely frozen events in every case.

### 2. Distinguish events, processes, and facts

Events and processes should not be blurred into propositions, facts, or states of affairs. Facts and states of affairs belong to the archive's structured-truthmaking surface; events and processes belong to its occurrent surface. Caesar's death, a storm's landfall, a court hearing, a species migration, or a conversation are happenings. They may make propositions true or figure in facts, but they are not simply identical with those truthmaking structures.

### 3. Treat bounded events and ongoing processes as related but non-identical

Some occurrents are naturally episodic: a collision, a vote, a birth, a treaty signing. Others are better understood as activities, operations, or developments: digestion, erosion, bargaining, learning, healing, institution-building. The archive should keep open that events may often be bounded episodes while processes are ongoing or dynamically organized continuities, even though some theories will model one in terms of the other.

### 4. Individuate occurrents with discipline rather than one crude master key

The theory of events should not be settled by one slogan. Pure spatiotemporal individuation is often too coarse; pure property-exemplification can be too fine; unconstrained causal individuation can become circular or theory-laden. The archive should prefer a disciplined mixed posture: an occurrent is typically individuated by some controlled package of bearer or participants, temporal profile, activity or form, and explanatory role. That keeps redescription possible without making every redescription a new entity.

### 5. Let occurrents carry explanatory weight in causation, action, and history

A metaphysics that treats happenings seriously can better explain causation, agency, and historical constitution. Causes are often token happenings, not just static objects. Actions are often events or organized processes, not merely bodies plus predicates. Histories are structured series of occurrents whose order matters to what later entities become. The archive should therefore allow occurrents to do real explanatory work.

### 6. Preserve process pressure without collapsing everything into process-only metaphysics

Process philosophy is a vital pressure source because it reminds the archive that being is often becoming. Stable entities may themselves be dynamically maintained organizations. Still, the archive need not infer that every individual is nothing over and above process, or that enduring bearers are merely convenient fictions. The better posture is to let process insight discipline substance-talk rather than annihilate it.

### 7. Keep cross-level and social occurrents in view

Not all real happenings are microphysical episodes. Elections, trials, recessions, scientific discoveries, rituals, and revolutions are socially organized occurrents. Some are constituted by lower-level happenings but are not exhausted by a micro-event inventory. A non-reductive archive should therefore allow higher-level occurrents to be real where organization, norms, roles, and histories make a genuine difference.

### 8. Use occurrent ontology to sharpen, not bloat, the archive

The point of adding occurrents is not to create a second ontology for every sentence fragment. It is to say more clearly when reality is best understood in terms of what persists and when in terms of what happens. Good occurrent ontology reduces blur across causation, persistence, identity, and social reality.

## Guardrails

- Do not reduce all happenings to static objects plus language.
- Do not identify events with facts, propositions, or states of affairs by default.
- Do not assume one simple criterion of event identity solves every case.
- Do not let process vocabulary erase enduring bearers where they are explanatorily real.
- Do not multiply occurrents recklessly every time a case admits of redescription.

## Failure modes

- **event erasure**: happenings are treated as mere shorthand for object-state descriptions.
- **redescription explosion**: every new way of describing a happening is treated as a distinct event.
- **process imperialism**: stable individuals and bearerhood disappear into all-purpose becoming talk.
- **truthmaking blur**: events are confused with facts, propositions, or states of affairs.
- **micro-episode chauvinism**: higher-level or socially organized occurrents are dismissed as unreal.

## Practical tests

1. Can the theory distinguish continuants from occurrents without reducing one entirely to the other?
2. Can it tell events and processes apart from facts, propositions, and states of affairs?
3. Can it individuate happenings without either excessive fine-graining or spatiotemporal flattening?
4. Can it explain action, causation, and history better by taking occurrents seriously?
5. Can it preserve the reality of higher-level or socially organized happenings without uncontrolled ontological inflation?

## Closing synthesis

The archive should adopt a **disciplined occurrent realism**. Events and processes are real parts of the world's furniture, needed to explain change, action, causation, and history. But they should be handled with careful distinctions: occurrents are not just truths, not every redescription yields a new event, and process insight should deepen rather than flatten the archive's category-plural realism.
