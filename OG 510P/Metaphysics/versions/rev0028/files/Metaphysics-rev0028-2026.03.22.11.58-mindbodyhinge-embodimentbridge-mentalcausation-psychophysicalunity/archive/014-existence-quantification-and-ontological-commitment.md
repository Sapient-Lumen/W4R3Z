# 014 — Existence, quantification, and ontological commitment

## One-line thesis

A salient non-reductive metaphysics should distinguish **what exists**, **what a theory is committed to**, and **what is fundamental**: existence is broader than fundamentality, ontological commitment is a useful but fallible diagnostic, and quantificational form should not be treated as the sole sovereign guide to reality.

## Why this matters

The archive already argues that derivative being is real being, that grounding and fundamentality matter, and that social, modal, and higher-level realities can be genuine without being basic. But that posture remains partly underdescribed until the archive says more clearly how existence itself is to be understood.

Without such a note, the archive is vulnerable to two opposite mistakes. One mistake is **quantifier capture**: assuming that whatever appears as the value of a bound variable in one favored formalization is therefore the settled ontology. The other is **fundamentality capture**: assuming that if something is derivative, socially instituted, realized, or grounded, it somehow does not exist at all. A better metaphysics needs room for real but non-fundamental entities, while still keeping a disciplined account of ontological cost.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish existence from fundamentality

The archive should continue to reject the slide from “not fundamental” to “not real.” Existing things can be basic, derivative, constituted, realized, socially instituted, or historically formed. Questions of **whether something exists** and **how basic it is** are therefore connected but not identical.

### 2. Treat ontological commitment as a tool, not a sovereign court

Ontological commitment is useful because it asks what must exist for a theory to be true. That is a powerful discipline against empty sloganizing. But it is not self-interpreting. Different views of truth conditions, logical form, modality, and truthmakers can yield different commitment verdicts. The archive should therefore treat commitment talk as a serious diagnostic rather than a final automatic oracle.

### 3. Do not read ontology straight off surface grammar

Ordinary language is ontologically suggestive but not transparent. Surface form can conceal structure, and formal paraphrase can reveal commitments that casual grammar hides. But the reverse problem also matters: one controversial paraphrase should not be allowed to legislate ontology by itself. Metaphysics needs argument about the world, not merely confidence about one regimentation.

### 4. Let quantification matter without letting it monopolize being

Quantifiers matter because many ontological disputes are illuminated by asking what must fall within a theory's domain for it to come out true. But not every commitment question is exhausted by first-order variable values alone. Extrinsic predicates, essential connections, truthmaker pressure, and broader entailment patterns can matter too. The archive should therefore use quantificational tests, but not treat them as exhaustive by default.

### 5. Preserve derivative existence

The archive's existing commitments already imply a broad existential generosity: institutions, laws, organisms, artworks, and perhaps facts or states of affairs may all be real even when they are grounded in more basic conditions. The right response to derivative status is usually to ask **what kind of being and dependence is involved**, not to issue an eliminative veto.

### 6. Keep ideology distinct from ontology

A theory can require certain concepts, distinctions, or representational resources without thereby adding corresponding entities to its ontology. Conversely, a theory may carry ontological commitments that are not obvious from its surface conceptual vocabulary. The archive should therefore continue to distinguish **what a theory says exists** from **what conceptual machinery it needs to say it**.

### 7. Use truthmaker pressure where quantifier tests underdescribe the case

Some theories look ontologically cheap if one inspects only explicit bound variables, yet they may still require a richer worldly backing in order to be true. Truthmaker pressure is useful here because it asks what in reality must obtain for the discourse to succeed. The archive should not turn truthmakers into a universal master test, but it should use them when quantifier-only bookkeeping makes ontology look thinner than the world required for truth.

### 8. Prefer a layered ontology over an existence monoculture

The archive should adopt a practical rule: when a discourse earns its keep through explanatory success, modal stability, dependence clarity, and disciplined truthmaking, the default question is not “does this really exist?” in a flat yes/no sense, but “what mode, level, or grade of being is at issue here?” This preserves seriousness without flattening all existence into one undifferentiated inventory.

## Guardrails

- Do not equate ontological commitment with fundamentality.
- Do not infer ontology directly from one favored paraphrase when rival regimentations remain live.
- Do not treat quantifier criteria as the only admissible test of existence.
- Do not inflate every useful concept into a new entity.
- Do not eliminate derivative entities merely because they are grounded, realized, or constituted.

## Failure modes

- **quantifier capture**: one formal criterion is treated as the whole of ontology.
- **fundamentality capture**: only the basic is counted as real.
- **grammatical literalism**: surface language is taken as an ontology detector.
- **ideology/ontology blur**: conceptual resources are confused with entities.
- **thin-world bookkeeping**: a theory is made to look ontologically cheap by ignoring what must really obtain for it to be true.

## Practical tests

1. Can the theory distinguish existence questions from fundamentality questions?
2. Can it explain why ontological commitment is useful without pretending it is automatic or theory-neutral?
3. Can it say when quantifier analysis helps and when truthmaker or entailment pressure is needed?
4. Can it preserve derivative entities without giving up ontological discipline?
5. Can it keep conceptual vocabulary and worldly commitment apart well enough to avoid both inflation and deflation?

## Closing synthesis

The archive should treat **existence, ontological commitment, and fundamentality as linked but non-identical axes of metaphysical discipline**. What there is cannot be read off surface grammar alone, but neither should ontology float free of logical and semantic constraint. Articulated layered realism is strongest when it says plainly that derivative being is still being, ontological commitment is a diagnostic rather than a dictator, and the deepest inventory of reality is not the whole inventory of reality.
