# 010 — Composition, constitution, and formed wholes

## One-line thesis

A salient non-reductive metaphysics should sharply distinguish parthood, composition, constitution, and identity, while treating many ordinary and organized wholes as real formed beings rather than as either mere verbal conveniences or uncontrolled explosions of coincident entities.

## Why this matters

The archive already speaks about substances, emergence, and historical beings, but those commitments remain unstable unless it can say when wholes exist, how they are related to their material bases, and why some organized entities are more than arbitrary heaps without becoming spooky additions to the world.

This is the pressure point where metaphysics often swings between two failures. One failure is eliminativism: deny ordinary objects or organized wholes whenever they are not identical with a favored microphysical base. The other is proliferation: allow every slight difference in persistence condition, interest, or description to generate a new coincident object. A compact archive needs a disciplined middle route.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish parthood from constitution

Parts compose wholes, but the material that constitutes a thing need not simply be identical with that thing. A statue and the clay that constitutes it may share matter while differing in persistence conditions, sortal profile, or explanatory role. The archive should not collapse constitution into identity by reflex.

### 2. Treat composition as a real question, not a mere verbal trick

When do many things compose one thing? Not every arrangement of parts yields a metaphysically salient whole. But neither should the archive pretend that ordinary objects, organisms, artifacts, and institutions are just linguistic conveniences. Composition questions track real structure.

### 3. Prefer formed-whole explanations where organization is doing the work

A promising middle route is to say that many wholes are real because matter is organized, informed, or structured in the right way. This need not commit the archive to a total hylomorphic system. It does mean that organization, form, function, and kind-membership can explain why some wholes count as genuine beings while random aggregates do not.

### 4. Keep ordinary objects and organized systems real unless there is a positive defeater

The archive should start from the reality of ordinary and organized wholes, then ask what dependence structure makes them what they are. That is better than beginning with a sparse micro-inventory and forcing tables, organisms, artworks, and tools to earn existence from scratch.

### 5. Avoid explosion by disciplined kind and organization criteria

The archive should reject arbitrary entity multiplication. Not every temporary, interest-relative, or ad hoc description marks a new object. The best brake is not eliminativism but criteria involving kind, organization, persistence profile, and explanatory role.

### 6. Connect composition to emergence and historical being

Wholes are often historically constituted and organizationally stabilized. That means composition is not only a static puzzle about parts, but also a question about formation, maintenance, dissolution, and the persistence conditions of organized beings.

## Guardrails

- Do not identify constitution with identity by default.
- Do not treat every pile of parts as an equally serious whole.
- Do not use worries about coincident objects as a shortcut to eliminativism.
- Do not let kind language become a mere projection of convenience.
- Do not let formed-whole talk become mystical or unconstrained.

## Failure modes

- **eliminative austerity**: ordinary objects and organized wholes are denied too cheaply.
- **coincidence explosion**: every descriptive variation yields a new object.
- **mereological flattening**: constitution, composition, identity, and dependence are run together.
- **formless aggregation**: organization is treated as metaphysically idle.
- **kind conventionalism**: sortals and persistence conditions are reduced to our interests alone.

## Practical tests

1. Can the theory distinguish composition, constitution, and identity in actual examples?
2. Can it explain why some organized wholes are real while arbitrary aggregates are not?
3. Can it preserve ordinary objects without licensing unlimited coincidence?
4. Can it connect organizational form to persistence conditions and explanatory role?
5. Can it integrate composition with the archive’s accounts of emergence, time, and social reality?

## Closing synthesis

The archive should treat **composition and constitution as central dependence questions** and should favor a view on which many ordinary, biological, technical, and institutional wholes are **real formed beings**: materially based, organizationally structured, historically sustained, and not reducible either to arbitrary heaps or to an uncontrolled proliferation of coincident objects.
