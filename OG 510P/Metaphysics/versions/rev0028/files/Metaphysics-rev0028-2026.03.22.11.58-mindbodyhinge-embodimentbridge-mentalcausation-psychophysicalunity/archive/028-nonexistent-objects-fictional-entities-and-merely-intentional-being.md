# 028 — Nonexistent objects, fictional entities, and merely intentional being

## One-line thesis

A non-reductive metaphysics should treat **aboutness as able to outrun existence** without therefore admitting one full-strength object for every target of thought, story, planning, or impossibility: fictional entities may be real in a derivative and anchored way, but merely intentional, merely possible, and impossible "objects" should not be imported into ontology by default.

## Why this matters

The archive now has explicit notes on intentionality, structured truthmaking, existence, modality, absence, abstracta, and social reality. But it still lacks one dedicated place to say what happens when discourse and thought are directed toward what is not there: Pegasus, Sherlock Holmes, the golden mountain, tomorrow's bridge, the merely planned device, the impossible object, the contradictory story-world. Without a note here, the archive risks oscillating between two bad extremes.

One extreme is **target-to-thing inflation**: whenever we can think, quantify, imagine, fear, admire, or predicate, we are told there must already be a corresponding object. The opposite extreme is **hard Russellian deflation**: empty or fictional discourse is treated as mere error, useful shorthand, or semantic debris, so that imagination, planning, literary discourse, and impossible-content cases become metaphysically underdescribed. The archive needs a middle path: **disciplined asymmetry about nonexistents**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish intentional directedness from ontological admission

A subject can think about, depict, fear, describe, or plan around something without that target existing. Aboutness therefore outruns successful acquaintance and actual existence. But this does not mean that every intentional target is already part of ontology on a par with tables, organisms, events, or abstract structures. The archive should separate **what thought is directed toward** from **what reality admits as a being**.

### 2. Distinguish fictional entities, merely possible objects, impossible objects, and absences

These categories create different pressures and should not be collapsed. A fictional entity is bound to stories, practices, works, and interpretive norms. A merely possible object is often a way of representing an unrealized configuration or candidate being. An impossible object pushes against consistency, content, and world-modeling. An absence or privation is a structured lack in actual reality. A good metaphysics should resist treating all four as one mixed bucket of "things that do not exist."

### 3. Treat fictional entities as derivatively real only where anchors exist

The archive need not choose between saying that fictional characters are literally concrete inhabitants somewhere and saying that they are nothing at all. A better posture is that **fictional entities may be derivatively real as culturally anchored content-structures, authorially initiated artifacts, or practice-bound abstracta**. That lets the archive explain why Sherlock Holmes can be discussed, compared, adapted, and historically tracked without pretending Holmes is just another resident of London.

### 4. Treat possible-object discourse as representational first

Possible-object talk is often useful, but the archive should not let it smuggle in an unrestricted ontology of merely possible beings. In many contexts, talking about a possible bridge, possible organism, or possible institution is a way of describing how reality could be organized, what a plan is aimed at, or what kind of being might be actualized. Possible-object language is therefore often **representational, modal, or projective first**, not automatically an ontological census of unreal residents.

### 5. Keep impossible objects and inconsistent contents under discipline

Impossible worlds, contradictory fictions, and impossible objects are important because they show that content can exceed not only actuality but possibility. Still, they should be handled with care. The archive should treat impossible-object discourse primarily as a pressure point on semantics, imagination, logic, and representation, not as a license to populate being with every contradiction someone can specify.

### 6. Preserve discourse, truth, and error without flattening them

Ordinary and theoretical discourse about fiction and nonexistence does real work. We explain stories, compare characters, plan unrealized artifacts, reject impossible designs, and distinguish failed reference from successful reference. The archive should preserve these practices by distinguishing **story-truth, representational content, social anchoring, reference-failure, and actual existence** rather than trying to force all such cases into one yes/no existence switch.

### 7. Let nonbeing pressure ontology without swallowing it

The point of this note is not to abolish ontological seriousness. The archive should remain cautious about admitting nonexistent objects wholesale. But it should also learn from nonbeing-cases: they reveal that existence, directedness, fictional practice, modal projection, and impossible content are not the same relation. The right lesson is not maximal ontology or maximal deflation, but a more articulated map of being and non-being.

## Guardrails

- Do not infer from thinkability, describability, or narratability straight to existence.
- Do not infer from empty reference that the discourse is automatically nonsense or metaphysically idle.
- Do not blur fictional entities with authors, inscriptions, concrete props, or flesh-and-blood persons.
- Do not treat merely possible objects as an automatic second population of beings.
- Do not let impossible-object talk become a universal solvent for semantic difficulty.
- Do not collapse absences, fictions, plans, and contradictions into one undifferentiated class.

## Failure modes

- **target-to-thing inflation**: every intentional target is promoted into ontology.
- **hard deflation**: fictional and empty discourse are treated as mere error or convenient noise.
- **fiction-reality blur**: fictional entities are confused with concrete beings, texts, or social practices.
- **possibilia sprawl**: possible-object talk silently becomes an uncontrolled census of unreal beings.
- **contradiction laundering**: impossible objects are admitted casually to patch every representational problem.
- **discourse erasure**: story-truth, planning, and failed reference lose their distinct metaphysical roles.

## Practical tests

1. Can the theory explain how we think and speak about Pegasus, Sherlock Holmes, and Vulcan without forcing the same ontological verdict in all three cases?
2. Does it distinguish fictional entities from absences, socially real statuses, and merely planned or merely possible artifacts?
3. Can it explain why some fictional entities seem historically trackable and discussable without treating them as ordinary concrete beings?
4. Can it handle impossible contents and contradictory stories without either semantic panic or unrestricted contradiction-friendly ontology?
5. Does it preserve the difference between actual existence, story-truth, intentional content, and representational usefulness?

## Closing synthesis

The archive should adopt a **disciplined asymmetry about nonexistents**: intentionality and discourse can outrun actuality and even possibility; fictional entities may be real in a derivative, socially and textually anchored way; but merely intentional, merely possible, and impossible "objects" should be treated representationally unless a stronger explanatory case earns them fuller ontological standing.
