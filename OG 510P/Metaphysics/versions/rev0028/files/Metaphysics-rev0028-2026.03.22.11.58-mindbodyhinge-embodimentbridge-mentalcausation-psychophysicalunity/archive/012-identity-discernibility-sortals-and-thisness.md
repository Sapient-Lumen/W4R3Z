# 012 — Identity, discernibility, sortals, and thisness

## One-line thesis

A salient non-reductive metaphysics should treat numerical identity as strict but not self-interpreting, pairing it with sortal and historical criteria of individuation, discernibility discipline, and only cautious appeal to primitive thisness.

## Why this matters

The archive already talks about persistence, constitution, modality, properties, and individuation. But those debates all quietly presuppose an answer to a deeper question: what makes something the very thing it is, and what counts as a real difference between one being and another?

Without a disciplined identity note, the archive risks oscillating between two bad habits. One is to treat identity as so thin that no criteria of sameness are ever needed. The other is to make identity do too much, smuggling into bare numerical sameness every issue about constitution, persistence, counting, and modality. A better posture is to keep strict identity in place while also asking which kinds, forms, histories, and structures make identity claims intelligible in concrete domains.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Keep numerical identity strict

Identity is not a loose similarity relation. If x is identical with y, there are not two things but one. The archive should therefore keep **strict numerical identity** as a real metaphysical notion rather than dissolving it into mere resemblance, practical equivalence, or linguistic convenience.

### 2. Distinguish identity from criteria of identity

Even if identity itself is strict, our warranted judgments of sameness usually depend on criteria. We ask whether this is the same organism, the same river, the same institution, or the same lump of gold. Those are not idle grammatical decorations. They track the fact that **sortals, forms, and histories often supply the criteria by which identity claims become intelligible in particular domains**.

### 3. Treat discernibility as a pressure, not a total theory

Discernibility matters because numerical difference should not be postulated cheaply. But the archive should not assume that every case of non-identity must be explained by one simple qualitative test. The Identity of Indiscernibles is philosophically powerful because it pressures theories to explain difference, yet it does not by itself settle how identity is grounded, and it may fail or need qualification in some domains.

### 4. Use constitution and persistence cases to test identity discipline

The statue and the clay, the ship repaired plank by plank, the person counted as one citizen but two passengers, and the institution that survives personnel turnover all show that identity questions are rarely free-standing. They are entangled with constitution, temporal persistence, counting practices, and kind-membership. The archive should therefore ask not only whether x=y, but **under what description, with what criterion, and across which change**.

### 5. Spend brute thisness sparingly

Haecceity or primitive thisness is a live metaphysical option, but it should not become the archive’s first reflex whenever individuation gets hard. In many cases, form, organization, origin, relational position, and history do more explanatory work than a bare thisness posit. The archive should therefore treat **haecceity as a reserve posit**: sometimes useful, never cheap.

### 6. Prefer strict identity plus domain-sensitive individuation as the archive’s current working posture

The best current posture for the archive is:

- keep strict identity as non-relative numerical sameness;
- allow that **criteria of identity are often sortal-, form-, or history-sensitive**;
- treat discernibility principles as explanatory constraints rather than as a universal master-key;
- prefer qualitative, structural, and historical individuation before appealing to brute thisness;
- let different domains have different identity profiles without collapsing into mere conventionalism.

This is not a surrender to relativism. It is a refusal to confuse one thin formal notion with all the metaphysical work needed to apply it.

## Guardrails

- Do not collapse identity into resemblance or practical equivalence.
- Do not assume bare numerical identity tells us, by itself, how to count or track beings through change.
- Do not treat relative-identity cases as automatic refutations of strict identity.
- Do not invoke haecceity whenever qualitative explanation becomes difficult.
- Do not let domain sensitivity degenerate into pure convention.

## Failure modes

- **identity minimalism**: identity is treated as so thin that no criterion of application is ever required.
- **criterion inflation**: sortal or counting criteria are mistaken for replacements of identity itself.
- **discernibility dogmatism**: one version of the Identity of Indiscernibles is treated as an all-purpose solution.
- **cheap thisness**: primitive haecceities are used to paper over every hard case.
- **tracking confusion**: questions of persistence, constitution, and social continuity are conflated without discipline.

## Practical tests

1. Can the theory distinguish strict identity from looser sameness relations?
2. Can it explain why sortals, forms, or histories matter for many identity judgments without making identity itself merely relative?
3. Can it handle constitution and persistence puzzles without verbal handwaving?
4. Can it say when discernibility pressures are decisive and when they merely constrain?
5. Can it avoid primitive thisness unless other individuation resources have been seriously tried?

## Closing synthesis

The archive should treat **identity as strict, criteria of identity as domain-sensitive, discernibility as a real but non-total constraint, and thisness as a costly reserve posit**. That posture fits articulated layered realism better than either bare formalism or indiscriminate haecceitism: reality contains real individuals, but the ways individuals are counted, tracked, and distinguished are often structured by kind, form, relation, and history.
