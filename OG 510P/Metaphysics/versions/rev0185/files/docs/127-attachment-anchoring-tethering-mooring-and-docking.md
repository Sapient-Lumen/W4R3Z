# Attachment, Anchoring, Tethering, Mooring, and Docking

## Why this file exists

The archive now has explicit files on **hosting / lodging / carriage / guest occupancy**, **scaffolding / support / environmental enablement**, **porous boundaries / selective exchange**, **fusion / merger / coalescence**, **overlap / shared parts**, **migration / transplantation / porting**, and **rerouting / bypass / shunting**, but it still lacks one direct file saying what to do when a case is described as **attached to**, **anchored to**, **tethered to**, **moored to**, **docked with**, **mounted on**, **fastened to**, **latched onto**, **plugged into**, or **hitched to**.

That omission matters because the archive still risks nine opposite mistakes:

- treating every genuine **attachment** as if it were already **parthood**, **constitution**, or **fusion**,
- treating every **anchoring** relation as if it were only background support or mere location,
- treating every **tether** as if it were only proximity rather than constrained connection across maintained separation,
- treating every **dock** as if it were already one fused bearer rather than a governed coupling relation,
- treating every **mooring** as if it were either total immobilization or no real relation at all,
- treating every **mounted** or **plugged-in** peripheral as if it were simply housed by a host,
- treating **same anchor** as if it meant **same attached thing**,
- treating **same attached thing** as if it meant **same anchor**,
- or smuggling the differences among **attachment**, **anchoring**, **tethering**, **mooring**, **docking**, **mounting**, and **coupling** into neighboring files without typing them.

This file gives the archive a compact **attachment / anchoring / tethering / mooring / docking profile** so future revisions can say whether a case is chiefly about fixed anchoring to a site, constrained motion around an anchor, reversible docking for transfer or service, mounted peripheral attachment, hitched or coupled transport, or stronger incorporation only in harder cases.

## Compressed picture

> **Things can be genuinely attached while remaining numerically distinct.**

And more carefully:

> **Attachment, anchoring, tethering, mooring, and docking are typed ways in which one target is connected to another so as to secure, orient, restrain, align, stabilize, transfer, or coordinate without that alone settling parthood, constitution, ownership, or identity.**

## Why the default view should absorb this operator-family

The archive's working view already says:

- **process** matters, because attachment relations often unfold across time and can be temporary, cyclic, or regime-bound,
- **form and organization** matter, because not every connector, mount, tether, dock, or anchor carries the same persistence conditions,
- **powers and functions** matter, because attachments transmit loads, constrain motion, permit transfer, preserve alignment, or create service interfaces,
- **dependence pluralism** matters, because attachment, support, hosting, composition, constitution, realization, and grounding should not be collapsed,
- **sortals** matter, because a passenger trailer, a docked spacecraft, a moored vessel, a clipped sensor, and an implanted device are not one flat kind of case,
- **boundary discipline** matters, because connection across a boundary need not erase the boundary,
- and **artifact-risk discipline** matters, because labels such as *attached*, *mounted*, *paired*, *docked*, or *plugged in* often overstate metaphysical intimacy.

So the default view should absorb attachment-language without flattening it.

## What this operator-family is for

This operator-family is for cases where one target is **connected to another under a typed attachment relation**.

The relation may involve:

- a **tool, device, or peripheral mounted on another system**,
- a **worker secured to an anchorage or tether line**,
- a **boat moored to a buoy or site**,
- a **spacecraft docked to another spacecraft or station**,
- a **trailer or payload hitched to a vehicle**,
- or a **sensor, fixture, or module fastened to a substrate or frame**.

But cases that are **chiefly adhesive-bonded, welded, brazed, or soldered joints** belong under the joining / bonding / welding profile rather than here.

The key question is not simply whether two things touch, but **what sort of connection is doing the work and whether it carries any stronger claim than secured relation, restraint, alignment, service interface, or load-bearing linkage**.

## Why the archive needs it

### 1. Attachment is not the same as parthood or fusion

Two things can be connected in a real and load-bearing way without thereby becoming one bearer.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **mere attachment or fastening**,
- **temporary coupling**,
- **reversible docking**,
- **attached but numerically distinct coordination**,
- and **genuine incorporation only in harder cases**.

Otherwise it will keep sliding from **attached to** into **part of**.

### 2. Anchoring is not the same as mere support or location

An anchor relation can fix orientation, secure position, or bound movement without becoming the whole explanatory story of the attached target.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **anchored relative to a site or point**,
- **merely located near that site**,
- **environmentally supported without attachment**,
- **constitutively integrated into the anchoring base**,
- and **identity only in much narrower cases**.

Otherwise it will keep treating every secure point of attachment as if it were just background context or already a constituting substrate.

### 3. Tethering preserves separation under connection

A tether relation is important precisely because the connected items remain apart while motion, drift, or escape is constrained by the line or coupling relation.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **connected-with-separation maintained**,
- **direct fusion or merger**,
- **mere proximity without restraint**,
- **free migration away from the anchor or partner**,
- and **mixed cases only where tethering later becomes incorporation**.

Otherwise it will keep misreading maintained distance as lack of relation.

### 4. Docking is not the same as merger

Docked systems may align, connect, exchange, service, or permit passage while still remaining two bearers related by a governed interface.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **docked for transfer, service, or coordination**,
- **merged into one bearer**,
- **hosted within another system**,
- **mere side-by-side alignment without connection**,
- and **stronger incorporation only in narrower architectural cases**.

Otherwise it will keep treating every successful docking relation as if identity had already been settled.

### 5. Same anchor does not mean same attached thing

A dock, hitch, mooring point, or anchorage can remain the same while different vehicles, tools, workers, vessels, or modules attach to it across time.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **same anchor under changing attachments**,
- **same site under successive moorings or dockings**,
- **same mount under swapped peripherals**,
- **same scaffold point under different secured agents or loads**,
- and **same anchor-object pair only when that relation itself is the target**.

Otherwise it will keep letting anchor continuity masquerade as attached-object continuity.

### 6. Same attached thing does not mean same anchor

One target can continue across changed anchors, docks, moorings, or mounting sites.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **same target under changed attachment point**,
- **same vessel under changed mooring**,
- **same spacecraft under changed docking port**,
- **same device under changed mount or connector**,
- and **same coupled pair only in stronger paired-system cases**.

Otherwise it will keep letting attached-object continuity masquerade as anchor continuity.

### 7. Mounted or hitched connection is not the same as hosting

A mounted or hitched target may be secured to another thing without being housed within it, executed by it, or residing in it as a guest.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **mounted on or hitched to**,
- **hosted in or on**,
- **carried by**,
- **constitutively integrated into**,
- and **mixed support/transport/attachment architectures**.

Otherwise it will keep flattening attachment into hosting or carriage.

Cases whose explanatory center is **bearing support**, **suspension**, **hanging load carriage**, or **bracing under imposed load** belong under the support / bearing / suspension profile rather than here.

### 8. Reversibility and release profiles matter

Some attachment relations are designed to be rapidly disconnected, some to hold through load or motion, and some to become quasi-permanent only after additional fastening or integration.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **quick-release or routine detachment**,
- **stable but reversible attachment**,
- **load-bearing secured coupling**,
- **one-way or destructive separation only in harder cases**,
- and **mixed architectures where detachment conditions define the relation**.

Otherwise it will keep treating all connection strength as one flat scale.

## Typology of attachment-like cases

### A. Fixed anchoring to a site or substrate

The key feature is that one target is secured relative to a base, point, or substrate in a way that stabilizes position, orientation, or working relation.

**Examples:** a secured work line, an instrument fixed to a frame, a device attached to a mounting point.

### B. Tethered separation and restrained motion

The key feature is that connected items remain distinct and spatially separated while a tether constrains range, drift, or escape.

**Examples:** a restraint line, a tethered payload, a secured object allowed limited movement around an anchoring point.

### C. Mooring and station-keeping attachment

The key feature is that one vessel or platform is secured relative to a site while still allowing some bounded movement, drift, or reorientation.

**Examples:** a vessel moored to a buoy, a floating platform held by mooring lines, temporary secured station-keeping at a site.

### D. Docking and interface coupling

The key feature is that two systems are aligned and connected so that transfer, passage, servicing, or coordinated operation can occur without the systems thereby becoming one bearer.

**Examples:** docked spacecraft, service docking, coupled modules joined by a governed interface.

### E. Mounted, fastened, or hitched peripheral attachment

The key feature is that one target is externally secured to another for support, alignment, transport, or operation without straightforward residence or fusion.

**Examples:** peripherals mounted on a host device, payloads fastened to a frame, trailers hitched to a vehicle.

### F. Continuity theater through attachment labels

The key feature is that language of *attachment*, *pairing*, *mounting*, or *docking* hides what is really replacement, incorporation, or a merely nominal relation.

**Examples:** “same module” talk that tracks only a connector slot across swaps; “still docked” talk that hides full merger into a rebuilt system; “attached team” talk that is really just administrative assignment.

## Cross-domain pressure

### Anchors, tether lines, and load-bearing restraint

Safety cases pressure the archive because a secured line or anchorage creates a real point of attachment and a real relation of restraint without turning anchor and worker into one bearer.

### Mooring and bounded but mobile securing

Marine cases pressure the archive because a vessel can be genuinely secured to a buoy or site while still remaining a numerically distinct vessel capable of bounded motion, departure, and replacement by another vessel.

### Docking and governed interface connection

Space cases pressure the archive because two spacecraft can approach, align, and connect for passage or coordinated operation while still remaining two bearers linked by a docking interface.

## False moves to avoid

- Do **not** infer parthood from every case of being **attached to**.
- Do **not** infer fusion from every successful **dock**.
- Do **not** infer immobility from every **mooring**.
- Do **not** infer mere proximity from every **tether**.
- Do **not** infer same attached thing from same anchor.
- Do **not** infer same anchor from same attached thing.
- Do **not** infer hosting from every **mount** or **attachment point**.
- Do **not** infer one flat relation from **anchored to**, **tethered to**, **moored to**, **docked with**, **mounted on**, **plugged into**, **fastened to**, or **hitched to**.

## Default diagnostic questions

When a case is described as **attached to**, **anchored to**, **tethered to**, **moored to**, **docked with**, **mounted on**, **plugged into**, **fastened to**, or **hitched to**, ask:

1. What exactly is the **target** — one worker, vessel, device, module, vehicle, payload, or mixed organization?
2. Is the case chiefly about **anchoring**, **tethered restraint**, **mooring**, **docking**, **mounted peripheral attachment**, **coupled transport**, **constitutive integration**, or a mixed architecture?
3. What exactly is the **base or partner** — one anchorage, buoy, dock, frame, connector, vehicle, or service interface?
4. What is doing the explanatory work — **load transfer**, **motion restraint**, **position stabilization**, **alignment for transfer**, **temporary securing**, **support during operation**, or mixed basis?
5. Does the anchor remain the same under changing attachments, does the attached target remain the same under changed anchors, or is the case really about a changing **attachment pair**?
6. What is the relevant **grain** — whole bearer, mounted peripheral, connector-level relation, port-level coupling, or mixed levels?
7. What is the relevant **reversibility profile** — quick release, routine detachment, stable but reversible, one-way fastening, or destructive separation?
8. What is the nearest **comparison class** — location, hosting, carriage, overlap, fusion, migration, or constitution?
9. What is the relevant **artifact-risk profile** — interface label continuity, slot-tracking confusion, support-language inflation, or mixed?
10. Whether the best verdict is **typed attachment**, **typed anchoring**, **typed tethered restraint**, **typed mooring**, **typed docking/interface coupling**, **mounted peripheral attachment**, **stronger incorporation only in harder cases**, or **still unsettled**.

## Compression rule

Future revisions should normally treat **attachment / anchoring / tethering / mooring / docking** as one operator-family whenever the explanatory burden is: **one target connected to another under a typed secure relation; unresolved question whether the relation is mere contact, stabilized restraint, service interface, load-bearing coupling, hosting, carriage, constitution, or fusion; and the need to distinguish continuing attachment from continuing bearer identity rather than letting connection-language settle the ontology by itself.**
