# Laws, Powers, and Structure

This file addresses one of the main unresolved joints in the archive: how **laws**, **powers**, and **structure** relate.

## Why this file exists

The archive already treats powers and structure as indispensable, and it already rejects a featureless basal layer. But without a clearer account of laws, the synthesis can wobble between four unsatisfying pictures:

For a dedicated power profile — including the distinction between dispositions, abilities, powers, manifestation, masking, and basis — now see `54-powers-dispositions-abilities-and-manifestation.md`. For a dedicated structure profile — including the distinction between relation, pattern, network, representation, and relational constitution — now see `55-structure-patterns-networks-and-relational-constitution.md`. This file remains focused on how laws relate to powers and structure rather than on either operator taken in its own right.

1. laws as mere summaries,
2. laws as external governors,
3. laws as nothing over and above local powers,
4. or laws as deep structure with no clear bearer story.

The archive needs a tighter answer.

## Four live options

### 1. Best-system / Humean treatment

**Picture:** laws are the axioms or theorems of the best deductive system balancing simplicity and strength.

**What it gets right:**
- parsimony,
- sensitivity to theory-choice,
- resistance to spooky necessity.

**What it gets wrong for this archive:**
- makes modality too thin,
- makes powers look derivative or dispensable,
- and risks treating lawfulness as bookkeeping over an already lawless mosaic.

### 2. Governing-law treatment

**Picture:** laws are ontologically basic principles that govern what happens.

**What it gets right:**
- preserves objective necessity,
- explains why regularities are not accidental.

**What it gets wrong for this archive:**
- risks reifying laws into quasi-legislators,
- can leave unclear how laws connect to concrete entities and processes.

### 3. Power-first treatment

**Picture:** laws are grounded in the dispositional natures or powers of things.

**What it gets right:**
- gives modality real ontological purchase,
- keeps law tied to what things can do and undergo.

**What it gets wrong if left alone:**
- may underdescribe relational or system-wide constraints,
- may become too local if every law is read off isolated bearers.

### 4. Structural-modality treatment

**Picture:** laws articulate real modal structure: constraints built into relations, symmetries, and organization.

**What it gets right:**
- explains why some necessities are system-level,
- fits cases where organization matters as much as local intrinsic profile.

**What it gets wrong if left alone:**
- can become too abstract,
- and may underdescribe powers, production, or bearerhood.

## Current archive answer

The archive's current answer is a **hybrid but disciplined** one:

> **Laws are objective articulations of stable modal-structural constraints in a world of power-bearing processes.**

That means:

- laws are **more than summaries**,
- laws are **not external police** hovering over reality,
- powers are **part of what makes lawfulness real**,
- structure is **part of what makes lawfulness global, relational, and system-sensitive**,
- and process matters because many lawful profiles concern maintenance, development, and dynamic stability rather than static snapshots alone.

## Archive commitments

### 1. Modality is prior to mere description

If powers belong in ontology, then lawfulness cannot be only a concise description of what happens to occur.

### 2. Laws are not a second layer of cosmic management

The archive should avoid talk that makes laws sound like ontological supervisors standing apart from the beings they govern.

### 3. Some lawfulness is local, some is structural

Some lawful regularities track what particular things can do in virtue of their powers. Others track system-level organization, symmetries, or structural constraints. A good metaphysics should allow both.

### 4. Law-talk is level-sensitive

There may be lawlike regularities at chemistry, biology, psychology, or social scales that are not fundamental but are still objective, stable, and explanatory. Such regularities need not be fundamental to be real.

### 5. The deepest laws question is really a modality question

When the archive asks what laws are, it is usually asking a deeper question: **where necessity, constraint, and possibility come from**.

## Compression rule

Do not let the archive use the phrase **laws of nature** without saying which of the following is doing the real work in the case at hand:

- best-system summary,
- local powers,
- global or relational structure,
- or some mixed account.

If the archive cannot say that, its law-talk is still too loose.

## Working conclusion

The archive should currently prefer this order of explanation:

1. **powers and structure are ontologically primary enough to ground modality,**
2. **process explains why lawfulness often concerns dynamic stability and change,**
3. **laws are derivative articulations of this modal organization, not merely verbal summaries and not free-floating governors.**

In compressed form:

> **Modality first, law second.**

That is the present archive default. It remains defeasible.
