# Circulation, Throughput, Recirculation, and Organized Throughflow

## Why this file exists

The archive now has explicit files on **turnover / renewal**, **porous boundaries / selective exchange**, **release / mobilization**, **leakage / uncontrolled escape**, **accumulation / pooling**, **depletion / drawdown**, **replenishment / recharge**, **gating / selective responsiveness**, and **organization / mechanism**, but it still lacks one direct file saying what to do when a case is described as **circulating**, **flowing through**, **recirculating**, **being flushed**, **having high throughput**, **having a short residence time**, **being kept in motion**, or **remaining the same system by organized passage rather than static stock**.

That omission matters because the archive still risks ten opposite mistakes:

- treating every **throughput** case as if it were already **turnover of constituents**,
- treating every **circulation** case as if it were already **mere transport to somewhere else**,
- treating every **recirculation** case as if it were already **simple repetition with no organizational role**,
- treating every **throughflow** case as if the system were just a passive corridor rather than an organized bearer,
- treating every **flushing** case as if it were already **release**, **leakage**, or **depletion**,
- collapsing **stock size** and **flow rate** into one flat variable,
- missing the difference between **same continuing route or circuit with changing parcels** and **same continuing stock with changing constituents**,
- missing the difference between **residence time inside a bearer** and **lifespan of the bearer itself**,
- missing the difference between **circulation that sustains a system** and **throughput that simply loads it**,
- or smuggling the difference between **circulation**, **recirculation**, **throughput**, **flushing**, **turnover**, **release**, **leakage**, and **transport** into neighboring files without typing it.

This file gives the archive a compact **circulation / throughput / recirculation / organized-throughflow profile** so future revisions can say whether a case is chiefly about closed-loop circulation, open throughflow across a continuing bearer, recirculation for reuse or repeated exposure, clearance by flushing, throughput-limited operation, or some mixed architecture of maintained identity through routed passage.

## Working default

The archive should now say explicitly:

> **Some real things persist not by keeping contents fixed, and not only by replacing constituents, but by organizing ongoing passage through channels, loops, reservoirs, queues, tissues, infrastructures, and institutions.**

In compressed form:

> **Not every stable bearer is static. Some persistence is throughflow-governed.**

And more carefully:

> **Circulation, throughput, recirculation, and organized throughflow are best understood as typed ways in which one real organism, tissue, organ, reservoir, wetland, network, queueing system, building, institution, or processual infrastructure remains the same continuing bearer while parcels, signals, workers, vehicles, fluids, or materials pass through, return through, or are cleared through its organized pathways.**

This fits the archive's wider commitments:

- **organization / constraints / mechanisms** still matter because circulation depends on pathways, pumps, valves, interfaces, checkpoints, and routing rules,
- **porous boundaries / selective exchange** still matter because throughflow presupposes controlled entry, passage, and exit,
- **turnover / renewal** still matter because some circulation refreshes a stock while other circulation merely moves parcels through without replacing the relevant bearer,
- **accumulation / depletion / replenishment** still matter because throughput can coexist with stock increase, stock decline, or near-steady-state abundance,
- **delay / lag** still matter because circulation can stall, queue, or slow without ceasing to be circulation,
- **friction / attenuation / interference** still matter because media and channels can shape cost, loss, and distortion during passage,
- and **life / organisms / living form** still matter because living systems are among the clearest cases where identity depends on ongoing circulation rather than static possession of one parcel-set.

## Core distinctions

### 1. Circulation is not the same as turnover

Turnover is about replacement of constituents in a continuing bearer. Circulation is about organized passage through a continuing bearer, often whether or not replacement of the relevant stock is the main point.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **same circuit with moving parcels**,
- **same bearer with replaced constituents**,
- **ongoing circulation under roughly stable pool size**,
- **ongoing turnover under local replacement**,
- and **mixed cases only where circulation is one mechanism of turnover**.

Otherwise it will keep mistaking motion-through for replacement-of.

### 2. Throughput is not the same as stock

A system can have high throughput with little stored stock, low throughput with large stock, or high throughput and high stock together. Flow rate and inventory are not the same variable.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **how much is passing through per unit time**,
- **how much is present at once**,
- **same system under high throughput with modest occupancy**,
- **same system under large stock with slow movement**,
- and **mixed cases only where flow and stock co-vary in one architecture**.

Otherwise it will keep reading active passage as mere abundance.

### 3. Recirculation is not the same as simple repetition

Something can loop back through one continuing system because reuse, repeated exposure, mixing, staged processing, or monitoring requires it. That is not just “again.”

So the archive should distinguish:

- **same parcel or class routed back through a loop**,
- **new parcel entering the same corridor for the first time**,
- **organized recirculation for reuse, checking, or conditioning**,
- **mere recurrence without routing significance**,
- and **mixed cases only where looping and recurrence coincide**.

Otherwise it will keep flattening loop architecture into generic repetition.

### 4. Flushing is not the same as release or leakage

A system can deliberately clear or wash through a region, queue, or compartment without thereby merely venting pressure or accidentally losing contents.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **organized clearance by throughflow**,
- **controlled release from prior holding**,
- **uncontrolled leakage across compromised containment**,
- **same corridor being washed or cleared**,
- and **mixed cases only where flushing produces release or stops leakage**.

Otherwise it will keep confusing clearing-through with letting-go or losing-control.

### 5. Residence time is not the same as bearer persistence

How long parcels stay in a system is not how long the system itself exists. A lake, tissue, queueing network, or institution can persist much longer or shorter than the mean time spent by what passes through it.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **parcel residence time**,
- **bearer lifespan or institutional career**,
- **same system with short or long dwell times**,
- **same bearer under rapid or slow throughflow**,
- and **timescale conflation only in weaker analysis**.

Otherwise it will keep inferring bearer persistence directly from transit time.

### 6. Throughflow is not the same as mere transport elsewhere

Sometimes the route itself matters: the bearer conditions, filters, mixes, exposes, nourishes, cools, clears, or coordinates what passes through.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **same bearer as organized route with functional passage**,
- **mere displacement from origin to destination**,
- **same organ, wetland, furnace, market, or platform doing work on what passes through**,
- **simple transport where the corridor is incidental**,
- and **mixed cases only where transport and processing coincide**.

Otherwise it will keep missing systems whose reality partly consists in routed passage itself.

### 7. High throughput is not the same as good functioning

More passage is not automatically healthier, truer, or better. Systems can be overloaded, starved, or destabilized by excessive flow just as they can be choked by too little.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **healthy circulation or throughput**,
- **overload under excess flow**,
- **under-circulation or stagnation**,
- **same system with changed operating regime**,
- and **value verdicts only where the role profile supports them**.

Otherwise it will keep smuggling efficiency norms into ontology.

## Typology of circulation and throughflow cases

### A. Closed-loop circulation

The key feature is that one continuing system routes material through a loop and back again.

**Examples:** blood circulation; coolant circulation; circulating air in a climate-control system.

### B. Open throughflow across a continuing bearer

The key feature is that inputs enter, pass through, and exit a continuing system that remains the same organized route or processor.

**Examples:** water flowing through a wetland; work items moving through a service pipeline; traffic moving through a transport network.

### C. Recirculation for reuse, conditioning, or repeated exposure

The key feature is that some of what has already passed is routed back through for another pass.

**Examples:** return-air recirculation; recirculated process water; recirculating immune-cell traffic through lymphoid organs.

### D. Flushing and clearance by organized passage

The key feature is that throughflow clears, dilutes, washes out, or removes burdens from a continuing region.

**Examples:** flushing a line; cerebrospinal or glymphatic-style clearance; washout of a reservoir or queue backlog.

### E. Throughput-limited operation

The key feature is that the system is typed in part by how much it can move through per unit time.

**Examples:** network throughput; manufacturing throughput; administrative case-processing throughput.

### F. Homeostatic steady-state flux

The key feature is that a system remains at one broad scale through continual passage at another.

**Examples:** organ-scale stability with continual cellular flux; metabolic steady state; reservoir turnover under roughly stable volume.

## Cross-domain pressure

### Physiology and circulation

Physiology pressures the archive because blood, lymph, air, cerebrospinal fluid, nutrients, wastes, and cells can remain in continuous passage while one organ or organism persists as the same organized bearer.

### Ecology, hydrology, and residence time

Wetlands, lakes, aquifers, reservoirs, and ecosystems pressure the archive because they are often best understood through residence time, flushing, retention, and throughflow rather than through one static contained stock.

### Queueing, logistics, and infrastructure

Infrastructures pressure the archive because one system can be the same network, court, warehouse, or platform while work items, vehicles, packages, passengers, records, and requests continuously pass through it.

### Institutions and administrative circulation

Institutions pressure the archive because documents, authorizations, cases, people, money, and signals can circulate through one continuing office-structure without that making the institution identical to any one parcel-set.

## False moves to avoid

- Do **not** infer turnover from every circulation case.
- Do **not** infer release from every flushing case.
- Do **not** infer leakage from every boundary-crossing loss.
- Do **not** infer abundance from every high-throughput case.
- Do **not** infer good function from every fast-moving system.
- Do **not** infer simple repetition from every recirculation loop.
- Do **not** infer short bearer life from short residence time.
- Do **not** infer that the route is metaphysically idle just because parcels change.
- Do **not** infer one flat relation from **circulating**, **flowing through**, **recirculating**, **flushing**, **clearing**, **washing out**, **processing throughput**, or **continuous passage**.

## Default diagnostic questions

When a case is described as **circulating**, **flowing through**, **recirculating**, **flushing**, **washing out**, **processing at throughput**, or **remaining the same system under ongoing passage**, ask:

1. What exactly is the **target** — one organ, one organism, one loop, one wetland, one reservoir, one queueing system, one building, one infrastructure, one office-structure, or one mixed organization?
2. Is the case chiefly about **closed-loop circulation**, **open throughflow**, **recirculation**, **flushing / clearance**, **throughput-limited operation**, **homeostatic steady-state flux**, or a mixed architecture?
3. What is actually **doing the routing work** — pumps, gradients, valves, corridors, schedules, queues, routing rules, returns, gates, or mixed?
4. What remains **real underneath the passage** — same bearer as route, same stock under active flow, same institution under moving cases, same tissue under cellular flux, stronger turnover, replacement, or ending only in harder cases, or mixed?
5. What is the nearest **comparison class** — turnover, replenishment, release, leakage, transport, delay, accumulation, or artifact?
6. What is the relevant **stock / flow profile** — high throughput at low occupancy, low throughput at high occupancy, recirculation with stable stock, flushing with depletion, or mixed?
7. What is the relevant **timescale profile** — parcel residence time, loop time, queue time, washout time, seasonal circulation, bearer lifespan, or mixed?
8. What is the relevant **artifact-risk profile** — sensor placement bias, throughput counted without losses, accounting lag, proxy circulation with little real passage, or mixed?
9. Is the best verdict **typed circulation**, **typed throughflow**, **typed recirculation**, **typed flushing / clearance**, **typed throughput constraint**, **typed homeostatic flux**, **apparent flow / proxy artifact**, or **still unsettled**?
