# Social Ontology, Status Functions, and Institutional Reality

## Why this file exists

The archive already has files on teleology, relations, levels, constitution, distributed being, causation, commitment, and truthmaking. But it still lacks a direct rule for a recurrent question:

> **How can institutions, offices, money, law, corporations, platforms, and other social realities be dependent all the way down and still be genuinely real?**

Without a direct answer, the archive keeps risking six predictable mistakes:

- reducing social reality to **mere words, beliefs, or conventions**,
- inflating it into **spooky supra-human entities** with no clear dependence basis,
- confusing **collective intentionality** with every form of organized social reality,
- confusing **normative force** with **causal efficacy**,
- treating **records, infrastructures, and enforcement** as mere evidence rather than sometimes part of what constitutes the case,
- and sliding between **construction**, **constitution**, **grounding**, **realization**, and **causal maintenance** as if social dependence were one flat relation.

This file gives the archive a compact **social-ontology profile** so future revisions can say what kind of social target is at issue, what sustains it, how it persists, and what sort of reality-status it earns.

## Working default

The archive should now say explicitly:

> **Many social realities are derivative but robust: they depend on persons, practices, records, recognition, enforcement, material supports, and organized roles, yet they are not therefore unreal, merely semantic, or metaphysically optional.**

This fits the archive’s wider commitments:

- **form** still matters, because institutions often depend on organized rule- and role-structure,
- **process** still matters, because social realities are often maintained through ongoing uptake, enactment, repair, transmission, and enforcement,
- **power** still matters, because offices, rules, permissions, liabilities, and authorities are modal profiles, not mere verbal decorations,
- **structure** still matters, because positions, standing relations, and institutional roles are often constitutive,
- and **grounding** still matters, because social facts are not free-floating even when they are normatively loaded and causally potent.

## Core distinctions

### 1. Collective behavior is not yet institutional reality

People can move together, copy one another, or converge strategically without thereby generating a durable social entity.

A target becomes more strongly social-ontological when some combination of the following appears:

- shared or coordinated uptake,
- rule-governed or norm-governed expectations,
- recognizable roles or statuses,
- durable records or public markers,
- sanctions, permissions, or obligations,
- organized reproduction through time.

So a crowd, a queue, a mob, a corporation, and a court are not one flat kind of case.

### 2. Recognition is important, but not always sufficient by itself

Some social facts depend heavily on recognition, acceptance, or collective uptake. But many also depend on:

- records,
- offices,
- legal codification,
- enforcement structures,
- material infrastructures,
- technological mediation,
- and patterned organizational routines.

The archive should therefore resist both extremes:

- **pure mentalism**, where institutions are nothing but shared attitudes,
- and **pure materialism**, where social status disappears into physical description.

### 3. Status, role, and organization are not one flat phenomenon

The archive should distinguish at least:

- **status assignment** — someone or something counts as having a standing position or entitlement,
- **role structure** — the target exists within a system of permissions, obligations, competencies, or tasks,
- **organization** — coordinated positions and processes reproduce a structured whole,
- **institution** — a relatively durable socially instituted order with normative and practical force,
- **group agency** — a case where action, intention, or responsibility may be attributable at the collective level,
- **social artifact** — documents, tokens, platforms, registers, and designed supports that help constitute or stabilize the social case.

Money, offices, marriages, corporations, courts, passports, property titles, and platform identities are not all built in the same way.

### 4. Normative force and causal force must be kept distinct but connected

Some social facts matter because they alter what people are allowed, required, empowered, licensed, or expected to do. Others matter because they causally reorganize incentives, traffic, documents, resources, access, or enforcement.

The archive should therefore distinguish:

- **normative standing** — rights, duties, permissions, liabilities, authorities,
- **causal organization** — what gets moved, blocked, enabled, stabilized, or reproduced,
- **mixed cases** — where normative standing and causal organization mutually reinforce one another.

A legal office is not only a belief-pattern, but neither is its reality exhausted by its causal downstream effects.

### 5. Social construction is not unreality

To say that a target is socially constructed is not yet to say:

- that it is merely projected,
- that it is optional at will,
- that it has no truth conditions,
- that it lacks causal force,
- or that it is “just semantic”.

Construction language should instead trigger a further question:

> **Constructed by what, through what, under what rules, with what anchors, and with what persistence conditions?**

### 6. Institutions are often multi-based

Many social realities depend on more than one sort of base at once. The archive should therefore expect mixed dependence profiles involving:

- persons and coordinated action,
- collective recognition or acceptance,
- language and public representation,
- written or digital records,
- legal or customary rules,
- material infrastructures,
- organizations and enforcement,
- and historical path-dependence.

So the right question is often not “is this mental or material?” but rather “what combination of bases is constitutively doing the work here?”

## What this file does **not** say

It does **not** say:

- that every social target is a group agent,
- that every institution is reducible to collective intention,
- that normative statuses are basic features of the universe,
- that derivative reality is second-rate or unreal,
- or that every social ontology must accept the same construction mechanism.

The point is only that **social reality needs a typed metaphysical profile rather than a flat decision between reduction and mystification**.

## Archive default

The current archive default is:

1. **Treat many social entities as derivative but robust.** Dependence on persons, practices, rules, records, and enforcement does not make them fictional.
2. **Do not reduce social reality to attitude alone.** Recognition matters, but many institutions also require records, offices, material support, and enforceable standing.
3. **Do not collapse normative status into causal pattern.** Some realities matter because they change what is permitted, required, or authorized, even when that standing is socially instituted.
4. **Keep construction mechanisms typed.** Count-as assignment, role allocation, legal codification, procedural reproduction, infrastructural embedding, and algorithmic mediation are not one flat process.
5. **Expect mixed dependence.** Social cases often combine grounding-like, constitutive, realizational, and causal-maintenance relations rather than one master link.
6. **Treat persistence as socially and materially sustained.** Institutions persist not merely because people happen to keep thinking of them, but because uptake, records, routines, enforcement, and infrastructure stabilize them through time.

## Social-ontology profile rule for future revisions

Before inferring a metaphysical verdict from a social or institutional case, specify:

1. **target type** — practice, norm, role, status, office, organization, institution, group agent, social artifact, platform-mediated system, or mixed case;
2. **construction basis** — shared intention, collective acceptance, habitual coordination, legal rule, customary norm, record system, technical infrastructure, enforcement regime, or mixed basis;
3. **dependence relation** — grounding, constitution, realization, causal maintenance, distributed organization, or mixed relation;
4. **normative profile** — permission, obligation, authority, liability, entitlement, validity, recognition condition, sanction profile, or none;
5. **causal profile** — what the case enables, constrains, routes, allocates, blocks, stabilizes, or reproduces;
6. **persistence mode** — continuing acceptance, role occupancy, record continuity, legal continuity, organizational continuity, infrastructural continuity, or mixed persistence;
7. **reality verdict** — basic, derivative but robust, thinly dependent, practice-dependent objective, merely representational, or still unsettled.

Otherwise the archive will keep sliding from “socially instituted” to “merely made up”, or from “causally influential” to “metaphysically basic”.

## Stress cases

### Money

Money is not just paper, metal, or digits; nor is it merely a shared hallucination. It is typically a socially instituted status sustained by recognition, record systems, legal structures, practical uptake, and enforcement.

### Offices and authorities

A judge, mayor, registrar, or moderator often exists as an office-bearer only within a larger rule- and role-structure. The office is not reducible to the occupant, but neither is it independent of the institutional system that constitutes it.

### Corporations and organizations

A corporation is not merely a heap of people or documents. It is often a structured, legally and organizationally sustained entity with role differentiation, persistence conditions, decision procedures, assets, liabilities, and sometimes group-level explanatory profiles.

### Borders, property, and jurisdiction

These are not merely lines or brute physical facts. They depend on records, recognition, enforcement, mapping practices, legal standing, and coordinated institutional reproduction.

### Platform-mediated identities and access systems

Digital accounts, permissions, credentials, and moderated spaces often show that contemporary social ontology is not just face-to-face recognition. Databases, interfaces, tokens, and procedural infrastructures can be constitutive rather than merely evidential.

## Compressions earned by this file

This file compresses several earlier tensions:

- the **teleology** file can now distinguish institutional role-ends from design purpose and agential intention more directly,
- the **relations** file can now distinguish thin social linkage from constitutive normative position,
- the **levels** file can now treat social organization as a domain with local autonomy without forcing basal independence,
- the **commitment** file can now classify institutions as derivative but robust without wavering between realism and fictionalism,
- the **constitution** file can now separate office-occupancy and institution-membership from ordinary material-coincidence puzzles,
- and the **distributed-being** file can now treat institutions as role-structured and often infrastructurally distributed rather than forcing them into compact-object talk.

## Current practical default for the archive

When in doubt:

- begin with the **least inflated** profile that still preserves durable normative and causal structure,
- prefer **typed social construction** over both eliminativism and spooky group-mind rhetoric,
- and ask not merely whether the target is “social”, but **how it is instituted, what sustains it, what powers it carries, and what would break if it were eliminated**.
