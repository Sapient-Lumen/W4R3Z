# Overlap, Shared Parts, Interpenetration, and Partial Commonality

## Why this file exists

The archive now has explicit files on **co-location**, **constitution**, **porous boundaries**, **succession-lineage continuity**, **duplication-branching continuity**, and **fusion-merger-coalescence**, but it still lacks one direct file saying what to do when things are said to **overlap**, **share parts**, **partially coincide**, **interpenetrate**, **have common members**, **share infrastructure**, **partly occupy the same region**, or **be distinct without being disjoint**.

That omission matters because the archive still risks eight opposite mistakes:

- treating every case of **overlap** as if it forced **identity**,
- treating every case of **shared parts** as if it forced **fusion** or one larger bearer,
- treating every case of **co-location** as if it settled whether overlap is spatial, mereological, constitutional, or only representational,
- treating **partial commonality** as if it were too weak to matter metaphysically,
- treating **distinctness** as if it required total disjointness,
- treating every **shared member / shared component / shared resource** case as if one bearer had automatically formed,
- treating **interpenetration** as if it were the same thing as ordinary shared-part overlap,
- or treating all overlap talk as if it were merely a notational nuisance rather than a pressure point for counting, persistence, and dependence.

This file gives the archive a compact **overlap-sharing profile** so future revisions can say whether a case is chiefly about shared proper parts, full material coincidence, overlapping location without shared parts, role/member overlap, temporal overlap, or only loose practical commonality.

## Working default

The archive should now say explicitly:

> **Distinct things need not be wholly disjoint.**

In compressed form:

> **Overlap is not identity, and disjointness is not the only alternative.**

And more carefully:

> **A later case can involve real overlap, shared parts, common location, or partial commonality without thereby forcing identity, fusion, constitution, or elimination; once overlap language appears, the verdict must be typed rather than forced into one flat story of sameness, disjoint plurality, or automatic merger.**

This fits the archive's wider commitments:

- **mereology** still matters because parthood and overlap are not optional if part-sharing is real,
- **space and location** still matter because overlapping regions do not automatically imply shared parts,
- **constitution** still matters because exact coincidence can involve one basis under several kind-sensitive descriptions without making every overlap case constitutional,
- **porous boundaries** still matter because exchange across boundaries is different from stable shared parts,
- **fusion-merger** still matters because overlap is weaker than many becoming one,
- **duplication-branching** still matters because one becoming many differs from several things partly sharing one basis,
- **social ontology** still matters because institutions, jurisdictions, offices, and networks can overlap in members, authority, or infrastructure without becoming one institution,
- **life and organization** still matter because host systems, symbioses, and nested individuals can involve sharing, hosting, or interpenetrating organization without one simple counting rule,
- **reference and revision** still matter because the same public label can hide whether the case is one thing with shared basis, two overlapping things, or only an underdescribed mixed case,
- and **comparative choice** still matters because some domains may tolerate overlap better than others.

## Core distinctions

### 1. Overlap is not identity

Two entities can share some parts, or even all currently relevant material parts, without that fact alone deciding whether they are identical.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **identity**,
- **partial overlap**,
- **complete coincidence**,
- **constitution**,
- and **distinctness with shared basis**.

Otherwise it will keep letting commonality do the work of sameness.

### 2. Mereological overlap is not the same as spatial overlap

Things can overlap by **sharing parts**, or they can have **overlapping locations** without sharing parts, depending on the locative and mereological profile.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **shared proper parts**,
- **exact co-location with shared parts**,
- **overlapping regions without shared parts**,
- **nested location**,
- and **mere adjacency or contact**.

Otherwise it will keep moving too quickly from place-talk to parthood-talk.

### 3. Shared parts are not yet fusion

Two things can overlap because they share some parts while remaining two things rather than one fused bearer.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **partial overlap**,
- **common substructure**,
- **temporary overlap**,
- **higher-order fusion**,
- and **mere coordination around a shared resource**.

Otherwise it will keep promoting part-sharing into one-bearer talk too quickly.

### 4. Complete coincidence is not the same as every overlap case

Some puzzles concern entities that share all their current material parts or location; others concern only partial commonality.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **partial overlap**,
- **all-part coincidence**,
- **constitution with divergent modal or historical profile**,
- **same thing under several descriptions**,
- and **still unsettled coincidence**.

Otherwise it will keep letting statue/clay-style pressure dominate every overlap case.

### 5. Interpenetration is a special pressure case

A case of interpenetration — if admitted — is not ordinary overlap by shared parts. It is a case where regions overlap though the entities may remain disjoint.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **ordinary shared-part overlap**,
- **co-location with common basis**,
- **interpenetration without shared parts**,
- **multilocation / unusual locative profile**,
- and **impossible-in-domain but metaphysically imaginable cases**.

Otherwise it will keep confusing strange locative cases with mundane part-sharing.

### 6. Shared members or infrastructure do not automatically yield one social bearer

Institutions, jurisdictions, research programs, networks, and offices can overlap in personnel, records, resources, or authority without collapsing into one entity.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **member overlap**,
- **role overlap**,
- **infrastructure overlap**,
- **jurisdiction overlap**,
- and **institutional merger**.

Otherwise it will keep mistaking common participants or platforms for one institution.

### 7. Overlap can be staged, local, or aspect-relative

One target may overlap another in matter, location, function, time-slice, role, or jurisdiction while remaining distinct in another respect.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **material overlap**,
- **spatial overlap**,
- **temporal overlap**,
- **functional overlap**,
- **normative/jurisdictional overlap**,
- and **mixed aspect overlap**.

Otherwise it will keep forcing one count-policy across heterogeneous dimensions.

### 8. Shared basis does not automatically settle persistence

When overlapping things change, the shared basis may persist while one bearer ends, both persist, or a new relation of constitution or merger emerges.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **persistent overlap**,
- **divergence after partial commonality**,
- **successor formation from a shared basis**,
- **fusion following overlap**,
- and **withdrawal from overlap without loss of one bearer**.

Otherwise it will keep treating overlap as if it froze all later persistence questions in advance.

## Typology of overlap-cases

### A. Partial mereological overlap

Two entities share some but not all proper parts.

**Examples:** partly shared material bases, overlapping systems, intersecting infrastructures, intersecting territories if a part-structure is in play.

### B. Complete coincidence / common basis cases

Two candidates share all currently relevant material parts or location, but differ in kind, modal profile, history, or persistence conditions.

**Examples:** statue/clay style cases, ordinary-object/material-basis cases, office/occupant style analogues where one basis supports more than one count.

### C. Spatial overlap without shared parts

The locative profile overlaps even though the entities are treated as disjoint in the relevant mereological sense.

**Examples:** interpenetration thought experiments, unusual field/object overlays, non-material or mixed-category co-location cases.

### D. Shared-member or shared-resource overlap

Two organizations, teams, jurisdictions, or systems share members, infrastructure, funding channels, records, or platforms without becoming one bearer.

**Examples:** overlapping boards, federated institutions, cross-appointed offices, interoperable platforms, nested governance structures.

### E. Temporal or stage overlap

Two temporally extended entities, stages, or processes overlap for part of their duration without being one continuous token.

**Examples:** overlapping projects, overlapping process-phases, temporal-part or stage-theoretic cases, transition periods between offices or systems.

### F. Overlap that matures into something else

A case begins as overlap but later becomes fusion, separation, constitution, or successor replacement.

**Examples:** shared infrastructure preceding merger, parasitic or symbiotic incorporation, staged institutional consolidation, temporary double-booking of one role-basis.

## Cross-domain pressure

### Material objects and constitution

Ordinary-object and constitution debates show that shared matter or coincident location do not by themselves answer whether there is one object, two coincident objects, one constituted thing plus its basis, or only one thing under different sortals.

### Space, regions, and interpenetration

Location theory pressures the archive to separate overlapping exact location from overlap by shared parts. The archive should not treat spatial overlap and mereological overlap as guaranteed mirrors.

### Organisms, symbioses, and nested biological individuals

Biology pressures the archive because individuals can share environments, scaffolds, and even durable incorporated components without every shared basis becoming either one simple organism or mere background noise.

### Institutions, jurisdictions, and networks

Social cases pressure the archive because shared members, records, platforms, and overlapping authority are common, but those do not always create one institution or dissolve one into another.

### Temporal and processual cases

Processes, stages, and events can overlap in duration or share a phase without being one event-token. The archive should not assume that overlap in time or transition-space forces one process identity.

## False moves to avoid

- Do **not** infer identity from every overlap.
- Do **not** infer fusion from every shared part.
- Do **not** infer shared parts from every shared location.
- Do **not** infer constitution from every coincidence case.
- Do **not** infer one institution from every shared member or platform.
- Do **not** infer impossibility from every strange overlap case without stating the regime/domain assumptions.
- Do **not** flatten partial overlap, complete coincidence, interpenetration, and loose commonality into one relation.
- Do **not** let one shared basis settle all later persistence and counting questions.

## Default test for future revisions

When a future revision says that two or more things **overlap**, **share parts**, **partly coincide**, **interpenetrate**, **share a basis**, **share members**, **share infrastructure**, or **are not wholly disjoint**, it should ask:

1. **What is the target?**
   - object, process, organism, institution, office, region, field, infrastructure, lineage, or mixed?
2. **What sort of overlap is claimed?**
   - shared parts, complete coincidence, overlapping location, temporal overlap, member/resource overlap, jurisdiction overlap, or mixed?
3. **What exactly is shared?**
   - matter, parts, location, boundary segment, role, office, members, records, infrastructure, time-slice, or only description?
4. **What remains unshared?**
   - kind, function, persistence conditions, authority, control, history, modal profile, or mixed?
5. **What is the locative profile?**
   - exact co-location, partial region overlap, nested location, interpenetration, diffuse distribution, or not mainly spatial?
6. **What is the dependence profile?**
   - constitution, composition, support, hosting, shared basis, mutual use, no dependence claim, or mixed?
7. **What is the stability profile?**
   - momentary overlap, stable overlap, staged overlap, overlap tending toward fusion, overlap tending toward separation, or mixed?
8. **What is the verdict?**
   - partial overlap between distinct bearers, complete coincidence needing constitution/identity analysis, interpenetration without shared parts, shared-member/resource overlap without one bearer, fusion in progress, or still unsettled?

## Compression rule

> **When the archive says that things overlap or share parts, it must state whether the case is one of partial mereological overlap, complete coincidence, overlapping location without shared parts, member/resource overlap, temporal overlap, or overlap maturing into fusion or separation, what exactly is shared and unshared, and why that profile does not by itself settle identity, constitution, or merger.**

## Why this improves the archive

It lets the archive say, in one place, that:

- some overlap cases are **shared-part pluralities**,
- some are **coincidence/constitution** cases,
- some are **locative overlap without shared parts**,
- some are **social or infrastructural overlaps without merger**,
- some are **temporal overlap cases**,
- and some are **transitional overlaps** that later harden into fusion, separation, or retyping.

That is a direct improvement over forcing every difficult case into the binary of **same thing** versus **wholly disjoint things**.
