# Succession, Inheritance, Descent, and Lineage Continuity

## Why this file exists

The archive now has explicit files on **dormancy**, **delegation**, **repair-resilience**, **onset**, **cessation**, and **return**, but it still lacks one direct file saying what to do when something is said to **continue through successors**, **pass down a role**, **persist as a lineage**, **inherit a charter**, **transmit a tradition**, **descend from an earlier bearer**, or **remain the same in one sense while changing bearer in another**.

That omission matters because the archive still risks eight opposite mistakes:

- treating every **successor chain** as if it were automatically **one numerically identical bearer**,
- treating every **replacement** as if it erased all real continuity,
- treating **lineage**, **office**, **institution**, **practice**, **species**, **version**, and **brand** continuity as if they were one flat phenomenon,
- treating inherited **name**, **design**, **record**, or **charter** as if they automatically guaranteed the very same thing,
- treating **genealogical descent** as if it were merely superficial resemblance,
- treating a stable **role** or **office** as if it could not outlast any individual office-holder,
- treating branching, fission, or contested inheritance as if continuity questions disappeared once ancestry was mentioned,
- or treating continuity-through-succession as if it settled nothing beyond historical narrative.

This file gives the archive a compact **succession-lineage profile** so future revisions can say whether a case is chiefly about persistence, office succession, lineage continuity, charter continuity, template inheritance, tradition transmission, or successor replacement under inherited authority or design.

## Working default

The archive should now say explicitly:

> **A real target can continue through succession, descent, inheritance, or lineage continuity without that continuity being one thing.**

In compressed form:

> **Continuity through successors is not identity.**

And more carefully:

> **A later bearer may inherit a role, lineage position, charter, design template, authority route, or practice-pattern from an earlier one; what persists may be one office, one lineage, one institution, one historically organized practice, or only a successor series, and the verdict must be typed.**

This fits the archive's wider commitments:

- **identity and sortals** still matter because not every inheritance relation carries the same persistence lesson,
- **process** still matters because succession is historically organized rather than a mere snapshot resemblance,
- **social ontology** still matters because offices, institutions, and rule-governed roles can outlast particular performers,
- **natural kinds and biological individuality** still matter because some real continuities are genealogical or lineage-like,
- **teleology and function** still matter because inherited roles and designs can preserve directed organization without preserving one bearer,
- **repair and return** still matter because restored operation differs from successor continuation,
- **delegation** still matters because acting through others differs from being replaced by them,
- **onset and cessation** still matter because succession often begins where one bearer ends,
- **reference and designation** still matter because one name can track a lineage, an office, an institution, or only a brand-like series,
- and **revision and elimination** still matter because successor theories or successor organizations can preserve some target-features while abandoning others.

## Core distinctions

### 1. Persistence is not succession

A thing may persist through time, or a later thing may succeed it while preserving some role, lineage, pattern, or function. Those are not the same relation.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **one bearer persisting**,
- **successor replacing a bearer in one continuing role or line**,
- **one lineage continuing through new members**,
- **one institution continuing through turnover**,
- and **mere series resemblance without continuity worth promoting**.

Otherwise it will keep mistaking continuity of office, line, or practice for strict identity.

### 2. Lineage continuity is not mere category continuity

That two items belong to the same kind is weaker than descent. That one descends from another is also not the same thing as one bearer simply surviving.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **same kind across many tokens**,
- **genealogical or descent-based continuity**,
- **historically maintained lineage identity**,
- **branching or splitting lineages**,
- and **similarity without descent**.

Otherwise it will keep flattening kind-membership, ancestry, and persistence.

### 3. Office continuity is not office-holder identity

A crown, seat, presidency, judgeship, captaincy, or research directorship can continue while office-holders rotate. In some cases the office is more stable than any occupant.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **same office with new office-holder**,
- **same office-holder continuing in role**,
- **same institutional authority exercised through succession rules**,
- **vacant office awaiting occupancy**,
- and **abolished office later re-created under a similar name**.

Otherwise it will keep confusing role continuity, authority continuity, and personal continuity.

### 4. Institutional continuity is not mere legal or nominal inheritance

An institution can continue through turnover, merger, rechartering, reorganization, exile, dormancy, or temporary shutdown. But sometimes only the name, assets, or charter route persists.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **same institution through member turnover**,
- **same charter-governed institution under reorganization**,
- **successor institution under inherited legal form**,
- **re-founded institution borrowing prior symbols or records**,
- and **brand/name continuity without deep institutional continuity**.

Otherwise it will keep reading every legal or nominal thread as full institutional sameness.

### 5. Inherited design is not the same artifact

A later artifact, software release, platform version, or rebuilt machine may inherit plans, interfaces, code, or standards. That can matter deeply without proving that the very same artifact persists.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **same artifact repaired and retained**,
- **same system with upgraded parts**,
- **new version in one design lineage**,
- **rebuild from backup or blueprint**,
- and **only family resemblance under reused branding**.

Otherwise it will keep confusing design lineage with token identity.

### 6. Tradition transmission is not static essence retention

A ritual, craft, language, legal practice, scientific program, or family custom can continue through teaching, rehearsal, interpretation, correction, and selective inheritance. What persists may be structured historical transmission rather than a timeless invariant core.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **faithful transmission with real turnover of performers**,
- **selective inheritance with controlled change**,
- **branching tradition streams**,
- **revived practice from archived sources**,
- and **invented continuity retroactively projecting a lineage**.

Otherwise it will keep forcing traditions into either frozen essence or total discontinuity.

### 7. Succession can branch, fuse, or be contested

Inheritance is not always linear. Some lines bifurcate; some offices are disputed; some institutions split and both claim continuity; some traditions merge.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **single-file succession**,
- **branching continuity**,
- **fusion or merger continuity**,
- **contested successor claims**,
- and **no uniquely privileged continuation route**.

Otherwise it will keep assuming that historical continuity always picks out one simple later bearer.

### 8. What is transmitted must be typed

Inheritance language hides what actually passes from earlier to later cases.

So the archive should distinguish transmission of:

- **material parts**,
- **control or governance authority**,
- **legal standing or charter rights**,
- **genealogical descent position**,
- **design, code, or template**,
- **memory, archive, or record**,
- **norms, practices, and recognition**,
- and **public name or interface only**.

Otherwise it will keep letting one verb like “inherited” do the work of many different continuity claims.

## Typology of succession-cases

### A. Lineage continuity

What continues is a historically connected line of descent, reproduction, or branching development.

**Examples:** species taxa, family lineages, clone lines, research lineages, some historically continuous schools.

### B. Office succession

What continues is a role, office, or authority position governed by rules of accession, vacancy, and transfer.

**Examples:** a presidency, bishopric, chair, trusteeship, or captaincy.

### C. Chartered institutional succession

What continues is an institution through records, rules, membership turnover, legal recognition, and organizational memory.

**Examples:** a university, court, guild, company, monastery, or legislature.

### D. Design-line or version continuity

What continues is one design family, code lineage, standard, or engineered platform across token replacement.

**Examples:** software releases, aircraft families, protocol versions, rebuilt machines under inherited specification.

### E. Tradition or practice transmission

What continues is a practice-pattern through teaching, performance, interpretation, correction, and normative uptake.

**Examples:** liturgy, martial art, craft discipline, common-law doctrine, laboratory method.

### F. Successor series after collapse

What continues is not one uninterrupted bearer but an authorized or historically linked successor sequence after rupture.

**Examples:** governments in exile, re-founded institutions, dynastic succession after vacancy, reconstructed projects under preserved archives.

## Cross-domain pressure

### Organisms, species, and biological lines

Biology makes vivid the difference between one organism persisting, one colony or collective reproducing, and one lineage continuing through descendants. Species talk especially pressures the archive to separate one lineage from one organism and genealogy from mere similarity.

### Institutions, offices, and collective reality

Many social realities are built to outlast turnover. The office, charter, or institution may continue through replacement precisely because rules, records, and recognition sustain succession. Yet the very same features can also mask re-founding or contested continuity.

### Artifacts and technical systems

Devices, codebases, and engineered platforms often exhibit versioned inheritance. Interfaces and standards can persist across replacement while token identity does not. The archive needs a way to say when continuity is tokenal, lineage-like, or merely market-facing.

### Persons, families, and roles

Families, titles, and entrusted roles pressure the archive to separate personal identity from genealogical belonging and office continuity. A successor can inherit responsibility-space or recognition-route without becoming the same person.

### Traditions and organized practices

Languages, rituals, schools, and disciplines often continue through transmission, correction, branching, and reappropriation. Their reality is neither simple timeless essence nor mere loose resemblance.

## False moves to avoid

- Do **not** infer strict numerical sameness from every inheritance relation.
- Do **not** infer total discontinuity from every bearer replacement.
- Do **not** let one stable name settle whether the target is a bearer, office, lineage, institution, or brand.
- Do **not** treat descent as mere similarity, or similarity as automatic descent.
- Do **not** let charter, archive, or blueprint continuity do more ontological work than the target kind allows.
- Do **not** assume that contested succession has one obvious metaphysical winner in advance.
- Do **not** collapse branching or merged continuities into one flat linear story.
- Do **not** assume that traditions persist only by remaining unchanged.

## Default test for future revisions

When a future revision says that something **continued through successors**, **was handed down**, **inherited**, **descended from**, **remained the same institution**, **passed to the next holder**, **continued as a lineage**, **survived as a tradition**, **had a successor**, or **remained in force across turnover**, it should ask:

1. **What is the target?**
   - bearer, office, lineage, institution, role, design family, practice, or mixed?
2. **What sort of earlier/later relation is claimed?**
   - persistence, succession, descent, inheritance, re-founding, branching, merger, or mixed?
3. **What is transmitted?**
   - material parts, control, legal authority, charter, recognition, archive, code, lineage position, norms, or only a name?
4. **What is the continuity basis?**
   - causal-historical connection, genealogical descent, legal record, organizational memory, design template, training transmission, public recognition, or mixed?
5. **What is the replacement profile?**
   - same bearer retained, new bearer in same office, new token in same lineage, rebuilt successor under archive, disputed successor, or mixed?
6. **What is the branching / merger / contestation profile?**
   - linear succession, split line, fused line, rival claimants, or no determinate answer yet?
7. **What is the timescale and gap structure?**
   - uninterrupted turnover, vacancy, dormancy, collapse then re-foundation, generational descent, version-release sequence, or mixed?
8. **What is the bearer verdict?**
   - same bearer persisted, same office with new holder, same lineage through new members, same institution under turnover, successor under inherited charter/design, or still unsettled?

## Compression rule

> **When the archive says that something continued through successors, it must state whether the case is one of persistence, office succession, lineage continuity, charter continuity, design inheritance, or tradition transmission, what is actually transmitted, and whether the later case is the same bearer or only a historically organized successor.**

## Why this improves the archive

Without this file, the archive would keep oscillating between two bad habits:

1. **cheap identity** — every inherited title, lineage, or charter is treated as the very same thing simply continuing, and
2. **cheap discontinuity** — every bearer replacement is treated as if nothing real persists beyond a name.

This file blocks both mistakes. It gives the archive one compact way to say that **continuity can be real without being numerical identity**, and that **successor continuity itself comes in kinds**.
