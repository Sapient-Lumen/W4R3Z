# Joining, Bonding, Welding, Brazing, and Soldering

## Why this file exists

The archive now has explicit files on **attachment / anchoring / tethering / docking**, **embedding / insertion / implantation**, **enclosure / encasement / housing**, **coating / cladding / lining**, **wrapping / sheathing / draping**, **lamination / interleaving / stratified stacking**, and **sealing / closure / plugging / gasketing**, but it still lacks one direct file saying what to do when a case is described as **joined together**, **bonded**, **glued**, **welded**, **brazed**, **soldered**, **stitched together**, **sutured**, or **made into one assembly by a joint-making operation**.

That omission matters because the archive still risks eight opposite mistakes:

- treating every **joint** as if it were already **fusion**,
- treating every **bonded assembly** as if it were only ordinary **attachment**,
- treating every **weld** as if it settled the identity of the larger bearer once and for all,
- treating every **adhesive bond** as if it were only **sealing** or **coating**,
- treating every **braze** or **solder joint** as if it were just superficial contact,
- treating **same assembly** as if it meant **same joint-token**,
- treating **same joint-token or joint-type** as if it meant **same larger system**,
- or ignoring how **debonding, crack growth, heat-affected zones, rework, revision, and joint replacement** change what continuity claim is plausible.

This file gives the archive a compact **joining / bonding / welding / brazing / soldering profile** so future revisions can say whether a case is chiefly about adhesive joint-making, metallurgical joining, low-temperature joining, soft-tissue closure, hybrid joint architecture, or stronger material fusion only in harder cases.

## Compressed picture

> **Two or more things can be genuinely joined into one functioning assembly without that alone settling whether they have become one substance, one bearer, one individual, or one unchanged continuant under every sortal.**

And more carefully:

> **Joining, bonding, welding, brazing, and soldering are typed ways in which items are made to hold together, transmit load, preserve continuity across an interface, or maintain closure across an intentionally formed joint by adhesive, mechanical, metallurgical, or tissue-approximating means without that alone settling identity, constitution, or fusion.**

## Why the default view should absorb this operator-family

The archive's working view already says:

- **process** matters, because joints are prepared, formed, cured, heated, stitched, stressed, cracked, reworked, and replaced over time,
- **powers and functions** matter, because joined things can transmit load, maintain alignment, approximate tissue, preserve conductivity, or stabilize an assembly,
- **dependence pluralism** matters, because joining should not be collapsed into attachment, sealing, layering, enclosure, constitution, or fusion,
- **boundary discipline** matters, because an interface can remain real even when it is deliberately crossed by one successful joint,
- **failure-mode discipline** matters, because weak bonds, cracked welds, bad solder joints, and failed closures reveal structure that coarse words like *joined* or *made one piece* hide,
- and **artifact-risk discipline** matters, because engineering and medical talk about something being *bonded*, *welded*, or *closed* often overstates metaphysical intimacy.

So the default view should absorb join-language without flattening it.

## What this operator-family is for

This operator-family is for cases where one target is **made jointly continuous with another under a typed joint-making relation**.

The relation may involve:

- **adhesive bonding between adherends**,
- **a welded seam or welded joint**,
- **a brazed or soldered conductive or structural join**,
- **tissue adhesive, sutures, or staples holding wound edges together**,
- **a hybrid assembly that uses several joining methods at once**,
- or **a repair that restores continuity by making a new joint across an old break or interface**.

The key question is not simply whether things now hold together, but **what sort of joint is doing the work and whether it carries any stronger claim than organized joining, load transfer, conductive continuity, edge approximation, or serviceable assembly continuity**.

## Why the archive needs it

### 1. Joining is not the same as fusion

A weld, braze, solder, adhesive bond, or suture can create a real joint without erasing every interface or automatically producing one metaphysically unproblematic bearer.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **typed joint-making**,
- **serviceable assembly continuity**,
- **persistent interface under one joint**,
- **local material intermixing or closure**,
- and **full fusion only in harder cases**.

Otherwise it will keep sliding from **joined to** into **became numerically one thing**.

### 2. Bonding is not the same as attachment

A bonded joint is not just one thing hanging on another. It is often formed precisely to make a joint-line transmit load, maintain geometry, or preserve continuity across an interface.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **mounted or tethered attachment**,
- **adhesive or metallurgical joint-making**,
- **temporary clamping used to create a bond**,
- **bonded but still diagnosable interface structure**,
- and **stronger incorporation only in harder cases**.

### 3. Joining is not the same as sealing

A seal chiefly blocks passage. A joint chiefly makes things hold together or act together across an interface. Some cases do both, but the explanatory center can differ.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **seal-first closure relation**,
- **joint-first continuity relation**,
- **hybrid seal-and-join architecture**,
- **same interface with changed sealing element**,
- and **same assembly with changed joint or closure only where separately earned**.

### 4. Joining is not the same as layering

A laminated or coated structure may contain bonds, but the relevant issue can still be whether several layers are arranged in order rather than whether one decisive joint makes two prior items into one functioning assembly.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **layered stack architecture**,
- **localized joint-making across one seam or edge**,
- **broad surface coating or lining**,
- **repair patch bonded into one larger structure**,
- and **same stack or same joint only where the relevant sortal supports it**.

### 5. Same assembly does not mean same joint-token

A plane, circuit, prosthesis, vessel, or wound can remain the same continuing target while one weld, adhesive bond, staple line, or solder joint is repaired, revised, or replaced.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **same larger assembly under joint replacement**,
- **same seam under rework**,
- **same wound under changed closure method**,
- **same conductive path under changed joining medium**,
- and **new bearer only in stronger breakdown cases**.

### 6. Same joint-type does not mean same assembly

One joining method can recur across different bearers, different repairs, or different system scales.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **same adhesive system across changing assemblies**,
- **same weld type across changing bearers**,
- **same suture pattern across changing wounds**,
- **same solder technique across changing circuits**,
- and **same larger system only in stronger organizational cases**.

### 7. Weak joints, cracks, and rework matter

A joined relation can be nominally present but weak, partially failed, misaligned, fatigued, overheated, poorly prepared, or reworked.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **intact joint**,
- **weak or degraded bond**,
- **cracked or partially failed joint**,
- **reworked or replacement joint**,
- and **joint failure that changes larger-system identity only in stronger cases**.

## Typology of joining cases

### A. Adhesive bonding relation

The key feature is that an adhesive-mediated interface joins adherends into one load-bearing, positioning, or closure-maintaining assembly.

**Examples:** bonded composite structures; glued repair patches; tissue adhesive joining approximated skin edges.

### B. Welded joining relation

The key feature is that heat and/or pressure produce a welded joint across an interface, often with local material plasticization, deformation, or fusion, while the larger metaphysical verdict still depends on grain and sortal.

**Examples:** friction stir welds; welded seams in tanks or vessels; welded repairs in one continuing frame.

### C. Brazed or soldered joining relation

The key feature is that a filler-mediated joint produces continuity across a connection without simply reducing the case to loose contact or to total fusion of the joined items.

**Examples:** soldered conductor splices; brazed joints in tubing or assemblies; low-temperature electrical joining.

### D. Soft-tissue approximation or closure joint

The key feature is that wound or tissue edges are deliberately brought and held together by adhesive, sutures, or staples without that alone settling whether one should talk about mere closure, healing support, or a stronger joined unit.

**Examples:** tissue adhesive closure; sutured approximation; stapled skin closure.

### E. Hybrid joint architecture

The key feature is that several joining methods cooperate in one assembly and no single term should be allowed to flatten the case.

**Examples:** bonded-and-fastened repairs; welded assemblies later sealed; layered structures whose performance depends on both lamination and localized bonded joints.

## Quick contrasts the archive should preserve

### Joining vs attachment

Attachment need not create a joint with continuity across an interface. Joining does.

### Joining vs articulation

Joining need not preserve controlled relative motion. For joints whose point is guided movement rather than rigid continuity, see `135-articulation-hinging-pivoting-and-socketed-coupling.md`.

### Joining vs fusion

Fusion is the stronger verdict. Joining is the typed relation that may or may not support it, depending on grain, interface persistence, and sortal.

### Joining vs sealing

Sealing chiefly blocks passage. Joining chiefly makes a joint hold or act together.

### Joining vs lamination

Lamination concerns ordered multilayer architecture. Joining concerns the making of a joint between items or across a seam.

### Joining vs constitution

A joined part can help constitute a larger bearer, but the mere presence of a joint does not settle the constitution question by itself.

## Default rules for handling joining cases

1. **Do not infer fusion from one successful joint.** A strong bond or weld can still leave typed interfaces and part-structure intact.
2. **Do not infer mere attachment from the word joined.** Some joints are doing much more than hanging or tethering.
3. **Track the joint-token separately from the larger assembly.** Replacement, rework, debonding, and reclosure often matter.
4. **Track preparation and process history.** Surface prep, cure profile, heat input, filler choice, and closure technique can matter metaphysically because they shape continuity and failure modes.
5. **Keep failure typed.** Weak bond, cracked weld, cold solder joint, loose staple line, and failed tissue approximation are not one flat kind of failure.
6. **Only infer one bearer or one substance when the stronger verdict is separately earned.**

## Diagnostic questions the archive should ask

When a case is described as **joined**, **bonded**, **glued**, **welded**, **brazed**, **soldered**, **sutured**, **stitched together**, or **stapled closed**, ask:

1. Is the case chiefly about **joint-making**, **sealing**, **attachment**, **layering**, or **full fusion**?
2. What exactly are the relata — **two prior parts, two materials, a repair patch and a host, wound edges, conductors, or one already larger assembly**?
3. What is the joining mechanism — **adhesive**, **pressure plus heat**, **filler-mediated join**, **staple line**, **suture pattern**, or **hybrid method**?
4. Does the interface remain a real explanatory site after joining, or is the stronger claim that the earlier interface has ceased to matter at the chosen grain?
5. What persists across time: **the larger assembly, the seam, the joint-token, the closure method, or only a role in one ongoing process**?
6. What is the failure profile — **weak bond, crack growth, heat-affected weakness, debonding, staple-line failure, wound reopening, cold solder joint, or rework**?
7. What is the relevant sortal — **same assembly, same part, same wound, same circuit path, same vessel, same organismal structure, or new joined artifact**?

## Compression rule

When later files say that one thing is **joined to**, **bonded with**, **welded to**, **brazed into**, **soldered onto**, **sutured**, or **stapled closed**, they should not force a verdict of **mere attachment**, **full fusion**, or **same bearer** by default. They should ask whether the case is best typed as adhesive bonding, welded joining, filler-mediated joining, soft-tissue approximation, hybrid joint architecture, or only stronger fusion in harder cases.

## Why this matters for metaphysics

Metaphysics keeps getting misled by verbs of making-one: **joined**, **bonded**, **welded**, **closed**, **stitched together**. Those verbs often name a real relation, but they do not by themselves decide identity, constitution, or persistence.

A better archive should be able to say all of the following without strain:

- **same larger assembly, new joint-token**,
- **same joint type, different bearer**,
- **strong joint without fusion**,
- **joint plus seal without one flat relation**,
- **closure by staples or adhesive without identity of every closed edge**,
- and **repair by rejoining without automatic verdict that the original bearer is gone**.

That is what this operator-family is for.
