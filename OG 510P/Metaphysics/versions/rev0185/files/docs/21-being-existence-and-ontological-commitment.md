# Being, Existence, and Ontological Commitment

## Why this file exists

The archive now has disciplined files on dependence, category, truthmaking, modality, causation, time, and space. But it still uses phrases like **real**, **exists**, **ontologically serious**, **derivative**, and **mere shorthand** without a single rule for how those claims should be made.

This file supplies that rule.

Its purpose is not to solve every metaontological dispute. Its purpose is to stop the archive from drifting between:

- surface grammar and ontology,
- existence and fundamentality,
- truth conditions and truthmakers,
- realism and inflation,
- or paraphrase and elimination.

## Archive default

The archive should be **realist but disciplined**.

That means:

- do **not** treat ontological questions as merely verbal by default,
- do **not** infer heavyweight ontology from every true sentence,
- do **not** infer nonexistence merely because a discourse admits paraphrase,
- do **not** confuse being **derivative** with being **unreal**,
- and do **not** confuse **what exists** with **what is fundamental**.

The archive should ask three questions separately:

1. **Existence** — is there really such a thing, fact, relation, event, process, kind, or structure?
2. **Status** — if yes, is it basic, derivative, constituted, realized, role-governed, institutional, abstract, or merely representational?
3. **Commitment** — what must reality contain or make obtain for the relevant claims to be true?

## First distinction: existence is not fundamentality

A domain can be real without being fundamental.

The archive has already committed itself to this in practice:

- organisms are real without being basal,
- institutions can be real while grounded in practices and enforcement structures,
- absences can be serious without becoming free-floating negative entities,
- and higher-level causal or modal profiles can be real without severing lower-level dependence.

So the archive should reject the shortcut:

> derivative -> unreal

A derivative entity may still be fully real if it has a stable unity profile, a genuine dependence profile, and explanatory work that is not exhausted by mere bookkeeping.

## Second distinction: existence is not automatic from grammar

Many expressions can look ontologically committing while doing less metaphysical work than their grammar suggests.

So the archive should not move straight from:

- “there is a hole”,
- “there is a shadow”,
- “there is a boundary”,
- “there is a recession”,
- “there is an obligation”,

to the conclusion that all such cases involve one and the same kind of entityhood.

Instead it should ask:

- what ontological category is in play,
- what dependence relation is doing the work,
- whether the case is truthmaker-sparse or truthmaker-heavy,
- whether the relevant thing is best understood as object-like, event-like, fact-like, relation-like, role-like, or privative,
- and whether the claim is best taken literally, structurally, institutionally, or representationally.

## Third distinction: quantifier tests are useful but not final

Quantificational and entailment-based approaches to ontological commitment are useful because they force discipline. They make us ask what a theory really says must exist in order to be true.

But the archive should not treat bound-variable tests as the whole story.

Why not?

Because many ontological disputes are not settled by logical form alone. They also depend on:

- what the predicates and category terms mean,
- whether the theory is committed to facts, properties, structures, or merely individuals,
- whether truthmaker discipline adds commitments not visible from first-order syntax,
- and whether a discourse is being used literally, instrumentally, structurally, or fictionally.

So the archive should use quantifier and entailment tests as **screens**, not as automatic verdict machines.

## Fourth distinction: ontological commitment is not identical to truthmaking

A theory may be committed to something because that thing must exist for the theory to be true. But the archive should keep open that:

- some commitments are visible at the level of what a theory explicitly or implicitly entails,
- while others appear only when we ask what in reality makes the theory true.

This matters because the archive already uses restrained truthmaker discipline. It does not want every truth to demand a bespoke entity, but it also does not want ontology to be read only from syntactic shape.

The practical rule is:

> start with ontological commitment; escalate to truthmaker analysis only when the case remains ambiguous or inflation-prone.

## Fifth distinction: ontological seriousness is not a single grade

The archive should stop using “real” as if it always meant one of two things: absolutely basic or merely fictional.

A better scale is:

### 1. Basic reality

What is least dependent or most fundamental.

### 2. Derivative but robust reality

What is grounded, constituted, realized, or institutionally sustained, but still earns explanatory, modal, causal, or normative weight.

### 3. Thin or dependent profile

What is real only in a highly dependent, aspectual, or privative way: holes, shadows, absences, boundaries, omissions, roles, or statuses may belong here depending on the case.

### 4. Merely representational or heuristic posit

What is useful for modeling, bookkeeping, idealization, or compression, but lacks independent ontological work once the world is described more adequately.

The archive should be especially careful with transitions between levels 2, 3, and 4.

## Archive decision rule for disputed domains

When the archive asks whether X exists, it should state:

1. **category profile** — object, process, event, fact, relation, power, kind, structure, role, privation, or mixed;
2. **dependence profile** — composition, constitution, realization, grounding, causation, institution, or some combination;
3. **truthmaker profile** — what in reality makes the relevant claims about X true;
4. **modal profile** — whether X supports counterfactual, lawful, essential, or persistence-sensitive claims;
5. **explanatory profile** — what is lost if X is eliminated or paraphrased away;
6. **status verdict** — basic, derivative, thinly dependent, or merely representational.

If a proposal cannot do this, it has not yet earned a confident existence claim or elimination claim.

## Default archive stances on several recurring cases

### Ordinary objects

Default stance: **derivative but robustly real**.

They are usually not fundamental, but they often have unity, persistence, causal, and modal profiles too strong to treat as mere shorthand.

### Institutions and social roles

Default stance: **derivative but robustly real** when grounded in practices, norms, records, recognition, and enforcement.

### Holes, shadows, and boundaries

Default stance: **thinly dependent realities** unless a case gives them heavier explanatory work.

They are often real enough for explanation, but their mode of being is not the same as that of paradigmatic material individuals.

### Absences and privations

Default stance: **case-sensitive**.

Some are best treated as bounded absences or privations; some are causal omissions; some are merely negative descriptions.

### Numbers, propositions, and other abstracta

Default stance: **open**.

The archive should neither force full-blooded realism nor flatten all abstracta into convenient speech before the domain-specific work is done.

## Anti-bloat rule

A new domain should not be admitted into the archive as ontologically serious merely because:

- the discourse is indispensable in practice,
- the language contains existential idioms,
- the domain is hard to paraphrase away in ordinary speech,
- or the denial of the domain sounds revisionary.

It should be admitted when some combination of the following is true:

- elimination causes explanatory loss,
- the domain supports distinctive modal or causal patterns,
- the domain has stable unity or persistence conditions,
- the domain figures in truthmaker analysis better than its rivals,
- or the domain is required by the best joint account of the archive's other commitments.

## Compression rule for future revisions

Future revisions should prefer one of four conclusions about a disputed ontology:

- **keep** — the domain is real and earns its current place,
- **demote** — the domain is real but only thinly or derivatively,
- **retype** — the domain was treated under the wrong ontological category,
- **eliminate** — the domain is doing no indispensable work.

This is better than treating every dispute as a forced yes/no question about bare existence.

## Current archive lean

The archive now leans toward the following metaontological posture:

> Use a single ordinary notion of existence unless a special reason is given otherwise; treat ontological commitment as real and not merely verbal; separate existence from fundamentality; and make disputes answerable to category, dependence, truthmaker, modal, and explanatory profiles.

That posture is modestly realist, anti-inflationary, and compatible with the archive's larger commitment to layered, processual, form-sensitive reality.

## What this file does not settle

This file does **not** settle:

- whether there is one privileged quantifier,
- whether all ontological disputes are substantive rather than partly verbal,
- whether abstracta should receive one unified treatment,
- whether truthmaker theory is the final account of commitment,
- or whether some domains are better reconstructed than literally affirmed.

It settles only this much: the archive should stop using “exists”, “is real”, “is serious”, “is derivative”, and “is merely representational” without saying what work those labels are doing.

The fuller treatment of when an apparent ontological dispute is genuinely substantive, merely verbal, framework-relative, or only a status disagreement now lives in `74-metametaphysics-substantiveness-verbalism-and-worldly-difference.md`.
