# Artifact Risk, Distortion, and Failure Modes

## Why this file exists

The archive now has explicit files on **appearance**, **representation**, **measurement**, **directness**, **evidence**, **abduction**, and **robustness/triangulation**. But it still lacked one direct file saying what the archive means when it moves from:

- illusion,
- hallucination,
- distortion,
- noise,
- drift,
- proxy failure,
- model-imposed pattern,
- setup artifact,
- shared-method echo,
- or apparent convergence under hidden background load

to a **metaphysical verdict**.

Without a dedicated file here, the archive risks seven recurrent mistakes:

- treating every **artifact-risk** as if it were already a proof of **unreality**,
- treating every **distortion** as if it were already **total fabrication**,
- treating every **error source** as if it settled the ontology rather than the access route,
- treating **proxy failure** as if it were automatically target absence,
- treating **theory-ladenness** as if it meant arbitrariness,
- treating **robustness against some failure modes** as if it meant immunity from all of them,
- or treating **negative cases** as if they did not themselves teach us about the structure of access, mediation, and dependence.

This file gives the archive a compact **artifact profile** so future revisions can say what kind of failure is at issue, where in the access chain it enters, what worldly target remains live, what correction profile exists, and what kind of ontological caution—if any—is actually earned.

## Working default

The archive should now say explicitly:

> **Treat artifact risk and distortion as typed failure modes of access, registration, representation, or interpretation rather than as automatic evidence either for unreality or for skepticism.**

And more specifically:

> **A failure mode matters metaphysically only after the archive states what target was being tracked, where the distortion enters, what alternative routes remain available, what correction regime is possible, and whether the case defeats target-contact, only this proxy, only this setup, or only one inflated ontological reading.**

This fits the archive's wider commitments:

- **appearance** matters because seeming can be world-involving while still vulnerable to illusion, hallucination, or selective presentation,
- **measurement** matters because readouts can fail through calibration, drift, thresholding, noise, and proxy mismatch,
- **directness** matters because mediation can preserve contact while also introducing interface-shaped distortion,
- **evidence** matters because some failure modes defeat support while others only scale confidence,
- **abduction** matters because explanatory success can ride on background assumptions that make some apparent virtues artifactual,
- and **robustness** matters because convergence can rule out some artifacts without ruling out all of them.

## Core distinctions

### 1. Artifact is not one flat kind of error

The archive should distinguish:

- **perceptual illusion**,
- **hallucination or target-absence case**,
- **setup artifact**,
- **instrument artifact**,
- **calibration or drift artifact**,
- **sampling or threshold artifact**,
- **model-imposed artifact**,
- **statistical artifact**,
- **social or training echo**,
- and **mixed failure modes**.

Otherwise the archive will keep using “artifact” as one vague dismissal rather than locating where the failure actually enters.

### 2. Distortion is not always fabrication

The archive should distinguish:

- **selective emphasis**,
- **perspectival compression**,
- **scale-limited distortion**,
- **proxy deformation**,
- **idealized simplification**,
- **misregistration**,
- and **wholesale fabrication or target-absence**.

Otherwise the archive will keep treating every mismatch as if it destroyed all contact with the target.

### 3. Proxy failure is not target nonexistence

The archive should distinguish:

- **bad proxy for a real target**,
- **proxy that tracks only one aspect of the target**,
- **proxy that works only under restricted conditions**,
- **proxy that has drifted away from the target**,
- and **no target there at all**.

Otherwise the archive will keep sliding from failed indicator to failed ontology.

### 4. Theory-ladenness is not arbitrariness

The archive should distinguish:

- **background dependence that enables detection**,
- **background assumptions that bias interpretation**,
- **model choice that shapes what is visible**,
- **self-sealing framing**,
- and **publicly criticizable, revisable theory-load**.

Otherwise the archive will keep oscillating between naïve givenness and cynical conventionalism.

### 5. Correction can fail locally without collapsing globally

The archive should distinguish:

- **one bad setup**,
- **one bad detector**,
- **one bad operationalization**,
- **one bad model family**,
- **one distorted perspective among many**,
- and **systematic defeat across routes**.

Otherwise the archive will keep mistaking local failure for total defeat or local success for total vindication.

### 6. Failure modes can reveal ontology indirectly

The archive should distinguish:

- failures that reveal **boundary conditions**,
- failures that reveal **grain mismatch**,
- failures that reveal **dependence on organization or context**,
- failures that reveal **response-dependence or setup-sensitivity**,
- and failures that reveal only **instrument weakness or representational inadequacy**.

Otherwise the archive will keep treating negative results as mere embarrassment rather than sometimes as clues to what kind of thing the target is.

### 7. Artifact resistance is graded, not all-or-nothing

The archive should distinguish:

- **artifact-aware caution**,
- **artifact-tested local confidence**,
- **cross-route resistance to known artifacts**,
- **residual but bounded artifact risk**,
- and **no serious handle on artifact structure at all**.

Otherwise the archive will keep treating inquiry as either pristine transparency or hopeless contamination.

## Four live options

### 1. Transparent-access optimism

**Picture:** failures are exceptional; once a measure, perception, or model works reasonably well, artifact-talk should recede.

**What it gets right:**
- many real cases do involve stable world-contact,
- and not every successful route needs permanent paranoia.

**What it gets wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- it underdescribes how much ontological overreach comes from ignoring setup effects, proxy mismatch, training loops, and scale-limited distortion.

### 2. Global artifact skepticism

**Picture:** because every route can fail, all access is too contaminated for serious ontological pressure.

**What it gets right:**
- mediation, theory-load, and artifact risk are ubiquitous,
- and apparent success often hides background dependence.

**What it gets wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- it cannot explain why some targets survive correction, perturbation, triangulation, and adversarial checking better than others.

### 3. Conventionalist deflation

**Picture:** artifact and correction vocabularies mostly track our practices, not the world; success is mainly stabilization inside a community.

**What it gets right:**
- communities, standards, and techniques matter,
- and some “failures” are relative to operational or classificatory choices.

**What it gets wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- it underdescribes resistance from the target side, especially when many different practices constrain one another.

### 4. Typed fallibilist realism

**Picture:** access is always vulnerable to failure, but failures are typed. Artifact analysis does not erase realism; it scales and disciplines it. Some routes survive known distortions well enough to support realism about entities, structures, processes, mechanisms, or response-linked profiles; some support only restricted or proxy realism; and some collapse under their artifact load.

**What it gets right:**
- it preserves realism without transparency mythology,
- it preserves fallibilism without skeptical flattening,
- and it turns negative cases into diagnostic resources.

**What it risks if left alone:**
- it can become slogan-like unless each case says exactly where failure enters and what remains live after correction.

## Archive commitments

### 1. Locate the failure in the access chain

Do not invoke “artifact” until the archive says whether the issue lies in target absence, interface, setup, model, training, interpretation, thresholding, or some mixture.

### 2. Distinguish distortion from defeat

A distorted presentation can still disclose a real target partially, selectively, or at the wrong grain.

### 3. Let proxy failure downrate the proxy before erasing the target

Many bad measures reveal bad indicators, not nonexistent realities.

### 4. Use known failure modes to scale confidence rather than to legislate metaphysics too quickly

Artifact analysis should change the confidence profile and sometimes the category verdict, not simply force either inflation or nihilism.

### 5. Treat correction as positive structure, not as an embarrassing afterthought

Calibration, adversarial testing, perturbation, reanalysis, route variation, and error modeling are part of how world-answerability is earned.

### 6. Keep the ontological verdict category-sensitive

Artifact resistance may support a process, mechanism, response-profile, or structure rather than a compact object or heavyweight kind.

## Artifact profile rule for future revisions

Before inferring a metaphysical verdict from language like **artifact**, **distortion**, **illusion**, **hallucination**, **proxy failure**, **noise**, **drift**, **bias**, **threshold effect**, **the apparatus created it**, **only a model effect**, **observer artifact**, **echo chamber**, **self-fulfilling measure**, or **merely an interface effect**, future revisions should answer at least these questions:

1. **target** — entity, process, mechanism, structure, property, response-profile, status function, or mixed target;
2. **failure location** — perception, setup, detector, indicator, operationalization, model, interpretation, social uptake, or mixed location;
3. **failure type** — illusion, hallucination, misregistration, distortion, proxy mismatch, calibration drift, threshold artifact, sampling artifact, model-imposed pattern, or mixed type;
4. **dependence profile** — setup-sensitivity, response-dependence, observer-sensitivity, training dependence, theory-load, institutionally constituted procedure, or mixed dependence;
5. **correction profile** — recalibration, route variation, adversarial checking, cross-scale comparison, alternative proxy, triangulation, or no serious correction available;
6. **residual contact** — whether the route still tracks the target partially, only one aspect of it, only a surrogate, or nothing target-revealing at all;
7. **promotion effect** — whether artifact analysis blocks entity-promotion, scales it down to structure/process/mechanism realism, leaves a response-dependent reality verdict, or only defeats one inflated reading;
8. **final reality verdict** — artifact-resistant realism, selective but corrected realism, proxy-only caution, strong defeat for this route, or still unsettled.

## What this file does **not** say

It does **not** say:

- that every distortion destroys world-contact,
- that every artifact risk forces skepticism,
- that successful correction yields transparency,
- that proxy failure always means target absence,
- or that residual artifact risk prevents all serious ontology.
