# Open Questions and Discriminating Tests

This is where future revisions should spend effort.

## 1. What is fundamental?

Is the most basic layer best thought of as:

- substances,
- events/processes,
- structures/relations,
- powers,
- a whole prior to its parts,
- a neutral or dual-aspect basis,
- or some mixed basis?

### Why this matters

The current synthesis is no longer neutral about everything: it rejects a featureless base and leans toward structured powers-in-process. But it is still officially open about the best positive account of fundamentality.

## 2. Which dependence relation is doing the work?

Many disputes become clearer once we stop asking only whether a phenomenon is “grounded”.

### Test

For any target case, state explicitly whether the main relation is:

- composition,
- constitution,
- realization,
- grounding,
- or causation.

### Failure mode

Without this step, metaphysical debates become verbally unified but explanatorily blurred.

## 3. What makes one thing one?

Before asking whether objects are basic or derivative, the archive should ask what unifies parts or phases into one entity at all.

### Test

For any candidate entity, state:

- its kind,
- its unity condition,
- its persistence condition,
- and its failure condition.

### Failure mode

Without this step, grounding and emergence claims float free of clear entityhood conditions.

## 4. Are objects basic or stabilized?

Are ordinary objects fundamental occupants of reality, or are they always derivative stabilizations of deeper processual organization?

### Test

Prefer the option that better explains persistence through material turnover, especially in living systems and artifacts with form-sensitive identity conditions.

## 5. What distinguishes real emergence from convenient description?

The archive now has a stricter criterion, but difficult cases remain.

### Stress cases

- living systems,
- consciousness,
- corporations and institutions,
- ecosystems,
- and mathematically structured physical systems.

### Failure mode

Without operational criteria, emergence becomes a permission slip for vagueness.

## 6. What is the relation between laws, powers, and structure?

The archive now leans toward a mixed answer: laws articulate real modal-structural constraints in a world of power-bearing processes. But several questions remain open.

### Residual tests

- How much of lawfulness should be read off local powers?
- How much is genuinely system-level or structural?
- Are higher-level sciences tracking derivative but objective laws, or only robust regularities?

### Importance

A non-reductive metaphysics must explain why the world is not just a sequence of happenings, and why law-talk is neither empty summary nor cosmic legislation.

## 7. Can consciousness be integrated without dualist panic?

The archive now has an explicit consciousness file, but the integration problem remains. If consciousness is real and not reducible in any easy sense, can the archive explain it as emergent, formally constituted, processually grounded, aspectual, or neutral-based without splitting reality into unrelated domains?

## 8. Which abstracta are basic, derivative, immanent, or merely representational?

The archive now has an explicit abstracta file, but hard cases remain.

### Test

For any candidate abstractum, state explicitly:

- whether it is mathematical, propositional, structural, idealized-model, normative, or mixed,
- what explanatory job it is doing,
- whether the best profile is basic, derivative but robust, immanent structural, practice-dependent objective, or merely representational,
- and what explanatory loss follows if it is paraphrased away.

### Failure mode

Without this step, the archive will keep sliding between platonism, structuralism, nominalism, and fictionalism as if they were one flat dispute.

## 9. Can social reality be fully metaphysical rather than merely semantic?

Institutions, money, law, offices, and obligations appear dependent yet real.

### Test

For any social target, state explicitly:

- whether it is a practice, status, role, office, organization, institution, group agent, record system, or mixed case,
- what sustains it: uptake, rule, record, enforcement, infrastructure, or mixed basis,
- whether the key dependence is grounding, constitution, realization, causal maintenance, or distributed organization,
- what normative standing it carries,
- and what would be lost if it were treated as merely semantic.

### Failure mode

Without this step, the archive will keep oscillating between anti-realism about institutions and spooky supra-human entity talk.

## 10. How many explanatory irreducibles are acceptable?

A theory can become so plural that it loses force. The archive should continually ask whether form, grounding, powers, process, and structure are genuinely distinct necessities or partly compressible.

## 11. What would disconfirm the archive's present direction?

The current synthesis should lose default status if one of the following happens:

- a leaner theory handles persistence, modality, levels, and dependence equally well,
- the operator kernel collapses cleanly into fewer primitives,
- or the emergence test fails repeatedly in core domains.

## Frontier test: review authority, certification, and claim warrant

The new review-governance layer raises a practical frontier question:

> **What exactly has been reviewed, by whom or what, under what authority, at what scope, with what evidence, and what may honestly be claimed because of that review?**

Discriminating tests:

1. **Self-check versus independent review.** If the same author or maintainer created and checked the revision, the permitted claim should be self-check or maintainer consistency review, not independent certification.
2. **Mechanical check versus conceptual review.** If the evidence is manifest validation, file continuity, version alignment, or fresh extraction, the permitted claim is package-mechanical, not philosophical.
3. **Source review versus metaphysical endorsement.** If a domain expert checks a source summary, the source-bound claim may be reviewed; the archive's metaphysical inference still needs its own conceptual review.
4. **Provenance audit versus truth warrant.** If custody, touched files, transformations, and reproducibility are checked, the artifact-trust claim is stronger; the operator verdict is not thereby settled.
5. **Adversarial review versus external validation.** If a verdict survives hostile internal variants, it may be called adversarially stress-tested; it should not be called externally validated unless an external reviewer actually reviewed it.
6. **Teaching review versus precedent status.** If a summary is clear for students, it may be teaching-safe at a stated compression level; it is not automatically a controlled precedent.
7. **Review scope versus whole-archive claim.** If only one file, family, source note, or release packet was reviewed, the claim should not imply all numbered docs, all examples, or all future uses were reviewed.
8. **Stale-review test.** If a claim, source, file, release class, lineage relation, or provenance packet changes after review, the old review must be refreshed, narrowed, or marked historical.
9. **Conflict test.** If the reviewer has authorship, source, institutional, time-pressure, tool, or access limits, the review may still help but must not be described as independent or unlimited.
10. **Allowed-wording test.** Replace reviewed/audited/certified/validated with the narrowest accurate wording. If the narrower wording would change user reliance, the stronger wording was probably laundering authority.

Open questions:

- When should an archive require independent philosophical review rather than maintainer review?
- Which source-dependent examples require domain review before public teaching use?
- What review statuses should expire automatically after source refresh, fork import, or major migration?
- Should future public releases add a machine-readable review ledger, or is the review-packet protocol sufficient while the archive remains single-maintainer and source-neutral?
- How should conflicting reviews be represented when one reviewer checks source accuracy and another disputes metaphysical use?
- When does repeated reception evidence become a reason to require review rather than merely clearer transmission warnings?

## Frontier test: public reliance, citation, dispute, withdrawal, and retraction

The new public-reliance layer raises a practical frontier question:

> **When a reviewed, released, transmitted, taught, or cited archive claim circulates publicly, what exactly may others rely on, how must it be cited, how can it be disputed, and how is reliance narrowed, withdrawn, retracted, or historically retained?**

Discriminating tests:

1. **Publication versus reliance.** If a claim is visible in a public package but lacks public-use status, treat it as available, not automatically safe to rely on.
2. **Review warrant versus public permission.** If review permitted narrow wording, do not infer teaching permission, operational use, source-bound export, or public reliance beyond that wording.
3. **Citation completeness.** If version, file, section, status, review scope, or warning is missing, repair citation before treating the dispute as doctrinal.
4. **Current versus historical use.** If an older version is cited, check lineage and compatibility before treating it as the current archive view.
5. **Teaching versus precedent.** If a derivative is clear enough for teaching, ask whether it is only orientation, a teaching illustration, or a controlled precedent.
6. **Source-bound public use.** If the public claim depends on law, science, clinical guidance, institutional practice, history, text, or empirical data, require source date, source scope, and refresh trigger.
7. **Dispute routing.** Classify whether the objection concerns citation, compression, review status, version lineage, source boundary, precedent transfer, substantive counterexample pressure, operational misuse, or withdrawal request.
8. **Withdrawal grain.** Ask whether the problem calls for wording correction, public clarification, citation warning, temporary hold, teaching withdrawal, source-bound withdrawal, conceptual retraction, or package recall.
9. **Historical retention.** If a claim is withdrawn or retracted, preserve what remains historically useful and state what is no longer safe to cite as doctrine.
10. **Public reliance overreach.** If a metaphysical classification is used as legal, clinical, policy, institutional, scientific, or operational authority, lower the public-use status unless the relevant external review and source-bound warrant actually exist.

Open questions:

- Which archive claims, if any, should ever receive U8 release-warranted public reliance without external review?
- Should future public releases include a machine-readable public-reliance ledger or citation-template file?
- How should public teaching derivatives be located and warned when a source-bound example is withdrawn?
- When does repeated citation of a compressed summary become a reception problem, a transmission problem, or a public-reliance problem?
- What counts as sufficient notice when a publicly cited claim is narrowed but not fully retracted?
- Should public-use statuses expire automatically after a major lineage break, source refresh, or review downgrade?

2. **Role-scope test.** If a reviewer, teacher, fork maintainer, or citation user is treated as having authority beyond their role, require a stewardship packet or retract the authority claim.
3. **Accepted/declined duty test.** If users assume a duty that was never accepted, state the declined duty explicitly rather than relying on silence.
4. **Delegation test.** If a task has been handed to another person, system, institution, or derivative project, require named delegate, authority limit, retained responsibility, transferred evidence, dispute path, and exit condition.
5. **Update-watch test.** If the artifact claims currentness, source-bound use, public teaching status, fork compatibility, or maintained derivative status, it needs a version/source/update trigger.
6. **Warning-attrition test.** If summaries, slides, generated outputs, or citations drop required warnings, treat this as a stewardship failure even if the original archive file was correct.
7. **High-stakes test.** If a source-neutral metaphysical distinction is used operationally in legal, clinical, engineering, safety, financial, policy, or institutional contexts, require domain responsibility rather than archive permission alone.
8. **Orphaned-derivative test.** If a derivative remains public after its maintainer stops tracking the archive, mark it historical, transfer stewardship, or withdraw it.
9. **Fork-authority test.** If a fork borrows archive authority without lineage, compatibility, public-use, and stewardship records, classify the fork as authority laundering until corrected.
10. **Accountability-consequence test.** If a duty fails but no correction route is named, the stewardship packet is incomplete.

## Frontier test: operational registers, watch queues, and continuity memory

The new continuity-register layer raises a practical frontier question:

> **When a stewardship duty, public-use limit, source-watch need, review expiry, correction notice, derivative-maintenance duty, fork declaration, or withdrawal rule is accepted, where is it recorded, what wakes it up, who is notified, what closes it, and what must a successor inherit?**

Discriminating tests:

1. **Stated duty versus registered duty.** If a duty exists only in prose, treat it as conceptually stated but operationally weak until a continuity entry exists.
2. **Trigger test.** If an entry lacks a version, source, dispute, review-expiry, fork-divergence, derivative-publication, high-stakes-use, or steward-exit trigger, it is not a watch queue.
3. **Notice test.** If the archive cannot say who is supposed to learn about a correction, withdrawal, migration, or source change, do not claim public notice has been provided.
4. **Closure-evidence test.** If no evidence would count as closing the entry, the entry is either an infinite watch or an unmanaged debt.
5. **Successor-memory test.** If a successor cannot find open debts, live disputes, public-use limits, review expiries, source-watch items, known derivatives, or withdrawals, the archive should not claim entrusted continuity.
6. **Machine-readable laundering test.** A table, YAML file, issue tracker, or dashboard improves findability; it does not by itself add review authority, source accuracy, public reliance, or philosophical warrant.
7. **Registry overclaim test.** If the archive says it has a public errata service, derivative registry, source-watch service, or public issue tracker, verify that such infrastructure exists. If not, record the absence as open continuity debt.
8. **Orphan test.** If a public derivative, fork, citation guide, or teaching artifact is no longer maintained but still presents itself as current, mark it historical, transfer stewardship, or withdraw it.
9. **Queue escalation test.** If a register item moves from due to blocked or escalated, state whether the relevant claim is still usable, historical-only, under hold, or unsafe.
10. **Memory versus authority test.** A register remembers duties; it does not decide doctrine unless routed through the substantive controls.

Open questions:

- Should future releases add an actual `REGISTERS/` directory with YAML or CSV continuity entries?

## Frontier test: validation harnesses, invariant checks, and machine-readable governance

The new validation layer raises a practical frontier question:

> **When a package, packet, register, derivative, fork, schema, script, checklist, or transcript claims to be checked, what exactly was checked, what failed, what was not checked, and what authority does the check actually warrant?**

Discriminating tests:

1. **Validation scope test.** If a script checked file continuity and manifest hashes, do not describe the result as conceptual, source, domain, public-use, or independent review.
2. **Schema execution test.** If a schema exists but was not executed, classify it as V2 rather than validation passed.
3. **Field-completeness test.** If required fields are present but values were not reviewed, classify the result as structural completeness, not correctness.
4. **Invariant-family test.** Name which invariants were checked: version identity, file continuity, manifest integrity, packet chain, status vocabulary, source boundary, public-claim limits, exceptions, or automation boundary.
5. **Failure-severity test.** Distinguish warning, repair-before-release, release hold, quarantine, rollback trigger, and unsafe validation claim rather than treating all failures as equal.
6. **Skipped-check test.** If independent rebuild, source-domain review, public infrastructure audit, or semantic review was not run, say so explicitly.
7. **Automation-boundary test.** If a validation artifact starts doing philosophical, source, domain, public-reliance, or high-stakes work, reroute the claim to the relevant human-review control file.
8. **Machine-readable laundering test.** If YAML, CSV, JSON, dashboards, or scripts are treated as public infrastructure or authority merely because they are neat, downgrade the claim.
9. **Exception visibility test.** If a waiver or known omission is hidden, validation has failed even if the mechanical checks pass.
10. **Successor-use test.** If a successor cannot rerun, inspect, or understand the validation record, the validation is not adequate continuity memory.

Open questions:

- Which future releases need strict schema validation rather than local YAML records?
- Should release packets, lineage packets, provenance packets, review packets, public-reliance packets, stewardship packets, and continuity packets each receive extractable machine-readable records?
- What validation failures should automatically block release rather than merely create declared open debt?
- How should future public packages publish validation transcripts without implying independent certification?
- Can generated summaries be checked for warning retention without pretending that semantic faithfulness has been automated?
- What minimum validation should be required before a fork imports archive authority?

## Rev0162 frontier: longitudinal monitoring, effectiveness review, sunset, and residual risk

`168-longitudinal-monitoring-effectiveness-review-sunset-and-residual-risk-governance.md` asks a new question: not whether a control was written, validated once, or recorded in a learning packet. The question is whether the control remains effective, how that effectiveness is evidenced, whether residual risk is changing, and when renewal, escalation, migration, or sunset is justified.

### Test

For any control claimed to be durable, answer:

1. What exact control, warning, validator check, runbook step, deployment boundary, public-use limit, source-dependent claim, derivative restriction, release gate, or post-incident preventive action is being monitored?
2. Which layer created the monitoring obligation?
3. Is the monitoring status MON0–MON9?
4. Is the effectiveness evidence EFF0–EFF9?
5. Is the residual-risk trend RRT0–RRT8?
6. Is the sunset or renewal class SUN0–SUN9?
7. What would count as failure, decay, migration, or obsolescence?
8. What event triggers review?
9. Who owns the review, or what responsibility is explicitly declined?
10. What claim language is allowed before the next review?
11. What claim language is forbidden?
12. What evidence would justify closing, renewing, escalating, or retiring the control?

### Rival explanations

A control that appears successful may instead be:

- merely present rather than effective;
- effective only in one release;
- effective only in one output format;
- effective locally but unsafe in public or domain use;
- made redundant by a stronger validator or release gate;
- obsolete but retained by inertia;
- missing from derivatives or teaching artifacts;
- dependent on a stale source;
- displaced into a fork, migration, or deployment context;
- evidence of a deeper upstream governance gap.

### Failure mode

The archive should not say "the risk is handled" when it only means one of the following:

- a checklist line exists;
- a register file exists;
- a validator passed once;
- a warning appeared in one release note;
- a CAPA packet was closed;
- a maintainer remembers the issue;
- no recurrence has been noticed;
- a control has not been challenged by derivative, public, source, or deployment use.

The discriminating test is whether the archive can state the monitoring packet without inflating it into public monitoring, independent effectiveness audit, source-watch automation, safety assurance, or domain compliance.
