# Transmission, Excerpt Compression, and Pedagogical Governance

## Why this file exists

`151-precedent-reuse-appeal-transfer-and-review-governance.md` governs the afterlife of a dossiered verdict. It asks whether a verdict may be reused, taught, transferred, appealed, narrowed, superseded, deprecated, or marked do-not-reuse. That is necessary, but it is still not enough for a large archive.

A verdict can have correct precedent status and still be damaged by the form in which it is transmitted. The archive can be careful internally while its summaries, study notes, paper drafts, prompt answers, lectures, diagrams, tables, and executive abstracts silently drop the target, basis, grain, rivals, source role, term status, commitment status, non-verdict, appeal trigger, or do-not-upgrade warning that made the result responsible.

The next danger is therefore **transmission drift**: a responsible case result is converted into an output that is easier to read but metaphysically stronger, flatter, more portable, or more settled than the archive actually licensed.

A second danger is **excerpt capture**: a short passage is cut from a file, used as if it represented the whole doctrine, and separated from the control layer that limited it.

A third danger is **pedagogical inversion**: the simplified teaching order becomes the remembered research order. Readers first learn a vivid example, then treat the example as the reason for the doctrine, even when the doctrine was earned by routing, stress-testing, calibration, terminology audit, status assignment, and appeal rules.

This file adds a transmission layer: **excerpt compression, pedagogical use, output packaging, and reader-facing governance**. Its job is not to add another metaphysical operator. Its job is to govern how already licensed material leaves the archive in shorter, clearer, audience-specific forms without losing its constraints.

## Compressed default

The archive should now say:

> **Do not merely decide whether a verdict may travel; control the form in which it travels. Every summary, excerpt, teaching example, diagram, table, prompt answer, article draft, or public-facing output must preserve the minimum limits that made the original verdict safe.**

In shorter form:

> **Transmission is a metaphysical risk surface.**

And more carefully:

> **A simplified output is responsible only if it preserves the governing target, basis, grain, route, status, non-verdict, allowed use, forbidden upgrade, and review trigger appropriate to its compression level. If an output cannot carry those limits, it should be marked as orientation, illustration, prompt, or local aid rather than doctrine, precedent, calibration, or synthesis support.**

This file therefore shifts the archive from **reuse-governed classification** to **transmission-safe classification**.

## Place in the control sequence

The intended hard-case sequence is now:

1. **Route** the case or proposed addition with `144`.
2. **Stress-test** the routed verdict with `145`.
3. **Ledger** reusable or precedent-setting cases with `146`.
4. **Propagate** accepted changes with `147`.
5. **Register terminology** with `148`.
6. **Assign commitment status** with `149`.
7. **Write the application dossier** with `150` when the result must travel.
8. **Govern reuse, transfer, appeal, and review** with `151` when the result is cited, taught, exported, challenged, superseded, or used as precedent.
9. **Govern transmission and compression** with this file when a verdict, file, family map, source interpretation, teaching example, or synthesis claim is summarized, excerpted, translated into another format, or prepared for reader-facing use.

The difference between `151` and this file is simple. `151` asks: **may this result be reused, transferred, taught, appealed, or superseded?** This file asks: **what must remain visible when an allowed use is compressed, excerpted, simplified, taught, diagrammed, or repackaged?**

## The basic unit: the transmission packet

A **transmission packet** is the smallest bounded output that can leave the archive without becoming stronger than the archive's licensed result.

A packet should contain:

1. **Source pointer** — the file, dossier, precedent packet, section, or synthesis passage being transmitted.
2. **Output type** — orientation note, study guide, teaching example, worked example, summary paragraph, table, diagram, paper section, prompt answer, decision memo, source-dependent explanation, or revision note.
3. **Compression level** — title-only, orientation, compact summary, teaching summary, applied decision, technical brief, full report, or archive-grade extraction.
4. **Allowed audience/use** — private orientation, student explanation, collaborator discussion, public summary, source-domain application, revision control, calibration, or formal publication.
5. **Minimum required limits** — target, basis, grain, route, status, non-verdict, term status, source role, future-use permission, appeal trigger, and forbidden upgrade.
6. **Dropped material** — what was omitted for brevity: rivals, hostile variants, controls, source qualifications, open debts, lineage, detailed examples, or broader family map.
7. **Do-not-upgrade warning** — what the output must not be taken to show.
8. **Return path** — which file, dossier, precedent packet, or control layer a reader should consult before using the output as more than orientation.

A transmission packet need not be heavy. For a short answer it may be one sentence of metadata plus one sentence of warning. For a public-facing paper or teaching module it should be explicit.

## Compression levels

### C0. Title or label only

Use this for navigation, file names, headings, and index entries.

Minimum limits:

- do not let the title carry doctrine by itself;
- preserve the file pointer;
- avoid synonyms that change the target or family.

Forbidden upgrade: treating a heading as an accepted thesis.

### C1. Orientation gloss

Use this when a reader only needs to know what a file or distinction is roughly about.

Minimum limits:

- target family,
- nearest risk of confusion,
- no claim of settled doctrine beyond orientation.

Forbidden upgrade: citing the gloss as a verdict.

### C2. Compact summary

Use this for README prose, file maps, overview paragraphs, and conversation-level summaries.

Minimum limits:

- target,
- basis,
- nearest rivals,
- status level,
- do-not-upgrade warning.

Forbidden upgrade: treating the summary as a substitute for the file or dossier.

### C3. Teaching summary

Use this for explanation, classroom examples, onboarding notes, or first-pass diagrams.

Minimum limits:

- what the example illustrates,
- what it does not decide,
- whether it is only illustration or a controlled precedent,
- where to return for the full route and stress tests.

Forbidden upgrade: treating memorability as evidential force.

### C4. Applied decision memo

Use this when a result guides a case but is not meant to revise the archive.

Minimum limits:

- prompt or case,
- diagnostic rewrite,
- route,
- basis,
- grain,
- verdict,
- non-verdict,
- review trigger.

Forbidden upgrade: treating a local application as synthesis-level doctrine.

### C5. Technical brief

Use this for collaborator review, source-domain work, or methodological comparison.

Minimum limits:

- route,
- rivals,
- hostile variants,
- controls,
- source role,
- terminology status,
- commitment status,
- transfer conditions,
- appeal triggers.

Forbidden upgrade: exporting a domain-local or source-bound result archive-wide.

### C6. Full report

Use this when an output is likely to be cited, taught repeatedly, used as precedent, or incorporated into another project.

Minimum limits:

- full dossier or precedent packet,
- update-footprint result,
- source note if relevant,
- open debts,
- supersession rule,
- return path.

Forbidden upgrade: treating the report as final if its review triggers later fire.

### C7. Archive-grade extraction

Use this when producing a paper, handbook, derivative archive, lecture sequence, model card, decision framework, or major external artifact from the archive.

Minimum limits:

- explicit extraction scope,
- omitted families,
- inherited statuses,
- deprecations and open debts,
- source-anchor policy,
- revision lineage,
- manifest or file-map basis,
- update plan for future archive changes.

Forbidden upgrade: creating a fork that sounds current without declaring what revision it depends on.

## Output-type rules

### 1. README or front-door prose

A front-door summary should favor clarity, but it must not erase control depth. It should name the newest governance layer only if the layer has been wired into method rules, frontier tests, synthesis, index, source note, and manifest.

Minimum rule: include both the positive shift and the blocked misreading.

Example form:

```text
This revision adds X so that Y may happen, but it does not license Z.
```

### 2. File-map descriptions

A file-map line should not become a mini-thesis. It should say what confusion the file blocks and where it routes cases.

Minimum rule: file-map entries should be short enough to navigate but typed enough not to misroute.

### 3. Teaching examples

A teaching example must carry an explicit status:

- orientation only,
- illustration,
- worked example,
- controlled comparison,
- calibration case,
- regression warning,
- source-bound example,
- live rival,
- deprecated caution,
- do-not-reuse.

Minimum rule: teaching examples should include one sentence beginning **"This does not show..."** or equivalent.

### 4. Tables and grids

Tables are dangerous because they create visual finality. A table should not place kernel commitments, live rivals, provisional probes, source-local rules, deprecated items, and open debts in identical-looking rows without a status marker.

Minimum rule: include a status column, scope column, or warning column whenever the table will guide interpretation.

### 5. Diagrams and maps

Diagrams are dangerous because arrows look like metaphysical dependence. Every arrow should have a typed relation: route, contrast, dependence, causal influence, support, source role, update dependency, appeal relation, or mere navigation.

Minimum rule: no unlabeled arrow should carry doctrine.

### 6. Prompt answers and conversational summaries

A prompt answer should be allowed to be short, but it should not pretend the full audit has been shown. If the answer is local, say so. If it relies on a dossier, precedent, or source-bound rule, preserve that status.

Minimum rule: include one of these markers when relevant: **local answer**, **teaching illustration**, **controlled precedent**, **source-bound**, **provisional**, **open debt**, **do-not-reuse**, or **needs dossier before reuse**.

### 7. Paper sections and public essays

A paper or essay may compress the archive's machinery, but it should declare what compression is being performed: synthesis-only, method-only, case-only, source-dependent, historical, pedagogical, or polemical.

Minimum rule: do not use a public essay to promote a claim beyond its `149` status or `151` precedent permission.

### 8. Source-domain explanations

When explaining a source-dependent case to readers from a technical, legal, clinical, historical, institutional, or scientific domain, distinguish source authority from metaphysical authority.

Minimum rule: state whether the source role is constitutive, evidential, classificatory, historical, illustrative, or merely vocabulary-providing.

### 9. Derivative checklists

Checklists are useful, but they can flatten judgment into compliance. A checklist should indicate which items are mandatory, which are optional, and which are escalation triggers.

Minimum rule: a checklist should preserve the difference between orientation, local answer, dossier, precedent, and archive revision.

### 10. Model cards, rubrics, or decision frameworks

If the archive is translated into a reusable rubric, tool, or model card, the rubric must carry status and update lineage.

Minimum rule: state the archive revision, included control files, excluded families, allowed use, forbidden use, and update trigger.

## Minimum viable warnings

When there is no room for a full packet, use a minimum warning appropriate to the risk.

### Orientation warning

> **Orientation only: do not cite this as a verdict without consulting the file.**

### Illustration warning

> **Illustration only: this example teaches the distinction but does not calibrate future cases.**

### Local-answer warning

> **Local answer: do not reuse as precedent without a dossier and transfer test.**

### Source-bound warning

> **Source-bound: the source classification does not automatically become archive-wide metaphysics.**

### Status warning

> **Status limit: this is a working default / live rival / provisional probe / open debt, not kernel doctrine.**

### Term warning

> **Terminology warning: the ordinary word is a prompt, not a canonical operator unless registered.**

### Appeal warning

> **Appeal pending: cite only as contested until review is complete.**

### Do-not-reuse warning

> **Do not reuse: this case is retained only as a caution or negative example.**

## Excerpt rules

### Excerpt rule 1: carry the local frame

Never excerpt a verdict sentence without the target and grain if the result could be reused.

Bad pattern:

```text
X is a case of support rather than hosting.
```

Safer pattern:

```text
At the load-path grain, X is treated as support rather than hosting; this does not decide identity, ownership, or incorporation.
```

### Excerpt rule 2: carry the non-verdict

If the original dossier included a non-verdict, the excerpt must not omit it unless the excerpt is explicitly orientation-only.

### Excerpt rule 3: carry status and maturity when claims look doctrinal

If an excerpt contains words like **is**, **must**, **counts as**, **shows**, **grounds**, **proves**, **establishes**, **real**, **objective**, **same**, or **part**, include status or scope.

### Excerpt rule 4: carry source role when source terms appear

A technical, legal, historical, institutional, or empirical source term should not be excerpted as if the archive itself had accepted the source's ontology.

### Excerpt rule 5: carry the return path

Every nontrivial excerpt should tell the reader where to go before using it as precedent: file, dossier, precedent packet, source note, or control file.

## Pedagogical sequence

Teaching should usually move through five levels.

### Level 1. Orientation

Give the family and the danger.

Question: **What confusion is this distinction meant to prevent?**

### Level 2. Minimal contrast

Give one positive example and one near-rival.

Question: **What would look similar but belong elsewhere?**

### Level 3. Basis and grain

State what makes the diagnosis non-accidental and at what level it holds.

Question: **What changes if the grain changes?**

### Level 4. Non-verdict

State what the case does not show.

Question: **What forbidden upgrade is most tempting?**

### Level 5. Return to controls

Show how the case would be routed, attacked, ledgered, status-assigned, dossiered, and transferred if it were to become reusable.

Question: **What would make this example more than a teaching aid?**

The archive should not begin teaching with the full bureaucracy. But it also should not let teaching hide the bureaucracy that would be required for reuse.

### Private reader mode

### Student or onboarding mode

Need: examples, non-verdict, family map, warning against upgrade.

### Collaborator mode

Allowed compression: C2-C5.

Risk: assuming shared background memory.

### Source-domain expert mode

Allowed compression: C2-C6.

Need: source role, domain-local status, term crosswalk, forbidden export.

Risk: source authority becoming metaphysical authority.

### Public essay mode

Allowed compression: C2-C6.

Need: scope, status, disclaimers, return path.

Risk: polished prose becoming stronger than the archive.

### Revision-maintainer mode

Allowed compression: C4-C7.

Need: propagation, manifest, dependency graph, source note, open debts, review triggers.

Risk: packaging an output whose public version and archive version diverge.

### Derivative-project mode

Allowed compression: C6-C7.

Need: revision lineage, included files, excluded files, inherited statuses, update plan, do-not-upgrade constraints.

Risk: a fork that presents old or partial doctrine as the archive's current view.

## Transmission anti-patterns

### 1. Gloss absolutism

A useful one-line gloss becomes the doctrine.

Correction: add status, scope, and return path.

### 2. Example supremacy

The easiest example becomes more authoritative than the file.

Correction: mark examples as illustration, worked example, calibration, or precedent.

### 3. Table flattening

A table lists unequal items as if they had the same status.

Correction: add status and scope columns.

### 4. Arrow metaphysics

A diagram arrow silently means grounding, causation, dependence, support, route, or update dependency depending on reader preference.

Correction: label the arrow type.

### 5. Source export

A domain term is transmitted as if the source's classificatory practice were the archive's ontology.

Correction: state source role and source-bound status.

### 6. Non-verdict deletion

A verdict sentence travels while its limits are dropped.

Correction: attach the non-verdict to every reusable excerpt.

### 7. Status bleaching

Kernel commitments, defaults, live rivals, provisional probes, deprecations, and open debts are all summarized as “the archive says.”

Correction: name the commitment status.

### 8. Appeal erasure

A result is taught after a review trigger has fired.

Correction: mark appeal pending or superseded.

### 9. Revision ghosting

A derivative output does not say which archive revision it relies on.

Correction: state revision lineage and update trigger.

### 10. Compression alchemy

A short version becomes more metaphysically ambitious because its qualifications were removed.

Correction: shorten the claim, not just the caveats.

## Future expansion rule

Before adding another control, application, or transmission file after this one, ask whether the pressure is really new. A new file is warranted only if the archive lacks a reusable answer to one of these questions:

- Where does the case route?
- Does the verdict survive attack?
- Should the case become calibration?
- Where must the change propagate?
- What terms are safe?
- What status does the claim have?
- How should the result be reported?
- How may the reported result be reused, transferred, appealed, superseded, or taught?
- How should permitted material be compressed, excerpted, taught, diagrammed, summarized, or exported without losing its limits?
- How was the transmitted material received, and what correction loop follows?

If the pressure is a variant of one of those questions, amend `144` through `161` rather than adding another governance file.

## Closing formulation

The archive's current applied sequence can now be compressed as:

> **Route the case. Attack the route. Ledger the hard result. Propagate the accepted change. Register the words. Assign the commitment level. Write the auditable verdict. Govern its reuse and appeal. Control its transmission. Audit its reception. Gate the release.**

The point is not to make every summary slow. The point is to make speed honest. A short output should be short because it has selected its task, not because it has forgotten the limits that made the archive's result worth transmitting. After transmission, `153` asks whether the output actually preserved those limits in reception.
