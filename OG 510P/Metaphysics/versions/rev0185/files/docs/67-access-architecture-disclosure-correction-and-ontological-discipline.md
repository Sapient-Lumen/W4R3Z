# Access Architecture, Disclosure, Correction, and Ontological Discipline

## Why this file exists

The archive now has explicit files on **appearance**, **truth**, **objectivity**, **indispensability/promotion**, **measurement**, **directness**, **evidence**, **abduction**, **robustness/triangulation**, and **artifact risk**. But it still lacked one compact file saying how those pieces fit together when the archive moves from:

- a way of getting access,
- to a way of registering or representing,
- to a way of correcting error,
- to a way of supporting a claim,
- to a way of promoting some target into the ontology.

Without a dedicated file here, the archive risks seven recurrent mistakes:

- treating **access** as if it had to be either raw givenness or mere construction,
- treating **mediation** as if it automatically cancelled world-contact,
- treating **correction** as if it were only an embarrassing patch rather than part of serious access,
- treating **support** as if it already fixed the ontology,
- treating **many routes** as if they automatically converged on one target,
- treating **artifact analysis** as if it had already settled whether the target is real,
- or treating one lucky route as if it carried the whole metaphysics by itself.

This file gives the archive a compact **access architecture** so future revisions can say what target is in play, what route is doing the work, what mediation and correction profile exists, what support has actually been earned, and what ontological verdict—if any—follows.

## Working default

The archive should now say explicitly:

> **Treat access to reality as mediated, corrigible, plural, and category-sensitive rather than as either raw transparency or free construction.**

And more specifically:

> **Ontology should answer to disclosure, correction, and convergent pressure from inquiry, but no single access route should be allowed to legislate the final ontology by itself.**

This fits the archive's wider commitments:

- **appearance** matters because reality can show up in a way that is perspectival, selective, and still world-involving,
- **measurement** matters because interaction, setup, proxy choice, and calibration help determine what kind of access has been earned,
- **directness** matters because mediation can preserve contact while shaping what is disclosed,
- **evidence** matters because support is stronger than raw data but weaker than automatic metaphysical settlement,
- **abduction** matters because explanation can create ontological pressure without becoming proof,
- **robustness** matters because route-variation can strengthen support without guaranteeing one heavy ontology,
- and **artifact analysis** matters because failures teach us where access breaks, what survives correction, and what sort of caution is actually earned.

## The access chain

Future revisions should distinguish at least these stages whenever inquiry is being used to bear on metaphysics:

1. **target** — what is supposedly being tracked,
2. **route** — perception, intervention, instrument, model, testimony, record, simulation, or mixed access,
3. **setup/interface** — the arrangement through which access is gained,
4. **registration/representation** — readout, model, display, variable, record, map, or score,
5. **reference/target-fixation** — what exactly the registration, term, or vehicle is latched onto,
6. **interpretation** — what the registration is taken to show,
7. **correction/checking** — calibration, route-variation, perturbation, adversarial testing, reanalysis, triangulation, or other defeat-handling,
8. **promotion verdict** — whether the case supports an entity, process, mechanism, structure, response-dependent profile, derivative status only, or no settled ontology.

Without this chain, the archive will keep letting one word like “observed”, “measured”, “direct”, “evidenced”, “robust”, or “artifact” do too much metaphysical work.

## Core distinctions

### 1. Access route is not target

The archive should distinguish:

- what is being tracked,
- what is doing the tracking,
- and what is merely carrying the registration.

Otherwise it will keep sliding from **vehicle success** to **target success**, or from **vehicle failure** to **target nonexistence**.

### 2. Disclosure, representation, and intervention are not one thing

The archive should distinguish:

- cases where reality is **presented**,
- cases where it is **represented**,
- cases where it is **perturbed or manipulated**,
- and cases where these are tightly mixed.

Otherwise it will keep treating observation, measurement, experiment, simulation, and model-use as one flat route.

### 3. Mediation is not defeat

The archive should distinguish:

- enabling mediation,
- selective mediation,
- proxy-based mediation,
- distorting mediation,
- constitutive interface dependence,
- and route defeat.

Otherwise it will keep sliding from “through a route” to “not really about the world”.

### 4. Correction is part of access, not a late afterthought

The archive should distinguish:

- brute initial uptake,
- calibration,
- adversarial checking,
- cross-route comparison,
- perturbation and stress-testing,
- and long-run error discipline.

Otherwise it will keep imagining access as if it were either pristine at the start or worthless after the first correction demand.

### 5. Support and promotion come apart

The archive should distinguish:

- support for a registration,
- support for a target,
- support for one description of the target,
- support for one explanatory profile,
- and full ontological promotion.

Otherwise it will keep sliding from “this is supported” to “this is what there fundamentally is”.

### 6. Convergence is not mere plurality

The archive should distinguish:

- repeated success inside one route,
- multi-route support with shared background load,
- genuine independence under relevant variation,
- robustness around one proxy,
- and strong convergence on one target.

Otherwise it will keep calling many similar echoes “triangulation”.

### 7. Access must remain category-sensitive

The archive should distinguish what a route can support about:

- compact objects,
- processes,
- mechanisms,
- structures,
- powers,
- response-dependent profiles,
- social or institutional realities,
- and merely representational scaffolds.

Otherwise it will keep assuming that every successful route should deliver the same kind of ontology.

## Four live options

### 1. Transparency-first realism

**Picture:** successful access is mostly a matter of direct world-presentation; mediation and correction are secondary complications.

**What it gets right:**
- some routes do yield strong world-contact,
- and not every successful case needs endless caution.

**What it gets wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- it underdescribes setup dependence, theory-load, proxy structure, and correctional work.

### 2. Construction-first anti-realism

**Picture:** since access is always mediated and practice-laden, inquiry reveals only our own schemes, tools, or stabilizations.

**What it gets right:**
- routes are shaped by practices, instruments, and models,
- and access is never from nowhere.

**What it gets wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- it underdescribes resistance from the target side and cannot explain why some routes survive correction better than others.

### 3. Local empiricist restraint

**Picture:** access can justify empirical adequacy, operational success, or narrow working commitments, but it should not create much ontological pressure.

**What it gets right:**
- route success does not force a full inventory of what exists,
- and historical theory change should keep humility alive.

**What it gets wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- it leaves too little room for disciplined promotion of processes, mechanisms, structures, powers, or derivative but real domains.

### 4. Typed corrective realism

**Picture:** access is mediated, corrigible, and plural. Some routes support only local or operational claims; some support selective realism about structures, mechanisms, or process profiles; and some support stronger ontological verdicts. What matters is not raw givenness but disciplined disclosure plus correction.

**What it gets right:**
- it preserves realism without transparency mythology,
- it preserves fallibilism without anti-realist flattening,
- and it makes correction part of how world-answerability is earned.

**What it risks if left alone:**
- it can become a slogan unless each case states target, route, correction profile, alternative routes, and promotion threshold explicitly.

## Archive commitments

### 1. Never infer ontology from one naked route-description

Do not let words like **observed**, **measured**, **directly given**, **evidenced**, **replicated**, or **artifact** settle the metaphysics by themselves.

### 2. Keep the route and the target explicitly separate

Successful access may disclose a target through a proxy, model, or interface without identifying the target with that vehicle.

### 3. Treat correction as positive structure

Calibration, error analysis, perturbation, reanalysis, and route variation are part of serious world-contact rather than signs that no contact was ever present.

### 4. Distinguish support from promotion

Even strong support may justify realism only about a process, structure, mechanism, or response-profile rather than a compact object or heavyweight kind.

### 5. Let failure verdicts stay local when they should

A route can fail without the target vanishing, and one proxy can collapse without defeating every route of access.

### 6. Keep realism graded and category-sensitive

Some domains earn stronger promotion than others, and some earn only derivative, structural, processual, or response-dependent reality.

## Access-architecture rule for future revisions

Before inferring a metaphysical verdict from language like **we access**, **it is disclosed**, **observed directly**, **measured**, **detected**, **evidenced**, **best explained**, **robust across methods**, **triangulated**, **artifact-prone**, **only a model**, **through an interface**, **the readout shows**, or **the world presents itself here**, future revisions should answer at least these questions:

1. What is the **target**?
2. What is the **route of access**?
3. What is the **setup, interface, or mediation layer**?
4. What exactly is being **registered or represented**?
5. What **background interpretation** is turning that registration into support?
6. What **failure modes** are live?
7. What **correction profile** exists?
8. What **alternative routes** remain available?
9. What kind of **support** has actually been earned?
10. What kind of **promotion verdict**—if any—follows?

If those questions cannot be answered, the archive should usually withhold heavy ontological promotion.

## Bottom line

The archive should now prefer a **typed corrective realism about access**:

- reality can be disclosed without being rawly given,
- mediation can preserve contact,
- correction is part of access,
- support can be strong without being final,
- promotion can be earned without being indiscriminate,
- and ontological seriousness should increase where disclosure, correction, and convergent pressure survive the relevant failure modes.

That is the compact access-architecture rule the archive was missing.
