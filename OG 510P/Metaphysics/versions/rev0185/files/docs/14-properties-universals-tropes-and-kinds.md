# Properties, Universals, Tropes, and Kinds

This file gives the archive a compact answer to a question that several earlier files relied on without stating directly:

**what, if anything, is genuinely shared across particulars, and what makes a kind ontologically serious?**

## Why this file exists

The archive now has explicit views about grounding, emergence, lawfulness, persistence, and truthmaking. But those views still depend on a quieter issue:

1. when two things are really similar rather than merely similarly described,
2. whether powers and forms are repeatable features,
3. whether concrete features are best treated as shared universals or particularized instances,
4. and whether kind-talk tracks reality or only our classificatory convenience.

Without a compact answer, the archive risks three failures:

- using **property** language as if every predicate named a real entity,
- using **kind** language as if classification automatically revealed ontology,
- or using **nominalist economy** to thin the ontology so much that real similarity, induction, and modal structure become mysterious.

## Core archive thesis

The archive should now adopt the following default:

> **Be realist about repeatable form- and power-profiles where explanation requires it, sparse about which properties are ontologically serious, open to trope-like particularization for concrete instances, and domain-sensitive about kinds.**

In compressed form:

> **Not every predicate marks a property; not every property is fundamental; not every kind has one rigid essence; but some repeatables and some kinds are real enough that metaphysics cannot do without them.**

## Four distinctions the archive should now keep explicit

### 1. Predicate is not yet property

A useful word or predicate does not by itself earn ontological promotion.

The archive should ask whether the candidate feature does explanatory work in:

- causal or modal description,
- induction,
- kind-membership,
- persistence criteria,
- or grounding and constitution.

If not, the archive should hesitate before reifying it.

### 2. Property is not yet universal

Even if a feature is ontologically serious, it does not follow that it must be a **universal** in the strongest sense.

The archive should keep open at least three possibilities:

- **immanent universal** — one repeatable feature instantiated in many cases,
- **trope** — a particularized instance of a feature in this case,
- **structured resemblance or nominalist surrogate** — a thinner account when realist commitment is not needed.

### 3. Universal is not yet kind

A property and a kind are not the same job.

A property may be repeatable without determining a persistence condition. A kind often does more:

- tells us what something is,
- constrains what counts as a good instance,
- supports inductive and modal expectations,
- and helps fix unity and persistence conditions.

Kinds are therefore closer to the archive's existing concern with **form** than to bare predicate application.

### 4. Kind is not yet essence in one rigid sense

Some kinds may be essence-laden. Others may be stabilized by clusters, structures, genealogies, or normative practices.

The archive should resist both extremes:

- the view that every real kind has one timeless, exceptionless essence stated in a neat formula,
- and the view that kinds are never more than optional human sorting devices.

## Main ontological options

### Option A: abundant universals

Every genuine predicate corresponds to a repeatable property.

#### Archive judgment

Too inflationary for this archive. It multiplies entities faster than the archive's compression rules allow.

### Option B: sparse realism about universals or repeatables

Only some features are ontologically serious enough to count as real repeatables.

#### Archive judgment

This is the current archive default.

It fits the archive's existing commitments:

- real forms,
- real powers,
- real modal structure,
- and kind-sensitive persistence.

### Option C: trope theory

Concrete particulars possess their own particularized feature-instances, and resemblance among those tropes helps explain similarity.

#### Archive judgment

Keep this live and locally useful.

Trope talk is especially helpful when the archive wants to emphasize:

- this organism's particular metabolic organization,
- this artifact's specific defect,
- this institution's actual authority profile,
- or this event's concrete modal setup.

But a pure trope-only picture may leave the archive working too hard to explain stable repeatability, kindhood, and lawfulness across cases.

### Option D: austere nominalism

There are particulars, perhaps classes or predicates, but no repeatable properties or universals.

#### Archive judgment

Useful as a discipline against ontological sprawl, but too thin as the archive default.

The archive already leans on real similarity, powers, forms, and kind-sensitive persistence. Those commitments are difficult to keep robust under an aggressively nominalist baseline.

## Current archive default: sparse immanent realism

The archive should now work with the following picture unless a later revision clearly improves on it.

### 1. Some repeatables are real

At minimum, the archive should be realist about repeatables where they are needed to explain:

- stable similarity,
- causal powers,
- lawful or modal profiles,
- kind-membership,
- and the persistence of forms across changing matter or phases.

### 2. Repeatables are better treated as immanent than transcendent by default

The archive does not currently need a heavy commitment to transcendent universals existing wholly apart from instantiations.

A better fit is:

- **immanent repeatables** for the shared form- or power-profile,
- **particularized instances** for the concrete case,
- and **structured organizations** for the way those repeatables are sustained in process.

### 3. Tropes are admissible as local feature-instances

The archive should be happy to say that this redness, this fragility, this authority-profile, or this wound is a particularized feature-instance when that helps track concrete constitution or process.

But trope-language should not silently replace all talk of shared powers, forms, or kinds.

### 4. Kinds are domain-sensitive ontological organizers

Kinds matter when they stabilize explanation and constrain what counts as an entity, process, defect, or persistence condition.

The archive should treat kinds as ontologically serious when they support some combination of:

- explanatory compression,
- inductive reliability,
- modal expectation,
- persistence criteria,
- boundary conditions,
- or norm-governed role conditions.

## Natural kinds: archive stance

The archive should keep **natural kind realism** live, but in a disciplined and plural way.

### Stronger cases

The archive has the strongest reason to be realist about kinds when a domain displays:

- recurrent structure,
- stable powers,
- non-arbitrary boundaries,
- and explanatory usefulness across inquiry.

### More mixed cases

The archive should allow that some kinds are:

- **homeostatic or clustered** rather than strictly essence-defined,
- **historical or genealogical** rather than timeless,
- or **institutional and role-governed** rather than purely natural-scientific.

This fits the archive's broader realism better than a one-model-for-all-kinds view.

## How this connects to earlier files

### With form

Kinds and properties are not foreign additions to the archive's form-talk. They help specify what is stably repeatable and intelligible across instances.

### With powers

If powers are real, then at least some repeatable profiles must be real enough to underwrite them.

### With structure

Some properties and kinds are not intrinsic point-features but structurally sustained profiles.

### With persistence

Kinds help fix what counts as surviving change. Without kind-discipline, persistence debates become too abstract.

### With truthmakers

Property and kind claims should not be allowed to generate ontology automatically. The truthmaker file already tells the archive to prefer the leanest adequate positive basis. This file says what sorts of repeatables and kinds may legitimately count among those bases.

## Archive decision rule

When a future revision uses property or kind language, it should answer four questions:

1. **Is this merely a predicate, or an ontologically serious feature?**
2. **If ontologically serious, is it best treated as a repeatable, a trope-like instance, or both at different levels?**
3. **Does the candidate kind do explanatory, modal, or persistence work?**
4. **Is the kind essence-like, cluster-like, genealogical, structural, or norm-governed?**

If the revision cannot answer those questions, it is probably using property or kind language too loosely.

## Working conclusion

The archive should now adopt the following property-and-kind policy:

> **Be sparse realists about repeatables, permissive about trope-like particularization where concrete cases demand it, and plural but serious about kinds.**

That is the cleanest fit with the archive's current commitments to form, process, powers, structure, grounding, persistence, and truthmaker discipline.
