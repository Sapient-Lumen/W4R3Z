# Ontological Revision, Selective Retention, and Elimination

## Why this file exists

The archive now has explicit files on **evidence**, **abduction**, **ontological promotion**, **access architecture**, **reference**, **objectivity**, and **ontological status**. But it still lacked one compact file saying how the archive should revise its ontology **across inquiry and theory change**.

Without a dedicated file here, the archive risks seven recurrent mistakes:

- treating any serious theory change as if it automatically meant **wholesale ontological overthrow**,
- treating **lexical continuity** or **stable reference-practice** as if it automatically meant **stable target continuity**,
- treating explanatory or predictive success of a successor theory as if it automatically erased every target posited by the predecessor,
- treating **selective retention**, **reinterpretation**, **target-splitting**, **target-merging**, **status-demotion**, and **elimination** as if they were one flat kind of revision,
- treating approximate retention of **structure**, **mechanism**, **causal role**, or **process-profile** as if it had to amount either to total sameness or total failure,
- treating category-shift cases—entity to process, fluid to field-profile, force to interaction law, office-holder to office, object to organization—as if they were semantically embarrassing exceptions rather than a normal part of disciplined ontology revision,
- or treating ontological revision itself as if it were a skeptical embarrassment rather than a routine sign that inquiry is reality-answerable, corrigible, and category-sensitive.

This file gives the archive a compact **revision profile** so future revisions can say what changed, what was retained, what was reinterpreted, what was split or merged, what was demoted or eliminated, and what sort of realism remains live after revision.

## Working default

The archive should now say explicitly:

> **Ontological revision should be treated as typed rather than flat: later inquiry can preserve, reinterpret, split, merge, demote, elevate, or eliminate what earlier inquiry posited.**

And more specifically:

> **The non-skeptical default should be selective retention under disciplined revision: continuity is earned, not presumed, and it may lie in target, structure, process-profile, mechanism, role, intervention-pattern, or only a thinner world-tracking handle.**

This fits the archive's wider commitments:

- **evidence** matters because support can improve without settling every ontological detail,
- **abduction** matters because explanatory superiority may create pressure for revision rather than only for preservation,
- **promotion** matters because not every retained explanatory role earns the same ontological status,
- **reference** matters because names and variables can preserve, drift, split, or retarget across theory change,
- **status discipline** matters because a target may remain real while moving from fundamental to derivative, or from compact entity to distributed process,
- **access architecture** matters because the route of access that first introduced a target may later be reinterpreted without being dismissed as wholly empty,
- and **realism under theory change** matters because inquiry can remain world-answerable without requiring one frozen ontology at every stage.

## Core distinctions

### 1. Theory change is not yet ontological overthrow

The archive should distinguish:

- **better theory about the same target**,
- **better theory about partly the same target**,
- **reinterpretation of the same worldly phenomenon under a different category**,
- **target-splitting**,
- **target-merging**,
- and **genuine elimination**.

Otherwise it will keep treating every deep revision as either triumphalist continuity or total metaphysical collapse.

### 2. Lexical continuity is not yet target continuity

The same word, variable, office label, or detector channel can persist while:

- the target stays stable,
- the target is narrowed,
- the target is broadened,
- one old target is split into several,
- several old targets are unified,
- or the label is preserved while the ontology changes underneath it.

Otherwise the archive will keep reading one reused term as if it guaranteed one unchanged ontology.

### 3. Retention is not always identity-level retention

What survives revision may be:

- a compact **entity**,
- a **process-profile**,
- a **mechanism**,
- a **structure**,
- a **causal role**,
- an **intervention handle**,
- a **response-dependent pattern**,
- an **institutional office or role**,
- or only a thinner **world-tracking approximation**.

Otherwise the archive will keep forcing selective realism either into all-or-nothing entity realism or into timid abstinence.

### 4. Elimination is not the same as total error about everything

The archive should distinguish:

- **elimination of one ontology while preserving the phenomenon**,
- **elimination of one explanatory posit while preserving the model target**,
- **elimination of one target because it was only a proxy or artifact**,
- **demotion to derivative or response-dependent status**,
- and **genuine empty posit with no retained target**.

Otherwise it will keep treating every rejected ontology as if it meant the inquiry never touched reality at all.

### 5. Revision may involve splitting, merging, or recategorizing targets

The archive should leave live that later inquiry may show that:

- one earlier target was really several,
- several earlier targets were one,
- a supposed object is better treated as a process, organization, or field-profile,
- a supposed intrinsic property is better treated as relational or response-dependent,
- or a supposed fundamental item is better treated as derivative but robust.

Otherwise the archive will keep assuming that ontology revision must be only preserve-or-delete.

### 6. Approximate truth and selective realism remain live under revision

A predecessor framework may remain valuable because it retained:

- real structure,
- a real process-profile,
- genuine mechanism fragments,
- intervention-guiding regularities,
- limited-range approximations,
- or public access to a target later better understood.

Otherwise the archive will keep oscillating between naive triumphalism and the pessimistic thought that once revision happens, nothing serious was ever known.

### 7. Status revision matters as much as existence revision

The archive should distinguish shifts such as:

- **fundamental → derivative but robust**,
- **compact entity → distributed organization**,
- **mind-independent → response-dependent but real**,
- **real target → idealized but world-tracking surrogate**,
- and **world-tracking surrogate → merely representational scaffolding**.

Otherwise it will keep using existence as if it were the only ontological verdict that could change.

## Four live options

### 1. Wholesale pessimism about theory change

**Picture:** major theory change shows that previous ontologies were largely false and abandoned.

**What it gets right:**
- inquiry is corrigible,
- and historical change should block cheap triumphalism.

**What it gets wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- it underdescribes selective retention, approximate truth, stable intervention handles, and continuity of worldly constraint across revision.

### 2. Static continuity triumphalism

**Picture:** later theories mostly preserve the same ontology under cleaner description.

**What it gets right:**
- some revisions do preserve targets,
- and continuity is often stronger than skeptical stories allow.

**What it gets wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- it underdescribes target drift, category-shift, splitting, merging, demotion, and genuine elimination.

### 3. Empirical-adequacy-only replacementism

**Picture:** theory change should mainly be read as change in successful representation, without strong ontological lessons either from retention or replacement.

**What it gets right:**
- ontological overreach is a real risk,
- and representational success should not be turned too quickly into metaphysics.

**What it gets wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- it underdescribes why some revisions preserve more than empirical fit: mechanism, structure, target continuity, intervention stability, and selective realism.

### 4. Typed selective revision realism

**Picture:** the right lesson of theory change is usually neither total continuity nor total overthrow, but typed revision. What persists may be an entity, process, mechanism, structure, role, or thinner world-tracking handle; what changes may be category, status, boundaries, grain, or explanatory embedding.

**What it gets right:**
- it preserves corrigibility without skepticism,
- it allows history to discipline current ontology,
- it fits target-splitting, merging, recategorization, and status-demotion better than flat continuity stories,
- and it lets realism survive revision in selective, typed ways.

**What it risks if left alone:**
- it can become hand-wavy unless each case states exactly what is retained, what is revised, and what sort of ontological verdict remains.

## Archive commitments

### 1. Always state the revision type

Do not let “the later theory replaced the earlier one” settle whether the case is preservation, reinterpretation, split, merge, demotion, elimination, or mixed revision.

### 2. Separate retained phenomenon from revised ontology

A change in the best ontology of a case does not automatically show that earlier inquiry failed to latch onto anything real.

### 3. Distinguish target retention from role retention

What survives may be the same target, or only the same explanatory role, measurement handle, or intervention profile.

### 4. Keep category-shift live

Allow that later inquiry may revise not only **whether** something is real, but **what kind of thing** it is.

### 5. Let status change without forced elimination

A target can be preserved while being demoted from fundamental to derivative, from intrinsic to relational, or from compact entity to organized process.

### 6. Do not infer sameness from one label

Lexical continuity is evidence at most for a revision question, not a settled answer.

### 7. Do not infer nonexistence from every replacement

Some eliminations are real; others are better read as reinterpretations, target-splitting, or thinner selective realism.

## Revision profile rule for future revisions

Before inferring a final metaphysical lesson from language like **the later theory replaced**, **same target**, **we now know that X was really Y**, **eliminated**, **retained**, **reinterpreted**, **approximately true**, **merely a useful fiction**, **split into**, **unified with**, **no longer exists**, **only a role**, **only structure remained**, **just a surrogate**, or **revisionary metaphysics now says**, future revisions should answer at least these questions:

1. **prior posit** — what earlier inquiry took itself to be talking about;
2. **successor posit** — what later inquiry now treats as the best target or targets;
3. **continuity basis** — reference continuity, causal role, structure, process-profile, intervention handle, institutional continuity, phenomenal anchor, or mixed basis;
4. **change driver** — new evidence, new measurement route, explanatory superiority, artifact correction, target-splitting, grain-shift, or mixed driver;
5. **revision type** — preservation, reinterpretation, split, merge, status-demotion, status-elevation, elimination, or mixed revision;
6. **category shift** — entity to process, entity to structure, property to relation, compact object to organization, mind-independent to response-dependent, fundamental to derivative, or none;
7. **what is retained** — entity, process, mechanism, structure, practical handle, lawlike role, approximate fit, or only historical continuity of discourse;
8. **what is lost** — ontology, category, explanatory embedding, boundaries, status, reference security, or only one misleading gloss;
9. **current realism verdict** — stable target realism, selective realism, derivative/status realism, approximation-only realism, empirical-adequacy-only caution, elimination, or still unsettled;
10. **forward-use rule** — whether future archive claims should continue the old term, qualify it, split it, retire it, or keep it only as historical scaffolding.

If those questions cannot be answered, the archive should usually refuse to let “theory change” language do heavy ontological work by itself.

## Bottom line

The archive should now prefer a **typed realism about ontological revision**:

- later inquiry can preserve, reinterpret, split, merge, demote, elevate, or eliminate targets,
- continuity is real but must be earned,
- revision may preserve entities, processes, mechanisms, structures, roles, or only thinner world-tracking handles,
- and theory change is not automatically either skeptical defeat or flat triumphalist continuity.

That is the compact revision-discipline the archive was missing.
