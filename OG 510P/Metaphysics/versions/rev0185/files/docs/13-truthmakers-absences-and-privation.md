# Truthmakers, Absences, and Privation

This file gives the archive a compact policy for **what in reality makes truths true**, especially when the truths concern **absence, lack, negation, omission, or failure**.

## Why this file exists

The archive now has a disciplined account of grounding, dependence, emergence, lawfulness, and persistence. But it still lacked a clear answer to three questions:

1. **What in reality should truth answer to?**
2. **Do negative truths require negative entities?**
3. **When should an absence count as ontologically serious rather than merely linguistic?**

Without a compact answer, the archive risks two opposite failures:

- **truthmaker inflation** — positing facts or states of affairs whenever a sentence is true,
- **deflation drift** — treating truth as so thin that metaphysical questions about what the world must contain never get a grip.

## Working archive thesis

The archive should now adopt the following rule:

> **Truth should answer to reality, but not every truth requires its own bespoke entity.**

And, more specifically:

> **Prefer the leanest adequate positive ontological ground for a truth before positing negative facts, totality facts, or sui generis absences.**

This is not eliminativism about truthmakers. It is a **restrained truthmaker discipline**.

The fuller typed treatment of truth itself now lives in `57-truth-correspondence-and-reality-answerability.md`.

## Minimal truthmaker discipline

The archive should ask four questions in order.

### 1. Is a truthmaker claim needed here at all?

Use truthmaker language when a metaphysical claim is load-bearing:

- existence claims,
- dependence claims,
- modal constraints,
- causal-explanatory claims,
- or disputes about what kinds of entities the world must contain.

Do **not** demand a bespoke truthmaker for every harmless logical, mathematical, or semantically regimented truth unless the metaphysical stakes require it.

### 2. What is the leanest positive ontological basis?

Before positing any exotic item, ask whether the truth is already secured by:

- an existing entity,
- an obtaining state of affairs,
- a stable structure,
- a realized organization,
- a lawlike or modal constraint,
- or a bounded totality.

### 3. Is the truth negative only in grammar, or also in ontology?

Many truths with a negative surface form may be grounded in positive arrangements plus incompatibility, exclusion, or closure conditions.

Examples:

- “There is no oxygen in the chamber” may be fixed by the chamber’s total gas composition.
- “This organism is blind” may not require a negative fact floating free of the organism; it may be a **privation** relative to the kind of organism and its expected capacities.
- “There are no more members of this set” may be fixed by a bounded totality condition.

### 4. If absence talk remains indispensable, what kind of absence is it?

Distinguish at least three cases:

1. **mere negation** — simple not-`p` talk with no further ontological lesson,
2. **bounded absence** — non-presence fixed by a totality, closure, or occupancy condition,
3. **privation** — absence of a form, capacity, or feature that is natural, proper, or expected for the relevant kind,
4. **omission/blocked process** — a causally salient non-occurrence anchored in what would normally sustain or interrupt a process.

These should not be treated as one phenomenon.

## Archive default on truthmakers

The archive should remain realist enough to say:

- truths are not free-floating,
- reality constrains what can be true,
- and some truths require ontological commitments.

But it should reject the stronger reflex that **every truth must have a one-to-one proprietary entity**.

A better rule for this archive is:

> **Truthmaker demands increase with metaphysical load.**

So the archive should be most demanding in cases about:

- what exists,
- what is fundamental,
- what grounds what,
- what powers and laws there are,
- what absences are causally or normatively serious,
- and what kinds of higher-level realities must be admitted.

## Negative truths: archive stance

Negative truths are a pressure point.

The archive should not default to an ontology crowded with indefinitely many **negative facts**. That is usually too expensive.

But it should also resist the opposite temptation of saying that negative truths are merely linguistic shadows with no ontological discipline at all.

The current archive preference is a three-step order:

### First preference: positive incompatibility or structured exclusion

Where possible, ground a negative truth in positive reality plus real incompatibility, exclusion, or boundary conditions.

### Second preference: bounded totality

Where a domain is closed or complete in the relevant respect, use a **totality-style** treatment.

### Third preference: local negative commitment only when forced

If a case cannot be handled by positive structure, incompatibility, or bounded totality, then allow a local negative commitment rather than multiplying negative entities indiscriminately.

## Absences and causation

The archive should not say simply that absences are unreal because they are “nothing”. Contemporary causation debates treat omissions and absences as a real metaphysical pressure point.

But the archive also should not treat absences as basic items on a par with rocks, cells, or fields.

The present default is:

> **Causally salient absences are usually derivative profiles of interrupted, missing, or blocked sustaining processes in a structured field of expectations and dependencies.**

Examples:

- failure to water a plant,
- lack of oxygen in a chamber,
- omission of a signal in a control system,
- absence of an immune response,
- failure to ratify a law in an institutional process.

In such cases, the archive should ask:

1. what positive process would normally have occurred,
2. what structure or norm fixed that expectation,
3. what blocked, removed, or failed to sustain it,
4. and whether the explanatory role is genuinely causal, constitutive, or normative.

This keeps omission-talk from becoming mystical while preserving its explanatory importance.

## Privation: the archive's preferred middle category

**Privation** is the archive’s most useful middle category between brute negative facts and pure semantic negation.

A privation is not just any absence. It is the **absence of what should naturally, properly, or structurally be present for a thing of that kind**.

That matters because many metaphysical and normative domains are better described in privative than purely negative terms:

- blindness in a sighted animal,
- decay in a living body,
- corruption in an institution,
- silence where signaling is functionally required,
- defect in an artifact relative to its form and function.

Privation fits the archive because the archive is already:

- **kind-sensitive**,
- **form-sensitive**,
- **power-sensitive**,
- and **process-sensitive**.

Privation language should therefore be used when an absence is indexed to:

- a kind,
- an expected capacity,
- a sustaining form,
- or a norm-governed role.

## Truthmakers and the current synthesis

This file modifies **Layered Process Realism** in a useful way.

The fuller typed treatment of **facts**, **states of affairs**, and **obtaining** now lives in `56-facts-states-of-affairs-and-obtaining.md`.

The synthesis should now say not only that higher-level realities are grounded and organized, but also that:

- truth is world-answerable,
- negative truths should be handled with restraint,
- absences are often derivative rather than basic,
- and privations are ontologically illuminating where form and function matter.

This helps in several domains at once:

### Life

Disease, deficiency, injury, and death often involve privation or process-failure, not merely positive replacement facts.

### Mind

Ignorance, forgetting, repression, inattention, and silence often involve organized lack rather than the presence of a positive mental item with negative content.

### Social reality

Vacancy, invalidity, default, noncompliance, and legal omission are frequently absential or privative statuses anchored in institutional forms and procedures.

### Modality and lawfulness

Constraint often appears negatively in description but positively in ontology: not every impossible configuration needs its own negative fact when a structured modal profile already excludes it.

## Archive compression rule

When a future revision introduces “absence”, “lack”, “nothing”, “failure”, “negation”, or “non-being”, it should specify which of the following it means:

- simple negation,
- bounded absence,
- privation,
- omission,
- or genuine negative commitment.

If it does not do this, the addition is under-disciplined.

## Working conclusion

The archive should now adopt the following policy:

> **Be realist about truth’s answerability to reality, sparse about bespoke truthmakers, cautious about negative facts, open to totality where bounded domains demand it, and positive about privation where kind, function, or form make an absence ontologically serious.**

That is the current archive default on truth, absence, and non-being adjacent talk.
