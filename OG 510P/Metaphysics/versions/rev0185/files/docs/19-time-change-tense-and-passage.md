# Time, Change, Tense, and Passage

This file gives the archive a compact policy for **time**.

## Why this file exists

The archive already treats:

- **process** as real,
- **persistence** as kind-sensitive,
- **causation** as dynamic rather than merely constitutive,
- and **modality** as disciplined by essence, powers, structure, and laws.

But without a direct temporal file, the archive risks sliding among at least five different questions as if they were one:

1. **temporal order and metric**,
2. **change and persistence**,
3. **tense and presentness**,
4. **passage or becoming**,
5. and **what physics does or does not settle**.

A compact non-reductive metaphysics needs all five in view, but it should not confuse them.

## Four live options

### 1. Tenseless / B-theoretic priority views

**Picture:** reality is fundamentally ordered by earlier/later and perhaps by metric or causal structure; past, present, and future are not ontologically distinguished by an objective moving now.

**What they get right:**
- they preserve temporal order without reifying a global spotlight,
- they fit naturally with much contemporary spacetime representation,
- and they avoid easy anthropomorphic pictures of time “flowing” as an extra ingredient.

**What they get wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- they can underdescribe lived and processual becoming,
- and they can make change sound thinner than the archive wants it to be.

### 2. Presentist views

**Picture:** only what is present exists.

**What they get right:**
- they take seriously the thought that presentness matters ontologically,
- they fit some ordinary intuitions about change and openness,
- and they preserve a robust distinction between what is and what merely was or will be.

**What they get wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- they face familiar pressure from truth about the past and cross-time relations,
- and they can overcommit the archive on what physics and persistence require.

### 3. Growing-block or no-futurist views

**Picture:** past and present exist, but future entities do not yet.

**What they get right:**
- they retain ontological becoming more explicitly than eternalism,
- they preserve reality for the settled past,
- and they give the archive one serious way to take “growth” in reality literally.

**What they get wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- they inherit difficult epistemic questions about why we should think we are at the leading edge,
- and they can harden one image of becoming too quickly.

### 4. Process / local-becoming views

**Picture:** the deepest temporal reality is not necessarily a single global moving present, but the real unfolding of events, processes, and proper-time-like succession along worldlines, systems, and organized histories.

**What they get right:**
- they fit the archive's process-first commitments,
- they preserve real change without forcing one monolithic A-theory,
- and they are more hospitable to physics-sensitive accounts of local succession and irreversible organization.

**What they get wrong if left alone:**
- they can become vague unless they distinguish local becoming from metaphor,
- and they can understate what tense or presentness is doing in ordinary and practical ontology.

## Current archive answer

The archive should adopt a **realist but non-dogmatic temporal policy**:

> **Temporal order, duration, and change are real; persistence is a temporally articulated achievement of organized things; tense and passage are not automatically illusory, but the archive should earn any heavy ontology of a global moving present rather than assume it at the start.**

That means:

- time is not merely a bookkeeping device,
- change is not derivative illusion,
- persistence and passage should not be conflated,
- presentism, eternalism, and growing-block views remain live options,
- and the archive presently leans toward **local or processual becoming** rather than toward a fully flat static picture or an already-settled global A-theory.

In compressed form:

> **be realist about temporal structure and becoming; stay cautious about inflating presentness into a global ontological primitive.**

## Archive commitments

### 1. Distinguish persistence from temporal ontology

Whether something persists by enduring, perduring, stage-like succession, organizational continuity, or domain-specific criteria is **not yet the same question** as whether only the present exists, all times exist, or reality grows.

### 2. Distinguish tense from passage

Tensed discourse may mark:

- perspective,
- indexical location,
- pragmatic orientation,
- institutional timing,
- or a genuine feature of temporal reality.

The archive should not assume in advance that tense is either purely linguistic dressing or a proof of a moving now.

### 3. Be cautious about global temporal pictures

The archive should prefer the weakest temporal commitment that still preserves:

- real change,
- ordered succession,
- irreversible organization where it is real,
- and domain-appropriate persistence.

This means that **global presentism, global eternalism, and global growing-block metaphysics remain live** rather than default.

### 4. Treat local becoming as more secure than cosmic flow-talk

At present the archive should treat the following as better grounded than a universal “flow” metaphor:

- event succession,
- process unfolding,
- clocked proper-time development,
- growth and decay in organized systems,
- and asymmetric histories of production and maintenance.

### 5. Temporal asymmetry may be layered

The archive should allow that different temporal structures can matter at different levels:

- metric or causal order in physical description,
- developmental and homeostatic direction in living systems,
- historical irreversibility in institutions and practices,
- and deliberative openness in agency-involving contexts.

Do not force one flat temporal vocabulary across all domains.

### 6. Physics constrains but does not simply settle metaphysics of time

The archive should remain hospitable to modern spacetime considerations, but it should not assume that formal representation alone settles questions about becoming, presentness, or ontological privilege.

### 7. Temporal claims need a profile, not just a slogan

When future revisions invoke time, they should say which of the following is doing the work:

1. **ordering** — earlier/later or succession,
2. **metric** — duration, rate, interval, or temporal scale,
3. **persistence** — what survives across change,
4. **tense/presentness** — what is being treated as present, past, or future,
5. **passage/becoming** — what is genuinely unfolding or coming to be,
6. **asymmetry/irreversibility** — what cannot simply be reversed or exchanged,
7. **temporal perspective or practice** — what is indexical, experiential, or institutional rather than basal.

If a proposal does not state this, its temporal talk is under-disciplined.

## Archive default on time

The archive should presently prefer a **temporal-structure-plus-local-becoming** view:

- **process** explains why reality includes unfolding, maintenance, and decay,
- **form and kind** explain why different entities persist differently,
- **powers and laws** explain why some temporal asymmetries are objective,
- **causation** explains productive direction and sustained histories,
- and **time talk** should be interpreted with enough flexibility to fit both scientific structure and organized becoming.

So the current order is:

1. **ordered temporal structure is real,**
2. **organized becoming is real,**
3. **persistence depends on kind and organization,**
4. **a heavy ontology of global presentness remains unearned unless future revisions force it.**

## Working conclusion

The archive should now adopt the following temporal policy:

> **Be realist about time, change, and temporal order; distinguish persistence, tense, and passage; treat local becoming as more secure than grand flow metaphors; and keep presentism, eternalism, and growing-block views live until a sharper discriminating test decides among them.**

That is the current archive default on time.
