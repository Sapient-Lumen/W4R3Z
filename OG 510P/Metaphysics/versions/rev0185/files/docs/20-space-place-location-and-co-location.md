# Space, Place, Location, and Co-Location

## Why this file exists

The archive had already become better at handling **time**, **parts and wholes**, **causation**, and **constitution**, but it still lacked a direct rule for a prior question:

> **How is a thing in space, where is it, what sort of place matters, and when does spatial coincidence matter metaphysically?**

Without that rule, the archive risks several recurring confusions:

- treating **location** as if it were the same as **parthood**,
- treating **co-location** as if it forced either **identity** or **ontological inflation**,
- treating all **places** as if they were one ontological category,
- or importing a verdict about **absolute vs. relational space** before the local metaphysical work has even been specified.

This file is meant to stop that slippage.

## Current archive default

The archive should now adopt a restrained **spatial discipline**:

1. **Location is ontologically serious.**  
   A thing's whereabouts are not always a dispensable gloss. Spatial profile can matter to identity, interaction, boundary conditions, causal accessibility, and constitution.

2. **Location is not identical to parthood.**  
   Something can occupy a region without being identical to that region, and something can have parts without its locational profile being exhausted by its mereology.

3. **Co-location does not automatically settle identity.**  
   A statue and its clay, an organism and its matter, or an institution and its physical supports can share or overlap regions without that by itself deciding whether we have one thing, two things, or a layered case of constitution.

4. **Place is not one flat notion.**  
   The archive should distinguish at least:
   - **region** — a spatial or spatiotemporal area,
   - **site** — a locally occupied setting relevant to interaction or boundary,
   - **position** — a place in a relational arrangement,
   - **locale** — a socially or functionally structured place,
   - **field of operation** — the zone in which a process, institution, or power-profile is effectively active.

5. **Space should remain metaphysically open but locally disciplined.**  
   The archive need not settle, in advance, whether space or spacetime is best conceived substantivally, relationally, structurally, or processually. But any proposal should say what locational work is being done and what ontology of place it presupposes.

## Minimal distinctions the archive should preserve

### 1. Region and occupant

Do not automatically identify a located thing with the region it occupies.

Questions about:

- what a thing is,
- where it is,
- what region it exactly or weakly occupies,
- and whether it shares parts with that region

are distinct questions.

### 2. Exact location and loose presence

The archive should distinguish between:

- **exact location** — the region a thing occupies strictly,
- **entire or weak location** — the larger region in which it is present,
- **distributed presence** — cases where a process, field, network, or institution is not well modeled as a compact occupant of one neat bounded region.

This matters because organisms, storms, supply chains, fields, corporations, and digital infrastructures are not all located in the same way.

### 3. Co-location, overlap, and constitution

The archive should distinguish:

- **mere overlap**,
- **shared occupation of a region**,
- **constitution without identity**,
- **layered support**,
- and **practical or institutional co-siting**.

Not every case of occupying the same place is a material-constitution puzzle, and not every constitution puzzle is just a question about spatial overlap.

### 4. Spatial boundary and operational reach

A thing's **material edge**, **functional boundary**, and **causal reach** may come apart.

Examples:

- an organism's skin is not the whole story about its metabolically relevant environment,
- a city has vague and mixed boundaries,
- a corporation's headquarters is not identical to the corporation's region of operation,
- a field may extend beyond the compact object it is associated with.

### 5. Place and category

Before making a spatial claim, the archive should ask what kind of thing the target is:

- object,
- process,
- event,
- field,
- relation-network,
- institution,
- or site-dependent role.

Different categories are located differently.

## The archive's current default spatial picture

The archive should presently lean toward the following:

> **Reality includes objective spatial or spatiotemporal organization, but the metaphysical work of location is usually more important than an early verdict on whether space itself is a substance, a relation-system, or a structural field-like framework.**

That means:

- be realist about **location, distance, region, adjacency, boundary, and spatial organization**,
- remain open about **absolute vs. relational** and **substantival vs. relational/structural** global pictures,
- and treat **co-location** as a diagnostic pressure point rather than an automatic solver.

This fits the archive's broader posture: local organization and situated interaction are real, but global metaphysical over-commitment should be avoided until it earns its keep.

## Spatial-profile rule for future revisions

For any substantial proposal involving place, occupancy, embodiment, environment, or distributed organization, state explicitly:

1. **what the target is** — object, process, field, institution, role, network, or mixed case,
2. **what sort of place or region matters** — exact region, operational site, environment, relational position, or institutional locale,
3. **whether the case involves exact location, weak location, overlap, or co-location**,
4. **whether the issue is about location, constitution, causation, access, or boundary**,
5. **how the spatial profile interacts with persistence and temporal profile**,
6. **and whether any larger thesis about space is being assumed** — substantival, relational, structural, processual, or left open.

If a proposal skips these steps, it will tend to confuse region-occupation with constitution, or boundary talk with identity conditions.

## Pressure cases

### 1. Statue and clay

This is not just a puzzle about what things are made of. It is also a puzzle about how two candidates can coincide spatially without that alone settling whether they are one object, two objects, or one materially constituting the other.

### 2. Organism and environment

Living systems often have compact bodily boundaries but wider operational and ecological profiles. A non-reductive metaphysics should not force all spatial seriousness into the skin-line alone.

### 3. Institution and address

A university, court, corporation, or church may have buildings, campuses, headquarters, and mailing addresses without being identical to any one of these regions. Institutional place is partly physical and partly normatively structured.

### 4. Event and path

An event may occur in a place, spread through a region, or trace a path. The archive should not assume event-location works like object-location.

### 5. Field and source

Where a field is and where its source-object is are related but not identical questions.

## What this file changes in archive behavior

Future revisions should now ask not only for a **temporal profile** and a **causal profile**, but also for a **spatial profile**.

A strong addition will clarify one or more of the following:

- how a domain is located,
- what kind of region or place matters,
- whether co-location is explanatorily central,
- how spatial distribution interacts with unity and boundary,
- and whether a case requires a global thesis about space or only a local locational discipline.

## Status summary

- **Default:** objective spatial organization and location are real; local locational discipline matters more than a premature global theory of space.
- **Live options:** substantival region realism, relation-first spatial realism, structural or field-oriented spatial realism, processual spatial realism.
- **Open questions:** whether the archive should eventually favor one global ontology of space/spacetime; how far extended, distributed, or institutionally located entities challenge compact-object assumptions; and when co-location should count as genuine dual occupancy rather than layered description only.
