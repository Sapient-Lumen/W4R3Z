# Normativity, Reasons, and Deontic Structure

## Why this file exists

The archive already has files on teleology, social ontology, representation, agency, truthmaking, commitment, and centered action-guidance. But it still lacked a direct rule for **normativity**, **reasons**, and **deontic structure**.

Without a dedicated file here, the archive risks six opposite mistakes:

- reducing every **ought**, **reason**, or **requirement** to a mere prediction about what usually happens,
- inflating every norm into a **basic cosmic command** with no typed dependence profile,
- confusing **normative reasons** with **motivating reasons** or with whatever causally explains an action,
- confusing **deontic status** with **causal force**, as if what is required automatically happens or what happens thereby counts as required,
- treating all normativity as one flat domain instead of distinguishing **practical, epistemic, semantic, institutional, and role-relative** cases,
- or treating rule-following, obligation, permission, commitment, authority, correctness, and rationality as if a single thin notion of “ought” settled them all.

This file gives the archive a compact **normativity profile** so future revisions can say what kind of normativity is at issue, what favors or requires what, how the norm is sustained, what kind of failure is possible, and what reality-status the case earns.

## Working default

The archive should now say explicitly:

> **Some normative structures are real as typed favoring, requiring, permitting, authorizing, or correctness-governing profiles anchored in agents, practices, institutions, functions, contents, or standards; they need not be fundamental to be objective and explanatory.**

This fits the archive's wider commitments:

- **form** still matters, because norms often attach to kinds of act, role, move, utterance, inference, office, or institution,
- **process** still matters, because guidance, compliance, violation, repair, sanction, and deliberation unfold over time,
- **power** still matters, because permissions, obligations, authorities, competences, liabilities, and reasons are modal profiles rather than inert labels,
- **structure** still matters, because normative order often depends on standing relations among rules, roles, records, sanctions, expectations, and reasons,
- and **grounding** still matters, because normative reality should not float free of practices, forms of life, institutions, rational standards, agential capacities, or content-bearing structures.

## Core distinctions

### 1. Normative reasons, motivating reasons, and causes are not the same

The archive should distinguish:

- **normative reasons** — considerations that count in favor of, against, or otherwise bear on what one ought, may, or should do, think, infer, or sustain,
- **motivating reasons** — what the agent took or used as their reason,
- **reason-explanations** — what makes an action, inference, or uptake intelligible from the relevant point of view,
- **causes** — what produced, enabled, inhibited, or sustained the event or state,
- **deontic status** — whether the act, belief, inference, or move is required, permitted, forbidden, optional, or defective.

Without this distinction, the archive will keep sliding from favoring to motivating, from motivating to causing, and from causing to justifying as if one relation settled all four.

### 2. Favoring, requiring, permitting, and authorizing are different normative jobs

The archive should not treat all “ought”-like vocabulary as one flat phenomenon.

At minimum it should distinguish:

- **favoring / pro tanto reason** — a consideration that counts for or against,
- **all-things-considered requirement** — what is finally required given the relevant profile,
- **permission / option** — what is allowed without being required,
- **prohibition** — what is ruled out,
- **power / authority / competence** — what an agent, office, or system can validly do or change,
- **correctness standard** — what counts as right use, valid move, proper representation, or proper inference.

This matters because the archive already tracks role powers, agency, rules, representation, and institutions, but those do not all work through one thin deontic relation.

### 3. Normativity is domain-typed rather than one flat ontological layer

The archive should keep at least five live normative profiles apart:

- **practical normativity** — what there is reason to do,
- **epistemic normativity** — what there is reason to believe, infer, or investigate,
- **semantic or representational normativity** — what counts as correct use, interpretation, or content-sensitive uptake,
- **institutional normativity** — what roles, rules, offices, procedures, and statuses require, permit, or empower,
- **functional or teleological normativity** — what counts as proper functioning, malfunction, success, or defect relative to an organized system.

A compact non-reductive metaphysics needs these apart. Otherwise it will keep arguing as if all normativity were either moral law, mere coordination, or only semantic bookkeeping.

### 4. Violation, mistake, and defect are crucial evidence

Normative reality often shows up not only in compliance but in **failure**:

- an inference can be invalid,
- a move can be impermissible,
- an office-holder can act ultra vires,
- a representation can be incorrect,
- an organism or artifact can malfunction,
- a policy can be violated,
- an agent can ignore a reason that still applies.

The archive should therefore treat **norm-guided failure** as a major clue. A structure that can be followed, broken, misapplied, repaired, or sanctioned is often more than a mere statistical regularity.

### 5. Derivative normativity can still be objective

The archive should resist two extremes:

- **flat reductionism**, where norms collapse into prediction, preference, force, or regularity,
- **flat primitivism**, where every norm is treated as metaphysically basic and ungrounded.

Many normative structures are better treated as **derivative but robust**. They may depend on:

- agential capacities,
- rational standards,
- semantic contents,
- institutional role structures,
- records and enforcement,
- functional organization,
- or some mixed basis.

But derivative does **not** mean unreal. A valid move in a game, a lawful office action, a correct inference, or a binding procedural requirement can be objective enough without being written into the cosmos as a primitive command.

### 6. Normative force and causal force are distinct but often interact

A norm can apply without being followed, and a causal process can produce behavior without thereby justifying it.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **norm applicability**,
- **motivational uptake**,
- **social enforcement**,
- **material enablement**,
- **causal efficacy**,
- **deontic validity**.

This matters especially for law, platforms, institutions, protocols, algorithms, and role-mediated agency. Some norms partly constitute those domains; others merely regulate them; still others are only externally imposed descriptions.

## Normativity profile rule for future revisions

When a future revision uses language like **ought**, **should**, **reason**, **requirement**, **permission**, **prohibition**, **authority**, **correct**, **incorrect**, **rational**, **binding**, **defective**, **valid**, **obligatory**, **optional**, **duty**, **rule**, or **norm**, it should specify at least the following:

1. **domain** — practical, epistemic, semantic, institutional, functional, prudential, legal, mixed, or still unsettled;
2. **norm type** — favoring reason, requirement, permission, prohibition, correctness condition, competence/power, liability, or mixed;
3. **target** — action, omission, inference, belief, utterance, role-performance, institutional move, representational act, design feature, or mixed case;
4. **bearer/subject** — person, organism, office-holder, collective, institution, subsystem, interface-mediated arrangement, artifact, practice, or mixed case;
5. **source basis** — value, reason relation, role structure, convention, law, function, content, rational standard, enforcement regime, or mixed basis;
6. **dependence profile** — basic, grounded, constituted, realized, practice-dependent objective, enforcement-supported, or still unsettled;
7. **failure profile** — violation, error, invalidity, malfunction, sanctionability, nullity, excusable defect, or none;
8. **reality verdict** — fundamental normative fact, derivative but robust normative structure, thinly dependent rule profile, merely representational or heuristic gloss, or still unsettled.

## Compression guidance

The archive should prefer the **lowest-inflation normative profile** that still preserves the objective difference between:

- what merely happens and what is guided,
- what is guided and what is justified,
- what is justified and what is finally required,
- what is institutionally valid and what is merely causally effective,
- what is correct and what is merely successful,
- what is a real norm-governed structure and what is only an observer's summary.

## Default stance for now

The archive should remain open between several larger pictures:

- **robust normative realism**,
- **typed derivative realism**,
- **institutional/practice-dependent objectivity**,
- **content-engendered or content-determining normativity in semantic domains**,
- and thinner deflationary views in some local cases.

But unless a future revision forces a stronger verdict, the archive should prefer:

> **typed derivative normative realism** — norms, reasons, permissions, authorities, and standards can be objective and explanatorily real without all being fundamental, irreducible, or domain-uniform.

That fits the archive's broader picture better than either global reductionism or a blanket ontology of freestanding oughts.
