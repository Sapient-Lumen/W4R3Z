# Delegation, Proxying, Handoff, and Distributed Enactment

## Why this file exists

The archive already has files on **agency**, **social ontology**, **organization**, **autonomy**, **scaffolding**, **porous boundaries**, **repair**, and **dormancy**, but it still lacks one direct file saying what happens when a target **acts through others**, **hands control across stages**, **recruits temporary auxiliaries**, or **performs through authorized roles, tools, or procedures**.

That omission matters because the archive already risks eight opposite mistakes:

- treating **delegation** as if it automatically destroyed the original agent or institution,
- treating every helpful contributor as if it automatically became part of one larger bearer,
- treating **proxying** as if it were the same thing as strict identity between principal and performer,
- treating **tool mediation**, **automation**, and **authorized office performance** as one flat phenomenon,
- treating **handoff** as if the same guiding center obviously persists across every transfer,
- treating **collective action** as if it were always reducible to many parallel individual acts,
- treating **role enactment** as if it were just personal action with public consequences,
- or treating every distributed success as if it settled either holistic fusion or eliminative dispersion.

This file gives the archive a compact **delegation-and-enactment profile** so future revisions can say whether a case is chiefly about authorized proxying, mediated execution, staged handoff, shared action, office enactment, distributed control, or genuine transfer to a new bearer.

## Working default

The archive should now say explicitly:

> **A real agent, institution, or organized process can act through delegates, proxies, authorized roles, recruited subsystems, or staged handoffs without either vanishing into those contributors or automatically absorbing them all into one giant bearer.**

In compressed form:

> **Delegation is not replacement. Proxying is not identity. Distribution is not automatic fusion.**

This fits the archive's wider commitments:

- **process** still matters because many actions and performances unfold across stages, relays, and controlled transfers,
- **form** still matters because what counts as delegation, proxying, or handoff is kind-sensitive,
- **power** still matters because authority, capacity, permission, competence, and standing role-power can be distributed unevenly,
- **structure** still matters because coordinated enactment depends on channels, procedures, offices, records, interfaces, and control architecture,
- **grounding** still matters because a delegate's act can ground one institutional outcome while still depending on a wider principal or office structure,
- **autonomy** still matters because a contributor may retain local discretion without becoming the ultimate governing source,
- **scaffolding** still matters because some enactment relies on external tools, scripts, and records without transferring bearerhood,
- **porous boundaries** still matter because contributors, signals, permissions, and materials may cross in typed ways,
- **repair-resilience** still matters because distributed systems often preserve continuity by rerouting tasks across deputies or backup channels,
- and **dormancy** still matters because a standing office, institution, or capacity may persist through quiet phases and later reactivate via new performers.

## Core distinctions

### 1. Delegation is not the same thing as replacement

An agent or institution may assign a task, authorize a deputy, or route performance through another actor while retaining some higher-order control, authorship, or accountability.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **direct personal performance**,
- **delegated execution under retained authority**,
- **authorized proxy action**,
- **outsourced contribution without principal continuity**,
- and **genuine replacement or succession**.

Otherwise it will keep sliding from “someone else did it” either to “the original bearer disappeared” or to “nothing important changed.”

### 2. Proxying is not the same thing as identity

A proxy may speak, sign, transmit, represent, vote, or perform on behalf of another without literally becoming numerically the same agent.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **representation or proxying**,
- **office enactment**,
- **tool-assisted execution**,
- **mere signaling or reporting**,
- and **full transfer of authorship or control**.

Otherwise it will keep confusing “for” with “as”.

### 3. Tool mediation is not agent transfer

A calculator, model, script, robot, interface, checklist, or institutional form can mediate performance without thereby becoming the agent.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **instrumental mediation**,
- **automated execution under prior policy**,
- **interactive co-control**,
- **delegated agency through a subordinate performer**,
- and **new agential center formed by a wider architecture**.

Otherwise it will keep flattening tools, deputies, and co-agents into one category.

### 4. Handoff is not automatic continuity of one controlling source

Some performances move across shifts, persons, organs, modules, procedures, or platforms. Sometimes one higher-order process persists across that transfer; sometimes the handoff marks a change of bearer.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **same policy across stages**,
- **same office across changing occupants**,
- **same process through controlled relay**,
- **restarted performance by a successor**,
- and **mere historical sequence with no persisting control center**.

Otherwise it will keep treating temporal succession as either obvious sameness or obvious discontinuity.

### 5. Shared action is not the same thing as corporate agency

Many individuals can act together without there being one robust group agent; conversely, some organized institutions may genuinely act as one derivative but robust agent.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **parallel individual action**,
- **shared action with meshing plans**,
- **role-structured coordination**,
- **corporate or institutional agency**,
- and **distributed performance with no single bearer verdict yet earned**.

Otherwise it will keep oscillating between methodological individualism and inflated group mysticism.

### 6. Role enactment is not just private action with public consequences

An office-holder, judge, pilot, committee chair, or API service may act under norms, permissions, and records that exceed personal preference.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **personal action**,
- **role-bound enactment**,
- **office continuity through changing occupants**,
- **institutional output generated by procedure**,
- and **personal improvisation merely tolerated by a structure**.

Otherwise it will keep blurring person, role, office, and institution.

### 7. Recruitment is not incorporation

A target may recruit immune cells, temporary staff, substitute modules, emergency procedures, or outside contractors without thereby incorporating them into one persisting bearer.

Otherwise the archive will keep sliding from task-relevant participation to ontological absorption.

### 8. Distributed enactment can be real and still derivative

A coordinated socio-technical or institutional arrangement may do real work through distributed channels while still depending on persons, offices, infrastructures, and records. That dependence does not by itself defeat its reality.

Otherwise the archive will keep forcing a false choice between solitary self-sufficient action and unreality.

## Four live options

### 1. Strict individual-performance picture

**Picture:** real action belongs primarily to immediately acting individuals; delegation, proxying, and distributed execution are secondary redescription.

**What it gets right:**
- not every coordinated performance deserves a new bearer,
- and agency should not be inflated lightly.

**What it gets wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- it underdescribes institutions, office continuity, socio-technical systems, role-bound action, and durable distributed control.

### 2. Holistic absorption picture

**Picture:** once many contributors, tools, or channels are reliably coordinated, the whole arrangement is simply one larger agent or bearer.

**What it gets right:**
- organization and coordination can be ontologically significant,
- and some systems really do support derivative but robust higher-order agency.

**What it gets wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- it annexes every scaffold, proxy, and helper too quickly,
- and it loses distinctions among delegation, instrumentation, shared action, and corporate agency.

### 3. Role-nominalist picture

**Picture:** offices, authorized spokespeople, institutional outputs, and proxy performances are only convenient ways of speaking about many individual acts plus social expectations.

**What it gets right:**
- institutions and roles need anchoring in persons, practices, and records,
- and representation language can overstate metaphysical unity.

**What it gets wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- it underdescribes robust authority structures, office continuity, procedural output, and the explanatory reality of organized enactment.

### 4. Typed delegated-enactment realism

**Picture:** delegation, proxying, handoff, shared action, role enactment, and distributed performance are all real but come in kinds; some preserve one principal or office, some support one derivative institutional agent, some merely coordinate many actors, and some mark succession or replacement.

**What it gets right:**
- it preserves non-reductive realism without giant-bearer inflation,
- it makes control architecture and accountability diagnostically central,
- and it lets persons, institutions, and socio-technical systems act through others in typed ways.

**What it risks if left alone:**
- it can become vague unless each case says who sets policy, who executes, what authority is transferred, what continuity basis preserves the target, and what would count as genuine bearer change.

## Current archive answer

The archive should adopt a **typed delegated-enactment realism**:

> **Many real agents, institutions, and organized processes act through delegated performers, proxies, offices, procedures, tools, and staged handoffs in ways that preserve a higher-order bearer or organized line of control without requiring either immediate solitary performance or untyped holistic fusion.**

In compressed form:

> **One target can sometimes act through others, but the kind of “through” must be stated.**

This does not mean:

- every delegated task preserves one and the same agent,
- every spokesperson or office-holder is numerically identical with the institution,
- every tool-mediated success reveals a wider agent,
- every collective performance earns corporate agency,
- or that distributed enactment by itself settles fundamentality, parthood, or moral responsibility.

It means only that the archive should stop treating **delegates**, **proxies**, **spokespersons**, **offices**, **handoffs**, **automation**, **shared action**, and **distributed control** as if one flat verdict settled them all.

## Archive commitments

### 1. Keep principal, performer, and office distinct unless the case earns their collapse

Many cases involve one policy source, one executing contributor, and one standing role or office. Those are often related but not identical.

### 2. Control has layers

A delegate may have local discretion while a principal or institution retains higher-order policy, authorization, or accountability.

### 3. Some enactment is office-bound rather than person-bound

What persists may be the office, office-power, or institutional capacity rather than the same individual performer.

### 4. Some distributed systems earn derivative but robust agency

Committees, firms, courts, and some socio-technical control architectures may support a real organized agent, but only when coordination, decision procedure, commitment structure, and continuity are strong enough.

### 5. Not every contributor becomes a part of one bearer

Temporary recruitment, expert consultation, emergency outsourcing, or infrastructural dependence can matter causally and explanatorily without settling constitution.

### 6. Handoff can preserve one target across changing local performers

A process, office, or institutional workflow may continue across relay if the relevant policy, records, and controlled continuity remain intact.

### 7. Delegation failures are metaphysically revealing

Cases of unauthorized drift, proxy betrayal, communication breakdown, automation error, or office vacancy often show what the real bearer and control structure were.

### 8. Distribution does not defeat reality

A target may be derivative, multi-channel, and record-mediated while still being real enough to ground explanation, normativity, and persistence claims.

## Working profile for future revisions

When a difficult case turns on delegation, proxying, handoff, or distributed enactment, state explicitly:

1. the **target** — person, organism, office, institution, socio-technical system, process, or mixed case;
2. the relevant **kind or stage-kind**;
3. the **control architecture** — direct control, delegated execution, proxy authorization, shared planning, procedural output, automated policy-following, staged relay, or mixed;
4. the **guidance source** — intention, standing policy, office authority, legal rule, algorithmic procedure, emergent organizational commitment, or mixed;
5. the **performer role** — principal, deputy, spokesperson, subsystem, tool, platform, committee member, contractor, or mixed;
6. the **continuity basis** — same individual, same office, same policy, same records, same workflow, same institutional commitment, or mixed;
7. the **transfer type** — temporary delegation, authorized proxying, relay/handoff, substitution, succession, or mixed;
8. the relevant **accountability profile** — retained by principal, shared, role-bound, procedural, displaced, or unclear;
9. the relevant **dependence profile** — scaffolded, role-structured, infrastructural, boundary-crossing, distributed, or mixed;
10. and the best verdict — **direct personal action**, **delegated enactment**, **proxy performance**, **office enactment**, **shared action**, **corporate or institutional agency**, **successor performance**, or **still unsettled**.

## Payoff for the archive

With this file in place, the archive can now say more clearly:

- how persons act through assistants, interfaces, scripts, and standing instructions,
- how institutions act through officers, records, procedures, and changing occupants,
- how organisms and mechanisms recruit temporary subsystems without automatically changing bearerhood,
- how socio-technical systems can exhibit real organized control without every distributed success becoming one giant agent,
- and how failures of delegation, relay, and proxying reveal where agency, authority, and continuity really lie.

That is a gain because the archive already rejected both atomistic simplification and decorative holism. What it still needed was a direct rule for **action-through-others** and the typed ontological lesson of controlled distribution.
