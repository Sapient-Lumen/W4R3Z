# Facts, States of Affairs, and Obtaining

## Why this file exists

The archive already has a restrained truthmaker policy, a category discipline that distinguishes **facts** from **events**, and a grounding file that keeps constitutive explanation from collapsing into causation. But it still lacked one direct file saying what a **fact** is supposed to be, how **facts** differ from **states of affairs**, what **obtaining** adds, and when fact-talk is doing real ontological work rather than merely shadowing a true sentence.

That omission matters because the archive already risks six opposite mistakes:

- treating facts as if they were just true propositions under another name,
- treating every true sentence as if it automatically required a bespoke fact,
- treating facts and events as interchangeable because both can be described with “that”-clauses,
- treating states of affairs as if they must either all obtain or all be mere representational devices,
- treating facts as automatically fundamental whenever they help with truthmaking,
- or treating fact-talk as dispensable everywhere, even when what **obtains** is exactly what needs to be said.

This file gives the archive a compact **fact profile** so future revisions can say whether a case is chiefly about a fact, an obtaining state of affairs, a non-obtaining state of affairs, an event, a proposition, a truthmaker candidate, or a leaner positive ontological ground.

## Working default

The archive should now say explicitly:

> **Facts are best treated as worldly obtainings or obtaining configurations, while states of affairs are a wider candidate category that may or may not obtain; the archive should remain open on whether facts are identical with obtaining states of affairs, but should use either only where they clarify truthmaking, obtaining, or constitutive explanation better than objects, processes, powers, structures, or bounded totalities alone.**

This fits the archive's wider commitments:

- **truthmaking** still matters because truths should answer to reality,
- **category discipline** still matters because facts are not the same category as propositions, events, or objects,
- **grounding** still matters because “in virtue of” claims often need a worldly obtaining without thereby requiring a new fundamental item,
- **process** still matters because many truths are about processual realities rather than static complexes,
- **structure** still matters because some facts obtain only because a relational or organizational structure is in place,
- and **metaontology** still matters because facts can be real without being fundamental, and useful without being mandatory everywhere.

## Core distinctions

### 1. A fact is not just a true proposition

The archive should distinguish:

- **proposition / thought / content** — what is true or false in a truthbearer-like sense,
- **fact** — what is the case or what obtains,
- **sentence** — a linguistic vehicle,
- and **judgment or belief** — a cognitive act or state.

Otherwise the archive will keep sliding from semantics to ontology.

### 2. A fact is not automatically an event

“The explosion occurred” can be true because there was an explosion. But the **explosion** is an event, while **the fact that the explosion occurred** is not obviously just the event itself under another name.

The archive should therefore distinguish:

- **event** — a happening,
- **process** — an ongoing or organized becoming,
- **fact** — an obtaining,
- and **event-fact** — a fact whose obtaining concerns an event.

Otherwise the archive will keep collapsing occurrence into obtaining.

### 3. States of affairs are broader than facts on some views

Some metaphysical frameworks treat **facts** and **obtaining states of affairs** as the same thing. Others distinguish them by saying that a **state of affairs** may exist whether or not it obtains, whereas a **fact** is what there is when such a state of affairs obtains.

The archive should keep this distinction live because it helps separate:

- **possibility-space articulation** from **actual obtaining**,
- **world-indexed candidate configurations** from **actual facts**,
- and **truthmaker work** from the wider modal role some philosophers assign to states of affairs.

### 4. Obtaining is doing real work

The archive should not use “obtains”, “is the case”, “holds”, and “is true” as if they were interchangeable.

It should distinguish:

- **truth** — a property of a truthbearer,
- **obtaining** — a worldly status,
- **existence** — whether a candidate entity or structure exists,
- and **fundamentality** — whether the obtaining is basic or derivative.

Otherwise the archive will keep confusing what is true, what there is, and what obtains.

### 5. Facts need not be ontological bedrock

A fact may be real without being fundamental. The fact that a corporation exists, the fact that a law is valid, or the fact that an organism is blind may all be derivative, structured, institutionally sustained, or privative.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **fundamental fact**,
- **derivative but robust fact**,
- **thinly dependent fact-profile**,
- and **mere representational or semantic convenience**.

This matters because the archive already distinguishes existence from fundamentality elsewhere and should not abandon that discipline when “fact” language appears.

### 6. Not every truth earns a bespoke fact

The archive should remain suspicious of **factualist maximalism** when it becomes a license to posit a separate fact for every truth, especially every negative, disjunctive, modal, or highly gerrymandered truth.

It should prefer, where possible:

- an object or bearer,
- an event or process,
- a power-profile,
- a structure or organization,
- a bounded totality,
- or a privation / blocked-process profile,

before multiplying facts indiscriminately.

### 7. Some fact-talk is indispensable

Even so, the archive should not flatten all fact-talk away. Facts can clarify:

- what exactly **obtains**,
- what a truth is answerable to,
- what a grounding claim ranges over,
- what a totality claim completes,
- and what makes a judgment, belief, or proposition correct.

In these roles, fact-talk can be ontologically serious without becoming the archive's universal solvent.

### 8. Granularity matters

Facts can be described more coarsely or finely. “The organism is alive” and “the organism maintains metabolic closure through these mechanisms” need not introduce rival worlds; they may be different grains on one obtaining reality.

So the archive should ask:

- whether the fact is **atomic, molecular, or highly gerrymandered**,
- whether the fact is **eventive, dispositional, structural, institutional, or mixed**,
- and whether the chosen grain tracks real explanation or only a convenient sentence-form.

Otherwise the archive will keep mistaking finer regimentation for deeper ontology.

## Four live options

### 1. Factualist maximalism

**Picture:** every truth has a fact as its truthmaker.

**What it gets right:**
- truth is answerable to reality,
- and facts can be a clear bridge between truth and world.

**What it gets wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- it risks ontological baroqueness,
- it pressures the archive toward negative-fact inflation,
- and it can make facts look more fundamental than the archive's broader layered realism supports.

### 2. Sparse obtaining-fact realism

**Picture:** facts are real and useful, but only some truths require explicit fact-positing; many others are better handled by objects, processes, structures, powers, or bounded totalities.

**What it gets right:**
- it preserves world-answerability,
- it keeps obtaining in view,
- and it fits the archive's anti-bloat truthmaker discipline.

**What it risks if left alone:**
- if too sparse, fact-talk becomes too optional to do any clarifying work,
- and the archive can drift back into using “is true” where it really means “obtains”.

### 3. States-of-affairs realism

**Picture:** states of affairs are a broader category than facts; some obtain and some do not; facts are obtaining states of affairs or stand close to them.

**What it gets right:**
- it gives a natural role to obtaining,
- it helps explain modal space without reducing everything to propositions,
- and it can separate actuality from wider possibility.

**What it risks if left alone:**
- the archive may import more ontology than it needs,
- and it may mistake every candidate state of affairs for a worldly item doing explanatory work.

### 4. Deflationary anti-factualism

**Picture:** true propositions, sentences, or judgments need no facts; fact-talk is only a façon de parler.

**What it gets right:**
- it resists baroque ontology,
- and it warns against easy reification of nominalized clauses.

**What it gets wrong for this archive if left alone:**
- it can underdescribe truthmaking, obtaining, and grounding,
- and it makes it harder to say what exactly is the case when truth and world are supposed to meet.

## Archive commitments

### 1. Facts are a live category, but not a universal solvent

The archive should allow facts where they clarify what obtains, but should not use them to blur distinctions among objects, events, processes, structures, and propositions.

### 2. Facts and states of affairs should stay typed

Some cases are best handled by obtaining facts; some by a broader states-of-affairs framework; some by leaner positive ontology without explicit fact-posits.

### 3. Events, processes, and facts should not be collapsed

An event can occur, a process can continue, and a fact can obtain. One case may involve all three, but the categories should stay distinct unless a domain-specific argument collapses them.

### 4. Facts may be derivative

The fact that an institution exists, that a person occupies an office, or that a biological function is impaired may all be real while depending on deeper organizational, normative, or material structure.

### 5. Negative and totality facts remain pressure points

The archive should keep preferring bounded totalities, positive incompatibility, and privation where those do the work more cleanly than a bespoke negative fact.

### 6. Fact-talk earns its keep when it clarifies obtaining

When the issue is not merely whether a sentence is true, but what in the world is the case, fact-talk can be the right tool.

## Fact profile rule for future revisions

Before inferring a metaphysical verdict from language like **fact**, **state of affairs**, **obtaining**, **is the case**, **holds**, **situation**, **circumstance**, **configuration**, **the fact that**, or **what obtains**, future revisions should answer at least these questions:

1. **target truth or claim** — what truthbearer or worldly claim is under pressure;
2. **work being asked of fact-talk** — truthmaking, correctness, obtaining, grounding, regress-stopping, modal articulation, or mixed role;
3. **candidate category** — fact, obtaining state of affairs, non-obtaining state of affairs, event-fact, structural fact, institutional fact, privative fact-profile, or no fact posit at all;
4. **relation to nearby categories** — whether the case is better handled by an object, process, event, property-exemplification, structure, organization, bounded totality, or proposition;
5. **obtaining status** — obtaining, non-obtaining-but-possible, impossible, merely represented, or still unsettled;
6. **granularity** — atomic, molecular, highly complex, coarse-grained, fine-grained, or gerrymandered;
7. **dependence profile** — fundamental, derivative but robust, thinly dependent, institutionally sustained, privative, grounded, or still unsettled;
8. **negativity / absence profile** — positive obtaining, bounded absence, privation, omission, totality, negative commitment, or mixed case;
9. **truthmaker profile** — whether the fact is itself the truthmaker candidate or whether leaner grounds already suffice;
10. **reality verdict** — indispensable fact, optional but clarifying fact, better treated as state of affairs, better treated without fact-positing, merely representational gloss, or still unsettled.

## Compression rules

When future revisions face fact-language, they should prefer the smallest adequate claim:

- if an object, process, power, or structure already does the truthmaking work, say that rather than adding a fact;
- if the issue is actuality versus possibility, consider whether a states-of-affairs distinction is doing the work;
- if the issue is merely sentence truth, do not silently promote it to ontology;
- if the issue is what obtains, say that directly rather than hiding behind semantic idiom;
- if the case is a negative or absential truth, try bounded totality, incompatibility, or privation first;
- and if a fact is admitted, say whether it is fundamental, derivative, or only thinly dependent.

## Working conclusion

The archive should now prefer a **typed factual realism**:

> **Facts are real enough to matter where obtaining and truthmaking need them, states of affairs remain a live broader category for actuality/possibility distinctions, but neither should be allowed to replace leaner ontological grounds or to collapse objects, events, processes, propositions, and truths into one flat category.**

That conclusion is strong enough to discipline future revisions, but restrained enough to keep factuality from becoming either a baroque census of everything true or a purely semantic shadow with no worldly grip.
