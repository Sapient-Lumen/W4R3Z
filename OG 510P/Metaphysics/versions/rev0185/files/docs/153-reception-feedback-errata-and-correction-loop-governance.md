# Reception, Feedback, Errata, and Correction-Loop Governance

## Why this file exists

`152-transmission-excerpt-compression-and-pedagogical-governance.md` made summaries, excerpts, teaching examples, diagrams, prompt answers, paper sections, rubrics, and derivative outputs safer. That was necessary, but it is still not the end of responsible archive use.

An output can be transmitted responsibly and still be **received** badly. A careful summary may be remembered as a slogan. A warning may be read as optional. A source-bound distinction may be repeated as archive-wide doctrine. A teaching example may become the reader's whole theory. A diagram may cause a false spatial or causal interpretation. A table may flatten status differences. A paper section may be cited without its non-verdict. A model card, rubric, or decision framework may be operationalized by users who never return to the governing file.

The next danger is therefore **reception drift**: the archive's output remains internally licensed, but the downstream understanding, citation, critique, teaching, or reuse pattern reveals that the output is not functioning as intended.

A second danger is **feedback laundering**: reader confusion, external criticism, downstream adoption, popularity, institutional uptake, or repeated citation is treated either as proof that the archive was right or as proof that the archive should immediately revise. Neither inference is safe. Reception is evidence about transmission, pedagogy, ambiguity, uptake, and failure modes. It is not automatically evidence about the underlying metaphysical verdict.

A third danger is **errata invisibility**: small corrections, warnings, clarifications, and misreading patterns accumulate outside the archive until future revisions repeat known failures because there is no governed place to record them.

This file adds a reception layer: **feedback intake, errata classification, misunderstanding analysis, correction-loop governance, and post-transmission learning**. Its job is not to add another first-order metaphysical operator. Its job is to make the archive learn from how its outputs are actually received without mistaking reception for truth, popularity for calibration, or confusion for refutation.

## Compressed default

The archive should now say:

> **Do not stop at safe transmission. Track how transmitted material is received, misunderstood, challenged, reused, and operationalized; then classify the feedback before revising doctrine.**

In shorter form:

> **Reception is a diagnostic layer, not a verdict.**

And more carefully:

> **Feedback should be routed by what it reveals: wording failure, missing warning, wrong compression level, terminology ambiguity, status bleaching, precedent misuse, source-boundary failure, genuine counterexample, calibration pressure, or package drift. Only some reception events justify doctrinal revision. Many justify a clearer summary, a stronger warning, a new return path, a teaching repair, an erratum, or a ledgered open debt.**

This file therefore shifts the archive from **transmission-safe classification** to **reception-audited classification**.

## Place in the control sequence

The intended hard-case sequence is now:

1. **Route** the case or proposed addition with `144`.
2. **Stress-test** the routed verdict with `145`.
3. **Ledger** reusable or precedent-setting cases with `146`.
4. **Propagate** accepted changes with `147`.
5. **Register terminology** with `148`.
6. **Assign commitment status** with `149`.
7. **Write the application dossier** with `150` when the result must travel.
8. **Govern reuse, transfer, appeal, and review** with `151` when the result is cited, taught, exported, challenged, superseded, or used as precedent.
9. **Govern transmission and compression** with `152` when a result is summarized, excerpted, diagrammed, taught, or packaged for readers.
10. **Audit reception and correction loops** with this file when transmitted material is misunderstood, challenged, operationalized, cited, taught, adopted, rejected, or turned into feedback for future revisions.

The difference between `152` and this file is simple. `152` asks: **what must the output carry when it leaves the archive?** This file asks: **what did the output actually do after it left, and what kind of correction, if any, follows?**

## The basic unit: the reception packet

A **reception packet** records a downstream event in which archive material was read, summarized, taught, criticized, operationalized, reused, misread, or cited.

A packet should contain:

1. **Transmission pointer** — the file, dossier, precedent packet, transmission packet, excerpt, diagram, table, answer, paper section, rubric, or derivative artifact that was received.
2. **Audience and setting** — private reader, student, collaborator, source-domain expert, public reader, reviewer, maintainer, derivative-project user, automated system, or mixed.
3. **Reception event** — accurate uptake, partial uptake, misreading, overextension, status upgrade, source export, term confusion, example dominance, diagram capture, criticism, counterexample, operational failure, or reuse request.
4. **Evidence of reception** — reader question, written objection, failed application, recurring confusion, external citation, teaching failure, implementation behavior, review comment, or maintainer observation.
5. **Severity level** — from harmless orientation gap to urgent doctrinal or package-risk pressure.
6. **Fault hypothesis** — reader error, output compression, missing warning, weak return path, term ambiguity, status bleaching, precedent ambiguity, source-boundary failure, real counterexample, obsolete file, or package drift.
7. **Action result** — no action, reply locally, revise wording, add warning, add return path, add teaching note, add erratum, open appeal, update dossier, update precedent packet, update transmission packet, update method rule, ledger calibration case, reopen doctrine, or quarantine output.
8. **Non-action result** — what the feedback does not show: not a refutation, not a precedent, not a source anchor, not a status upgrade, not an archive-wide rule, not a new operator, or not yet enough evidence.
9. **Propagation result** — which files, if any, need updates under `147`.
10. **Review trigger** — what future reception would escalate the issue.

The packet can be compact. But if an external criticism, teaching failure, repeated misreading, or downstream implementation will guide revision, the reception result should not remain private memory.

## Reception statuses

### 1. Accurate uptake

The output was understood at the intended compression level with its target, status, warning, non-verdict, and return path intact.

Action: no revision required, though the output may become a positive transmission example.

Forbidden upgrade: treating easy uptake as proof of metaphysical truth.

### 2. Benign orientation gap

The reader needs more context, but no specific wording, term, status, or warning failure is visible.

Action: answer locally or add optional teaching support.

Forbidden upgrade: creating a new control file just because one reader needed orientation.

### 3. Wording ambiguity

The output uses a phrase that invites a wrong reading even when the underlying verdict is sound.

Action: revise wording, register the ambiguous term under `148` if durable, and update the transmission packet under `152`.

Forbidden upgrade: treating a wording repair as doctrinal change.

### 4. Warning failure

The output carries a warning, but the warning is too weak, too late, too technical, or too easy to omit.

Action: strengthen minimum warning, move it closer to the claim, or lower the permitted compression level.

Forbidden upgrade: blaming the reader when the output predictably encouraged overreading.

### 5. Return-path failure

The reader cannot find the governing file, dossier, precedent packet, source note, or control layer needed to check the claim.

Action: add or repair return path, index entry, cross-reference, file-map wording, or front-door guidance.

Forbidden upgrade: letting a summary become self-standing merely because the full route is hard to find.

### 6. Status bleaching

A live rival, provisional probe, domain-local rule, source-bound claim, deprecated item, open debt, or teaching illustration is received as settled doctrine.

Action: revise status label, add a local non-verdict, update `149`, and review `152` compression level.

Forbidden upgrade: converting status confusion into stronger doctrine.

### 7. Precedent misuse

A local answer, teaching illustration, or source-bound example is cited as controlled precedent, calibration, or synthesis support.

Action: update or create a precedent packet under `151`, or mark the case do-not-reuse.

Forbidden upgrade: letting repeated citation become permission.

### 8. Source-boundary failure

A source-local legal, engineering, scientific, clinical, historical, textual, or institutional term is exported as archive-wide metaphysics.

Action: update the source note in `05`, register the source term under `148`, and add source-bound warning under `152`.

Forbidden upgrade: letting source authority carry metaphysical generality.

### 9. Genuine counterexample pressure

The feedback identifies a case that appears to preserve the archive's target while defeating its basis, grain, route, rival contrast, status, or non-verdict.

Action: open an adversarial test under `145`, ledger if reusable under `146`, and prepare an application dossier under `150` if the result will travel.

Forbidden upgrade: treating every objection as a counterexample before target and basis are fixed.

### 10. Package drift signal

Reception reveals that the archive itself is inconsistent: front door, synthesis, method rules, file map, status register, source note, control-layer sequence, manifest, or revision note no longer agree.

Action: run `147` propagation and package-drift checks before adding more theory.

Forbidden upgrade: hiding package drift inside a new conceptual file.

## Feedback kinds

Feedback should be typed before it is used.

### Clarification request

A reader asks what a term, route, status, or example means. This usually indicates orientation need, wording ambiguity, or return-path weakness. It does not by itself defeat a verdict.

### Misapplication

A reader applies the output to a case outside the licensed target, grain, source role, or precedent status. This usually indicates missing transfer conditions or weak non-verdicts.

### Overgeneralization

A reader treats a local, family-bound, domain-bound, or source-bound result as a global claim. This usually indicates status bleaching or compression failure.

### Objection

A reader challenges the argument, distinction, or application. Route the objection by target and basis before deciding whether it is wording, rival pressure, counterexample pressure, or status dispute.

### Counterexample candidate

A reader presents a case that may defeat the distinction. Treat it as a candidate until it passes the target-preservation and basis-preservation tests.

### Citation or adoption

A downstream user repeats or relies on the result. This is evidence of uptake, not evidence of truth. Adoption may increase the need for warning, not the strength of the claim.

### Operational failure

A rubric, checklist, model card, teaching module, or derivative framework produces bad classifications in use. This may reveal poor transmission design, missing calibration, or a genuine doctrinal problem.

### Appeal request

A reader asks to reopen a dossiered verdict or precedent. Use `151` to decide whether appeal triggers have fired.

### Erratum notice

A reader identifies an error in wording, citation, file map, numbering, manifest, cross-reference, or stale revision note. Classify it as editorial, propagation, source, status, terminology, or doctrinal before revising.

## Severity levels

### R0. No action

Accurate uptake or irrelevant feedback. Record nothing unless it is useful as a positive example.

### R1. Local reply

A single clarification can answer the issue without archive change.

### R2. Wording repair

The output should be rephrased, but the doctrine, status, dossier, and transmission level remain intact.

### R3. Warning or return-path repair

The output needs stronger warning, explicit non-verdict, clearer status label, or better return path.

### R4. Transmission-packet revision

The compression level, audience mode, allowed use, omitted-material note, or format-specific warning was wrong.

### R5. Dossier, precedent, or status review

The issue affects application record, future-use permission, precedent status, commitment status, or transfer conditions.

### R6. Calibration or adversarial reopening

The issue introduces a hard case, failed transfer, hostile variant, or counterexample candidate that should be stress-tested and possibly ledgered.

### R7. Doctrine or package revision

The issue shows genuine conceptual failure, source-boundary failure, package drift, stale front-door doctrine, or broken manifest / index / dependency structure.

Severity is not determined by how loudly a reader objects or how often a slogan circulates. It is determined by what the feedback reveals about target, basis, grain, status, source role, non-verdict, and downstream use.

## Intake protocol

When feedback arrives, do not immediately revise doctrine. First ask:

1. **What was transmitted?** Identify the exact file, section, dossier, packet, diagram, summary, table, or answer.
2. **At what compression level?** Use the C0–C7 scale from `152`.
3. **To whom and for what use?** A student, collaborator, source expert, public reader, maintainer, or derivative project will expose different risks.
4. **What was received?** State the reader's apparent interpretation without correcting it yet.
5. **What differs from the licensed output?** Target, basis, grain, route, status, warning, source role, non-verdict, allowed use, or return path.
6. **Is the difference caused by the output, the reader, the setting, the term, the example, the format, or the archive itself?**
7. **Which earlier control should reopen?** `144` route, `145` stress test, `146` ledger, `147` propagation, `148` terminology, `149` status, `150` dossier, `151` precedent, or `152` transmission.
8. **What is the minimal correction?** Local answer, wording repair, warning, return path, erratum, packet revision, dossier update, precedent appeal, calibration entry, or doctrinal revision.
9. **What should not be inferred?** Block feedback laundering.
10. **What future pattern would escalate the issue?** One reader's confusion may be local; repeated confusion across audiences may be transmission failure.

## Errata classes

### Editorial erratum

Typo, grammar, duplicated numbering, broken sentence, or formatting error. Fix without changing doctrine.

### Cross-reference erratum

Broken file link, stale file map, missing index entry, wrong document number, or incorrect sequence statement. Fix through package propagation.

### Terminology erratum

Ambiguous, unsafe, deprecated, aliased, or source-bound term used without status. Use `148`.

### Status erratum

Claim presented with wrong commitment strength, maturity level, scope, or future-use permission. Use `149` and possibly `151`.

### Transmission erratum

Summary, table, diagram, excerpt, prompt answer, or teaching packet drops a required warning, non-verdict, source role, or return path. Use `152`.

### Source erratum

Citation, source role, source-boundary warning, or external-anchor interpretation is wrong or stale. Use `05` and, if specific sources matter, update source anchors before revising source-dependent doctrine.

### Dossier erratum

Application record omits target, basis, grain, rival, hostile variant, artifact risk, review trigger, or non-verdict needed for reuse. Use `150`.

### Precedent erratum

A previous case has wrong allowed use, transfer condition, appeal status, supersession rule, or do-not-reuse label. Use `151`.

### Doctrinal erratum

The feedback defeats a claim at its licensed target, basis, grain, and status. Use `145`, `146`, `147`, and `150` before changing front-door doctrine.

## Correction actions

### Reply locally

Use when the output is sound and the issue is one reader's ordinary orientation need. Do not update the archive unless the same confusion recurs or exposes a design flaw.

### Revise wording

Use when the output invites a wrong reading through avoidable phrase choice. Keep the doctrinal result stable but update terminology and transmission records if needed.

### Add a warning

Use when readers overupgrade a claim, miss source limits, drop a non-verdict, or confuse teaching illustration with precedent.

### Add a return path

Use when readers cannot reconstruct the governing route, dossier, precedent packet, or source note.

### Change compression level

Use when a C1 or C2 output is doing C4 or C5 work, or when a diagram/table needs technical-brief support.

### Add an erratum

Use when the archive itself has a discrete error that later users should be able to see and not rediscover.

### Open an appeal

Use when a dossiered verdict or precedent may no longer transfer under `151`.

### Ledger a reception case

Use when a misreading, failed transfer, or counterexample candidate is reusable as a calibration or regression case under `146`.

### Propagate a correction

Use when the same correction touches front door, synthesis, method rule, frontier test, source note, index, control-layer sequence, or package metadata.

### Quarantine an output

Use when a teaching packet, diagram, summary, or derivative artifact repeatedly causes unsafe upgrade and cannot be repaired without changing its form.

### Reopen doctrine

Use only when feedback survives target fixing, rival reconstruction, hostile variation, source-role audit, and status review.

## Learning without popularity bias

Reception data can be useful, but it should not be confused with metaphysical evidence.

- **Frequent citation** may show usefulness, fashion, ambiguity, or accessibility; it does not by itself raise commitment status.
- **Reader confusion** may show poor pedagogy, difficult doctrine, terminology ambiguity, source-boundary failure, or false doctrine; it does not by itself lower commitment status.
- **External criticism** may identify a real defeater or only reject the archive's terminology; it must be routed before revision.
- **Successful teaching** may show good explanation or oversimplification; it does not automatically license stronger compression.
- **Operational success** in a rubric or checklist may show stable local discrimination, but not necessarily archive-wide truth.
- **Institutional uptake** may create new downstream stakes, but not new metaphysical warrant.

The archive should learn from reception by identifying where understanding succeeds or fails, not by counting applause or objections.

## Audience-specific reception checks

### Private reader mode

Check whether the reader can find the governing file and understand what the claim does not decide.

### Student or onboarding mode

Check whether the teaching example has become the doctrine. If so, add contrast cases and non-verdicts.

### Collaborator mode

Check whether collaborators preserve target, basis, grain, route, and status when using the result in new work.

### Source-domain expert mode

Check whether the expert reads a source-local term as metaphysical export or correctly sees the source-boundary warning.

### Public essay mode

Check whether public prose makes live rivals, provisional probes, and open debts visible enough to block sloganization.

### Revision-maintainer mode

Check whether maintainers can reconstruct the update path and know which control file to reopen.

### Derivative-project mode

Check whether a model card, rubric, checklist, decision framework, or teaching packet produces classifications within its licensed use rather than silently becoming a substitute archive.

## Regression patterns worth ledgering

A reception case should become a calibration or regression entry when it recurs, crosses audiences, or reveals a stable failure mode. Good candidates include:

- a term that repeatedly causes wrong-family routing,
- a teaching example that repeatedly becomes a false precedent,
- a diagram that repeatedly suggests a stronger causal or spatial relation than intended,
- a table that repeatedly bleaches status differences,
- a source-bound example that repeatedly becomes archive-wide metaphysics,
- a prompt answer that repeatedly drops non-verdicts,
- a public summary that repeatedly hides live rivals,
- a derivative rubric that repeatedly overclassifies local cases,
- an external objection that repeatedly preserves target and basis while challenging the verdict,
- or a package-drift error that future revisions are likely to reintroduce.

When such a pattern is ledgered, record the transmission form that produced it. The failure may belong to the output design, not the underlying doctrine.

## Future expansion rule

Before adding another governance file after this one, ask whether the pressure is really new. A new file is warranted only if the archive lacks a reusable answer to one of these questions:

- Where does the case route?
- Does the verdict survive attack?
- Should the case become calibration?
- Where must the change propagate?
- What terms are safe?
- What status does the claim have?
- How should the result be reported?
- How may the reported result be reused, transferred, appealed, superseded, or taught?
- How should permitted material be compressed, excerpted, taught, diagrammed, summarized, or exported without losing its limits?
- How was the transmitted material received, and what correction loop follows?
- What kind of release, deprecation, maintenance debt, migration path, or rollback obligation follows before a package is published?

If the pressure is a variant of one of those questions, amend `144` through `161` rather than adding another governance file.

## Closing formulation

The archive's current applied sequence can now be compressed as:

> **Route the case. Attack the route. Ledger the hard result. Propagate the accepted change. Register the words. Assign the commitment level. Write the auditable verdict. Govern its reuse and appeal. Control its transmission. Audit its reception. Gate the release.**

The point is not to make every reader's reaction into doctrine. The point is to keep the archive answerable to the way its outputs actually function. A responsible system should not confuse uptake with truth, misunderstanding with refutation, adoption with warrant, or criticism with defeat. It should classify reception, repair transmission, ledger recurring failures, reopen doctrine only when the feedback survives the archive's own tests, and gate the resulting release so package polish does not hide maintenance debt.
