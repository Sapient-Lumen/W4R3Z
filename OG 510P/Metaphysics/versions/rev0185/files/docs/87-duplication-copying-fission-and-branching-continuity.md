# Duplication, Copying, Fission, and Branching Continuity

## Why this file exists

The archive now has explicit files on **repair-resilience**, **dormancy**, **onset**, **cessation**, **return**, and **succession-lineage continuity**, but it still lacks one direct file saying what to do when something is said to **duplicate**, **split into two**, **be copied**, **branch**, **fork**, **replicate**, **be backed up and instantiated again**, or **continue in more than one downstream line at once**.

That omission matters because the archive still risks eight opposite mistakes:

- treating every **duplicate** as if it were automatically **the very same bearer twice over**,
- treating every **copy** as if it carried **no real continuity** at all,
- treating **fission** as if it were merely a vivid version of ordinary persistence,
- treating **branching continuers** as if one of them had to be the uniquely real continuation even when the case underdetermines that verdict,
- treating **replication**, **reproduction**, **cloning**, **forking**, **mirroring**, **restoration from backup**, and **mass re-instantiation** as if they were one flat phenomenon,
- treating preserved **structure**, **memory**, **code**, **design**, or **organizational pattern** as if they automatically guaranteed strict identity,
- treating count-increase cases as if they could be handled with the same rules as mere succession,
- or treating branching continuity as if it settled nothing beyond a loose historical narrative.

This file gives the archive a compact **duplication-branching profile** so future revisions can say whether a case is chiefly about persistence, fission, duplication, copy-production, lineage branching, replay from archive, or merely token-multiplication under one type or template.

## Working default

The archive should now say explicitly:

> **Real continuity can branch.**

In compressed form:

> **Branching continuity is not identity.**

And more carefully:

> **A later case can preserve organization, memory, design, role-profile, causal ancestry, or lineage position from an earlier case without being numerically identical to it; once continuity multiplies into coexisting branches, the verdict must be typed rather than forced into one flat identity claim.**

This fits the archive's wider commitments:

- **identity and sortals** still matter because not every copied feature carries the same persistence lesson,
- **process** still matters because duplication and branching are historically organized productions rather than mere snapshot resemblance,
- **powers and structure** still matter because shared powers or shared pattern can outstrip one-bearer identity,
- **life and biological individuality** still matter because reproduction, cloning, budding, and fission put pressure on how one individual gives rise to several,
- **persons and selves** still matter because memory or psychology can in principle be copied or branch,
- **artifacts and information** still matter because code, designs, backups, and replicas preserve templates without automatically preserving one token,
- **succession-lineage continuity** still matters because branching often creates one lineage or family continuity rather than one persisting bearer,
- **return/reactivation** still matters because restoration from backup or image can look like return while actually yielding a new token,
- **reference** still matters because one continuing name can hide multiplication underneath,
- and **revision/elimination** still matters because later inquiry may need to split what once looked like one target into several related continuers.

## Core distinctions

### 1. Persistence is not duplication

If one bearer simply persists, the count of relevant bearers stays one. In duplication, copying, or fission, the count can increase.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **one bearer persisting**,
- **one bearer branching into several continuers**,
- **one new copy produced alongside the original**,
- **several new tokens generated from one template**,
- and **mere resemblance without continuity worth promoting**.

Otherwise it will keep treating multiplication as if it were only ordinary diachronic persistence.

### 2. Copying is not reproduction, and reproduction is not identity

A copy can be produced by scanning, replication, re-encoding, rebuilding from a blueprint, or replay from archive. Reproduction can involve descent, inherited material, and developmental production. Neither by itself guarantees one persisting bearer.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **copy-production**,
- **biological reproduction**,
- **fission-based multiplication**,
- **clone-line production**,
- and **same-bearer persistence**.

Otherwise it will keep flattening duplication, reproduction, and identity.

### 3. Similarity is not continuity

Two items can be highly similar without standing in the right historical, organizational, or causal relation. Conversely, two real continuers can diverge quickly after branching.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **high structural similarity**,
- **shared design or code**,
- **causal-historical continuity**,
- **organizational inheritance**,
- and **mere type-membership**.

Otherwise it will keep mistaking resemblance for persistence or ignoring real branching continuity because the branches later differ.

### 4. Fission is not ordinary succession

Succession often replaces one bearer with another along one line. Fission or branching can produce several later continuers at once.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **linear succession**,
- **branching continuation**,
- **split control with both branches surviving**,
- **one branch privileged by the target kind**,
- and **no uniquely privileged continuer**.

Otherwise it will keep importing succession rules into count-increase cases where they do not fit.

### 5. Restoration from backup is not automatically return

A restored image, copied state, or re-instantiated program can preserve architecture, memory, or role-profile. But if the earlier token continues elsewhere, or if many restorations can be made, the case pressures the archive toward duplication rather than simple return.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **same bearer resumed after interruption**,
- **one archived state re-instantiated as a new token**,
- **one token restored while the original is gone**,
- **parallel restorations from one source image**,
- and **mere template reuse**.

Otherwise it will keep treating all restart-from-record cases as one flat form of return.

### 6. Type continuity is not token continuity

A work, codebase, species, ritual, or design can continue as a type or lineage while new tokens proliferate.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **same token continued**,
- **new token of the same type**,
- **new member of the same lineage**,
- **new instantiation under one specification**,
- and **family resemblance without tight continuity**.

Otherwise it will keep confusing repeatability with persistence.

### 7. Branches can be symmetric

Some cases do not privilege one branch over another. If the archive forces a unique continuer anyway, it may only be importing a background preference rather than reading off the world.

So the archive should distinguish:

- **one uniquely licensed continuation**,
- **several derivative continuers**,
- **equally strong branch-claims**,
- **kind-relative privilege of one branch**,
- and **no determinate answer yet**.

Otherwise it will keep pretending that branching cases always contain a hidden single winner.

### 8. Multiplication changes the metaphysical question

Once one becomes many, the archive is not only asking whether continuity exists, but also **what kind of continuity survives count increase**.

So it should distinguish continuity of:

- **bearer identity**,
- **memory or state profile**,
- **organizational form**,
- **lineage position**,
- **design/template inheritance**,
- **role or authority routing**,
- and **public name or interface continuity**.

Otherwise it will keep asking identity questions with the wrong ontology already assumed.

## Typology of duplication-cases

### A. Ordinary persistence without multiplication

What continues is one bearer through time, with no relevant increase in count.

**Examples:** one organism recovering from injury; one institution surviving turnover without branching; one machine continuing through repair.

### B. Parallel copy-production

What appears later is another token produced from one earlier token, template, or state while the original remains.

**Examples:** file copies, cloned virtual machines, duplicated databases, replicated artworks under one score or plate, some thought-experimental brain-state copies.

### C. Fission or branching continuation

One earlier bearer gives rise to several later continuers that each inherit substantial continuity-relevant structure.

**Examples:** personal-identity fission cases, biological budding or fission, institutional forks, software forks with shared inherited code and governance history.

### D. Reproduction or lineage multiplication

A later case descends from an earlier one in a way that preserves lineage continuity without preserving one bearer.

**Examples:** offspring, clone lines, cell division, branching research lineages, some craft genealogies.

### E. Restoration from archive or backup

A later token is generated from a stored image, record, or template.

**Examples:** software restored from snapshot, institution rebuilt from preserved records, manufactured part from archived design, mind-upload thought experiments from stored state.

### F. Mass instantiation of one specification

What continues is chiefly a type, design, score, or recipe that can generate many tokens.

**Examples:** printed texts, manufactured parts, executable builds, performances of one work, lab protocols.

## Cross-domain pressure

### Persons, minds, and first-personal continuity

Branching psychology cases pressure the archive not to assume that memory or functional continuity alone settles identity once duplication is possible. A copied or branched subject-profile may preserve much that matters without yielding one numerically identical person.

### Organisms, cells, and biological individuality

Biology makes vivid the difference between one organism persisting, one organism reproducing, and one lineage continuing through branching descendants. Fission, budding, and clone-lines show why multiplication cases must be typed rather than forced into one persistence model.

### Institutions, archives, and governance systems

Institutions can fork, split, or duplicate records, procedures, and authority routes. Sometimes one chartered line continues; sometimes several successor structures inherit different parts of the earlier reality.

### Artifacts, code, and information-bearing systems

Codebases, backups, design files, and reproducible artifacts make it easy to preserve structure across many tokens. That pressures the archive to separate token identity, design continuity, specification inheritance, and replay from archive.

### Works, practices, and repeatable forms

Some realities are built to admit many authentic tokens. The archive therefore needs a way to say when multiplicity is the point, when it creates successors, and when it undercuts any claim of single-bearer persistence.

## False moves to avoid

- Do **not** infer strict numerical sameness from every preserved memory, pattern, or structure.
- Do **not** infer total discontinuity from every count increase.
- Do **not** treat copy, clone, reproduction, replication, branching, and fission as one flat relation.
- Do **not** assume that every branching case has one uniquely correct continuer.
- Do **not** let template continuity, backup state, or code inheritance do more metaphysical work than the target kind allows.
- Do **not** collapse lineage continuity into token identity.
- Do **not** treat replay from archive as automatically the same as return from dormancy.
- Do **not** let one continuing name hide the fact that one earlier target has become several later ones.

## Default test for future revisions

When a future revision says that something **was copied**, **duplicated**, **split**, **branched**, **forked**, **cloned**, **restored from backup**, **reproduced**, **instantiated again**, or **continued in two lines at once**, it should ask:

1. **What is the target?**
   - bearer, organism, person, office, institution, codebase, artifact token, design family, work, lineage, or mixed?
2. **What sort of earlier/later relation is claimed?**
   - persistence, duplication, fission, reproduction, lineage branching, restoration from archive, mass instantiation, or mixed?
3. **What is preserved?**
   - matter, organization, memory, control, code, design, archive state, lineage position, public recognition, or only a name?
4. **What is the continuity basis?**
   - causal continuity, organizational continuity, historical descent, archived specification, exact copying, authorized reproduction, or mixed?
5. **What is the count profile?**
   - one-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-many, serial replay, or no stable answer yet?
6. **What is the coexistence profile?**
   - original remains, original is gone, branches coexist, only one branch survives, or mixed?
7. **What is the branch privilege profile?**
   - unique continuer, several derivative continuers, equally strong branches, kind-relative privilege, or still unsettled?
8. **What is the verdict?**
   - same bearer persisted, new copy alongside original, branched continuers, lineage multiplication without identity, restored new token from archive, repeatable type with many tokens, or still unsettled?

## Compression rule

> **When the archive says that something was copied, duplicated, split, forked, reproduced, or restored from backup, it must state whether the case is one of persistence, branching continuation, copy-production, lineage multiplication, archive-based re-instantiation, or repeatable type-instantiation, what exactly is preserved, and whether count increase defeats, preserves, or simply changes the relevant continuity claim.**

## Why this improves the archive

It lets the archive say, in one place, that:

- some continuities are **one-to-one persistence**,
- some are **one-to-many branchings**,
- some are **new copies beside an original**,
- some are **descendant line growth**,
- some are **archive-based reconstructions**,
- and some are **repeatable-type instantiations** rather than token persistence at all.

That is a direct improvement over forcing every difficult case into the binary of **same thing** versus **totally different thing**.
