# Entry 370 — Dependency lifecycle work deepened around seam proof, transition posture, exception ledgers, and source-aware boundaries

This pass continued the broad “worthy missing crates” research by choosing **implementation readiness over proposal proliferation** again.

The archive already knew that **P-0535 Dependency Lifecycle Transition Kit** was one of the strongest remaining cross-sector opportunities.
What it still lacked was enough concrete product shape and worked examples to answer a tougher question:

> what should a dependency-lifecycle crate actually give other people, in theory and in practice, beyond a dependency graph and some vague cautionary prose?

This pass answers that more concretely.

## Why this lane deserved more work now

The latest official signals still converge on the same pressure point:

- the January 2026 safety-critical write-up explicitly says teams often use third-party crates early, then constrain, contain, fork, vendor, or replace them as criticality rises;
- the March 2026 Rust challenges write-up says ecosystem pain becomes more domain-specific with experience, which makes local containment and transition planning more important than global crate folklore;
- the January 2026 crates.io development update adds stronger imported context such as Security-tab visibility, Trusted Publishing Only Mode, SLOC, and `pubtime`, but those facts still do not answer where a dependency is allowed to live in *your* architecture;
- Cargo’s override, source-replacement, and vendoring docs are now explicit enough to clarify which source moves are temporary patching, which are exact-source replacement, and which are read-only vendoring;
- and the Rust Foundation’s 2026–2028 strategy keeps stable infrastructure and sustainable maintenance front and center, which reinforces boring architectural review artifacts over another recommender or dashboard.

That combination makes one archive lesson sharper:

**A worthy dependency-lifecycle crate is not a graph viewer and not a trust score.  
It is a reviewable contract for where dependencies may live, what seam makes them replaceable, which exceptions were consciously accepted, and how posture changed.**

## Main repo judgment from this pass

The strongest next move for **P-0535** is to make three review objects first-class:

1. **`transition-plan.manifest.json`** — how a dependency is expected to move from its current lane to its intended next lane.
2. **`dependency-exception.ledger.json`** — typed, explicit waivers for cases where a restricted lane still depends on a third-party crate directly.
3. **`imported-signal.receipt.json`** — imported context from crates.io or adjacent tooling, kept clearly separate from local architectural policy.

The repo also now states a sharper source boundary:

- moving from registry → fork → vendor → internal replacement can absolutely be part of a lifecycle transition story;
- but **source-identity equivalence, offline coverage, and mirror honesty remain owned by P-0496 Cargo Vendor & Source Parity Kit**.

That separation matters.
Otherwise the archive would quietly confuse “we changed where this dependency comes from” with “we proved that our source boundary is equivalent and complete.”

## What a worthy crate in this lane should provide other people

The archive now has a tighter answer:

1. **One lane snapshot** that distinguishes wide prototype/tooling use from restricted or high-criticality lanes.
2. **One seam receipt** that names the real replacement boundary instead of promising “we can swap it later.”
3. **One transition plan** that says whether the next move is pinning, containment, vendoring, forking, internalization, or removal.
4. **One exception ledger** for consciously accepted policy breaks.
5. **One imported-signal receipt** so registry trust/support facts inform review without impersonating local architecture policy.
6. **One lifecycle drift artifact** so a release cannot quietly leak a third-party crate into a more sensitive lane.
7. **One support bundle** that a reviewer, downstream integrator, or safety lead can read without spelunking the workspace.

If a crate idea in this territory cannot supply those review objects, it is still probably substrate, scorekeeping, or dashboard work.

## New repo-level lesson

The archive is more useful when the top lanes are kept in **schema-and-scenario parity**.

That means future passes should now prefer:

1. deepening one of the top-ranked shared lanes,
2. adding any missing first-class review objects for that lane,
3. and bringing the fixture family up to worked-example quality

before adding another new proposal that mostly overlaps existing territory.
