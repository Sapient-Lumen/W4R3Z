# Entry 448 — add reviewable exceptions and expiry so temporary relief stops acting like silent policy truth

This pass again chose **deepen over append**.

The archive already had a much better answer for:
- compare,
- freeze,
- intake,
- materialize,
- adjudicate,
- carry forward,
- and reopen review.

What it still lacked was a clean answer to the next operational question:

> when a team cannot satisfy the ideal answer yet, how does a worthy crate let them take a **temporary exception** without letting that exception silently harden into permanent policy truth?

That seam matters now because the current Rust substrate already contains several **tool-specific escape hatches**:
- Cargo Vet can initialize a project by adding `exemptions` so `check` passes immediately, can regenerate exemptions, and explicitly frames exemptions as backlog that should usually shrink over time;
- Cargo Vet also has trust entries and wildcard expirations that can be renewed, which means time-bounded trust already exists but remains tool-specific;
- cargo-deny supports ignored advisories with reasons plus per-crate license exceptions, which is useful but still leaves scope/owner/expiry discipline fragmented;
- cargo-semver-checks is explicitly headed toward a publish-time override path, while also developing witness programs that show how a downstream could break;
- crates.io, docs.rs, and Cargo now expose enough route/time/build metadata that exceptions can be tied to visible evidence instead of becoming oral tradition;
- and the safety-critical work says higher-integrity teams often narrow, internalize, or replace dependencies later, which makes temporary exceptions and removal paths part of the real adoption story rather than an edge case.

## Main judgment

A worthy crate in the practical core should now publish not only:
- compare packets,
- basis locks,
- intake/materialization receipts,
- adjudication sessions,
- carry-forward receipts,
- and recheck tickets,

but also a credible **exception packet**.

That means it should provide:
1. one typed exception receipt with owner, scope, and reason,
2. one explicit evidence basis for why the exception exists,
3. one expiry or renewal date,
4. one removal path or replacement condition,
5. one budget/report view so temporary exceptions stay visible,
6. and one expiry ticket that reopens review without pretending the exception was ever the ideal answer.

## Practical consequence

This pass still does **not** justify a major salience rerank.

But it sharpens the first practical slice again:

1. **P-0509 + P-0536 + minimal P-0535 loop** — comparator packets, basis locks, intake/materialization receipts, adjudication sessions, policy-exception receipts, carry-forward receipts, expiry tickets, and recheck tickets
2. **P-0486** — debug witness bundles that can also carry bounded exceptions instead of vague local notes
3. **P-0496** — restricted-delivery/source-parity bundles that may justify temporary local exceptions but must expose their sunset conditions
4. **P-0472** — docs.rs parity bundles that can support exception decisions without collapsing hosted posture into general support truth

Interpretation:
- the front door is no longer just “choose, freeze, import, adjudicate, and reopen honestly”; it is now also “**exception honestly, with owner and expiry**”.
- the sharpest missing layer is still not a bigger ranking engine; it is one more boring policy artifact above existing packet families.

## What this pass says a worthy crate should provide other people

A worthy crate in the practical core should now answer **sixteen** operational questions:

1. compare — what are the plausible choices now?
2. freeze — what exact basis did the chosen answer stand on?
3. watch — which surfaces are being watched later?
4. trigger — what exact event opened review?
5. intake — what imported material was actually consumed?
6. materialize — what can be made portable or offline-friendly?
7. conflict — which claims disagree and on which route or time basis?
8. adjudicate — what decision was made about that disagreement, by whom, and under what policy?
9. exception — what temporary relief was granted, to whom, for which scope, and why?
10. carry forward — which old claims remain inherited and which became superseded?
11. ticket — what review packet should be opened next?
12. refresh — what later facts changed and which did not?
13. revalidate — does the old choice still stand under the same task and policy?
14. transition — if not, what posture follows now?
15. degrade — what became partial, unresolved, or manual-review-only?
16. ceiling — what still requires manual judgment even after the workbench runs?

If a proposal cannot answer those, it may still be interesting, but it is not yet ready for the practical core.
