# Mile-high foundation — rev0014

This cube should not become a database of things that were wrong.

That is the obvious version, and it is too small. A list of failed claims would be useful, but it would miss the central opportunity: to build the memory of epistemic motion. The important question is not only *what failed?* It is: what was tried, what was believed, how did it become believable, what evidence moved it, what evidence failed to move it, what institution made the failure visible, what practice continued anyway, and what pattern can be learned without overclaiming?

The seed names four corpora: negative results / file drawer, failed replications, engineering mistakes / catastrophic failures, and genealogy of ideas. Rev0001–rev0013 have shown that these are not merely adjacent. They are four ways of looking at the same hidden half of knowledge.

- Negative results preserve the denominator of attempted knowledge.
- Failed replications show whether a claim survives return, scale, adversarial care, or changed context.
- Engineering failures show empirical disconfirmation in built systems, where warnings, institutions, and consequences become visible.
- Genealogy explains transmission: why some fragile ideas spread, why some corrections failed to travel, and why some fields learned patterns that others did not.

The cube's telos is therefore not negation. It is correction memory.

## What this wants to become

A mature MissingKnowledgeHalf cube should be able to answer questions like:

- What was tried and quietly failed before the visible literature began?
- Which claims died in the literature but survived in practice, policy, textbooks, product design, or popular belief?
- Which warnings existed before a disaster, who received them, and what authority did they have?
- Which correction infrastructure eventually made the hidden denominator visible?
- Which effects shrank under replication, and under what boundary conditions did anything remain?
- Which ideas spread by evidence, and which spread by lineage, institutional authority, incentives, or narrative fit?
- Which failed claims later revived in narrower or different form?
- Which patterns recur across fields, and what counterexamples stop the pattern from becoming a slogan?

The dream is not merely archival. It is diagnostic, pedagogical, civic, and machine-readable.

## The correction-memory commons

The phrase that still fits is denominator graph, but rev0014 adds a second phrase: correction-memory commons.

A denominator graph asks: where are the missing attempts?

A correction-memory commons asks: once an error, null, reversal, failure, or warning becomes visible, what happens to it? Does it enter a registry, guideline, accident report, post-publication thread, inquiry archive, textbook correction, practice recommendation, training module, design standard, or institutional memory? Or does it disappear again?

This is why infrastructure records have become central. ASRS, CHIRP, CIRAS, ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO ICTRP, OSF Registries, Retraction Watch/Crossref, PubPeer, NTSB recommendations, Cochrane, GRADE, Choosing Wisely, NICE, and similar surfaces are not side notes. They are the instruments by which the missing half can be seen.

## The cube must track motion, not finality

A mature record should avoid saying simply: this claim is false.

It should say: here is the claim, here is the original evidence route, here is the attempted replication or hard endpoint, here is the status change, here is what remains true or plausible, here is what practice did, here is where the idea traveled, here is how the field updated or failed to update, and here is what would change our status label again.

Some negative results are false negatives. Some failed replications are boundary discoveries. Some engineering failures are also successful investigations. Some program audits are pessimistic and later partly wrong. Some abandoned ideas revive because instruments change. Some claims are not false; they are smaller than advertised.

This cube must preserve that humility.

## Why future sessions must stay unconstrained

The current record types are useful, but they are scaffolds. A 10k+ revision cube will discover phenomena that do not fit rev0014 vocabulary. Future sessions should be allowed to invent, split, demote, merge, and replace surfaces when the phenomenon requires it.

The hard constraints are not the specific schemas. The hard constraints are:

- source before story;
- claim status before verdict;
- status history before static truth;
- dignity before drama;
- denominator before numerator;
- practice status separate from literature status;
- pattern records earned, not asserted;
- handoff surfaces maintained so the work can resume.

The cube should be disciplined without being domesticated.
