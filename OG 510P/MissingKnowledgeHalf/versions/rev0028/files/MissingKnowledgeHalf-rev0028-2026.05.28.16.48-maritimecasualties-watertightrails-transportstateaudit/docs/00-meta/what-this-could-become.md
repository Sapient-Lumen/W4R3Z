# What this could become

Revision: `rev0002`  
Date: `2026-05-24T18:50:42-04:00`  
Move type: direction memo captured from conversation.

## One-sentence future

MissingKnowledgeHalf should become a public, versioned, source-grounded **denominator graph** for knowledge: the record of claims that failed, narrowed, reversed, did not replicate, broke in practice, were abandoned, or travelled through intellectual lineages in ways that explain later error and correction.

It should not become a trivia list of famous mistakes. Its mature value is not that it says "people were wrong." Its mature value is that it lets users see what the visible canon usually hides: the denominator of attempts, the sequence of correction, the lag between literature and practice, and the social routes by which lessons were transmitted or lost.

## The wrong shape to avoid

A tempting but weak version would be a searchable encyclopedia of failed claims: cold fusion, power posing, Mars Climate Orbiter, Therac-25, candidate genes, and so on. That would be useful, but it would plateau quickly. It would invite gotcha culture, encourage shallow debunking, and make each case feel isolated.

The project should instead avoid these failure modes:

- **Wall-of-shame drift:** records become humiliating portraits of mistaken people rather than accounts of incentives, evidence, systems, and correction.
- **Debunking monoculture:** every record gets flattened into true/false, even when the real status is narrowed, contingent, revived, partially replicated, or still contested.
- **List-only growth:** thousands of entries accumulate without cross-links, pattern records, source permanence, or status history.
- **Presentism:** records describe what looks obvious now without reconstructing what was knowable at the time.
- **Source amnesia:** future builders cannot tell why one source was trusted, why another was rejected, or when a source vanished.

## The right shape

The right shape is a linked archive with several mutually reinforcing layers.

### 1. Record layer

The archive needs small, source-backed units: claim records, negative-result records, failed-replication records, engineering-failure and near-miss records, source records, genealogy / anti-genealogy records, recurring-pattern records, and practice-lag records.

The key move is separation. A source is not a claim. A claim is not a case. A case is not a pattern. A pattern is not a moral. Keeping these separate makes the corpus composable and correctable.

### 2. Source permanence layer

Every serious record should answer: where did this come from, how permanent is that source, what was extracted, what was not extracted, what was searched and rejected, and what would make the record change?

This layer is the archive's immune system. Without it, the corpus becomes another confident summary of fragile internet memory.

### 3. Status-history layer

Many entries should not have one status. They should have histories:

```text
promising → widely taught → challenged → failed direct replication → narrowed by meta-analysis → still used in practice
```

or:

```text
dismissed → weak early evidence → revived under better measurement → narrowed rather than vindicated
```

Status history is where the project becomes more than a negative-results database. It becomes a record of epistemic motion.

### 4. Pattern atlas layer

After enough records exist, the archive should surface recurring patterns across domains: warnings ignored, warnings misunderstood, interface ambiguity, units mismatch, authority pressure, production pressure, underpowered nulls, textbook lag, practice lag, observational-to-RCT reversal, fragile proxy metrics, common-mode redundancy failure, replication shrinkage, theory-protective reinterpretation, and charismatic result persistence.

The pattern atlas should never be promoted before evidence. A pattern earns itself by linking multiple records.

### 5. Genealogy / transmission layer

The genealogy corpus should not be ornamental. It explains why error repeats and why correction spreads unevenly.

The archive should ask: who taught the method, who inherited the assumptions, who read the critique, who ignored it, which conference or lab carried the idea, which textbook froze it, which institution rewarded it, and which lost lineage already knew the flaw?

This is the bridge between "what failed" and "why did it keep happening?"

### 6. Pedagogical layer

At maturity, the archive can become a curriculum for failure literacy. It can teach graduate students, engineers, policymakers, journalists, and AI systems how to reason with non-results, reversals, contested status, source fragility, and the difference between evidence failure and moral blame.

The pedagogical layer should come after the record layer. Teaching from unsourced seed examples would betray the project.

## What the first mature product might feel like

A user should eventually be able to ask questions like:

- What claims in my field were widely taught before they failed replication?
- Which null results were genuinely informative and which were merely underpowered?
- Which engineering failures involved warnings that were given but not institutionally absorbed?
- Which medical practices persisted after the literature had turned against them?
- Which positive findings are mostly supported by small, flexible-analysis studies?
- Which ideas travelled through a specific advisor lineage, lab, conference, textbook, or citation cluster?
- Which failures in software look structurally like failures in aviation, chemical plants, or medicine?
- Where did a correction happen in literature but not in practice?

This is the reason the four seed corpora belong together: they let the archive connect evidential failure, practical failure, social transmission, and delayed correction.

## Suggested near-term shape

The archive should grow through narrow, complete pilots rather than breadth-first enthusiasm.

A good first portfolio would be:

1. **Three official engineering-failure records** with public reports and recommendation follow-through.
2. **Three failed-replication records** with original claim, replication attempt, effect comparison, and current status.
3. **Three medical reversal / clinical-trial records** with trial or registry sources and practice-lag fields.
4. **Three genealogy scene records** where influence evidence is explicit: advisor relation, attendance list, correspondence, acknowledgement, or documented reaction-against.
5. **One pattern record** only after at least two or three cases justify it.

This small portfolio would test whether the schema actually works before the cube pretends to scale.

## The should-become claim

This should become **civilizational error memory**: an open infrastructure that lets knowledge work remember not just conclusions, but failed paths, abandoned instruments, disconfirmed hopes, narrowed claims, institutional lag, and the human routes by which both error and correction move.

The codename for this revision is `failureliteracy`, because the archive's deepest public good may be teaching people how to be less surprised by correction.
