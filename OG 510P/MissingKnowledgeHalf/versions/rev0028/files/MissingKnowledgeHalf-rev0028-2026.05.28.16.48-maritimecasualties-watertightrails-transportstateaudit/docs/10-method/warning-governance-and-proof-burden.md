# Warning governance and proof-burden fields

Rev0006 adds a method surface for engineering records in which the pre-event signal matters as much as the final broken component.

A failure record should not only ask “what failed?” It should ask:

1. What warning signals existed before the event?
2. Where did each signal land?
3. Who or what had authority to stop, redesign, delay, test, inspect, or escalate?
4. What proof burden governed action: proof of danger, proof of safety, or an explicit precautionary threshold?
5. What counterpressures competed with the warning: schedule, production, cost, certification, prestige, personal-safety metrics, or routine-success memory?
6. How was the signal categorized: anomaly, in-family, maintenance issue, turnaround issue, no-safety-of-flight issue, acceptable risk, pilot-recoverable condition, or management-of-change issue?
7. What post-event recommendation tried to repair the warning path?

The core distinction is between **warning existence** and **warning authority**. Many disasters do not lack information; they lack an institutional route by which information becomes binding action.

The proof-burden field should be used carefully. It is not a moral verdict. It asks whether the system effectively required dissenters to prove unsafety, or required the operating program to prove safety before continuing.

This method surface is now tested on Challenger, Columbia, Boeing 737 MAX, and BP Texas City. The test is biased: all four are famous severe failures with unusually strong official reports. Future revisions need near-misses, successful stops, and false alarms.
