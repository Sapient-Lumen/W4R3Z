# Non-extractive wealth formation

## Thesis

A morally acceptable wealth order is not judged only by **how much wealth exists** and **who holds it**.
It also has to be judged by **how ordinary wealth is being made**.
A society should count a distribution as only partially successful if a large share of broad household wealth depends on scarcity rents, monopoly markups, fee extraction, or asset-price revaluation that makes entry harder for the next cohort.

This is not a brand-new axis so much as a cross-cutting **source-quality condition**.
The archive should prefer wealth growth that comes from decent earnings, real saving, productive investment, social inheritance, cooperative accumulation, and public/social wealth-building over wealth growth that comes mainly from bottleneck ownership and politically protected extraction.[S16][S20]

## Why this condition is needed

The archive already says immoral inequality involves **extraction without contribution**.
But the current cube still risks treating wealth gains too neutrally once they show up on balance sheets.
That is a problem because the same-looking wealth gain can come from very different social processes:

- a household saves out of decent wages and buys into a cooperative, pension, or diversified portfolio,
- a household gains a secure non-speculative housing position,
- or an incumbent owner becomes richer because land scarcity, monopoly power, fee-heavy intermediation, or publicly created land uplift pushes more of everyone else's spending upward into asset values.

Those are not morally equivalent pathways. rev0018 sharpens the point: when public infrastructure or rezoning helps create the gain, the case for common recapture becomes stronger still.[S34][S35]

OECD's 2025 distributional-wealth paper says inequality is driven by financial assets, moderated by housing wealth, and that housing is the largest household asset.[S16]
OECD's 2025 economic-outlook work also says rising market concentration, higher markups, and business investment increasingly concentrated in a small group of highly profitable "superstar" firms have coincided with weaker dynamism, while common ownership can weaken competitive incentives.[S20]

That means a society can look wealthier, including in the middle, while still reproducing a bad wealth order through:

- land and housing appreciation that rewards incumbents while pricing out entrants,
- monopoly profits and markups that lift capital incomes without broad productive spillovers,
- fee extraction that quietly transfers household cash flow upward,
- asset inflation that makes owners look richer without widening secure access,
- politically protected windfalls that are later misread as ordinary success.

## The source-quality test

A proposed wealth order should therefore be asked four extra questions:

1. **Are broad gains coming from better earnings, saving, and genuine access — or mainly from incumbent revaluation?**
   - A middle class made richer mainly by price appreciation on already-held assets is not the same thing as a middle class widened by new entry and durable saving.
2. **Do the main wealth-building channels make someone else more dependent?**
   - Wealth that depends heavily on high rents, monopoly markups, captive fees, or exclusionary scarcity should count less than wealth built through productive contribution and open access.
3. **Would the same gains still look good if measured from the entrant side?**
   - If existing owners gain because younger or poorer households must pay more to enter, the archive should treat those gains as morally discounted.
4. **Is capital income linked to productive renewal or to bottleneck control?**
   - Persistent profits are not automatically illegitimate, but wealth built mainly on restricted entry, weak competition, or politically defended bottlenecks should trigger stronger scrutiny.[S10][S20]

## What should count as warning signs

The archive should treat the following as warning signs even when headline shares improve:

- wealth growth in the broad middle is driven mainly by housing revaluation while first-entry affordability worsens,
- profit growth and capital gains accelerate while productive investment remains weak or concentrated,
- fees, rents, or markups absorb a large part of wage gains or transfer gains,
- public subsidies or tax privileges capitalise quickly into asset values rather than widening secure access,
- the same reform improves incumbent wealth but leaves new entrants thinner, later, or more indebted.[S07][S10][S13][S20]

## Program implications

This condition pushes the archive toward a slightly stricter reading of reform quality.
A serious anti-inequality program should prefer:

- wage growth with bargaining power rather than household reliance on asset booms,
- housing abundance, secure tenure, and non-speculative ownership rather than incumbent-only appreciation,
- competition, anti-monopoly, and anti-fee policy where profits look increasingly bottleneck-based,
- taxes, land-value-capture tools, and public-wealth instruments that lean against windfall and rent-heavy accumulation,
- social inheritance and early footholds that widen entry rather than merely compensating after prices have already run away.

## Archive rule

The archive should therefore ask one more question whenever the measured distribution improves:

**Did wealth broaden through open and durable access, or did existing owners simply gain through scarcity, bottleneck power, and revaluation?**

If the second is closer to the truth, the society may look richer on paper but it is not yet approaching the archive's moral target.
