# Protected minima and anti-seizure rules

## Thesis

A morally acceptable wealth order should not treat modest footholds as fully real when they are **easy to seize, sweep, garnish, force into liquidation, or otherwise strip back** under ordinary creditor pressure or compulsory depletion rules.

A household can appear to hold a first foothold on paper while still knowing that one routine collection action, one small judgment, one debt spiral, or one administrative liquidation rule can erase that foothold before it changes bargaining power.
The archive should therefore discount early or modest wealth when it is **too weakly shielded to survive ordinary enforcement pressure**.

## Why the archive adds this refinement

The archive already had nearby doctrines:

- [`minimum-asset-floor.md`](minimum-asset-floor.md) says the bottom half needs real resilience rather than a merely statistical share,[S16][S17]
- [`protected-footholds-and-anti-spend-down-traps.md`](protected-footholds-and-anti-spend-down-traps.md) says modest savings should not be punished by low asset thresholds, forced spend-down, or sharp benefit cliffs near the floor,[S17][S31][S39]
- [`encumbrance-and-debt-quality.md`](encumbrance-and-debt-quality.md) says debt-fragile ownership should count less than resilient ownership,[S17][S24][S25]
- and [`present-accessibility-and-lock-up-of-claims.md`](present-accessibility-and-lock-up-of-claims.md) says legally owned claims can still be too locked up or penalty-laden to count as present independence.[S19][S41]

What the archive had not yet said compactly is that a foothold can be **liquid enough, portable enough, and nominally retained under means tests while still being too easy for creditors or compulsory depletion rules to strip away**.

That gap matters because the archive's existing sources already point to the surrounding failure pattern.
OECD's financial-fragility work says many ordinary households do not have enough liquid assets to stay above the poverty line for three months after an income loss.[S17]
OECD's household-wealth work says the bottom quintile often has negative wealth.[S16]
And the archive's existing debt-quality and asset-test anchors already show how thin early ownership can be when households are pushed into arrears, refinancing pressure, or destructive spend-down.[S24][S25][S39]

Taken together, those sources imply a further rule:
**modest footholds do not count like real independence if the legal and administrative environment makes them too easy to strip away before they can stabilize life.**

## The anti-seizure distinction

The archive should distinguish between two very different ideas.

### 1. Legitimate enforcement against substantial surplus wealth

A society may reasonably enforce ordinary debts, support obligations, or judgments against households with clearly substantial realizable wealth.
The archive does not require full immunity for all assets in all circumstances.

### 2. Failure to protect a basic foothold

A society is doing something constitutionally worse when it leaves modest emergency savings, starter accounts, basic household tools, small productive assets, or other early footholds so exposed that ordinary financial trouble repeatedly drives households back toward near-zero.

The archive's compact intuition is:
**a decent wealth order should protect some minimum foothold against routine stripping, not merely help people rebuild from zero after each loss.**

## What should count as anti-seizure failure

Treat weak asset shielding as a serious warning sign when several of the following are true.

1. **Modest liquid buffers are easy to sweep or garnish**
   - Small reserves that are supposed to serve as emergency resilience can be quickly reached by ordinary collection tools before they function as shock absorbers.

2. **First durable claims are legally ownable but weakly protected**
   - Starter accounts, modest retirement claims, matched savings, or similar footholds are easy to raid, offset, or liquidate once households face modest debt pressure.

3. **Housing or essential productive assets are too easy to force into destructive liquidation**
   - The line between fair enforcement and forced downward reset is drawn so weakly that ordinary trouble regularly turns footholds back into dependence.

4. **Insolvency or debt-repair systems preserve too little restart wealth**
   - After formal or informal debt workout, households re-emerge with too little protected reserve, too little housing security, or too little productive equipment to make the restart meaningful.

5. **Public systems protect incumbent wealth while exposing tiny footholds**
   - The legal order is lenient toward large, subsidized, or opaque wealth above while being strict toward small visible buffers below.[S31][S36][S37][S39]

6. **Households learn that visible saving is unsafe**
   - Families reasonably infer that keeping savings in formal accounts or building modest legal claims simply paints a target on them.

## What should count as real relief

The archive should count the following as constitutional improvement because they make modest wealth harder to strip away before it becomes real independence.

### 1. Protected minimum liquid reserves

A basic level of emergency savings should be shielded strongly enough that households can actually use it as resilience rather than watching it disappear at first contact with collection pressure.

### 2. Shielding of early foothold instruments

Starter accounts, simple retirement claims, matched savings, baby-bond style endowments, and other first durable claims should not be designed as if they are immediately available for routine depletion.

### 3. Stronger restart rules after debt distress

Debt-repair, insolvency, and workout systems should leave households with enough protected wealth, housing security, and productive capacity to make recovery plausible rather than merely formal.[S24][S25]

### 4. Distinguishing essential footholds from surplus realizable wealth

Rules should be much more cautious about sweeping modest buffers, essential tools, or other small independence-supporting assets than about reaching clearly substantial surplus wealth.

### 5. Program design that aligns anti-spend-down and anti-seizure logic

A society should not remove anti-saving asset tests with one hand while leaving routine enforcement or compulsory depletion rules free to recreate the same bottom-half assetlessness through another route.

## What follows for the archive

### 1. The floor should be partly shielded, not merely observed

The archive should not only ask whether households hold a liquid buffer or a first claim.
It should also ask whether they can **keep** that foothold through ordinary financial stress rather than only in periods of calm.

### 2. Debt quality and asset protection should be read together

Heavy debt-service pressure, predatory fees, and weak asset shielding form one constitutional pattern.
A foothold that is easy to seize is weaker than a foothold of the same nominal size that is legally protected against routine stripping.[S24][S25]

### 3. Anti-seizure protection is different from blanket impunity

This rule does not imply that debts never matter or that all assets should be judgment-proof.
It implies that a decent wealth order should preserve **some protected minimum of restart-capable wealth** for ordinary households.

### 4. Path quality should include shield quality

A reform deserves more credit when it not only increases bottom-half balances, but also raises the share of those balances that are realistically protected from routine depletion, garnishment, or destructive liquidation.

## Program implications

This rule sharpens several nearby archive surfaces.

- [`minimum-asset-floor.md`](minimum-asset-floor.md) should now be read as requiring not just positive buffers and footholds, but footholds with some real protection against routine stripping.
- [`protected-footholds-and-anti-spend-down-traps.md`](protected-footholds-and-anti-spend-down-traps.md) should now be read together with creditor-side and compulsory-depletion exposure rather than means-testing alone.
- [`encumbrance-and-debt-quality.md`](encumbrance-and-debt-quality.md) should now ask not only whether households are debt-fragile, but whether modest footholds remain meaningfully shielded once debt distress appears.

## Provisional default

When ordinary households can technically build modest savings or early claims but those footholds are **too easy to sweep, garnish, force into liquidation, or otherwise deplete under ordinary pressure**, the archive should discount them for constitutional judgment.
A foothold counts more when it is not only present, portable, and usable, but **protected enough to survive routine financial conflict without resetting a household back toward zero**.[S17][S24][S25][S39]
