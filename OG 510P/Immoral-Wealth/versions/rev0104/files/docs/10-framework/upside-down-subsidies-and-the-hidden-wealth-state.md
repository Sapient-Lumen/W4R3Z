# Upside-down subsidies and the hidden wealth state

## Thesis

A society should not count as moving toward morally acceptable wealth distribution if the state is quietly rebuilding incumbent advantage through **tax expenditures, credit preferences, exclusions, and wealth-building subsidies** that scale with existing asset position or mainly capitalise into prices.

rev0033 adds a compact framework rule:
**treat the hidden wealth state as part of the constitution of wealth, not merely as a technical program-cleanup issue.**
If public support for housing, retirement saving, or other asset-building channels systematically rewards already-positioned owners more than entrants or lower-wealth households, that support should count against claims of broad, open, and durable wealth formation.[S31][S36][S37]

## Why the archive needs this rule

The archive already had nearby ideas:

- public action should not simply feed private windfalls through land and development uplift,[S34][S35]
- broad wealth should count less when it is generated mainly through scarcity rents, incumbent revaluation, and entrant exclusion,[S07][S13][S16][S20]
- and major tax expenditures and wealth-building subsidies belong inside the archive's policy program rather than outside it.[S31][S36][S37]

What the archive had not yet said compactly is that **state-made upside-down subsidy design is itself part of the moral diagnosis**.

That gap matters because a society can look progressive on the visible side of the ledger while running a hidden wealth state underneath:
mortgage relief that scales with loan size or tax rate,
owner privileges that mainly raise bids,
retirement incentives that reward large contributors more than low-balance savers,
or asset subsidies that show up as higher land values, higher fees, or stronger incumbent balance sheets rather than wider entry.[S31][S36][S37]

Without a direct framework rule, the archive risks treating these as secondary implementation details when they are often part of how the wealth order reproduces itself.

## What counts as upside-down subsidy failure

Treat a subsidy architecture as a serious warning sign when several of the following are true:

1. support scales mechanically with **tax rate, mortgage size, contribution size, or existing account balance**;
2. the main first-order beneficiaries are **incumbent owners or higher-balance savers**, not first footholds or lower-wealth households;
3. much of the apparent benefit is **capitalised into prices, rents, fees, or seller gains** rather than delivered as durable entrant advantage;
4. the support remains partly hidden in the tax code or in weakly scrutinised credit and guarantee structures;
5. equivalent fiscal effort could be delivered through **capped, refundable, matched, fixed-amount, direct, or supply-linked** instruments with flatter incidence and better entry effects.[S31][S36][S37]

This is the core archive intuition:
**public support for wealth-building should be judged by who actually gains durable position after incidence and capitalisation, not by gross statutory generosity alone.**

## Why this matters for the archive's target

A society can move somewhat closer to the archive's headline wealth bands and still remain constitutionally off-course if its policy architecture keeps steering public support toward existing owners.

That is because upside-down subsidies can:

- thicken incumbent housing or financial balances while leaving later entrants worse off,
- weaken public balance sheets without building common wealth,
- make apparent middle broadening more debt-fragile or more price-dependent,
- and quietly rebuild reconcentration pressure even while the visible program claims to be inclusive.[S31][S36][S37]

This note is close to [`publicly-created-value-and-common-asset-capture.md`](publicly-created-value-and-common-asset-capture.md), but it is not the same.
That note asks whether collective uplift is recaptured.
This note asks whether ongoing policy architecture is itself **topping up private accumulation in a regressive or entrant-closing way**.

## The non-equivalence rule

For regime judgment, the archive should read wealth-relevant subsidies **net of incidence and net of capitalisation**, not as a gross list of nominal supports.

That means:

- an open-ended homeownership subsidy that mostly raises prices should not count like an entry-opening reform,[S36]
- a retirement subsidy that mainly rewards high earners or large balances should not count like bottom-half asset building,[S37]
- and a tax expenditure should not escape scrutiny merely because it sits on the revenue side of the budget rather than the spending side.[S31]

Two instruments with the same fiscal cost can have very different constitutional meaning.
The archive should therefore prefer support that reaches ordinary households as **real footholds, real buffers, or real common wealth**, not support that mainly inflates the paper value of already-held assets.

## Operational rule

When reading a reform package or an existing wealth order, apply this compact test:

1. **who gets the first-order benefit?**
2. **how much of the support leaks into prices, fees, or incumbent seller gains?**
3. **does the instrument widen first footholds, or mostly reward existing position?**
4. **could the same fiscal effort be delivered in a flatter, capped, refundable, matched, fixed-amount, direct, or supply-linked form?**
5. **does the package reduce or increase future reconcentration pressure?**

If the answers point toward incumbent reward, price capitalisation, and weak entrant benefit, the archive should treat the subsidy architecture as a **negative cross-cutting condition**, not as neutral background.

## Program implication

The archive should prefer wealth-building support that is:

- **entrant-weighted** rather than incumbent-weighted,
- **capped or flat** rather than open-ended,
- **refundable, matched, fixed-amount, or direct** rather than mainly tax-rate-driven,
- **supply-aware** where housing or land bottlenecks would otherwise swallow the benefit,
- and **visible in distributional budgeting** rather than hidden offstage.[S31][S36][S37]

This does not mean every universal or broad subsidy is bad.
It means the archive should stop calling support pro-ownership merely because it carries that label.
The question is whether it actually builds earlier, broader, more durable, and more open footholds.

## What this changes

rev0033 makes four archive claims explicit:

1. **the hidden wealth state is part of the wealth constitution;**
2. **upside-down subsidy design can make a society more unequal even when visible taxes or transfers look progressive;**
3. **beneficiary incidence and capitalisation belong inside the core moral judgment;**
4. and **the archive should prefer entry-opening, flatter, and more visible subsidy designs over open-ended incumbent reward.**
