# Reconcentration pressure

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's older dynamic judgments into a **stable reconcentration rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the fuller time-side rule in [`durability-windows-and-ratchet-tests.md`](durability-windows-and-ratchet-tests.md):
**even when a distribution improves, is the surrounding system still organised to pull wealth back upward under ordinary conditions?**

The archive does not treat a better snapshot as enough.
A society can lower top shares for a while, raise middle wealth for a while, or improve bottom-half balances for a while and still remain constitutionally bad if the ordinary flow regime keeps rebuilding hierarchy through rent extraction, markup power, asset-price inflation, top-tail tax asymmetry, debt drag, and concentrated control over the main compounding channels.[S04][S06][S07][S10][S13][S16][S17][S19][S20]

So this note keeps the archive's dynamic side **compact but functional**.
It distinguishes between:

- a case where gains are merely visible for a time, and
- a case where the main reconcentration machinery is materially weaker than before.

That is meant to stop two opposite mistakes at once:

1. mistaking one-cycle equalisation for constitutional repair, and
2. acting as if reconcentration pressure is only an after-the-fact implementation detail rather than one of the things that decides whether the target has been reached at all.

## Minimum reading rule

Read every apparent improvement through a **hold-against-updraft** rule.
A case should count as approaching the archive's target only when broad gains are not being predictably reabsorbed by the ordinary channels that usually rebuild concentration.

That means better levels should count for less when the system still reliably does most of the following:

- converts wage or transfer gains into higher rents, fees, markups, or debt service,[S07][S10][S17]
- lets incumbent owners benefit from asset-price inflation while entrants fall further behind,[S13][S16][S19]
- preserves tax, legal, and cross-border asymmetries that let large fortunes regroup faster than states can discipline them,[S04][S05][S06]
- leaves public/social wealth too weak to keep up when private asset values rise,[S02]
- or keeps productive control concentrated enough that new gains still compound through narrow chokepoints.[S10][S20]

This is not the same as saying that all inequality dynamics can be frozen permanently.
The archive's claim is narrower and stricter:
**a society should not count as repaired if its ordinary operating logic keeps steering broad gains back upward on schedule.**

## The six main false passes

### 1. One-cycle equalisation is mistaken for constitutional success

A country can look better for one tax cycle, one housing cycle, or one rebound period while the old upward-pulling machinery remains largely intact.
That should count as improvement, but not yet as a solved wealth order.[S06][S20]

### 2. Incumbent revaluation is mistaken for broadening

Measured middle or mass wealth can rise because existing owners benefit from housing or financial-asset appreciation while younger or poorer entrants face later, thinner, or more debt-heavy access.
That is often reconcentration deferred, not reconcentration defeated.[S13][S16][S19]

### 3. Broad gains are washed back upward through everyday extraction

Wage gains, transfer gains, or starter-capital gains should count less when landlords, monopolists, fee-heavy finance, or routine debt burdens absorb them back upward quickly.[S07][S10][S17]

### 4. Top-tail regrouping capacity is mistaken for a secondary detail

A society should count less progress when large fortunes can still re-form rapidly through lightly taxed gains, trust and gift channels, legal arbitrage, opacity, or easier cross-border planning than the state can match.[S04][S05][S06]

### 5. Public weakness is mistaken for neutral background

If public/social wealth, public-value recapture, and common institutional ownership remain too weak to keep pace with private asset inflation, then even broad private gains can prove temporary.[S02][S34][S35]

### 6. Entry gestures are mistaken for reopened accumulation

Small grants, tax-favored accounts, or symbolic access measures should count less when asset inflation, housing scarcity, or carrying costs outrun them almost immediately.
A foothold that is always being outpaced is not a stable widening of ownership.[S13][S19]

## The six standing reconcentration surfaces

### 1. Cash-flow recapture surface

Ask whether ordinary households are allowed to keep a meaningful share of new gains, or whether rent, debt service, fees, insurance burdens, and markups recapture too much of the improvement before it becomes durable wealth.[S07][S10][S17]

### 2. Entry-versus-revaluation surface

Ask whether asset gains are broadening access or mainly rewarding incumbency.
When appreciation consistently beats new entry, the system is still rebuilding concentration through price dynamics.[S13][S16][S19]

### 3. Top-tail regrouping surface

Ask how quickly very large fortunes can rebuild their relative position through capital gains, trusts, gifts, entity planning, valuation asymmetry, and cross-border options compared with the speed of ordinary public enforcement.[S04][S05][S06]

### 4. Labor-share and bargaining surface

Ask whether workers and ordinary earners are receiving a durable enough claim on output that they can retain gains without depending on asset bubbles, emergency relief, or constant compensatory transfer.
If labor remains weak, broad wealth gains are usually easier to claw back.[S02][S08][S14]

### 5. Public-counterweight surface

Ask whether public/social wealth, land-value recapture, common ownership, and other non-private counterweights are growing strongly enough that rising asset values do not simply ratchet private hierarchy upward again.[S02][S34][S35]

### 6. Control-and-compounding surface

Ask whether the main channels of profit, stewardship, and compounding are still concentrated in a narrow set of firms, owners, or intermediaries.
When compounding stays bottlenecked, broad ownership improvements usually remain fragile.[S10][S20]

## Fast use rule

Use the reconcentration side this way in quick classification:

1. start with the visible improvement,
2. ask whether the six standing reconcentration surfaces are easing together or whether some are still pulling sharply upward,
3. discount the case immediately when broad gains are being recycled into rents, debt service, or incumbent-side revaluation,
4. treat top-tail regrouping capacity and weak public counterweight as first-order rather than background conditions,
5. classify the case as **fragile broadening** rather than successful broadening when the ordinary flow regime still points strongly upward.

Default shortcut:
- **better levels + strong recapture through rents / fees / debt** -> likely fragile broadening;
- **better levels + worsening entrant access** -> likely false pass;
- **better levels + intact top-tail regrouping channels** -> improvement may be real, but concentration remains too easy to rebuild;
- **better levels + easing recapture across most surfaces** -> stronger evidence that the order is moving toward durable broadening.

## Program handoff

This note should sharpen the archive in five places.

First, it should make [`../20-program/measurement-and-scoreboard.md`](../20-program/measurement-and-scoreboard.md) stricter about treating one-cycle gains as success when they are still being reabsorbed by rents, fees, or asset inflation.

Second, it should make [`durability-windows-and-ratchet-tests.md`](durability-windows-and-ratchet-tests.md) easier to read: durability asks whether gains have held; reconcentration asks whether the underlying updraft is still strong enough that they are likely to be pulled back upward again.

Third, it should make [`../20-program/housing-land-and-anti-monopoly.md`](../20-program/housing-land-and-anti-monopoly.md) and [`../20-program/predistribution-labor-and-wages.md`](../20-program/predistribution-labor-and-wages.md) feel less optional, because those notes are two of the archive's main anti-reconcentration lanes rather than merely supplementary social policy.

Fourth, it should make [`../20-program/tax-capital-gains-inheritance-and-wealth.md`](../20-program/tax-capital-gains-inheritance-and-wealth.md) and [`../20-program/wealth-legibility-registries-and-enforcement.md`](../20-program/wealth-legibility-registries-and-enforcement.md) more clearly about limiting the speed and secrecy with which the top tail can regroup.

Fifth, it should make [`../20-program/social-inheritance-public-wealth-and-baby-bonds.md`](../20-program/social-inheritance-public-wealth-and-baby-bonds.md) and [`publicly-created-value-and-common-asset-capture.md`](publicly-created-value-and-common-asset-capture.md) more clearly part of the answer to reconcentration rather than only entry-side fairness.

## Bottom line

The archive should now read reconcentration pressure in this order:

1. **Treat better levels as incomplete unless ordinary gains can also hold against upward recapture.**
2. **Judge the entrant side, not only incumbent balance sheets.**
3. **Treat rents, fees, debt drag, and markup recapture as constitutional problems, not merely efficiency leaks.**
4. **Ask how fast the top tail can regroup compared with how fast the state can discipline it.**
5. **Count public/social counterweight and common-value recapture as part of anti-reconcentration architecture, not decorative add-ons.**
6. **Withhold full success from any order whose normal operating logic still rebuilds hierarchy after each short improvement.**

The point is not to demand stasis.
The point is to stop calling a wealth order acceptable when its normal motion still carries broad gains upward toward the same narrow poles of power and inheritance.[S04][S06][S07][S10][S13][S16][S17][S19][S20]
