# Stress-tested footholds and ordinary-shock survivability

## Thesis

A morally acceptable wealth order should not treat modest footholds as fully real when they survive only in **calm weather**.

A household can show positive net wealth, some housing equity, or a small long-run claim on paper while still knowing that one ordinary income interruption, one mild rate reset, one modest local price correction, or one predictable expense shock can wipe that foothold out before it changes bargaining power.
The archive should therefore discount early or modest wealth when it is **too weak to survive ordinary stress without forced sale, arrears, or a fast return to near-zero**.

## Why the archive adds this refinement

The archive already had several nearby doctrines:

- [`minimum-asset-floor.md`](minimum-asset-floor.md) says the bottom half needs real resilience rather than a merely statistical share.[S16][S17]
- [`encumbrance-and-debt-quality.md`](encumbrance-and-debt-quality.md) says debt-fragile ownership counts less than resilient ownership.[S17][S24][S25]
- [`durability-windows-and-ratchet-tests.md`](durability-windows-and-ratchet-tests.md) says one soft snapshot should not count as durable constitutional improvement.
- [`protected-minima-and-anti-seizure-rules.md`](protected-minima-and-anti-seizure-rules.md) says modest footholds count less when ordinary creditor pressure can strip them away.[S24][S25]
- and [`present-accessibility-and-lock-up-of-claims.md`](present-accessibility-and-lock-up-of-claims.md) says a legally owned claim can still count less as present independence when it is too delayed or too penalty-laden.[S19][S41]

What the archive had not yet said compactly is that a foothold can be **owned, usable, and formally retained** while still being too exposed to mild ordinary shocks to count as stable independence.

That gap matters because the archive's existing sources already point to the surrounding failure pattern.
OECD's financial-fragility work says many ordinary households lack enough liquid assets to stay above the poverty line for three months after an income loss.[S17]
OECD's distributional-wealth work says housing is the largest household asset while the bottom quintile often has negative wealth.[S16]
And the archive's existing debt and fragility anchors already show how quickly thin equity or weak buffers can flip into arrears, negative equity, or asset poverty under stress.[S24][S25]

Taken together, those sources imply a further rule:
**modest footholds do not count like real independence if they disappear under mild ordinary stress rather than only under extraordinary catastrophe.**

## The calm-weather illusion

The archive should distinguish between two very different situations.

### 1. A real foothold that survives ordinary stress

A household may still be exposed to rare catastrophe while holding a genuinely meaningful foothold.
The archive does not require perfect insulation from every imaginable event.

### 2. A calm-weather foothold

A society is doing something constitutionally worse when a household appears to hold a foothold only as long as rates stay benign, hours are uninterrupted, prices keep rising, and no routine repair or care cost arrives.

The archive's compact intuition is:
**a decent wealth order should be judged partly by whether ordinary households can keep a modest foothold through normal stress, not only during expansion or temporary calm.**

## What should count as stress-survivability failure

Treat weak ordinary-shock survivability as a serious warning sign when several of the following are true.

1. **A mild income interruption quickly destroys buffer security**
   - One short spell of unemployment, reduced hours, or missed shifts pushes households into arrears, forced borrowing, or asset liquidation because the foothold was too thin to absorb ordinary volatility.[S17][S25]

2. **Small price moves wipe out early equity**
   - Entry requires so much leverage that a modest local house-price correction or refinancing problem wipes out the supposed ownership gain.[S13][S24][S25]

3. **The same shock hits both earnings and assets**
   - Households are concentrated in one local labor market, one housing market, or one fragile sector, so job loss and balance-sheet loss arrive together rather than diversifying risk.

4. **Ordinary rate resets or carrying costs make the foothold unstable**
   - Mortgage resets, rent-plus-ownership costs, insurance jumps, or other recurring burdens mean the asset can be held only while conditions remain unusually favorable.[S07][S24]

5. **A predictable life expense forces a rapid downward reset**
   - Basic repairs, care costs, transport replacement, or similar foreseeable expenses repeatedly consume the entire foothold because no ordinary stress margin exists.[S17][S21][S22]

6. **Bottom-half gains vanish in one normal cycle**
   - The society repeatedly shows brief floor improvement in benign periods and then loses it quickly in the next mild downturn, tightening episode, or local market correction.[S25]

## What should count as real relief

The archive should count the following as constitutional improvement because they make modest wealth more likely to survive ordinary stress.

### 1. Stronger liquid shock buffers

Households should be able to absorb a short earnings interruption without immediate arrears or destructive borrowing.[S17]

### 2. Safer entry leverage and thinner debt-service exposure

The path into housing or other durable claims should not rely on leverage so high that small price or rate moves erase the gain.[S13][S24][S25]

### 3. More diversified or countercyclical footholds

The archive should give more credit when bottom-half claims are not all tied to the same fragile local asset or employment cycle.

### 4. Common protection that prevents routine asset liquidation

Healthcare, unemployment, disability, housing, and care protections should reduce the chance that ordinary shocks force households to burn through or sell their footholds merely to survive.[S17][S21][S22]

### 5. Stress-tested floor design

A society should ask not only whether households can build a foothold in expansion, but whether that foothold remains above a protected minimum after a normal stress event.

## What follows for the archive

### 1. The floor should be stress-tested, not only observed

The archive should ask whether households hold a buffer, a housing position, or a long-run claim **after** ordinary stress, not only in benign snapshots.

### 2. Encumbrance and stress survivability should be read together

Heavy debt-service pressure and weak buffers are not just distributional details.
They are warning signs that the foothold may be a calm-weather illusion rather than durable independence.[S24][S25]

### 3. Durability should not be read only at the aggregate level

A country can pass a provisional durability window in the aggregate while still failing if bottom-half footholds repeatedly collapse under ordinary household stress, local market swings, or modest tightening cycles.

### 4. Path quality should include shock survival

A reform deserves more credit when it not only creates new bottom-half balances, but also increases the share of those balances that survive routine income, rate, or price stress without forced sale, arrears, or rapid depletion.

## Provisional default

When ordinary households can technically build a modest buffer, housing stake, or long-run claim but that foothold **cannot survive mild ordinary stress without forced sale, arrears, destructive borrowing, or a fast return toward near-zero**, the archive should discount it for constitutional judgment.
A foothold counts more when it is not only present, portable, accessible, and somewhat shielded, but **stress-tested enough to survive normal household and market volatility rather than only calm weather**.[S13][S17][S24][S25]
