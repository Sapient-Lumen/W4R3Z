# Downside discipline and loss-bearing symmetry

## Thesis

A morally acceptable wealth order is not judged only by **who gets the gains**.
It also has to be judged by **who is protected from the losses**.
A society should count a distribution as only partially successful if large owners, major financial intermediaries, or systemically important asset holders can repeatedly privatise upside while shifting major downside onto taxpayers, workers, tenants, or the public balance sheet.

This is not a sixth axis so much as a cross-cutting **loss-bearing condition**.
The archive should prefer wealth orders in which risk is borne more symmetrically, rescue is targeted at households and critical functions rather than incumbent asset prices, and any unavoidable public backstop comes with strict burden sharing, pricing, temporary status, and public upside capture.[S32][S33]

## Why this condition is needed

The archive already says immoral wealth inequality involves **domination**, **extraction**, and **reconcentration**.
But it still risks treating large private balance sheets too neutrally once gains are observed.
That is a problem because the same-looking top wealth can rest on very different downside regimes:

- owners and creditors bear real losses when bets fail,
- or the public sector repeatedly steps in to protect asset values, funding structures, or going-concern status while ordinary households bear the downstream fiscal and economic costs.

Those are not morally equivalent wealth orders.

An IMF 2025 technical note says public solvency support to banks has often been used in systemic crises, but should remain a **last resort** and be designed to maximise burden sharing, minimise moral hazard, and protect taxpayers.[S32]
The same note points to privately financed resolution funds, ex post recovery from the industry, and recovery of state support as far as possible from unsecured creditors or the financial system more widely.[S32]
A companion IMF 2025 note on nonbank financial intermediaries says broad asset purchases may create moral hazard by providing a **broad, untargeted safety net for asset holders**, while more targeted, collateralised support can better limit risk transfer.[S33]

That means a society can look broad-owner on paper while still operating under a bad constitutional bargain:

- upside remains private,
- downside is implicitly public,
- concentrated wealth compounds under a safety net ordinary households do not receive on comparable terms,
- and later fiscal repair is socialised through weaker public wealth, weaker services, or slower broad-based asset building.

## The loss-bearing test

A proposed wealth order should therefore be asked four extra questions:

1. **Who actually absorbs first losses when large fortunes or major intermediaries fail?**
   - Shareholders, managers, and unsecured creditors should bear meaningful losses before taxpayers do, except where strictly necessary to protect critical functions.[S32]
2. **Is support targeted at households and core payment / credit / market functioning, or at incumbent asset prices?**
   - Generalised asset-price support can quietly operate as wealth protection for large asset holders rather than social stabilisation.[S33]
3. **Is any public backstop temporary, priced, conditional, and recoverable?**
   - Support that is cheap, open-ended, or weakly recoverable teaches large actors to expect rescue.[S32][S33]
4. **Does the public capture upside when it must absorb downside?**
   - If the state must intervene, it should receive control rights, warrants, levies, clawbacks, or comparable recovery mechanisms rather than merely socialising losses.[S32]

## What should count as warning signs

The archive should treat the following as warning signs even when headline shares improve:

- repeated rescue of large intermediaries or asset markets without meaningful shareholder, creditor, or executive loss,
- public guarantees or purchases that stabilise incumbent portfolios more than household security or core functions,
- weak ex post recovery of rescue costs from the sector that generated the losses,
- compensation, dividend, buyback, or payout practices that resume quickly after publicly supported stabilisation,
- fiscal repair that falls later on ordinary taxpayers or weaker public services while concentrated owners retain much of the upside.[S32][S33]

## Program implications

This condition pushes the archive toward a stricter view of crisis architecture and state support.
A serious anti-inequality program should prefer:

- credible resolution and bail-in regimes for systemically important institutions,
- ex ante industry-funded insurance / resolution tools or ex post levies where public support was unavoidable,
- targeted household and real-economy protection over broad asset-price support,
- temporary public equity, warrants, voting rights, restructuring authority, or strong clawbacks when the public balance sheet must step in,
- rescue governance that distinguishes **saving critical functions** from **saving incumbent wealth**.[S32][S33]

## Archive rule

The archive should therefore ask one more question whenever a society claims to have broadened wealth:

**Are gains private while major downside still lands on the public?**

If the answer is often yes, then even a better-looking distribution remains morally discounted.
