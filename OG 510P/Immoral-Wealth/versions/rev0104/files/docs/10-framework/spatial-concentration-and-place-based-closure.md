# Spatial concentration and place-based closure

## Thesis

A society should not count as close to a morally acceptable wealth distribution if wealth, secure tenure, and first footholds remain **territorially closed in the places where opportunity and value compound fastest**.

rev0034 adds a compact framework rule:
**treat place-based closure as part of the wealth constitution, not merely as a housing-policy side issue.**
If ordinary adults can only afford durable footholds in low-opportunity or weakly connected places while high-opportunity neighborhoods, metros, school catchments, and transit-linked corridors remain closed by scarcity, concentrated land position, or displacement, then national broadening is being overstated.[S07][S13][S19][S34][S35]

## Why the archive needs this rule

The archive already had nearby ideas:

- housing and land are major sites where the share target and the control target intersect,[S07][S13]
- excluded groups can remain shut out of wealth-building even when national averages improve,[S13][S19]
- and publicly created land and development uplift should not simply harden into private windfalls.[S34][S35]

What the archive had not yet said compactly is that **nationally broader wealth can still coexist with territorial closure in the places that most shape life chances**.

That gap matters because a country can look less unequal in aggregate while still operating a harsh spatial hierarchy:

- incumbent owners keep control over high-opportunity places,
- later entrants are pushed farther out or into thinner-tenure positions,
- infrastructure and rezoning raise incumbent site values faster than they widen access,
- and national statistics flatter the settlement even though the compounding places remain closed.

A morally acceptable wealth order should not merely allow ownership **somewhere**.
It should make secure access meaningfully open in the places where jobs, schooling, care, transport, civic power, and long-run appreciation are actually concentrated.

## What counts as place-based closure failure

Treat territorial closure as a serious warning sign when several of the following are true:

1. **High-opportunity places are persistently closed to non-rich entrants**
   - Secure renting, buying, or staying in those places requires parental wealth, extreme leverage, or unusually high income.[S07][S13][S19]
2. **Local scarcity is being actively maintained**
   - Exclusionary land-use rules, incumbent vetoes, weak social or non-market housing supply, or concentrated landholding keep access tighter than underlying capacity would require.[S07][S35]
3. **Public action raises local land values faster than it widens local access**
   - Transport, rezoning, infrastructure, or public-land strategy create windfalls for positioned owners while affordable entry, tenure security, or public/social wealth capture lag behind.[S34][S35]
4. **Displacement and screening sort excluded groups away from compounding places**
   - Apparent national broadening masks the fact that racialised, migrant, disabled, caste-marked, Indigenous, or otherwise excluded groups are more likely to be priced out, filtered out, or moved out of high-opportunity locations.[S13][S19]
5. **The local fiscal or political model rewards exclusion**
   - Localities gain by protecting incumbent asset values, limiting new entry, or pushing lower-income households elsewhere rather than by broadening access and sharing uplift.

This is the core archive intuition:
**cheap ownership in low-opportunity or weakly connected places is not constitutionally equivalent to secure access in the places where wealth and life chances compound.**

## The non-equivalence rule

For regime judgment, the archive should not treat all footholds as spatially interchangeable.

That means:

- a national homeownership gain does not count the same if it is achieved mainly by pushing households to distant or weak-opportunity peripheries,
- a secure foothold in a declining labor market does not fully substitute for exclusion from the main opportunity-rich metros or corridors,
- and a local affordability program does not count as enough if large shares of the population remain screened out of the places where schooling, transport, health access, and asset appreciation are bundled together.[S07][S13][S19]

## Operational rule

When reading a wealth order or a reform package, apply this compact territorial test:

1. **Where are the main opportunity-rich places?**
2. **Can ordinary households obtain secure tenure or ownership there, or within realistic access distance?**
3. **Are new infrastructure, zoning, and public-land decisions widening access or mostly inflating incumbent site values?**
4. **Are excluded groups able to stay, enter, and accumulate there, or are they being displaced or screened away?**
5. **Would the same national wealth picture still look acceptable if judged from the entrant side inside the main compounding places rather than from the incumbent side across the whole country?**

If the answers point toward closure, displacement, and incumbent protection, the archive should treat territorial structure as a **negative constitutional condition**, not as neutral background.

## Program implication

The archive should prefer housing, land, and local-governance reforms that are:

- **entry-opening** rather than merely owner-protecting,
- **transit-linked and place-aware** rather than geographically blind,
- **anti-displacement** rather than satisfaction with raw production alone,
- **value-capturing** where public action lifts site values,
- and **regionally or nationally disciplined** where local veto points would otherwise preserve closure.[S07][S13][S19][S34][S35]

That means the housing program should not stop at “more units” or “more ownership.”
It should also ask whether new supply, subsidy, transport, public-land strategy, and fiscal design are actually opening the geography of opportunity.

## What this changes

rev0034 makes four archive claims explicit:

1. **territorial closure is part of the wealth constitution;**
2. **nationally broader wealth can still fail morally if high-opportunity places remain closed;**
3. **cheap footholds in low-opportunity places do not fully substitute for access to compounding places;**
4. and **housing, land, anti-displacement, value-capture, and local-governance design should be judged partly by whether they open the geography of opportunity rather than merely soften national averages.**
