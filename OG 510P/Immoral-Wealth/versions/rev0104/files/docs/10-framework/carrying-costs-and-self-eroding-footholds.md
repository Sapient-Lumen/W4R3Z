# Carrying costs and self-eroding footholds

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's core floor-and-housing judgments into a **stable carrying-cost rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the fuller floor, encumbrance, housing, and shock-survivability notes:
**when should apparently broadened ownership count less because the ordinary cost of holding the claim steadily eats away the independence it was supposed to create?**

The archive does **not** treat all recurring holding costs as moral failure.
A real foothold can come with taxes, maintenance, utilities, insurance, service charges, or other routine obligations.
But a household with modest positive wealth is in a very different moral position when those obligations are manageable than when they are so heavy that the foothold never matures into retained security, usable margin, or bargaining power.[S07][S13][S17][S42]

So this note keeps the question compact but operational.
It treats carrying burden as a **cross-cutting discount rule** on apparently broadened ownership, then supplies a small set of false passes and standing surfaces that should make headline gains count for less.
That is meant to stop two opposite mistakes at once:

1. treating any ongoing cost as proof that a foothold is fake, and
2. treating gross ownership as equally emancipatory even when routine holding costs steadily consume the gain.

## Minimum reading rule

Read ownership through a **net-holdability screen**.
A claim counts more when an ordinary holder can keep it through routine years while still retaining enough margin for savings, ordinary maintenance, small setbacks, and non-panicked life choices.
It counts less when insurance, taxes, utilities, fees, mandatory charges, repairs, or similar recurring costs absorb so much of the foothold's practical value that the household remains pinned in near-zero security even without a new shock.[S07][S17][S42]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**wealth that is too expensive to hold does not count like wealth that can actually be kept.**

Read this note more harshly when the apparent success of the lower half depends on:
- housing metrics that ignore total housing cost rather than just rent or mortgage payments,[S07][S42]
- outright ownership that still behaves like burdened tenure because taxes, insurance, utilities, or repairs remain heavy,[S42]
- small claims whose value is repeatedly drained by account, platform, management, or other recurring fee structures,
- or footholds that look adequate only while maintenance, insurance, or other ordinary cost conditions remain unusually favorable.[S17][S42]

## The six main false passes

### 1. Gross ownership is mistaken for retained security

A society should count less success when households technically own a modest asset or durable claim, but routine holding costs leave little surplus after the basic cost of keeping the claim alive is paid.[S07][S42]

### 2. Debt-free ownership is mistaken for low-burden ownership

Outright ownership should count for less when recurring taxes, insurance, utilities, service charges, or maintenance demands still make the position too expensive for ordinary households to hold with confidence.[S42]

### 3. Mortgage-or-rent affordability is mistaken for total affordability

The archive should discount apparent progress when entry looks manageable only because the measurement stops at rent or mortgage while ignoring the larger total-cost burden that follows occupancy.[S07][S42]

### 4. Small-balance inclusion is mistaken for net wealth-building

A country should count less success when smaller holders technically own a claim, account, or asset share but recurring fees, mandatory intermediaries, or administrative charges repeatedly absorb the value that was supposed to broaden ownership.

### 5. Temporary cost calm is mistaken for sound holdability

The archive should count less success when a foothold looks adequate only during unusually low insurance, tax, utility, or maintenance periods rather than under ordinary cost conditions.[S17][S42]

### 6. Deferred upkeep is mistaken for affordable ownership

A society should count less success when households keep the asset only by postponing repairs, accepting deterioration, or skipping maintenance that later recreates insecurity, damage, or forced higher-cost repair.

## The six standing carrying-cost surfaces

### 1. Total-holding-cost surface

The archive should ask what it actually costs to keep the foothold in normal time.
A claim counts more when total recurring costs are manageable in relation to ordinary income; it counts less when those costs quietly eat the margin that was supposed to create security.[S07][S42]

### 2. Net-retained-margin surface

A foothold counts more when households still retain cash-flow room for small savings, repair, and non-emergency choice after carrying costs are paid.
It counts less when the asset consumes the entire surplus before any shock arrives.

### 3. Cost-volatility surface

The archive should ask how sensitive the foothold is to ordinary swings in insurance, utilities, fees, local taxes, or other recurring charges.
A claim counts less when mild cost drift quickly turns it from manageable to precarious.[S17][S42]

### 4. Upkeep-and-decay surface

The archive should ask whether the asset can be kept in sound condition without chronic deferral.
Ownership counts less when the only affordable path is gradual deterioration, hidden maintenance debt, or postponed replacement.

### 5. Fee-extraction surface

Smaller claims should not be drained by fee architecture that is trivial for large holders but heavy for modest ones.
The more the value of a small foothold is repeatedly skimmed away by routine charges, the less the archive should count it as real broadening.

### 6. Ordinary-life-compatibility surface

A foothold counts more when it remains compatible with ordinary living rather than demanding continuous sacrifice just to keep the claim standing.
It counts less when holding the asset blocks buffer formation, care, mobility, or basic maintenance of the rest of life.

## Fast use rule

Use this note in four steps:

1. **Ask what the holder must pay in ordinary time just to keep the claim.**  
   Do not stop at purchase, mortgage, or nominal ownership; ask what total recurring costs attach to the foothold.[S07][S42]
2. **Check the retained margin.**  
   Discount the pass when routine carrying costs leave little room for savings, small repairs, or ordinary volatility.[S17][S42]
3. **Check whether affordability depends on temporary calm.**  
   Count less progress when the claim works only while insurance, utilities, taxes, or maintenance burdens stay unusually soft.
4. **Check whether the asset is being maintained or merely postponed.**  
   Give less credit when households preserve the appearance of ownership by allowing the foothold itself to decay.

Default shortcut:

- **manageable total costs plus retained margin plus maintainable upkeep plus low fee drag** -> count strongly because the foothold is actually holdable;
- **high total costs plus no retained margin plus heavy fee drag plus deferred upkeep** -> discount because the gain is quietly self-eroding;
- **mixed case** -> ask whether ordinary time is strengthening the holder or slowly eating the claim away.

## Program implication

Once carrying burden is read clearly, the archive becomes stricter about a common false comfort: the idea that modest ownership is doing enough merely because people still technically retain the asset.[S07][S42]
That changes program judgment in three directions.
First, housing and wealth policy should care much more about **net holdability through time**, not just entry price or headline tenure.
Second, lower-cost common provision, protected minima, fee discipline, maintenance support, and stronger public insurance deserve more weight, because they determine whether a foothold gradually thickens or quietly erodes.[S17][S21][S22][S42]
Third, measurement should stop treating gross balances and simple tenure categories as decisive when recurring carrying costs still leave ordinary households with almost no practical margin.

This is why carrying burden belongs inside the archive's constitutional reading of distribution rather than as a technical housing-expense footnote.
A society is closer to justice when broad ownership is becoming **easier to keep, less fee-drained, less upkeep-fragile, and less dependent on unusually favorable recurring cost conditions**.

## Bottom line

The archive should now read carrying costs in this order:

1. **Ask what it costs in ordinary time to keep the claim, not just to acquire it.**
2. **Discount footholds that leave too little retained margin after recurring costs are paid.**
3. **Treat debt-free but high-burden ownership as a real failure mode rather than a technical exception.**
4. **Count fee-light, maintainable, ordinary-life-compatible footholds as real progress.**
5. **Refuse to let gross ownership count like freedom when normal holding costs quietly eat the gain away.**
