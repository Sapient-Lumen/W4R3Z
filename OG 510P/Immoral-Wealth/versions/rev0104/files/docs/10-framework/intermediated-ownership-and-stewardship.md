# Intermediated ownership and stewardship concentration

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's older control-side claims into a **stable constitutional reading rule**.
It answers a narrower question than [`asset-composition-and-control.md`](asset-composition-and-control.md):
**why should a society still count as only partially broadened when many households hold capital claims, but a narrow intermediary layer still casts most of the votes, sets most of the engagement priorities, and normalises most of the governance expectations?**

The archive does not treat all ownership alike.
It distinguishes between: 

- **claim distribution** — who receives returns and bears risk,
- **stewardship distribution** — who actually directs votes, engagement, escalation, and governance norms,
- **control consequences** — who therefore shapes firm conduct, sector priorities, and the long-run political economy of corporate power.

That distinction matters because a society can broaden pension claims, mutual-fund balances, and retirement exposure without broadly dispersing stewardship.
Households may then look more invested while remaining civically weak, because a small manager layer still decides how underlying firms are governed.
OECD's 2025 stewardship work says that, as of 2024, asset managers held **65%** of listed equity in the United States and **59%** in the United Kingdom, with at least **30%** in several other advanced markets, and that the largest 20 asset managers held **USD 56 trillion**, or **38% of global assets under management**.[S20][S26]

## Minimum reading rule

Read intermediated ownership through a **claim-versus-stewardship** rule.
The archive should count a case as closer to justice only when broadened financial ownership is accompanied by materially broader voice over how those underlying claims are voted, governed, and constrained.
That means the case should be read less generously when:

- households mainly receive passive beneficial exposure while voting remains concentrated in a few giant managers,
- asset-owner mandates are too vague for stewardship to track member or beneficiary priorities,
- public or worker capital is outsourced into the same thin manager layer without meaningful democratic direction,
- or broad portfolio ownership is used rhetorically to suggest plural capitalism even though the stewardship map remains oligarchic.[S20][S26]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**broad cash-flow exposure is not yet broad ownership in civic substance when the stewardship layer stays bottlenecked.**

## The six main false passes

### 1. Beneficial exposure is mistaken for democratic ownership

A society should count less success when many households hold retirement or fund claims but have little ability to observe, contest, direct, or exit the stewardship posture attached to those claims.
Owning the return stream is not the same thing as sharing the governance power.[S20][S26]

### 2. Passive-index breadth is mistaken for stewardship pluralism

Indexing can lower costs and broaden exposure, but the archive should not treat passive scale as stewardship dispersion when the same concentrated manager layer continues to cast votes across huge swathes of the corporate economy.
Household breadth and governance breadth can move in opposite directions.[S20][S26]

### 3. Disclosure is mistaken for control

Published voting reports and stewardship policies matter, but the archive should count less success when beneficiaries can merely inspect manager behaviour after the fact rather than shape it before the fact.
Transparency without usable direction is only a partial pass.[S20][S26]

### 4. Public or pension ownership is mistaken for member power

Large pension or sovereign pools can look socially broad on paper while remaining manager-defined in practice if mandates are generic, member voice is thin, and outsourced stewardship is only weakly supervised.
The archive should count less success when social ownership is broad but stewardship remains technocratically centralised.[S20][S26]

### 5. Taxing the top is mistaken for dispersing corporate rule

A top tail can pay more tax while stewardship power remains concentrated in private intermediaries that sit between millions of savers and the real governance decisions.
Where that bottleneck survives, the control surface should still be read harshly.[S20][S26]

### 6. More retail exposure is mistaken for anti-oligarchic governance

The archive should discount narratives in which households gain modest financial exposure while the same proxy, voting, engagement, and agenda-setting infrastructure remains clustered in a few firms.
That is a broader beneficiary base layered onto an old command structure, not a full democratic broadening of ownership.[S20][S26]

## The six standing stewardship surfaces

### 1. Beneficiary-to-vote visibility

The archive should ask whether ordinary beneficiaries can actually trace how the firms they indirectly own are being voted and governed.
Opaque intermediation should count against the case even when balances are widely spread.[S20][S26]

### 2. Mandate specificity and reversibility

Stewardship broadens more credibly when asset-owner mandates are specific enough to constrain manager discretion, and when owners can revise, reallocate, or terminate mandates without prohibitive friction.
Generic convenience mandates should count less as dispersed governance.[S20][S26]

### 3. Usable pass-through or member-directed voting

The archive should read the case more favorably when beneficiaries, members, workers, or public constituencies can direct voting on at least some material questions in ways that are not merely symbolic or operationally unusable.
OECD's stewardship work reviews pass-through voting precisely because it can loosen this bottleneck where it is well-designed.[S26]

### 4. Countervailing governance institutions

A society should read more favorably when public funds, union funds, cooperative funds, or other member-governed vehicles exercise meaningful stewardship at scale rather than leaving the entire governance field to a few giant private managers.
Plural stewardship matters, not just plural claims.[S20][S26]

### 5. Conflict, competition, and accountability discipline

Where the same managers sit across large shares of competing firms and strategic sectors, the archive should ask whether there are credible conflict rules, competition discipline, fiduciary enforcement, and reporting structures strong enough to prevent stewardship scale from quietly becoming political-economic rule.[S20][S26]

### 6. Strategic-sector anti-bottlenecking

The case is stronger when governance over strategic sectors is not routed mainly through a thin private manager layer.
The archive should count less success when broad household capital exposure still leaves core infrastructure, finance, energy, media, or systemically important firms subject to highly concentrated stewardship power.[S20][S26]

## Fast use rule

Use this note in the following order:

1. ask whether broad portfolio ownership has actually widened **voice** or mainly widened **exposure**,
2. ask whether beneficiaries can see and influence how votes are cast,
3. ask whether the owner-manager mandate is specific enough to move stewardship away from manager convenience,
4. ask whether public, worker, union, or cooperative capital provides countervailing governance capacity,
5. discount the case quickly when the same thin manager layer still sets the effective governance posture of much of the corporate economy.

Default shortcut:

- **broader claims + same manager bottleneck** -> only partial success;
- **broader claims + more disclosure + no usable member influence** -> still only partial success;
- **broader claims + broader stewardship voice + plural counterweights + conflict discipline** -> much stronger pass.[S20][S26]

## Program handoff

This note should make the archive stricter in four places.

First, it should stop the archive from treating broadened fund ownership as equivalent to fully dispersed ownership.
See [`asset-composition-and-control.md`](asset-composition-and-control.md) and [`wealth-to-rule-conversion.md`](wealth-to-rule-conversion.md).

Second, it should make anti-capture design more attentive to stewardship infrastructure rather than focusing only on campaign finance or lobbying.
See [`../20-program/political-firewalls-against-wealth-rule.md`](../20-program/political-firewalls-against-wealth-rule.md).

Third, it should harden the common-wealth lane against simply outsourcing broad public or quasi-public savings into the same narrow private manager architecture without governance reform.
See [`../20-program/social-inheritance-public-wealth-and-baby-bonds.md`](../20-program/social-inheritance-public-wealth-and-baby-bonds.md).

Fourth, it should make legibility and reporting questions stricter around proxy voting, engagement, outsourcing chains, and beneficial-owner direction.
See [`../20-program/wealth-legibility-registries-and-enforcement.md`](../20-program/wealth-legibility-registries-and-enforcement.md).

## Bottom line

The archive should now read intermediated ownership this way:

1. **Separate claim distribution from stewardship distribution.**
2. **Do not confuse broad fund exposure with broad governance power.**
3. **Count ownership less generously when votes and engagement remain concentrated in a few intermediaries.**
4. **Look for usable beneficiary voice, plural counterweights, and real mandate discipline.**
5. **Treat a stewardship bottleneck as a control failure, not a minor implementation detail.**

The point is not to abolish intermediation.
It is to stop calling a society broadly owned when millions hold the returns but a narrow manager layer still governs the firms that shape ordinary life.[S20][S26]
