# Immoral wealth inequality

## Working definition

Wealth inequality becomes **immoral** when it predictably produces one or more of the following:

1. **Mass assetlessness** — a large share of the population lacks durable savings, housing security, or investment capital.
2. **Domination** — the wealthy can regularly bend politics, media, law, or market structure to preserve or deepen their position.
3. **Dynastic lock-in** — inheritance and parental position overwhelm contribution as determinants of life chances.
4. **Extraction without contribution** — rents from land, monopoly, or financial position dominate productive contribution.
5. **Public weakness** — governments become too balance-sheet-poor to provide countervailing institutions or universal capabilities.
6. **Closure of the future** — people without wealthy parents lose realistic routes into ownership, security, or independent adulthood.
7. **Automatic reconcentration** — even after reform, the main channels of accumulation keep pulling wealth and control back upward.
8. **Forced self-insurance** — households must privately accumulate large balances just to survive essential risks that a decent social floor should partly absorb.
9. **Opaque command of wealth** — major fortunes, transfers, or control rights sit behind wrappers too illegible for democratic oversight, fair taxation, or anti-capture enforcement.[S04][S23]
10. **Stewardship bottlenecks** — households may hold capital claims through pensions or funds while voting power and engagement remain concentrated in a narrow intermediary layer that shapes corporate rule without equivalent beneficiary voice.[S20][S26]
11. **Late footholds** — people without wealthy parents may eventually receive wealth, but too late in life for it to shape independent adulthood, housing access, or ordinary life planning.[S05][S13][S19]
12. **Internal dependence** — wealth may look broadly held at the household level while one adult still lacks direct title, liquid control, pension rights, or credible exit security inside the household itself.[S13][S27][S28][S29][S30]
13. **Group closure** — wealth may look broader in national averages while historically subordinated groups remain structurally excluded from safe credit, secure tenure, entrepreneurship, inheritances, land title, or other main channels of long-run asset building.[S13][S19]
14. **Publicly socialised downside** — concentrated owners may keep gains in normal times while major downside is repeatedly shifted onto taxpayers, public balance sheets, or later retrenchment in common provision.[S32][S33]

## Why wealth, not income alone

Income matters, but wealth is what compounds, collateralizes, and reproduces itself across generations. It funds political influence, buffers shocks, buys legal optionality, and changes bargaining power long before any annual income statistic fully registers the shift. In an intermediated financial system, the archive also has to ask who exercises the governance rights attached to that wealth, not only who ultimately receives the cash flows.[S26] It also has to ask whether the relevant claims belong to persons in an enforceable way, rather than only to households in the aggregate.[S27][S28][S29]

## Moral test

A society can have unequal wealth without being morally intolerable. The line is crossed when inequality stops being a byproduct of free variation and starts functioning as a **governing technology** and a **birth lottery amplifier**.

A useful test is this:

- Can the bottom half absorb shocks and make meaningful life choices?
- Does the middle majority hold a large enough asset base to avoid dependence?
- Is the top tail small enough that it cannot casually purchase rulemaking?
- Is public wealth strong enough to underwrite universal capability?
- Can people without affluent parents still enter ownership and long-run security at scale?
- Can broad gains survive normal rent, profit, and asset-price cycles, or do they quickly wash back upward?
- Do households need excessive private wealth simply to buy healthcare, education, care, or old-age safety that could be more fairly socialised?
- Is major ownership visible enough that the society can actually measure concentration, tax large fortunes and transfers, and police rule-conversion?
- Do first durable footholds arrive early enough that adulthood is not organized around waiting for inheritance?
- Do adults inside households have direct enough claims that broad household wealth is not masking private dependence or trapped exit?
- Do historically subordinated groups have genuinely open access to the main wealth-building channels, or are the gains still being captured mainly by already-advantaged groups?
- Are broad gains coming through better entry and durable saving, or mainly through scarcity rents, markups, fees, and incumbent revaluation that tighten the next round of access?[S07][S10][S13][S20]
- When large private bets fail, do owners bear meaningful downside first, or does the public repeatedly absorb the losses?[S32][S33]

If the answer is no, the inequality is no longer merely descriptive. It is institutionalized subordination.

## Current empirical backdrop

The backdrop is stark. In the 2026 World Inequality Report, every world region is described as having extreme wealth concentration: the **bottom 50% holds only 1% to 5% of wealth**, while the **top 10% holds 60% to 74%**. In North America & Oceania the **top 1% holds about 34%**, and in Europe about **25%**.[S02] OECD reporting likewise notes that wealth is much more unequally distributed than income, with the top 10% owning **more than half of household wealth on average across the OECD** and **79% in the United States**.[S03]

OECD's 2025 opportunity work adds a second warning. It reports that **over 25%** at least of household market income inequality across the OECD can be attributed to inherited circumstances, and that parental background accounts for **over 60%** of inequality of opportunity in most countries.[S18] Companion OECD analysis also reports that in OECD European countries in 2023 having homeowning parents increased the likelihood of owning a home by age 38-46 by **18.7%**, up from **12.7%** in 2011.[S19]

Those are not merely unequal societies. They are societies in which asset ownership is thin for the many, thick enough at the top to create durable asymmetry in power, sticky enough across generations to threaten democratic openness, and often shielded by legal and informational structures that complicate measurement and enforcement at the top.[S04][S23] OECD and World Bank evidence also warns that household-level security can overstate person-level independence, especially for women, when individual earnings, account access, land ownership, property rights, and pension entitlements remain more unequal than pooled household measures suggest.[S13][S27][S28][S29][S30] Recent OECD wealth work adds that significant gaps also remain by migrant status and geographic location, and that many countries still lack the equality data needed to see wealth barriers clearly for racialised communities, LGBTI people, religious minorities, and persons with disabilities.[S13]
