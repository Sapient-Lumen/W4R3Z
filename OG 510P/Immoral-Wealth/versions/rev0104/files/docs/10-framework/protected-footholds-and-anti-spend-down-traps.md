# Protected footholds and anti-spend-down traps

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's core floor-conditioning claims into a **stable protected-foothold rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the fuller target procedure in [`target-selection-rules.md`](target-selection-rules.md):
**how much apparent floor protection or broadening should count when households near the bottom are still forced to stay almost assetless, liquidate first footholds, or fear visible saving in order to keep access to basic support?**

The archive does **not** treat spend-down traps as a minor means-testing detail.
A society can talk about resilience, saving, ownership, and dignity while still organizing basic protection around anti-asset rules that require families to run down modest buffers before help appears.[S17][S39]
That is not neutral targeting.
It is a way of quietly reproducing bottom-half wealthlessness while claiming to protect need.

This note therefore focuses on a specific constitutional failure:
**the state punishes modest footholds near the floor instead of protecting them.**
When that happens, the archive should count less progress even if transfer spending rises, legal eligibility expands, or headline poverty relief looks stronger on paper.[S17][S31][S39]

## Minimum reading rule

Read punitive asset tests, forced spend-down, and sharp support cliffs as a **direct attack on the floor**, not as a neutral administrative refinement.
A society should count as more just only when ordinary households can build and keep modest emergency reserves, early child accounts, simple productive tools, safer housing positions, or other first footholds **without that progress itself triggering loss of essential protection**.[S17][S39]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**a decent wealth order should not require people to prove need by remaining nearly assetless.**

This note therefore downgrades apparent progress when access to basic support still depends on conditions like:
- very low asset thresholds near the floor,
- liquidation of modest liquid reserves before help arrives,
- harsh treatment of developmental or productive assets,
- abrupt cliffs rather than gradual tapers,
- interaction rules that neutralize baby bonds, matched savings, or debt relief,
- or administrative regimes that teach households visible saving is dangerous.[S17][S31][S39]

## The six main false passes

### 1. Anti-poverty spending is mistaken for floor-building

A society should count less progress when it spends more on hardship relief but still requires households to stay nearly assetless in order to receive that help.[S17][S39]

### 2. Excluding large idle wealth is mistaken for punishing modest footholds

The archive should distinguish between refusing to subsidize clearly comfortable wealth and penalizing the first small buffers or starter assets of households near the floor.
Conflating those is a constitutional mistake.[S39]

### 3. Emergency relief after liquidation is mistaken for protection of independence

A system counts less favorably when households can reach help only after running down the very reserves, tools, or early claims that would otherwise support later stability.[S17][S39]

### 4. Precision targeting is mistaken for low asset ceilings and sharp cliffs

Apparent targeting quality should count less when rules are so strict that small gains in savings or assets cause disproportionate losses of essential support, making first-foothold accumulation irrational.[S39]

### 5. Asset-building programs are mistaken for real broadening when means tests claw them back

A state should count less progress when it advertises matched savings, baby bonds, debt relief, or small-asset promotion while adjacent support rules still erase their value through withdrawal, delay, or liquidation pressure.[S17][S31][S39]

### 6. The hidden wealth state is ignored

A regime looks worse, not better, when it lightly subsidizes incumbent wealth at the top while tightly policing tiny buffers at the bottom.
The archive should read these together as one distributional constitution.[S31][S36][S37][S39]

## The six standing protected-foothold surfaces

### 1. Baseline asset disregards and protected small reserves

The archive should ask whether households can hold modest emergency savings, child accounts, simple retirement balances, essential tools, or other first footholds without immediate disqualification from basic support.[S39]

### 2. Tapers, grace periods, and transition rules

Protected footholds count more when movement upward is gradual: grace periods, partial disregards, and smoother tapers are constitutionally better than abrupt cliffs that punish small gains.[S39]

### 3. Treatment of productive, developmental, and early-capital assets

The archive should read regimes more harshly when they force liquidation of assets that help later independence, such as work tools, education-linked savings, child accounts, safer vehicles, or other modest productive claims.[S17][S39]

### 4. Interaction with claiming and visible saving

Spend-down traps are worse when households must disclose and repeatedly justify tiny balances through complex administrative channels, or when program design teaches families that remaining informal, unbanked, or unrecorded is safer than visible saving.[S39]

### 5. Interaction with social inheritance and public-capital policy

Protected footholds count less as a public commitment when baby bonds, matched savings, debt relief, or public-capital grants are formally offered but then rapidly neutralized by nearby means-tested withdrawal rules.[S17][S31][S39]

### 6. Budget and scoreboard treatment

The archive should treat anti-spend-down design as part of package quality.
A regime should count more favorably when budgeting, implementation, and scoreboarding ask whether reforms let households **retain** early gains while receiving essential protection, rather than only whether benefits exist in statute.[S31][S39]

## Fast use rule

Ask a compact question:

**Can an ordinary non-rich household build and keep a modest first foothold without losing access to basic protection?**

If the answer is no, the archive should discount the apparent strength of the floor.
A wealth order counts as better not only when households can acquire a little more, but when they are also allowed to **remain protected while owning a little more**.[S17][S31][S39]
