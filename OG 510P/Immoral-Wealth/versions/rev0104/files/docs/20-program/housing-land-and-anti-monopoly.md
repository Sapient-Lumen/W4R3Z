# Housing, land, and anti-monopoly

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's oldest claims into a **stable operating stack**.
A society can raise wages, widen savings, or even improve headline wealth shares and still fail morally if those gains are repeatedly capitalised into land prices, rent burdens, monopoly markups, or other gatekeeper tolls.
So the archive treats **housing, land, and anti-rent policy** as part of wealth-distribution infrastructure rather than as a side chapter about one market.[S07][S08][S10][S13][S16][S31][S34][S35][S36]

## Minimum operating rule

Any serious wealth-broadening package should do five things at once:

1. widen **secure housing access** for households without wealthy parents,
2. lower the share of ordinary life financed through **scarcity rents and landlord tolls**,
3. recapture a material share of **publicly created land uplift** for common use,
4. stop tax and credit policy from mainly **bidding up incumbent asset values**,
5. and prevent concentrated firms in necessity sectors from **reabsorbing household gains** through markups or labor-market power.[S07][S08][S10][S13][S16][S34][S35][S36]

No single subsidy, homeownership headline, or nominal affordability measure is enough if the rest of the stack still drives entry prices, rents, or recurring tolls upward.

## The main false passes

### 1. Owner wealth rises while entry closes

Rising house values can flatter middle-wealth statistics while younger and lower-wealth households are locked out of ownership, pushed into thinly protected rental positions, or forced to wait for family wealth to enter at all.[S13][S16][S19]

### 2. Help arrives mainly as bigger bids

Buyer subsidies, tax breaks, or easier credit can look pro-middle while mostly capitalising into land prices or seller windfalls where supply is tight.[S31][S35][S36]

### 3. Access improves only on the periphery

National housing broadening should count less when the main opportunity-rich places stay territorially closed and households are pushed toward long commutes or weaker-opportunity peripheries instead of real access to the places where life chances compound.[S19][S34][S35]

### 4. Measured affordability improves while dependence stays crowded or insecure

Cheaper units or nominal tenure gains count less when crowding, claimant dilution, weak living space, eviction risk, coercive fees, or poor habitability still leave households without a durable foothold.[S07][S43]

## The standing housing and anti-rent stack

### 1. Secure rental floor and non-speculative supply

Core moves:

- expand social, cooperative, community-land-trust, or similarly non-speculative supply,
- strengthen tenant security, fee discipline, and habitability enforcement,
- reduce coercive churn and repeated involuntary moves,
- and treat rental stability as **wealth-floor infrastructure**, not merely residual poverty policy.[S07][S13][S35]

### 2. Entry-opening land-use reform in high-opportunity places

Core moves:

- legalise more homes where scarcity is binding,
- reduce exclusionary zoning and local veto structures that preserve incumbent scarcity,
- align housing growth with transport and services,
- and judge success partly by whether entrants can access productive places rather than merely cheaper distance.[S13][S19][S34][S35]

### 3. Public-value capture and strategic land policy

Core moves:

- use land value capture, developer obligations, infrastructure levies, land banking, public land assembly, or equivalent tools,
- manage public land strategically rather than treating it as disposable cash,
- and return part of rezoning- or infrastructure-led uplift to affordable housing, infrastructure, or public/social wealth.[S34][S35]

The archive does not treat value created by public action as a pure private windfall by default.

### 4. Housing-tax and subsidy hygiene

Core moves:

- cap or remove upside-down mortgage-interest relief where it mainly rewards incumbent owners,
- review uncapped main-residence gain preferences and other open-ended housing tax advantages,
- prefer capped, visible, entrant-oriented support over open-ended deductions,
- and test buyer subsidies and credit expansions for capitalisation before calling them egalitarian.[S31][S36]

### 5. Anti-hoarding and anti-withholding tools where concentration is binding

Core moves:

- use vacancy, holding, development, or land-use obligations where strategic withholding blocks access,
- deploy land banks or public options where private concentration can otherwise ration entry,
- and watch whether concentrated ownership is preserving artificial scarcity rather than productive use.[S13][S34][S35]

### 6. Anti-monopoly and anti-monopsony in necessity sectors

Core moves:

- strengthen merger control and anti-monopsony enforcement,
- scrutinise sectors that turn necessity into recurring extraction — including housing platforms, logistics bottlenecks, health, utilities, payments, and finance,
- and treat concentrated toll-taking as a wealth-policy concern because household gains that are immediately re-taxed by private gatekeepers do not become durable wealth.[S08][S10]

## How to read the stack

### 1. Judge from the entrant side, not only the incumbent side

Ask whether people without property-owning parents can get secure housing positions early enough to shape adulthood, not just whether current owners gained paper wealth.[S13][S19]

### 2. Supply without tenure security is incomplete; subsidies without supply discipline are dangerous

The archive wants more homes and easier entry, but also secure claims and anti-extraction discipline.
Either side without the other produces false passes.[S07][S31][S35][S36]

### 3. Place access matters as much as national counts

A country should count less housing success when it broadens units somewhere, but keeps the main opportunity-rich places effectively closed.[S19][S34][S35]

## Practical shortcut

Ask seven things:

1. can lower-wealth households enter secure housing without wealthy parents,
2. are high-opportunity places genuinely opening rather than only distant peripheries,
3. is public action recapturing some of the land uplift it creates,
4. are housing subsidies capped and tested against capitalisation,
5. are rents, fees, and eviction or churn risks being disciplined,
6. is concentrated land or platform control blocking access,
7. and are household gains elsewhere being reabsorbed by landlord or monopoly tolls?

If several of those answers are weak at once, the archive should assume that broadening gains will leak back upward through rent extraction even when headline distribution measures improve.
