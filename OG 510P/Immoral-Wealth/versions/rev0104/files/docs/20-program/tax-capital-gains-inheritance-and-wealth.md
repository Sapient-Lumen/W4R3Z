# Tax capital gains, inheritance, and wealth

## What this note is for

This note turns the archive's top-end tax lane into a **stable operating stack**.
The archive does not treat tax as the whole answer to immoral wealth inequality, but it does treat top-tail tax design as one of the main ways a society can slow reconcentration, compress concentrated capital claims, and weaken the birth-lottery transmission of life chances.
So the question here is not "how do we raise revenue from rich people somehow".
It is how to tax concentrated capital and large transfers **in a way that is anti-dynastic, administrable, resistant to mobility blackmail, and connected to early broadening rather than late repair**.[S04][S05][S19][S23][S38]

The current archive judgment remains narrower than many slogan-level debates.
IMF's 2024 guide says governments concerned with wealth inequality should usually **prioritise improving capital income taxation** rather than assuming an annual net wealth tax is always the first-best universal instrument.[S04]
OECD's inheritance-tax work says there is a good case for greater use of inheritance taxation, especially through **recipient-based design**, exemptions for small inheritances, and broader bases.[S05]
OECD's 2025 opportunity work adds the larger moral reason: by taxing high-value transfers, inheritance taxation can reduce wealth concentration and improve equality of opportunity, while parental homeownership and family transfers still shape who gets into ownership and who does not.[S19]

## Minimum operating rule

A serious anti-concentration tax package should usually do **seven things at once**:

1. tax large **capital returns** more coherently and less preferentially,
2. tax large **intergenerational transfers** in a way that targets recipients and dynasty rather than merely estates on paper,
3. treat **trusts, gifts, wrappers, and cross-border reporting** as part of tax design rather than later enforcement garnish,
4. begin from the **most visible and least mobile bases** rather than granting a blanket veto to mobility threats,
5. use recurrent net wealth taxes **selectively**, where records and political conditions make them likely to bite rather than merely headline,
6. clean the **hidden wealth state** so open-ended subsidies do not quietly rebuild incumbent wealth,
7. and judge tax success by whether it reduces **dynastic dependence and wealth-to-rule capacity**, not only by short-run revenue or one-year top-share movement.[S04][S05][S19][S23][S31][S36][S37][S38]

No single rate rise, one-off levy, or symbolic wealth-tax announcement is enough if the rest of the stack still lets concentrated capital hide, roll forward, or reappear through subsidy channels.

## The main false passes

### 1. Revenue rises but dynastic structure barely changes

A society should count less tax-side success when it raises some money from the top but still allows large fortunes to pass between generations through exemptions, trusts, inter vivos gifts, valuation games, or lightly taxed family holding structures.[S04][S05][S19][S23]

### 2. A wealth tax is announced before the state can really see the top tail

The archive should count less success when a recurrent net wealth tax is asked to do heroic work while ownership records, beneficial-ownership tracing, trust reporting, valuation capacity, and cross-border information are still weak.
In that setting the likely result is not clean compression of extreme wealth but selective taxation of the visible rich and strategic escape by the truly difficult top tail.[S04][S23][S38]

### 3. Capital income rules tighten while transfer rules stay soft

A society should count less progress when it improves annual taxation of capital returns but leaves inheritances, gifts, or donor-side estate rules too weak to interrupt dynastic renewal.
The archive's concern is not only yearly cash flow at the top, but the reproduction of class position across generations.[S05][S19]

### 4. The visible tax schedule improves while the hidden wealth state keeps refilling upper-tier balance sheets

Headline progress counts less when open-ended housing or retirement tax preferences still scale support with existing balances, marginal tax rates, or appreciating assets.
That can let the state claim top-end discipline while quietly rebuilding incumbent wealth on the side.[S31][S36][S37]

### 5. Mobility threats are allowed to become a standing veto

The archive should count less tax-side seriousness when every attempt to tax large fortunes is abandoned at the first mention of exit, relocation, or non-cooperation.
Good design starts from more immobile rents, visible domestic choke points, and already-operating information channels before moving outward to the hardest cross-border bases.[S04][S23][S38]

## The standing tax and anti-dynasty stack

### 1. Capital-income alignment and base integrity

Core moves:

- reduce large preferential gaps between labor and capital where the tax system systematically rewards concentrated capital more lightly than ordinary work,
- tax capital gains more consistently,
- reduce preferential treatment of large unrealised gains at transfer where that remains available,
- and treat base design as part of distributional design rather than a technical side issue.[S04][S06]

The archive's standing rule is simple: a society that taxes wages heavily while leaving concentrated capital income lightly taxed is financing equality from the wrong side.

### 2. Recipient-based inheritance and gift taxation

Core moves:

- prefer **recipient-based inheritance taxation** where possible,
- exempt low-value inheritances rather than high-value dynastic engineering,
- integrate gifts and inheritances so inter vivos transfers do not become the obvious avoidance route,
- and use lifetime-receipts logic where administrative conditions make it credible.[S05][S19]

This matters because what the archive is trying to interrupt is not only the donor's act of bequest, but the recipient's accumulation of unearned starting advantage.

### 3. Strong treatment of trusts, wrappers, and top-tail avoidance channels

Core moves:

- integrate trust, gift, inheritance, and ownership reporting,
- trace beneficial ownership through legal entities,
- build valuation and audit capacity for hard-to-price assets,
- and make cross-border information exchange a normal part of top-tail administration rather than an exotic extra.[S04][S23][S38]

A nominally progressive tax package that cannot see control, transfer, and beneficial ownership clearly will often overtax the legible upper middle while undertaxing the real dynastic core.

### 4. Selective use of recurrent net wealth taxes

Core moves:

- treat recurrent net wealth taxes as a **possible complement**, not the automatic universal first instrument,
- use them more confidently where elite wealth is extreme and asset records are already strong,
- and avoid making them carry the whole anti-concentration burden when easier and more robust reforms to capital-income and inheritance taxation are still sitting undone.[S04][S05]

This is a judgment about sequence and bite, not a moral endorsement of extreme fortunes.
The archive remains open to wealth taxes where they are likely to work, but skeptical of building the whole tax lane around the hardest instrument first.

### 5. Anti-blackmail sequencing from visible bases outward

Core moves:

- start from the most visible and least mobile bases,
- move first on domestic rents, large inheritances, clear capital-income preferences, and other bases that are already partly legible,
- harden enforcement and information exchange before promising the most ambitious mobile-base measures,
- and refuse the idea that cross-border difficulty makes all top-end taxation futile.[S04][S23][S38]

The archive does not want timid design.
It wants design that bites early enough to matter and survives long enough to ratchet.

### 6. Clean the hidden wealth state

Core moves:

- review major housing and retirement tax expenditures as distributional choices,
- prefer capped, refundable, matched, or fixed-amount support to open-ended upside-down relief,
- and test whether nominal support capitalises into prices, fees, or incumbent balances before calling it pro-middle-class or pro-ownership.[S31][S36][S37]

The archive should therefore read top-end tax reform together with subsidy hygiene.
Otherwise the state can take with one hand and rebuild incumbent wealth with the other.

### 7. Route tax design toward earlier broadening, not only later cleanup

Core moves:

- judge tax design partly by whether it reduces dependence on family wealth for early-adult footholds,
- read inheritance taxation together with social inheritance, public wealth, and early-life entry policy,
- and remember that later and later inheritances are a poor substitute for timely, broad, person-level claims.[S05][S19]

A society that relies on late private inheritance to do the work that earlier public or social inheritance should do is leaving too much of the distributive order in family hands.

## How to read the stack

### 1. Tax is necessary, but not sufficient on its own

The archive does **not** claim that better top-end taxes solve wealth inequality by themselves.
A society can improve capital-income and inheritance taxation and still fail if labor institutions are weak, housing entry is closed, public wealth is too thin, or political firewalls are too weak.
But a society that leaves concentrated capital and large transfers lightly taxed is asking every other lane to row against a stronger dynastic current.

### 2. Judge tax policy by anti-dynastic force, not revenue alone

Revenue matters, but the archive's deeper test is whether tax design reduces the dependence of opportunity, ownership entry, and political leverage on family wealth.[S05][S19]
A modest-revenue reform that clearly hardens anti-dynastic rules may matter more than a noisier measure that raises money but leaves family transmission largely intact.

### 3. Legibility is part of tax design, not an administrative appendix

## Practical shortcut

Ask eight things:

1. are large capital returns still taxed much more lightly than ordinary work,
2. are big inheritances and gifts taxed in a recipient-focused anti-dynastic way,
3. can trusts, wrappers, and cross-border structures still defeat the rules routinely,
4. is the state starting from visible bases or surrendering to mobility blackmail immediately,
5. are recurrent wealth taxes being used where they can genuinely bite rather than merely headline,
6. are housing and retirement tax expenditures still refilling upper-tier balance sheets,
7. are these taxes helping fund earlier and broader footholds rather than only later cleanup,
8. and after all of that, is the top tail still able to reconcentrate wealth faster than the rest of the program can spread it?

If several of those answers are weak at once, the archive should assume that the tax lane is still underbuilt even if the statutory schedule looks newly progressive.
