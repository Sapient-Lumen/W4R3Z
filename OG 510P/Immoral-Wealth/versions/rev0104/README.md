# Immoral-Wealth — rev0104

## When ownership is expensive to keep

Imagine a household that has paid off its home but cannot meet insurance, utilities and repairs without using nearly all its spare income. Its debt has disappeared; its room to choose has not necessarily grown by the same amount. If repairs are postponed to make the budget work, the household may retain the title while the condition of its home deteriorates.

The [carrying-cost essay](files/docs/10-framework/carrying-costs-and-self-eroding-footholds.md) asks how much credit a wealth order deserves for broadening ownership when the ordinary cost of keeping a claim consumes the independence it was meant to provide. Its question applies beyond housing: a small account or asset share can exist in someone's name while recurring charges drain much of its practical value.

This is rev0104's particular entrance into Immoral-Wealth. The [revision history](files/CHANGELOG.md) calls it a carrying-cost cleanup, not a doctrine expansion. The edition reorganizes an existing argument into a clearer reading rule, examples of misleading success, and routes to related questions. For the larger argument about inequality, domination and usable independence, start with the [main guide](../../README.md).

## Three questions a balance sheet can hide

The source separates three inquiries that can overlap in one household:

- **Debt burden:** What obligations stand behind the asset? The [encumbrance essay](files/docs/10-framework/encumbrance-and-debt-quality.md) asks whether thin equity, heavy repayments, unstable terms or dependence on refinancing leave the holder subject to creditor pressure. It does not condemn every use of borrowing.
- **Ordinary carrying-cost erosion:** What does it cost to keep the claim in normal years? Taxes, insurance, utilities, fees and maintenance can leave too little margin even without a new shock, and even when no loan remains.
- **Shock survivability:** What happens when ordinary life becomes harder? The [stress-survivability essay](files/docs/10-framework/stress-tested-footholds-and-ordinary-shock-survivability.md) asks whether a short earnings interruption, modest price movement or predictable expense rapidly produces arrears, forced sale or renewed dependence. It does not require protection from every imaginable catastrophe.

The distinctions are about what is being tested, not a claim that these mechanisms never interact. A rise in insurance can increase routine holding costs and expose a weak stress margin. Debt repayments can crowd out maintenance. Removing a mortgage does not by itself answer either of the other questions.

## What remains after the costs are paid?

The carrying-cost note calls its approach a *net-holdability screen*: judge whether people can retain a foothold while also maintaining it, saving a little and making ordinary choices without constant emergency pressure.

Its practical sequence is straightforward. Look beyond the entry price or mortgage payment to the whole recurring burden. Ask what margin remains. Check whether apparent affordability depends on unusually favorable costs. Then ask whether the asset is being maintained or merely allowed to deteriorate.

Three examples make the concern concrete:

- A debt-free home may still consume the holder's usable margin.
- A small balance may be disproportionately diminished by recurring fees.
- Deferred upkeep may make this year's bills look manageable while weakening the asset that is supposed to provide security.

None of these examples makes every tax, fee or repair bill illegitimate. The note explicitly allows ongoing obligations. Its concern is the difference between manageable costs compatible with ordinary life and costs that keep the holder near zero security. It offers a qualitative rule for judging apparent progress, not a numerical deduction from an asset's market price or a universal household budget threshold.

## How far this test reaches

The [minimum-floor essay](files/docs/10-framework/minimum-asset-floor.md) places holdability alongside liquidity, secure shelter, person-level claims, protected retention and debt quality. Passing the carrying-cost test alone therefore cannot establish a just wealth order. The floor also allows secure renting: ownership's label is not the decisive good.

The [forced-self-insurance essay](files/docs/10-framework/forced-self-insurance-and-private-buffer-coercion.md) adds an institutional question. How much private wealth must people accumulate merely because common protection is weak? In this account, demanding ever-larger personal reserves can leave the underlying insecurity untouched.

The carrying-cost essay consequently gives greater weight to lower-cost common provision, maintenance support, fee discipline and public insurance. These are the archive's proposed directions, not findings that a particular intervention has worked. An assessment would still need evidence about actual costs, retained margin and upkeep, including how these differ across households. The source's repeated categories make its questions easier to retrieve; they do not supply that evidence.

Read this edition as normative and policy research, not personal financial advice or a statement of current law. Its useful challenge is specific: when ownership spreads, does ordinary time strengthen the holder, or quietly consume the gain?
