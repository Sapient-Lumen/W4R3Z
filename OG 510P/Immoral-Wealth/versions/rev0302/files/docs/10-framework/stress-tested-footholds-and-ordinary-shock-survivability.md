# Stress-tested footholds and ordinary-shock survivability

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's floor-and-fragility judgments into a **stable shock-survivability rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the fuller floor, encumbrance, carrying-cost, protected-minimum, and durability notes:
**when should modest wealth count less because it survives only in calm weather rather than through routine income loss, mild rate resets, modest price corrections, or predictable ordinary expenses?**

The archive does **not** require every foothold to survive catastrophe.
A household can hold a real foothold while still remaining vulnerable to rare disaster.
But that is different from calling a foothold real when one short work interruption, one normal repair bill, one mild local downturn, or one routine refinancing problem can erase it before it changes bargaining power.
OECD's 2025 regional financial-fragility paper sharpens this by stressing that many shocks are geographically localised and by measuring two related thresholds: financial fragility, meaning too few financial assets to cover three months of income at the national poverty-line level, and asset poverty, meaning too little net wealth to do the same.[S17][S51]
Taken together with the archive's debt, floor, and protected-minimum anchors, that means the archive should judge not only whether households can briefly cross into positive wealth, but whether the foothold remains above a meaningful minimum after **ordinary stress** arrives.[S13][S16][S17][S24][S25][S50][S51]

## Minimum reading rule

Read bottom-half and lower-middle wealth through an **ordinary-shock survivability screen**.
A foothold counts more when it can absorb a short income interruption, a mild rate or carrying-cost shock, a modest local price correction, or a predictable life expense **without** forced sale, destructive borrowing, rapid arrears, or a fast fall back toward near-zero.[S07][S17][S21][S22][S24][S25][S50][S51]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**wealth counts less when ordinary households can technically reach a foothold but cannot reliably keep it through normal volatility.**

That means the archive should read apparent progress more harshly when:
- positive net wealth exists mostly because conditions stayed unusually benign,
- early equity disappears after small rate, price, or earnings moves,
- the same local shock hits both the household's job path and its main asset at once,
- predictable repairs, care costs, or replacement costs repeatedly consume the whole buffer,
- or the society keeps posting short-lived bottom-half gains that wash out in the next ordinary tightening or downturn.[S07][S13][S17][S24][S25][S51]

## The six main false passes

### 1. A benign snapshot is mistaken for a durable foothold

The archive should not treat one calm-period balance sheet as proof of independence.
A foothold counts less when it exists mainly because hours stayed uninterrupted, rates stayed soft, and no routine expense shock arrived.

### 2. Paper equity is mistaken for usable shock margin

A household does not count as securely asset-bearing merely because it has thin home equity or a small positive net-worth reading on paper.
The archive should discount the case when little of that position can absorb an ordinary shock before the household is pushed into arrears, refinance trouble, or forced sale.[S13][S24][S25]

### 3. Housing exposure is mistaken for diversification

A society counts less success when most lower-half wealth is tied to the same local housing cycle that shapes rents, commuting burdens, and labour-market access.
OECD's regional fragility work underlines that shocks are often localised, so tying work and wealth to the same fragile place can make the apparent foothold weaker than the headline suggests.[S07][S13][S51]

### 4. Entry affordability in expansion is mistaken for robust entry

A path into ownership or saving counts less when it works mainly in rising markets, low-rate periods, or unusually supportive labor conditions.
The archive should ask whether the same entry path still leaves a meaningful foothold after a normal rate reset or local correction.[S24][S25]

### 5. Formal access to relief is mistaken for actual shock survival

A society should count less progress when healthcare, unemployment support, disability support, family support, or insolvency relief exists in law but arrives too late, too narrowly, or too frictionfully to stop ordinary shocks from destroying the foothold first.[S21][S22][S39][S40][S50]

### 6. Aggregate resilience is mistaken for bottom-half resilience

National recovery or rising average wealth does **not** show that modest footholds are surviving.
The archive should reject cases where the aggregate picture stabilizes while ordinary households still cycle rapidly from thin ownership back toward debt, liquidation, or near-zero.[S16][S17][S25][S51]

## The six standing shock-survivability surfaces

### 1. Liquid-buffer surface

Ask how much truly liquid margin ordinary households hold before trouble starts.
A foothold counts more when households can absorb a short earnings interruption without immediately selling assets, missing payments, or borrowing at destructive terms.[S17][S51]

### 2. Leverage and debt-service surface

Ask how much of the foothold depends on leverage so tight that small rate or earnings changes destabilize it.
A society counts less when early entry is structurally paired with debt-service exposure that leaves almost no ordinary stress margin.[S24][S25]

### 3. Price-correction and refinancing surface

Ask whether modest local price declines or refinancing friction can wipe out early gains.
A foothold counts more when small adverse price moves do not instantly erase the entrant's position or lock the household into a fragile rollover path.[S13][S24][S25]

### 4. Joint-shock concentration surface

Ask whether labour-market risk, housing-market risk, and place-based risk are piled onto the same households at the same time.
A society counts less when the main shocks are correlated because work, housing, transport, and school access all depend on the same local bottleneck or boom-bust corridor.[S07][S13][S51]

### 5. Predictable-expense surface

Ask what happens when normal life expenses arrive: repairs, care bills, insurance jumps, transport replacement, or similar foreseeable costs.
A foothold counts more when these events are absorbable without burning through the full buffer or undermining housing stability.[S07][S21][S22]

### 6. Ratchet and recovery surface

Ask what remains after a mild downturn or tightening episode.
A society counts more when bottom-half footholds recover from ordinary shocks without repeated full resets, and when each cycle leaves more households with retained margin rather than returning them to near-zero.[S17][S25][S51]

## Fast use rule

Use this note in four steps:

1. **Identify the foothold being judged.**  
   Separate a real buffer, modest equity position, or long-run claim from purely nominal or illiquid balance-sheet improvements.
2. **Ask what one ordinary shock does to it.**  
   Stress the case with a short earnings interruption, mild rate move, modest local price correction, or predictable expense.
3. **Ask which protections arrive before forced reset.**  
   Count less progress when the social floor, debt-relief path, or household buffer comes too late to stop arrears, liquidation, or destructive borrowing.[S21][S22][S39][S40][S50]
4. **Ask what survives the cycle.**  
   Judge the foothold by what remains after the ordinary shock, not by the headline position from just before it.[S17][S24][S25][S51]

Default shortcut:
- **liquid buffer + manageable leverage + mild-shock survivability + non-destructive recovery** -> count as a stronger foothold rather than a calm-weather foothold;
- **thin paper equity + tight debt service + correlated local shocks + fast reset to arrears or near-zero** -> discount strongly because the foothold is too cycle-fragile to anchor independence;
- **mixed cases** -> weight the judgment by post-shock position and recovery path, not by pre-shock ownership alone.

## Bottom line

The archive should now read stress-tested footholds and ordinary-shock survivability in this order:

1. **Ask whether the foothold survives normal volatility rather than only calm periods.**
2. **Treat mild income, rate, price, and expense shocks as constitutional tests, not as bad luck outside the frame.**
3. **Read leverage, carrying costs, local concentration, and floor quality together rather than as separate footnotes.**
4. **Judge apparent floor gains by what remains after the first ordinary stress event.**
5. **Count a society less favorably when bottom-half wealth repeatedly appears in expansions and disappears in the next normal tightening or downturn.**

The point is not to demand invulnerability.
The point is to stop calling a foothold real when it survives only under unusually favorable conditions.
In this archive, a modest foothold counts more when it is not only visible, legally owned, and somewhat protected, but **stress-tested enough to live through ordinary household and local-market volatility without collapsing back toward dependence**.[S17][S24][S25][S50][S51]
