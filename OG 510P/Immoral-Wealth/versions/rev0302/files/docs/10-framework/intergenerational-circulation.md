# Intergenerational circulation

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's core closure judgments into a **stable circulation rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the fuller target procedure in [`target-selection-rules.md`](target-selection-rules.md):
**is a society genuinely open across generations, or are better-looking shares still being reproduced mainly through parental position, housing inheritance, and delayed family transfer?**

The archive does **not** treat circulation as a soft moral afterthought.
A country can post a better snapshot, a respectable middle share, or a somewhat stronger bottom-half position while still failing because adult entry into housing, saving, entrepreneurship, and long-run security remains too tightly predicted by who one's parents were.[S18][S19]

That means this note is not just about whether large inheritances exist.
It is about whether the next generation can build adult footholds **before** inheritance, **without** rich parents, and **without** being sorted too early into separate ownership destinies by housing tenure, family wealth, or background.[S05][S18][S19]

## Minimum reading rule

Read circulation as a **constitutional openness condition**, not as a decorative supplement to the share picture.
A society should count as more just only when adult asset position is becoming materially less inherited: younger cohorts can reach secure footholds before large family transfer, the housing ladder is less parent-backed, and life chances are less sorted by birth background than in a dynastic order.[S05][S18][S19]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**a wealth order is not morally acceptable if adulthood is still quietly pre-assigned by family position, even when the current snapshot looks less extreme than before.**

This note therefore reads weak circulation as a serious downgrade.
It should be read more harshly when the apparent openness of the order depends mainly on:
- incumbent comfort rather than entrant access,
- late inheritance standing in for early footholds,
- parental homeownership or wealth strongly predicting adult tenure,
- one cohort looking secure while younger cohorts are more closed out,
- or symbolic starter-capital measures being capitalised back into scarce assets.[S05][S18][S19]

## The six main false passes

### 1. Better shares are mistaken for open entry

A society should count less progress when aggregate or middle-heavy wealth shares improve while the path into ownership is still strongly sorted by parental wealth, education, tenure, or location.[S18][S19]

### 2. Late inheritance is mistaken for open adulthood

The archive should count less success when families eventually pass wealth on, but too late for non-rich adults to shape housing choice, family formation, study, work risk, or entrepreneurship during the years when those decisions matter most.[S05][S19]

### 3. Older-owner comfort is mistaken for intergenerational justice

A country should count less favorably when older cohorts look broadly secure while younger entrants face worse affordability, thinner buffers, and greater dependence on parental help to reach the same milestones.[S13][S19]

### 4. Small universal grants are mistaken for reopened circulation

Starter-capital or savings incentives should count less when housing scarcity, tuition inflation, fees, or debt structures immediately absorb the gain back upward, leaving entrants only nominally less dependent on family backing.[S07][S13][S19]

### 5. Lower top shares are mistaken for lower dynastic force

A society should count less success when the top tail gives up some share but large gifts, trusts, inheritances, or parental balance-sheet advantages still dominate entry into key assets and life opportunities.[S05][S18][S19]

### 6. One cohort pass is mistaken for a live circulation rule

The archive should count less progress when one generation benefited from easier entry in the past but the current operating logic still pushes each new cohort back toward more inheritance dependence and later footholds.[S05][S13][S19]

## The six standing circulation surfaces

### 1. Background-to-adulthood linkage

The archive should ask how strongly parental wealth, tenure, and educational position still predict adult housing access, positive net wealth, and durable saving position.
Circulation is stronger when those links weaken materially rather than merely becoming a little less harsh.[S18][S19]

### 2. Pre-inheritance foothold surface

A society counts more favorably when adults without rich parents can reach at least one durable foothold before major family transfer: secure housing access, liquid buffer, pension or savings claim, business start capacity, or equivalent position that changes bargaining power in ordinary life.[S05][S18][S19]

### 3. Housing inheritance surface

Circulation is weaker when parental homeownership or parental asset ownership increasingly determines who can enter housing on tolerable terms.
Because housing is such a major entry asset, this surface is often where dynastic closure becomes visible first.[S13][S19]

### 4. Cohort replacement surface

The archive should ask whether younger cohorts are entering with comparable or stronger asset chances than older cohorts had at the same stage of life.
If each new cohort needs more debt or more parental help, circulation is weakening even if current aggregate holdings still look acceptable.[S05][S13][S19]

### 5. Non-family entry architecture

Circulation is stronger when the society builds non-family routes into capital: social inheritance, public/common wealth, fair credit, broad pension rights, secure renting or ownership, and labor institutions that allow saving without prior collateral.[S18]

### 6. Delay and dependence surface

The archive should ask whether people must wait too long for a real foothold or remain dependent on family fallback for too many adult transitions.
When early adult freedom still depends on kin wealth, circulation remains too weak even if later-life security looks better.[S05][S18][S19]

## Fast use rule

Use this note in four steps:

1. **Ask whether adult footholds are still mainly inherited.**  
   How strongly does parental position still shape who gets into secure housing, saving, and durable ownership?
2. **Discount late and incumbent-sided success.**  
   Count less progress when later inheritance or older-owner comfort is doing most of the work.
3. **Check the entrant side.**  
   Read the case more harshly when younger adults need more debt, more family transfer, or more waiting than earlier cohorts did.
4. **Prefer non-family entry routes.**  
   Count more progress when public, social, labor, and housing institutions reduce the amount of birth advantage needed to begin adult life with a real foothold.[S05][S18][S19]

Default shortcut:

- **better snapshot plus strong parental sorting** -> read the case as only partly open, because the next generation still enters through birth-gated channels;
- **older broad ownership plus worsening youth access** -> read the case more harshly, because circulation is weakening beneath the aggregate picture;
- **mixed case** -> ask whether the next non-rich cohort can build before inheritance, not merely whether today's owners look safer.

## Program implication

Once this circulation surface is read clearly, two policy mistakes become easier to avoid.
First, the archive should stop confusing flatter stocks with open adulthood when the real route into security still runs through family transfer, parental housing, or delayed inheritance.
Second, it should stop treating social inheritance, housing entry, and early footholds as optional fairness add-ons when they are actually part of the answer to dynastic closure itself.

That is why this note sits between target choice and the broadening lanes.
A society moves closer to justice when it makes adult life less prewritten by family position and gives the next non-rich cohort more ways to build before they inherit.

## Bottom line

The archive should now read circulation in this order:

1. **Ask how strongly parental position still predicts adult ownership and security.**
2. **Discount later inheritance and incumbent comfort when early entrant access stays weak.**
3. **Treat worsening youth access as circulation failure even if older cohorts look secure.**
4. **Count non-family routes into capital as constitutional openness infrastructure, not optional supplements.**
5. **Refuse to call a society open across generations when the next cohort still needs rich parents to begin ordinary adult life.**
