# Gatekeeper dependence and unilateral interruption risk

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's most important but often under-read judgments into a **stable gatekeeper rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the full floor, claimability, portability, person-level ownership, and defensibility notes:
**when should a society count less success because one ordinary sponsor, administrator, provider, household controller, or other chokepoint can still stall, freeze, or condition a foothold?**

The archive already asks whether a foothold exists, whether it is person-level rather than only household-level, whether people can actually claim it, whether it survives ordinary movement through life, and whether it can be proved and defended when conflict arrives.[S28][S29][S30][S40][S41][S44]
But another question sits underneath all of those:
**can the holder keep and use the foothold without continued deference to one ordinary gatekeeper?**
A dwelling, pension right, matched-savings account, social-inheritance claim, tenancy protection, platform account, business right, or beneficiary record counts less when the answer is no, even if the claim looks legally valid or administratively visible.[S28][S29][S30][S40][S41]

This note therefore sits between the archive's person-level ownership, claimability, portability, defensibility, and implementation notes.
A country can look better on enrolment, title, vesting, owner counts, or administrative modernisation while still failing a more basic question:
**can ordinary adults leave, contest, switch, separate, or move without one gatekeeper being able to make them wait, beg, or stay put in order to preserve what is already supposed to be theirs?**[S28][S29][S30][S40][S41]

## Minimum reading rule

Read a foothold through an **exit-without-permission screen**.
A claim counts more when an ordinary holder can continue, transfer, verify, and operate it through normal life change without one gatekeeper being able to materially block continuity or practical use.
It counts less when continuity depends on one employer, one benefits administrator, one provider, one household controller, one local office, or another chokepoint whose delay or refusal can degrade the holder's bargaining power even before the claim is formally lost.[S28][S29][S30][S40][S41]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**a foothold does not count fully when one ordinary gatekeeper can still turn a nominal right into a waiting room.**

Read this note more harshly when the apparent success of the lower half depends on:
- sponsor-attached claims that become fragile at employer change, sector change, separation, migration, or provider switch,[S40][S41]
- household or relationship structures that leave one adult operationally dependent on another,[S28][S29][S30]
- single-channel administrative systems with weak fallback or transfer rails,[S40]
- or nominal person-level rights that still require repeated brokerage, passwords, approvals, paperwork, or continued cooperation from one controlling actor.

## The six main false passes

### 1. Legal portability is mistaken for real exit-compatibility

A society should count less success when a claim is technically portable but ordinary people still face enough delay, consent dependence, or continuity loss that leaving a job, provider, or household remains practically punishing.[S40][S41]

### 2. Person-level title is mistaken for operational independence

The archive should count less success when a claim is attributed to a person on paper but another actor still mediates passwords, records, transfers, timing, or practical use strongly enough that the holder cannot operate it independently.[S28][S29][S30]

### 3. Claimability is mistaken for continuity

A country should count less progress when people can initially claim a benefit or account but cannot keep it running smoothly once they change status, switch channels, or encounter conflict with the sponsoring gatekeeper.[S40]

### 4. One functioning channel is mistaken for resilient access

The archive should count less success when the claim works only so long as one office, one provider portal, one employer payroll channel, or one household intermediary continues cooperating.
Single-channel operability is weaker than it looks when fallback rails are absent.[S40]

### 5. Eventual transfer rights are mistaken for present bargaining power

A society should count less success when people may eventually recover or move the claim, but only after a delay long enough to weaken their ability to leave a bad job, unsafe relationship, or failing provider now.
A right that arrives too late still leaves the holder chokepointed.

### 6. Sponsor dependence is mistaken for ordinary security

The archive should count less success when broad ownership or benefit coverage is real only for people who can maintain stable attachment to one employer, one scheme, one household controller, or one administrative sponsor for long enough not to trigger disruption.[S28][S29][S30][S41]

## The six standing gatekeeper surfaces

### 1. Sponsor-attachment surface

The archive should ask how far the foothold remains tied to continued attachment to one employer, scheme operator, provider, tax unit, or comparable sponsor.
The more continuity depends on one relationship, the less secure the foothold is.[S40][S41]

### 2. Operational-control surface

A claim counts more when the holder can independently view balances, retrieve records, update details, nominate recipients, change channels, and authorize routine actions.
It counts less when ordinary use still depends on one intermediary, household head, or sponsor.[S28][S29][S30]

### 3. Exit-without-loss surface

The archive should ask what happens when a person leaves a job, provider, locality, relationship, or household.
A foothold counts more when ordinary exit does not trigger freezing, forfeiture, prolonged delay, or meaningful bargaining loss.[S40][S41]

### 4. Fallback-and-transfer-rail surface

A claim counts more when the system offers simple receiving accounts, backup channels, transfer defaults, interoperable records, and low-friction continuity bridges.
It counts less when failure of one gatekeeper leaves the holder with no practical fallback.[S40]

### 5. Household-and-provider-mediation surface

The archive should ask whether another adult, administrator, or service provider can still mediate the holder's practical access strongly enough to dilute independence.
This matters especially where household power asymmetries or provider lock-in are common.[S28][S29][S30]

### 6. Interruption-timeliness surface

A foothold counts more when ordinary disputes, status changes, or provider changes can be resolved quickly enough that the holder does not lose effective control in the meantime.
It counts less when even short interruptions predictably force dependence, retreat, or acceptance of bad terms.

## Fast use rule

Use this note in four steps:

1. **Ask who can stall the claim in ordinary time.**  
   Do not score a foothold fully before asking whether one ordinary actor can freeze, delay, or condition continuity.
2. **Check whether the holder can exit without begging.**  
   Ask what happens at job change, separation, provider switch, migration, or conflict.
3. **Discount single-channel continuity.**  
   Count less success when the claim works only so long as one sponsor or administrative channel keeps cooperating.
4. **Count low-gatekeeper continuity as real progress.**  
   Give strong credit when person-attached records, transfer defaults, fallback rails, and direct holder control let ordinary people keep the foothold while moving or contesting.[S28][S29][S30][S40][S41]

Default shortcut:

- **person-attached continuity plus easy transfer plus direct holder control** -> count strongly because the foothold is likely to remain usable through normal exit and change;
- **nominally owned but sponsor-dependent or single-channel claims** -> discount because one gatekeeper can still materially condition independence;
- **mixed case** -> ask whether the holder can leave a bad arrangement without waiting, bargaining down, or risking practical loss.

## Program implication

Once gatekeeper dependence is read clearly, the archive becomes stricter about one common false comfort: broadening that exists only for people who can remain obedient to the sponsor or channel that delivered it.[S28][S29][S30][S40][S41]
That changes program judgment in three directions.
First, person-attached continuity rails deserve more weight, because real freedom requires that ordinary adults keep their footholds while moving or contesting rather than only while staying put.
Second, transfer defaults, fallback channels, live support, and direct holder operability are not administrative niceties but part of whether broadening is real.
Third, measurement should stop treating enrolment, legal vesting, or nominal ownership counts as morally complete when one ordinary gatekeeper can still switch the claim off in practice.

This is why gatekeeper dependence belongs inside the archive's constitutional reading of distribution rather than as a small implementation footnote.
A society is closer to justice when ordinary adults can leave bad jobs, bad providers, and bad household arrangements **without leaving their footholds behind**.[S28][S29][S30][S40][S41]

## Bottom line

The archive should now read gatekeeper dependence in this order:

1. **Ask whether one ordinary actor can still stall, freeze, or condition the foothold.**
2. **Discount claims that remain too tied to one sponsor, provider, household controller, or administrative channel.**
3. **Treat exit-punishing continuity designs as a real constitutional failure.**
4. **Count person-attached continuity, fallback rails, and direct holder control as real equality gains.**
5. **Refuse to let nominal ownership or enrolment count like secure independence when one gatekeeper can still make the holder wait or stay put.**
