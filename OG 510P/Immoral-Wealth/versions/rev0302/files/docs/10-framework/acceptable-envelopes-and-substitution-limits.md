# Acceptable envelopes and substitution limits

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's core target-side judgments into a **stable acceptable-envelope rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the fuller target procedure in [`target-selection-rules.md`](target-selection-rules.md):
**when should a wealth distribution near the archive's preferred center still count as acceptable, and when should that claim fail because the apparent deviation is being bought with bad substitutions or hidden veto breaches?**

The archive keeps **20 / 50 / 20 / 10** as its central attractor.
But it does **not** treat one exact percentile tuple as the only morally admissible destination.
Different societies can need somewhat different mixes of private lower-half wealth, middle thickness, public/social wealth, secure tenure, and pooled capital depending on how much the common floor really lowers exposure and how much top-tail rule is actually contained.[S02][S13][S18][S19][S21][S22][S26]

At the same time, the archive rejects an equal and opposite mistake:
turning that limited flexibility into an all-purpose excuse.
A society does **not** become near-ideal just because one share vector looks numerically close while the bottom half remains near-assetless, the top tail still buys rule, footholds still arrive too late, family background still governs adult entry, or apparently broad ownership is still fragile, locked up, or manager-bottlenecked.[S05][S13][S16][S18][S19][S23][S26]

So this note keeps the envelope question **narrow but constitutional**.
It explains what kinds of deviation from the center can still count, what can vary across that family, what definitely cannot substitute for what, and how to sort real near-passes from flattering corridor talk.

## Minimum reading rule

Read envelope flexibility as **narrow and conditional**, not as a free-floating permission slip.
A lower private bottom-half share can count as near-acceptable only when common provision, secure tenure, and public/social wealth really reduce household exposure.
A higher private bottom-half share can count as near-acceptable only when it reflects real footholds rather than leveraged owner counts, incumbent revaluation, thin-liquidity home equity, or fragile paper balances.[S07][S13][S16][S21][S22]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**the target is a small constitutional family, not one sacred point but also not an all-purpose corridor.**

This note therefore downgrades or rejects near-target claims when they rely on some combination of:
- common wealth with too little person-level floor,
- housing wealth standing in for liquidity and debt quality,
- late inheritance standing in for early adult entry,
- pooled ownership standing in for democratic stewardship,
- household totals standing in for person-level control,
- or flattering share proximity while top-tail rule or dynastic closure remains active.[S05][S13][S18][S19][S23][S26]

## The three working acceptable envelopes

These are **not** three unrelated ideals.
They are three nearby ways a society might remain broadly free, broadly owning, and non-oligarchic while differing somewhat in how much common insurance, public/social wealth, private lower-half wealth, and middle thickness do the work.

### 1. Common-wealth-heavy envelope — roughly **15 / 55 / 20 / 10**

This can count as near-acceptable only when public wealth is clearly positive and institutionally usable,[S02] the common floor is strong enough to reduce required private self-insurance,[S21][S22] secure tenure is real where owner-occupation is not,[S07] early adulthood still receives real footholds rather than mostly later parental transfer,[S18][S19] and top-tail money-into-rule channels remain tightly constrained.[S15][S23]

This is **not** permission for a near-assetless bottom half.
It is acceptable only when common wealth and common protection are doing real anti-domination work rather than merely coexisting with private fragility.

### 2. Balanced broad-owner envelope — roughly **20 / 50 / 20 / 10**

This remains the archive's **default attractor**.
It is still the clearest all-purpose target for rich and upper-middle-income societies that need both visible lower-half thickening and a large plural middle without pretending that exact equality is the aim.

The archive should start here unless there is a positive reason to move off-center.
This envelope is the easiest one to defend when the evidence is mixed, because it asks less substitutional ingenuity than the other two and therefore leaves fewer places to hide a false pass.

### 3. Bottom-thick dispersed-owner envelope — roughly **25 / 45 / 20 / 10**

This can count as near-acceptable only when the extra lower-half share reflects **real footholds**: liquid buffers, safer debt structures, secure housing positions, direct long-run claims, wider access to productive capital, and lower exposure to quiet clawback through fees, leverage, or locked stewardship.[S13][S16][S17][S24][S25][S26]

A somewhat thinner middle can be tolerated here only if the middle still remains large enough to function as a plural civic majority rather than a squeezed belt between a large lower half and a re-powering elite top.
The archive should be especially alert here to fake broadening driven by home-price inflation, subsidised leverage, or narrow incumbent gains that temporarily flatter the lower half without truly widening durable control.[S13][S16][S36]

## The six main false near-passes

### 1. Numerical closeness is mistaken for constitutional success

A society should not get near-acceptable treatment simply because one share vector sits near the preferred center if floor failure, dynastic closure, or money-into-rule conversion is still severe.[S05][S16][S18][S19][S23]

### 2. Public/social wealth is mistaken for permission to leave the bottom half near-assetless

The archive should count less progress when common wealth exists on paper but ordinary households still need unusually strong family backing, high private buffers, or lucky housing timing to reach stable adulthood.[S02][S18][S19][S21][S22]

### 3. Housing wealth is mistaken for a thicker lower-half floor

Apparent broadening counts less when the lower half looks better mainly because owner counts, home values, or nominal equity rose while liquidity, debt quality, carrying costs, and entrant access remained weak.[S07][S13][S16][S24][S25][S36]

### 4. Late family transfer is mistaken for circulation

A country should not count as near-ideal if apparently softer concentration still relies heavily on parental deposits, inherited housing position, or transfers arriving after early adulthood has already been sorted.[S05][S18][S19]

### 5. Pooled capital is mistaken for democratised control

Broad fund ownership should count less when engagement, voting, and stewardship remain bottlenecked inside a narrow intermediary layer that ordinary asset holders do not meaningfully govern.[S26]

### 6. Middle thickness is mistaken for safety from oligarchy

A large middle does not rescue the case if the top tail still controls politics, media, procurement, rescue design, agenda-setting, or major chokepoints of valuation and information.[S15][S23]

## The six standing substitution surfaces

### 1. Common floor versus required private wealth

The archive should ask how much strong health protection, pensions, care support, and income insurance actually reduce the amount of private wealth ordinary households need just to remain safe.[S21][S22]

### 2. Secure tenure versus owner-occupation

Housing security can partly travel through durable tenancy, social housing, cooperative tenure, or other protected use rights when households still have stable, affordable, non-arbitrary control over their homes.[S07]

### 3. Public/social wealth versus private household balances

Public wealth matters more when it is **usable**, politically defended, and actually routed into early-life opportunity, common security, or shared capital access rather than being treated as a decorative macro balance-sheet fact.[S02][S18][S19]

### 4. Direct ownership versus pooled capital claims

Some pooling is compatible with justice only when stewardship, voting, transparency, and claim portability keep the capital meaningfully legible and governable from below rather than merely expanding passive exposure.[S26]

### 5. Lower-half thickness versus middle resilience

The archive can tolerate some movement between bottom-half and middle shares only if both remain strong enough that neither ordinary entry nor plural non-elite civic power is being quietly hollowed out.
That means a thicker lower half should not come from destroying the middle, and a thicker middle should not come from leaving the bottom half stuck below a real floor.

### 6. Cross-country path differences versus universal vetoes

Different starting states can justify somewhat different envelope positions, but they do **not** suspend the archive's veto families.
Variation in country path, tenure regime, pension architecture, or public-wealth structure never cancels the requirements against bottom-half fragility, dynastic closure, top-tail rule, opacity, or rapid reconcentration.[S02][S05][S18][S19][S23]

## Fast use rule

Use this note in five steps:

1. **Start from the default attractor.**  
   Begin with **20 / 50 / 20 / 10** rather than searching first for clever exceptions.
2. **Ask why the case belongs off-center.**  
   Movement toward **15 / 55 / 20 / 10** needs an unusually strong common-floor and public/social-wealth story. Movement toward **25 / 45 / 20 / 10** needs unusually real entrant-friendly lower-half thickening.
3. **Screen for forbidden substitutions.**  
   Reject the case if apparently softer shares are being bought with late inheritance, debt fragility, household masking, stewardship bottlenecks, or top-tail rule.
4. **Stop when a veto family is still live.**  
   If floor failure, strong opacity, severe timing failure, or money-into-rule conversion remains active, nearby numerical envelopes are not yet morally interesting.
5. **Escalate when the case is flattering or close.**  
   Use [`target-selection-rules.md`](target-selection-rules.md) and [`../20-program/measurement-and-scoreboard.md`](../20-program/measurement-and-scoreboard.md) when the comparison depends on many companion judgments.

Default shortcut:

- **common wealth genuinely lowering required private exposure** -> treat some movement toward **15 / 55 / 20 / 10** as potentially real;
- **thicker lower-half wealth that is early, durable, liquid enough, and entrant-friendly** -> treat some movement toward **25 / 45 / 20 / 10** as potentially real;
- **nice-looking shares with active veto breaches or bad substitutions** -> refuse the near-pass and read the case through the companion rule stack instead.

## Program implication

Once this note is read clearly, two policy mistakes become easier to avoid.
First, the archive should stop treating every society that sits somewhere near the central share vector as morally similar.
Second, it should stop treating every departure from the center as equally suspicious.
Some deviations reflect real public counterweight or unusually strong lower-half footholds; others are just flattering substitutions hiding floor failure, dynastic closure, or control bottlenecks.

That matters for the program stack.
A country should not be told to chase one frozen tuple mechanically.
It should be told to move toward the nearest **real** acceptable envelope by strengthening the bottom-half floor, lowering required self-insurance, widening early footholds, protecting plural middle ownership, and containing wealth-to-rule conversion at the top.
The point is not numerical choreography.
The point is to build a society that has genuinely earned any deviation it claims from the default center.

## Bottom line

The archive should now read acceptable envelopes in this order:

1. **Start from 20 / 50 / 20 / 10 as the default attractor, not as a sacred point but as the cleanest center.**
2. **Allow only a small family of nearby envelopes rather than an open corridor.**
3. **Ask what real institutional strength justifies any movement off-center.**
4. **Refuse substitutions that hide floor weakness, late entry, fragile housing wealth, person-level masking, stewardship bottlenecks, or top-tail rule.**
5. **Treat near-target status as something a society must earn constitutionally, not something it gets for free from approximate arithmetic alone.**
