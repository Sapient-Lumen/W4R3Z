# Immoral-Wealth

A compact research archive for thinking about **immoral wealth inequality**: what it is, what an acceptable wealth distribution would look like, and what policy mix can move a society toward it without collapsing productive capacity or democratic legitimacy.

## Core judgment in one paragraph

This archive takes the view that wealth inequality becomes **immoral** when it crosses from difference into **domination and closure**: when the bottom half lacks durable asset security; when the top tail can routinely purchase political influence and shape rulemaking; when inheritance matters more than contribution; when housing, land, monopoly, and financial position function as extraction machines; when public institutions are too asset-poor to counter private concentrations; when key assets remain under oligarchic control; when birth background hardens adult life chances so strongly that society starts behaving like a softened caste order rather than an open democracy; when the flow of gains keeps pulling wealth back upward even after reform; when households must privately self-insure against basic risks that a decent society should partly socialise; when major ownership and transfer channels remain too opaque to measure, tax, or govern credibly; when nominal ownership is mostly a thin equity slice sitting on destabilising leverage rather than a durable claim; when households hold capital claims while the votes and stewardship remain bottlenecked inside a narrow intermediary layer; when secure ownership arrives too late in life to shape adulthood for people without wealthy parents; when historically subordinated groups remain structurally locked out of wealth-building even if national or household averages improve; and when apparently broad wealth growth is driven mainly by scarcity rents, markups, fees, or incumbent asset revaluation rather than open and durable access, and when large owners can repeatedly privatise upside while major downside is shifted onto taxpayers or the public balance sheet, and when value created by infrastructure, rezoning, public land strategy, or other collective action is allowed to harden into private windfalls with too little return to common wealth and wider access, and when the state quietly subsidises asset accumulation through open-ended tax expenditures or poorly targeted wealth-building support that disproportionately rewards already-positioned owners.[S10][S13][S16][S20][S31][S32][S33][S34][S35][S36][S37]

## Current working target

- **Bottom 50%:** target **20%** of private household wealth (acceptable long-run band: **15-25%**).
- **Middle 40%:** target **50%** (band: **45-55%**).
- **Next 9%:** target **20%** (band: **15-25%**).
- **Top 1%:** target **10%** (band: **8-15%**).
- **Top 0.1%:** target **3% or less** inside the top-1 slice.
- **Public wealth:** target **clearly positive** net public wealth and a growing public/social wealth pool, rather than the negative or near-zero public balance sheets now common in rich regions.[S02]
- **Bottom-half asset floor:** the bottom half should not merely own a slice on paper; most households should have positive net wealth, a liquid shock buffer, secure housing tenure, and a durable long-run asset claim.[S07][S16][S17]
- **Intergenerational circulation:** parental wealth, education, and homeownership should not dominate adult asset position; a morally acceptable society must leave genuine routes into ownership and security for people without wealthy parents.[S18][S19]
- **Reconcentration pressure:** a society does not count as acceptable if broad-based gains are routinely clawed back through rents, markups, asset-price inflation without entry, tax asymmetry, or concentrated ownership of the main compounding channels.[S06][S10][S20]
- **Social-floor condition:** the private target must be read together with healthcare, pensions, housing security, education, care, and other public/social protections. Weak public protection may force households to hold more precautionary private wealth just to remain secure; the archive should not mistake that forced self-insurance for freedom.[S21][S22]
- **Legibility precondition:** a society cannot credibly claim a decent distribution if major ownership and transfer channels are too opaque to measure, tax, or police. Beneficial ownership, land, firm, trust, procurement, and political-finance visibility are part of making the target governable.[S04][S23]
- **Encumbrance condition:** broad household wealth should be durable enough that ownership is not mostly a thin equity slice resting on heavy debt-service exposure, fragile collateral values, or routine refinancing dependence.[S13][S17][S24][S25]
- **Stewardship condition:** broader capital claims should not be mistaken for broad control when votes and engagement over listed firms are pooled inside a narrow asset-manager layer rather than shared with asset owners, members, or public-interest institutions.[S20][S26]
- **Non-extractive formation condition:** a society should not count broad wealth gains as fully acceptable when they come mainly from scarcity rents, monopoly markups, fee extraction, or incumbent asset revaluation that makes entry harder for the next cohort, rather than from better earnings, saving, productive investment, social inheritance, and open access.[S07][S10][S13][S16][S20]
- **Public-value-capture condition:** windfalls created by infrastructure, rezoning, development permissions, or strategic public-land decisions should flow partly back into public/social wealth, affordable access, or common infrastructure rather than capitalising almost entirely into private fortunes.[S34][S35]
- **Life-course timing condition:** durable ownership and meaningful wealth transfers should arrive early enough to shape adult independence, rather than mainly after long rent, debt, and waiting periods or only through late inheritance.[S05][S13][S19]
- **Person-level ownership condition:** a society should not be treated as acceptable if wealth looks broad only at the **household** level while title, liquid control, pension rights, or exit security remain badly skewed across adults within households.[S13][S27][S28][S29][S30]
- **Group-stratification condition:** a society should not be treated as acceptable if wealth broadens in the aggregate while historically subordinated groups remain systematically excluded from durable ownership, secure tenure, safe credit, or asset-building pathways.[S13][S19]
- **Downside-discipline condition:** a society should not count broad ownership as fully acceptable if concentrated owners or major intermediaries can repeatedly privatise upside while major downside is shifted onto taxpayers or the public balance sheet; unavoidable public support should be last-resort, targeted, conditional, and recoverable.[S32][S33]

rev0018 kept the headline bands stable and the five-axis doctrine intact while adding a compact **public-value-capture condition**. rev0019 stays off the main-axis count and instead adds a compact **tax-expenditure / subsidy-hygiene discipline**: the archive should now ask not only what the visible tax-and-transfer system does, but whether hidden spending through housing and retirement preferences is quietly rebuilding incumbent wealth or capitalising into prices rather than widening entry.[S31][S36][S37]

The archive therefore still turns on:

1. aggregate percentile shares,
2. a bottom-half **minimum asset floor**,
3. dispersed control over key assets,
4. real **intergenerational circulation** rather than dynastic closure,
5. low **reconcentration pressure** so reforms hold rather than wash out.

Those five axes now have to be read together with ten cross-cutting requirements: **broad private ownership inside a strong social floor**, **ownership visible enough to govern**, **ownership unencumbered enough to survive ordinary shocks without creditor domination**, **publicly created value recaptured enough that public action is not quietly feeding private windfalls**, **stewardship broad enough that asset-manager intermediation does not quietly recreate concentrated control**, **timing early enough that ordinary adults do not have to wait until late life for a first durable foothold**, **person-level rights strong enough that household-level broadening is not masking internal dependence**, **group-level openness strong enough that historically subordinated groups are not still being sorted away from wealth-building channels**, **wealth formation clean enough that broad gains are not mainly being manufactured through rents, bottlenecks, and entrant exclusion**, and **loss-bearing fair enough that the public is not repeatedly forced to absorb the downside of concentrated private risk-taking**.
A society does **not** count as morally acceptable if it improves only one or two of those axes while leaving the others to fail, if it improves them only temporarily before wealth rapidly reconcentrates, if households still need unusually large private buffers just to survive healthcare, housing, education, care, or old-age risks, if the top tail remains too illegible for the public authority to measure, tax, and restrain it credibly, if broad household ownership rests mainly on fragile leverage rather than durable claims, if mass savers own financial claims while a narrow manager layer still speaks and votes for most of the economy, if ownership and security arrive too late to shape adulthood for households without wealthy parents, or if household-level wealth broadens while many adults still lack direct title, direct liquid control, or credible exit security, or if group-based exclusion remains entrenched behind improved national averages, or if broad gains are coming mainly through scarcity rents, monopoly markups, fee extraction, or incumbent revaluation that worsens entry for later households, or if concentrated owners can repeatedly keep the upside while large downside is transferred onto taxpayers or public balance sheets, or if infrastructure, rezoning, and public-land decisions keep creating large private windfalls with weak common recapture.[S04][S05][S06][S07][S10][S13][S15][S16][S17][S18][S19][S20][S21][S22][S23][S24][S25][S26][S27][S28][S29][S30][S32][S33][S34][S35]

## What is in this revision

This revision sharpens the archive in three places:

- a new **publicly created value and common-asset capture** framework note so the cube can distinguish broad wealth that widens through common recapture from wealth that is quietly capitalised out of public action,
- a tighter scoreboard and doctrine surface for asking whether infrastructure, rezoning, development permissions, and public-land strategy are strengthening public/social wealth and affordable access,
- a small cleanup pass across the top-level surfaces so the archive's running summary stays compact and current.[S34][S35]

The empirical emphasis stays compact: OECD's land-value-capture compendium says governments can capture land-value uplifts generated by public interventions such as infrastructure investment or land-use change, and OECD's 2025 housing-reform work says affordable-housing requirements can be based on increases in land value from development approvals and that public land can be managed strategically to recover value created by public investment or zoning changes.[S34][S35]
