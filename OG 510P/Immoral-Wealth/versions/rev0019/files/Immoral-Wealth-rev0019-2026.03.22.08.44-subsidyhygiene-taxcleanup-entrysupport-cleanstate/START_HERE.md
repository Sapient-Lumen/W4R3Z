# START HERE

This archive is trying to answer two questions at once:

1. **What wealth distribution would count as morally acceptable in an advanced society?**
2. **What policy program can get us there without magical thinking?**

## The key move

The archive's main move is to stop treating wealth inequality as a one-instrument tax problem.
The working view here is that immoral concentration is produced by **six interacting systems**:

- labor market power,
- unequal capital ownership,
- inheritance,
- land/housing rents,
- weak public balance sheets,
- conversion of wealth into political rule.

It matters not only how much wealth each percentile owns, not only who controls the key assets, not only whether ordinary households can survive shocks, and not only whether people without wealthy parents can still enter ownership. It also matters whether that entry happens **early enough**, whether a better distribution can **hold** once rents, markups, tax arbitrage, and asset-price cycles resume, whether households still need large private balances just to survive risks that strong public institutions could partly absorb, whether broad household ownership is thick enough to survive ordinary debt-service and collateral shocks, whether household-level broadening still leaves one adult inside many households without direct title, liquid control, pension rights, or exit security, and whether historically subordinated groups are still being sorted away from credit, secure tenure, entrepreneurship, inheritances, or high-opportunity places, and whether apparently broad gains are coming mainly through scarcity rents, monopoly markups, fee extraction, or incumbent asset revaluation that worsens entry for the next cohort, and whether concentrated owners still keep the upside while major downside is shifted onto taxpayers or the public balance sheet, and whether infrastructure, rezoning, and public-land decisions keep producing large private windfalls with weak common recapture, and whether hidden spending through the tax code is still disproportionately rewarding existing owners or higher-balance savers instead of widening entry.[S05][S06][S07][S10][S13][S17][S18][S19][S20][S21][S22][S24][S25][S27][S28][S29][S30][S31][S34][S35][S36][S37]

## The current answer in one sentence

The archive's current best judgment is that a morally acceptable rich society would combine:

- a **20 / 50 / 20 / 10** private-wealth distribution across bottom 50 / middle 40 / next 9 / top 1,
- **clearly positive public wealth**,
- a real **bottom-half asset floor**,
- enough **intergenerational circulation** that parental wealth and homeownership do not quietly determine adult outcomes,
- low enough **reconcentration pressure** that those gains survive a normal cycle of rents, markups, and asset booms,
- and a strong enough **social floor** that households do not need oversized private war chests just to manage healthcare, housing, care, education, or old-age risk,
- read together with **visible-wealth**, **encumbrance**, **public-value-capture**, **stewardship**, **timing**, **person-level ownership**, **group-stratification**, **non-extractive formation**, **downside-discipline**, and **subsidy-hygiene** disciplines so acceptable ownership is governable, not mostly debt-fragile, not quietly fed by public action without common return, not merely broad in cash-flow terms while governance stays bottlenecked, not delivered only after the main adult life-course decisions are already constrained, not counted as broad when title and exit security remain too skewed within households, not flattered by national averages while excluded groups remain asset-poor, not praised when broad wealth growth is being manufactured mainly through scarcity rents and bottleneck power, not counted as secure broad ownership when major private downside is repeatedly socialised, and not quietly rebuilt through open-ended asset subsidies that reward incumbents more than entrants.[S02][S05][S06][S07][S10][S13][S18][S19][S20][S21][S22][S23][S24][S25][S26][S27][S28][S29][S30][S31][S32][S33][S34][S35][S36][S37]

## What this implies programmatically

The archive does **not** think one tax can solve the problem.
It points instead toward a bundle:

- labor-market reforms that raise bargaining power,
- stronger taxation of capital gains, inheritances, and monopoly/rent channels,
- housing and land reforms that compress rent extraction and reopen entry,
- direct broadening of capital ownership through social inheritance and public wealth,
- liquid-buffer and debt-pressure reduction so gains can survive ordinary shocks,
- direct firewalls against policy capture and wealth-backed rule,
- and a budgeting discipline that tests major packages early enough to catch who gains, who remains excluded, which measures will wash out into rents, prices, debt pressure, or public loss absorption, and which tax expenditures are acting as upside-down subsidies that reward incumbents more than entrants.[S31][S32][S33][S36][S37]

## What this revision does not claim

- It does **not** claim there is one uniquely correct numerical distribution.
- It does **not** claim recurrent net wealth taxes are always the first-best tool.
- It does **not** claim income redistribution alone is enough.
- It does **not** claim high growth automatically cures inequality.
- It does **not** claim anti-capture reforms can wait until after distributional reforms have already succeeded.
- It does **not** claim aggregate percentile shares are sufficient by themselves.
- It does **not** claim a paper wealth gain counts as success if households remain financially fragile.[S17]
- It does **not** claim a better snapshot counts as success if advantage remains strongly inherited.[S18][S19]
- It does **not** claim a better snapshot counts as success if the next normal cycle quickly reconcentrates wealth upward.[S06][S10][S20]
- It does **not** claim a better private balance-sheet picture counts as success if basic security remains too privatized.[S21][S22]
- It does **not** claim a better net-wealth snapshot counts as success if broad ownership is mostly a thin equity slice resting on heavy debt-service exposure.[S13][S17][S24][S25]
- It does **not** claim broader fund or pension holdings count as full democratic ownership if stewardship and votes remain bottlenecked inside a narrow intermediary layer.[S20][S26]
- It does **not** claim that a better late-life wealth picture counts as success if secure ownership still arrives too late for non-rich adults.[S05][S13][S19]
- It does **not** claim that a better household balance-sheet picture counts as success if one adult inside many households still lacks direct title, direct liquid control, pension rights, or credible exit security.[S13][S27][S28][S29][S30]
- It does **not** claim that a better national wealth picture counts as success if historically subordinated groups remain systematically excluded from durable asset-building channels.[S13][S19]
- It does **not** claim that a pro-ownership subsidy counts as success if most of its value accrues to existing owners, higher-balance savers, or land prices rather than to wider entry and durable floor-building.[S31][S36][S37]
