# Minimum asset floor

## Thesis

A morally acceptable wealth distribution must give ordinary households not just **some share** of national wealth, but a real **asset floor**.

That floor is what keeps the bottom half from living one shock, one landlord decision, one illness, or one job loss away from renewed dependency.

## Why the archive adds this layer

WIR 2026 shows that the bottom 50% holds only **1% to 5%** of wealth across world regions.[S02]
OECD's 2025 distributional-wealth paper says the bottom quintile often has **negative wealth**, wealth inequality is driven by financial assets, and housing is the largest household asset.[S16]
OECD's policy work on economic insecurity also underlines that liquid buffers matter: around 2017, **50%** of people in middle-income households in OECD EU countries were financially fragile, with insufficient liquid assets to stay above the poverty line for at least three months after a sudden income loss.[S17]

Those facts make one thing clear: a better percentile share is not enough if households remain too fragile to survive ordinary shocks.

## The floor bundle

The archive's current best judgment is that a morally acceptable society should aim for most bottom-half households to clear a compact **five-part floor**:

1. **Positive net wealth**
   - Persistent negative net wealth should be unusual, not normal.
2. **Liquid shock buffer**
   - Households should have enough liquid resources to absorb a short income interruption without immediate debt distress or eviction pressure.[S17]
3. **Secure housing position**
   - Secure tenancy or ownership should be normal, and severe housing-cost overburden should not be routine.[S07]
4. **Durable long-run asset claim**
   - Retirement savings, cooperative equity, social inheritance accounts, or other lasting claims should complement liquid buffers and housing.
5. **Non-predatory debt position**
   - Debt should not routinely wipe out the gains from asset ownership or lock households into chronic extraction.

This is not a demand that every household hold the same bundle in the same proportions.
It is a demand that the bottom half not be left **wealth-poor in function**, even if the national accounts say its share has improved.

## What does not count as success

The archive should treat the following as weak or false progress:

- a rising bottom-half wealth share driven mainly by temporary asset-price inflation,
- retirement claims that look large on paper while households lack liquid buffers,
- owner-occupation achieved through fragile leverage and crushing housing costs,
- social grants that reduce hardship but leave families without any durable asset foothold.

## How this changes the archive's target

rev0006 therefore treats the target as explicitly four-axis, with the floor as one essential axis:

1. **aggregate shares** — the 20 / 50 / 20 / 10 working target,
2. **asset floor** — whether ordinary households clear the floor bundle,
3. **control** — whether key assets remain too concentrated to reproduce dependence and rule,
4. **circulation** — whether adult asset position remains too strongly inherited.

A country does not pass because it looks better on only one axis.

## How to use this in practice

Use the floor bundle as a constitutional test when evaluating reforms:

- Did the reform leave more households with positive net wealth?
- Did it raise liquid resilience or only long-lock-up wealth?
- Did it reduce rent and debt pressure enough for gains to stick?
- Did it produce a durable asset claim that can survive ordinary shocks?
