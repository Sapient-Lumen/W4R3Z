# Ideal wealth distribution bands

## Bottom line

This archive's current best judgment is that a morally acceptable advanced society should aim for a **broad-middle private wealth distribution**, **positive public wealth**, a real **bottom-half asset floor**, enough **intergenerational circulation** that asset security is not mostly inherited, a person-level ownership pattern that does not leave one adult inside many households weakly titled or trapped, a group-level openness pattern that does not leave historically subordinated groups structurally excluded from the main channels of asset building, and a strong enough **social floor** that households do not need oversized private balances just to stay secure, and a sufficiently **non-extractive formation pattern** that broad wealth is not being built mainly through scarcity rents and entrant exclusion, and a sufficiently **disciplined loss-bearing pattern** that concentrated owners cannot repeatedly keep the upside while the public absorbs major downside.

## Private household target

- **Bottom 50%:** target **20%** of private household wealth (acceptable long-run band: **15-25%**).
- **Middle 40%:** target **50%** (band: **45-55%**).
- **Next 9%:** target **20%** (band: **15-25%**).
- **Top 1%:** target **10%** (band: **8-15%**).
- **Top 0.1%:** target **3% or less** inside the top-1 slice.
- **Public wealth:** target **clearly positive** net public wealth and a growing public/social wealth pool, rather than the negative or near-zero public balance sheets now common in rich regions.[S02]

## How to read these bands

These are **working constitutional bands**, not a claim that one exact ratio is morally mandatory in every country. The archive now treats them as one axis of a broader decision device:

1. **shares** — how much each broad slice owns,
2. **floor** — whether ordinary households have resilient assets,
3. **control** — whether the key asset classes remain oligarchic, including when voting power over pooled household capital is bottlenecked in a narrow manager layer,
4. **circulation** — whether adult asset position remains too strongly inherited,
5. **reconcentration pressure** — whether gains are likely to hold through an ordinary cycle.

These axes are now read together with a cross-cutting **social-floor condition**: the same private shares mean different things under strong versus weak public/social protection.[S21][S22]
rev0013 adds another cross-cutting **person-level ownership condition**: the same household shares mean different things depending on whether adults inside households have direct title, direct liquid control, pension rights, and fair exit protection.[S13][S27][S28][S29][S30]
rev0014 adds another cross-cutting **group-stratification condition**: the same national shares mean different things depending on whether historically subordinated groups are actually reaching the main channels of durable asset building.[S13][S19]
rev0016 adds a cross-cutting **non-extractive formation condition**: the same shares mean different things depending on whether broad gains come through open access and productive renewal or through scarcity rents, markups, fees, and incumbent revaluation that worsen entry.[S07][S10][S13][S16][S20]
rev0017 adds a cross-cutting **downside-discipline condition**: the same shares mean different things depending on whether concentrated owners also face meaningful downside, or instead rely on repeated public backstops, untargeted asset support, or taxpayer absorption of major losses.[S32][S33]

For the selection logic behind the bands, see [`target-selection-rules.md`](target-selection-rules.md).
For the floor doctrine, see [`minimum-asset-floor.md`](minimum-asset-floor.md).
For the conditioning rule, see [`social-floor-and-private-wealth.md`](social-floor-and-private-wealth.md).

## Why these bands

These bands are designed to satisfy six conditions at once:

1. **Real security at the bottom.** The bottom half must own enough to withstand shocks, form households, move jobs, and participate in civic life.
2. **A thick middle.** The middle 40% should own roughly half of private wealth so society is not split between renters/debtors and a thin owner class.
3. **A non-ruling top tail.** The top 1% can remain richer than average, but not rich enough to function as a de facto governing estate.
4. **Positive collective capacity.** Public wealth must be positive and purposeful so universal services and social inheritance are not financed entirely against a weak public balance sheet.[S02]
5. **Open generational turnover.** The next cohort must have realistic routes into ownership without depending mainly on rich parents or late bequests, and those routes must arrive early enough to shape adulthood rather than only late in life.[S05][S18][S19]
6. **Political plausibility.** The target must be far more equal than current regimes, but not so flattened that the program becomes administratively or politically unserious.
7. **Clean formation.** Broad gains should come from more open and durable access to saving, housing, enterprise, and productive capital, not mainly from revaluation that enriches incumbents while tightening the bottlenecks facing later entrants.[S07][S10][S13][S20]

## Floor override

rev0005 adds a stricter interpretive rule alongside the topology override: **good shares are not enough if the bottom half still lacks a real asset floor**.

OECD's 2025 distributional-wealth paper reports that the bottom quintile often has negative wealth and that housing is the largest household asset.[S16] OECD policy work on economic insecurity also reports that, around 2017, half of people in middle-income households in OECD EU countries lacked sufficient liquid assets to stay above the poverty line for at least three months after a sudden income loss.[S17]

So the archive now reads the bands with two extra questions attached:

- do bottom-half households have positive and resilient net wealth?
- do they have a liquid buffer and secure housing position, not only long-lock-up claims?

A society that reached 18 / 50 / 21 / 11 but left much of the bottom half financially fragile would still fail the archive's moral standard. See [`minimum-asset-floor.md`](minimum-asset-floor.md).

## Topology override

rev0004 added a second interpretive rule: **good shares are not enough if ownership topology is still oligarchic**.

OECD's 2025 distributional-wealth paper reports that wealth is concentrated in the top quintile, the bottom quintile often has negative wealth, inequality is driven by financial assets, and housing is the largest household asset.[S16]

So the archive also asks:

- what kinds of assets sit in the bottom-half share?
- how diversified is the middle?
- how much control over firms, land, finance, infrastructure, or media remains in the top tail?

A society that reached 18 / 50 / 21 / 11 but left the top with concentrated control over key assets would still fail the archive's moral standard. See [`asset-composition-and-control.md`](asset-composition-and-control.md).

## Stewardship override

rev0011 adds one more control refinement: **good shares are not enough if broad ownership is mostly silent while stewardship remains concentrated**.

OECD's 2025 stewardship report says that, as of 2024, asset managers held **65%** of listed equity in the United States and **59%** in the United Kingdom, and that the largest 20 asset managers held **USD 56 trillion**, or **38% of global assets under management**.[S26]

So the archive also asks:

- who actually votes the shares attached to pooled household wealth?
- can asset owners, members, or beneficiaries direct those votes in meaningful ways?
- does broader capital ownership disperse governance, or only disperse cash-flow exposure?

A society that reached 18 / 50 / 21 / 11 but left most corporate stewardship pooled in a narrow manager layer would still count as only partially successful. See [`intermediated-ownership-and-stewardship.md`](intermediated-ownership-and-stewardship.md).

## Circulation override

rev0006 adds a third interpretive rule: **good shares are not enough if the distribution is too inherited**.

OECD's 2025 opportunity work reports that inherited circumstances explain at least **over 25%** of inequality in household market income across the OECD, with parental background contributing **over 60%** of inequality of opportunity in most countries.[S18] Companion OECD analysis shows that the parental homeownership advantage has strengthened in many OECD European countries.[S19]

So the archive also asks:

- can people without affluent parents still enter ownership and durable security?
- are housing access and first-asset acquisition becoming more or less inherited?
- do younger cohorts gain assets through their own pathway, or mainly by waiting for family transfers?

A society that reached 18 / 50 / 21 / 11 but left ownership entry strongly dependent on parental status would still fail the archive's moral standard. See [`intergenerational-circulation.md`](intergenerational-circulation.md).

## Timing override

rev0012 adds one more refinement inside circulation: **good shares are not enough if secure ownership arrives too late in the life course**.

OECD's 2025 opportunity work says wealth transfers are associated with a **larger wealth premium when they are received earlier in life**.[S19] OECD's inheritance-tax report also says wealth is expected to remain concentrated among older cohorts for longer and that the age at which people inherit will likely continue to rise.[S05]

So the archive also asks:

- do non-rich households reach a first durable foothold before typical inheritance age?
- is late inheritance being treated as a substitute for early adulthood security?
- do policy gains widen ownership across cohorts, or mostly protect incumbent older owners?

A society that reached 18 / 50 / 21 / 11 but left secure ownership arriving mainly too late for younger adults would still count as only partially successful. See [`life-course-timing-and-early-footholds.md`](life-course-timing-and-early-footholds.md).

rev0013 adds one more conditioning rule: **good household shares are not enough if person-level claims remain badly skewed inside households**.

OECD and World Bank evidence warns that household-level aggregation can flatter the distribution when gendered person-level ownership, account access, property rights, and pension entitlements remain much more unequal.[S13][S27][S28][S29][S30]

Questions to ask alongside the headline bands:

- how often do adults hold direct title or co-title to primary residences and other major assets?
- do social inheritance, pensions, and savings architecture create direct person-level claims rather than only household-level pooling?
- can lower-earning or care-burdened adults exit relationships without falling into sharp asset insecurity?

A society that reached 18 / 50 / 21 / 11 but left major person-level ownership and exit security badly skewed would still count as only partially successful. See [`individual-ownership-and-intra-household-power.md`](individual-ownership-and-intra-household-power.md).

rev0014 adds one more conditioning rule: **good national shares are not enough if group-based exclusion remains entrenched**.

OECD's recent wealth work says wealth gaps remain significant across migrant status and geographic location and warns that most countries still lack the equality data needed to see many wealth barriers by race/ethnicity, sexual orientation and gender identity, and disability clearly.[S13] OECD's opportunity work adds that financial-inclusion and asset-building policies can help support historically disadvantaged groups, especially where general systems miss them.[S19]

The archive should therefore ask:

- are gains in wealth, secure tenure, and business ownership reaching groups that have historically faced exclusion?
- are high-opportunity places, mortgage markets, and entrepreneurship channels opening across groups or only within already-advantaged populations?
- is the state measuring these gaps well enough to know?

A society that reached 18 / 50 / 21 / 11 but left durable ownership structurally closed across historically subordinated groups would still count as only partially successful. See [`group-stratification-and-excluded-groups.md`](group-stratification-and-excluded-groups.md).

## Non-extractive formation interpretation

rev0016 adds one more rule: **good shares are not enough if broad wealth is being built mainly through scarcity rents, markups, fees, or incumbent asset revaluation**.

OECD's 2025 distributional-wealth paper says inequality is driven by financial assets and moderated by housing wealth, while housing remains the largest household asset.[S16] OECD's 2025 economic-outlook work adds that rising market concentration, higher markups, and investment increasingly concentrated in a small group of highly profitable firms have coincided with weaker dynamism.[S20]

So the archive also asks:

- are household wealth gains widening secure access, or mainly rewarding already-positioned owners?
- would the same gains look good from the entrant side, or are they being financed by worse affordability, higher rents, or thinner room for saving?
- are public subsidies, tax changes, or market shifts capitalising into prices rather than broadening entry?[S07][S13]

A society that reached 18 / 50 / 21 / 11 mainly by making existing owners richer through scarcity and revaluation would still count as only partially successful. See [`non-extractive-wealth-formation.md`](non-extractive-wealth-formation.md).


## Downside-discipline interpretation

rev0017 adds one more rule: **good shares are not enough if concentrated owners keep the upside while the public repeatedly absorbs the downside**.

An IMF 2025 technical note says public solvency support to banks should remain a **last resort** and should maximise burden sharing, minimise moral hazard, and protect taxpayers.[S32]
A companion IMF 2025 note says broad asset purchases may create moral hazard by providing a **broad, untargeted safety net for asset holders**.[S33]

So the archive also asks:

- are critical-function backstops being confused with protection of incumbent asset prices?
- when public support is unavoidable, does the state recover value through equity, levies, warrants, restructuring authority, clawbacks, or similar devices?[S32]
- would the same distribution still look acceptable if repeated untargeted rescue support were no longer available?[S33]

A society that reached 18 / 50 / 21 / 11 while repeatedly socialising major private downside would still count as only partially successful. See [`downside-discipline-and-loss-bearing.md`](downside-discipline-and-loss-bearing.md).

## Social-floor interpretation

rev0008 adds one more rule: **good shares are not enough if households must still self-insure too much of basic life**.

OECD's 2025 health reporting says inadequate financial protection can deepen poverty and worsen inequality, while OECD's 2025 pension reporting says first-tier benefits and progressive pension design reduce old-age inequality.[S21][S22]

So the archive also asks:

- how much wealth must a non-rich household hold just to remain safe in sickness, parenthood, disability, unemployment, or old age?
- are the bands being met inside a strong common floor, or inside a privatized-risk regime?
- is positive public wealth actually lowering household dependence, or merely sitting on the state balance sheet?

A society that reached 18 / 50 / 21 / 11 but still required large private war chests for basic security would count as only partially successful. See [`social-floor-and-private-wealth.md`](social-floor-and-private-wealth.md).

## Legibility precondition

rev0009 adds one more interpretive rule: **good shares are not enough if the wealth that matters most is too opaque to govern**.

OECD and Global Forum reporting describes beneficial ownership transparency as critical for combating tax evasion and illicit flows, and reports that beneficial ownership transparency remains a major implementation challenge across jurisdictions.[S23] The IMF's 2024 note on taxing wealth likewise stresses anti-avoidance, enforcement, and cross-border information sharing for top-tail taxation.[S04]

So the archive also asks:

- can the state identify the natural persons behind major firms, funds, trusts, foundations, and land holdings?
- can large gifts, inheritances, and control shifts be seen well enough to enforce the rules?
- is measured equality partly an artifact of hidden or thinly reported top-tail wealth?

A society that reached 18 / 50 / 21 / 11 but left major ownership and transfer channels materially opaque would still fail the archive's moral standard. See [`../20-program/wealth-legibility-registries-and-enforcement.md`](../20-program/wealth-legibility-registries-and-enforcement.md).

A society that reached 18 / 50 / 21 / 11 mainly by loading new entrants and ordinary households into high leverage would also count as only partially successful. See [`encumbrance-and-debt-quality.md`](encumbrance-and-debt-quality.md).

## Why not demand equality?

Because some inequality is compatible with liberty, experimentation, and ordinary differences in savings and enterprise. The aim here is not sameness. It is to eliminate the specific forms of concentration that create **oligarchy, inherited caste, and mass dependency**.

## Why not accept current rich-country patterns?

The available evidence suggests current patterns are far more concentrated than this archive judges acceptable. WIR 2026 reports that in all world regions the bottom 50% holds at most 5% of wealth, and in North America & Oceania the top 1% alone holds about 34%.[S02] OECD data show that the richest 10% own 52% of household wealth on average across OECD countries, and as much as 79% in the United States.[S03]

## Practical interpretation

Treat these bands as:

- a **directional constitutional target**,
- one part of a five-axis evaluation plus a social-floor condition,
- a red-flag detector when a policy raises incomes but leaves ownership concentrated,
- a red-flag detector when a policy raises paper wealth but leaves households fragile,
- a red-flag detector when a policy improves the snapshot but leaves the birth lottery intact,
- a red-flag detector when a policy improves private balances while leaving essential risks too privatized,
- a red-flag detector when a policy looks better on paper because the top tail remains hard to see,
- a red-flag detector when a policy broadens financial claims without broadening the stewardship attached to them,
- a red-flag detector when a policy improves household wealth without broadening direct person-level claim and exit security,
- a red-flag detector when a policy improves eventual wealth while leaving early-life footholds too delayed,
- a red-flag detector when a policy improves incumbent wealth mainly through scarcity rents, markups, or revaluation rather than through open and durable access,
- a red-flag detector when a policy broadens wealth while also expanding broad untargeted public backstops for concentrated private risk.

## Current red lines

For this archive, the following should count as strong warning signals:

- bottom-50 share below 10%,
- top-1 share above 20%,
- top-0.1 share above 5%,
- persistent negative net public wealth,
- low-income housing burdens above 40% of disposable income for large parts of the population.[S02][S07]

rev0004 added a structural red-line family:

- opaque ownership over major assets,
- concentrated control of key assets despite improving headline shares,
- bottom-half gains driven mainly by asset-price inflation rather than secure access to assets.[S13][S15][S16]

rev0016 adds a formation red-line family:

- broad wealth gains are driven mainly by incumbent revaluation while first-entry affordability worsens,
- wage or transfer gains are quickly absorbed by higher rents, fees, or markups,
- policy support capitalises mainly into asset prices rather than widening secure access.[S07][S10][S13][S20]

rev0005 adds a floor red-line family:

- large parts of the bottom half remain in negative net wealth,
- liquid-buffer failure remains common well into the middle of the distribution,
- nominal wealth gains coexist with severe housing insecurity or debt distress.[S07][S16][S17]

rev0006 adds a circulation red-line family:

- inherited circumstances explain a large and persistent share of adult inequality,
- the parental-homeownership advantage is high or rising,
- younger adults without wealthy parents face a structurally narrowing path into ownership and security.[S18][S19]

rev0012 adds a timing red-line family inside circulation:

- secure tenure, positive net wealth, or first durable asset ownership arrive too late for large parts of non-rich households,
- policy progress shows up mainly in older-cohort balance sheets while younger cohorts remain locked out,
- delayed inheritance is doing too much of the system's distributive work.[S05][S13][S19]

These red lines are not only descriptive. rev0003 treated the numerical red lines as **trigger conditions**. rev0004 kept that rule and added structural control concentration. rev0005 added the warning that widespread floor failure can also block exit from correction or anti-oligarchy mode even when percentile shares move in the right direction. rev0006 adds the warning that dynastic closure can do the same even when cross-sectional shares improve. rev0008 adds the warning that the same is true when households still need unusually high private precautionary wealth because the social floor is too weak. rev0012 adds the warning that the same is true when secure ownership arrives mainly too late in life, even if eventual inheritance makes later-life balances look less unequal.[S05][S21][S22] rev0013 adds the warning that the same is true when household-level gains leave person-level title, liquid control, pension rights, or fair exit protection badly skewed.[S13][S27][S28][S29][S30] rev0014 adds the warning that the same is true when national gains still leave historically subordinated groups structurally excluded from durable asset building.[S13][S19] rev0016 adds the warning that the same is true when apparent broadening is being generated mainly through scarcity rents, markups, fees, or incumbent asset revaluation that worsen entry for later households.[S07][S10][S13][S20] rev0017 adds the warning that the same is true when concentrated owners repeatedly keep the upside while public authorities absorb major downside through rescue, guarantees, or broad untargeted support.[S32][S33]


rev0011 adds a stewardship red-line family:

- a few asset managers or proxy intermediaries effectively determine the voting posture of a large share of listed firms,
- pension savers and other beneficiaries have little meaningful say over stewardship mandates or voting policies,
- broad household ownership expands mainly as silent beneficial exposure while exercised control stays highly pooled.[S20][S26]

rev0017 adds a downside-discipline red-line family:

- major rescues leave shareholders, unsecured creditors, or senior managers largely protected while taxpayers or public balance sheets absorb the losses,
- broad asset-price support functions as a recurring safety net for concentrated asset holders,
- public support is not meaningfully recovered through levies, restructuring, equity, warrants, or comparable burden-sharing tools.[S32][S33]

rev0009 adds a legibility red-line family:

- beneficial ownership of major firms, trusts, or real estate remains substantially opaque,
- large transfers and trust structures routinely outrun gift / inheritance scrutiny,
- governments cannot reliably link ownership, tax, procurement, and political-finance records for the top tail.[S04][S23]

The archive now treats these as reasons to doubt not only enforcement capacity but also the credibility of the measured distribution itself.


## Cross-cutting calibration — Publicly created value and common-asset capture

rev0018 adds one more cross-cutting calibration: ask whether land, housing, and development wealth are being shaped by **publicly created uplift with meaningful common recapture**.

61. **Share of major infrastructure, rezoning, or development-approval decisions covered by explicit value-capture tools or binding affordability / infrastructure obligations**
   - Tracks whether collective action that raises site value is being partly socialised rather than simply privatised.[S34][S35]
62. **Affordable-housing, infrastructure, or public-purpose contributions relative to estimated land-value uplift**
   - Tests whether obligations are scaled to the gain rather than treated as token add-ons.[S35]
63. **Strategic public-land retention / leasehold / disposal discipline**
   - Tracks whether public land is managed as a long-run common asset instead of sold off cheaply with weak future public claim.[S35]
64. **Public/social-wealth gains from land-value capture relative to major publicly created uplifts**
   - Measures whether public action strengthens the common balance sheet as well as private site values.[S02][S34][S35]

Use these measures to interpret, not replace, the five main axes.
A country can improve wealth shares and still fail morally if infrastructure, zoning, and public-land decisions keep manufacturing large private windfalls with little return to common wealth or wider access.
