# Publicly created value and common-asset capture

## Thesis

A morally acceptable wealth order should not let large amounts of value created by **public action** simply harden into private windfalls.
When infrastructure investment, rezoning, development rights, public guarantees, or strategic public land decisions raise asset values, part of that uplift should normally flow back into **public/social wealth**, affordable access, or common infrastructure rather than being fully capitalised into private fortunes.

This is not a brand-new axis so much as a cross-cutting **public-value-capture condition**.
The archive should count a society as only partly successful if it broadens ownership in some places while repeatedly giving away socially created uplift in the places where wealth compounds fastest.[S34][S35]

## Why this condition is needed

The archive already says immoral inequality involves rents, extraction, and weak public balance sheets.
But it still risks treating some large wealth gains too neutrally once they appear on private balance sheets.
That is a mistake when the gain was generated substantially by:

- public infrastructure,
- public planning and land-use decisions,
- development permissions that raise density or development potential,
- strategic public land disposal,
- or other collectively created conditions that make a site, corridor, or project far more valuable than before.

OECD's global land-value-capture compendium defines land value capture as a family of instruments that allow governments to capture land-value uplifts generated by public interventions such as infrastructure investments or administrative actions like land-use changes.[S34]
OECD's 2025 housing-reform work on Czechia and Poland sharpens the same point for housing policy: affordable-housing requirements and other obligations can be based on increases in land value from development approvals, and public land can be managed strategically to recover value created by public investment or zoning changes.[S35]

That means the archive should not treat all appreciation alike.
A wage earner saving into a durable claim, a cooperative gaining productive equity, and a landowner receiving a rezoning windfall are not morally equivalent pathways.

## The public-value-capture test

Whenever a society reports rising housing wealth, rising land values, or rising development fortunes, ask four extra questions:

1. **How much of the uplift was created by collective action?**
   - If a major part of the gain came from infrastructure, zoning, public guarantees, or public land strategy, the public has a stronger claim than it does over purely idiosyncratic gains.[S34][S35]
2. **Was any of that uplift recaptured for common purposes?**
   - The archive should prefer systems that use infrastructure levies, developer obligations, charges for development rights, strategic land management, or comparable tools to return part of the uplift to affordable housing, infrastructure, or public/social wealth.[S34][S35]
3. **Did the value-capture design widen access, or only raise revenue?**
   - A good system should not merely tax windfalls after the fact; it should also widen secure entry through affordable housing, serviced land, lower future costs, or stronger public balance sheets.
4. **Was public land managed strategically rather than sold off cheaply with no public claim on later appreciation?**
   - Repeated unconditional disposal of public land is often a quiet transfer of future collective wealth into private hands.[S35]

## Warning signs

The archive should treat the following as warning signs even when headline wealth shares improve:

- rezoning or transit investment produces large private site gains with little or no common recapture,
- housing subsidies or infrastructure spending capitalise quickly into land prices without durable affordability conditions,
- public land is sold into hot markets with weak affordability, leasehold, or public-purpose conditions,
- local governments compete to grant development windfalls while remaining fiscally too weak to build affordable housing or infrastructure,
- the public balance sheet stays weak while private land fortunes rise on the back of public decisions.[S02][S34][S35]

## Program implications

This condition pushes the archive toward a stricter stance on land, planning, and public wealth:

- use land-value capture instruments where public action generates predictable uplift,
- tie development permissions and density gains to affordable-housing or infrastructure contributions when value uplift is large,
- manage public land strategically rather than treating it as disposable cash,
- feed part of socially created uplift into public/social wealth funds, land banks, infrastructure, or permanent affordability institutions,
- judge housing and planning reforms by whether they widen durable access, not only by whether incumbent asset values rise.

## Archive rule

The archive should therefore ask one more question whenever land, housing, or development wealth rises:

**Was the gain mainly a private windfall from collective action, or did the society recapture a meaningful share for common wealth and wider access?**

If the first description is closer to the truth, the measured distribution may look better while the underlying wealth order remains too extraction-friendly and the public balance sheet remains too weak.
