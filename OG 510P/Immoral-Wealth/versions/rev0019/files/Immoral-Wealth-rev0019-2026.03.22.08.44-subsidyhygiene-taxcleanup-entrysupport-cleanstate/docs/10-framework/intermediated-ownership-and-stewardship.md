# Intermediated ownership and stewardship concentration

## Thesis

A morally acceptable wealth order is not only about **who ultimately benefits from assets**.
It is also about **who actually exercises the votes, sets engagement priorities, and shapes governance norms** when those assets are held through pensions, mutual funds, insurers, and other intermediated vehicles.

A society can broaden household exposure to capital while still leaving corporate governance concentrated in a narrow manager layer.
That is not the same thing as broad ownership in civic substance.[S20][S26]

## Why this layer is needed

OECD's 2025 stewardship report says that, as of 2024, asset managers held **65%** of listed equity in the United States and **59%** in the United Kingdom, with at least **30%** in several other advanced markets.[S26]
The same report says the **largest 20 asset managers hold USD 56 trillion**, or **38% of global assets under management**, and that institutional-investor ownership is highly concentrated at the company level in many markets.[S26]

OECD also stresses that asset owners often rely on managers to execute stewardship, creating information asymmetries, and reviews **pass-through voting** as one way to let asset owners, and sometimes retail investors, influence how their underlying shares are voted.[S26]

Those facts matter because a society can look better on wealth distribution while still failing on governance:

- broad households may hold retirement or fund claims but have little say over how those claims are voted,
- a few giant managers can become de facto standard-setters across large parts of the corporate economy,
- passive or index-based intermediation can diffuse financial claims while pooling stewardship infrastructure,
- households can be exposed to corporate conduct without meaningful influence over it.

## The stewardship split rule

The archive should therefore distinguish two layers of ownership:

1. **claim distribution** — who receives returns and bears risk,
2. **stewardship distribution** — who directs votes, engagement, and governance expectations.

A society should not count intermediated capital ownership as fully de-oligarchised unless at least one of the following is substantially true:

- asset owners can choose or direct voting policies on material issues,
- public, union, cooperative, or member-governed funds exercise meaningful stewardship at scale,
- mandates between asset owners and managers are specific enough that stewardship follows beneficiary priorities rather than manager convenience,
- concentrated manager voting power is balanced by strong disclosure, competition, fiduciary, and conflict rules,
- key strategic sectors are not governed only through a thin private manager layer.

## What should count as failure

The archive should treat the following as warning signs:

- a few asset managers or proxy intermediaries routinely determine the voting posture of a large share of listed firms,
- pension savers and other beneficiaries have little ability to direct or even observe how votes are cast,
- stewardship policies are broad, vague, or opaque enough that real priorities remain manager-defined,
- the same managers dominate across competing firms without credible member, public, or countervailing voice,
- broad household "ownership" exists mainly as a silent beneficial claim.

## Program implications

This is mainly a refinement of the archive's **control** layer, not a brand-new distributive axis.

It suggests that a serious anti-inequality program may need, depending on country type:

- pass-through or member-directed voting in major retirement and pooled-investment channels,
- stronger asset-owner stewardship mandates and disclosures,
- public, union, or cooperative funds with real governance capacity,
- transparency around proxy voting, engagement priorities, and outsourced stewardship,
- competition and conflict rules that prevent asset-manager scale from quietly becoming governance power.

## Archive rule

The archive should therefore ask one more question whenever it sees broader portfolio ownership:

**Did control actually broaden, or did only beneficial exposure broaden while stewardship stayed bottlenecked?**

If the second is true, the society may be less oligarchic than before in cash-flow terms, but not yet fully democratic in ownership terms.
