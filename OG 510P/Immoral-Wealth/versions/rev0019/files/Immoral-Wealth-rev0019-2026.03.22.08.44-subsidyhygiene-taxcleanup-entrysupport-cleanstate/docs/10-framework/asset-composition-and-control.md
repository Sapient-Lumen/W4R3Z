# Asset composition and control constraints

## Thesis

An acceptable wealth distribution is **not only a set of percentile shares**.
It is also a pattern of **who owns what kinds of assets, in what form, and with how much control**.

A society can improve its headline bottom-50 or top-1 numbers and still fail morally if ownership of housing, land, firms, infrastructure, finance, or media remains concentrated enough to reproduce dependency and rule. rev0011 sharpens this further: where ownership is intermediated through funds and pensions, the archive should distinguish between **who owns the claim** and **who actually exercises the stewardship** over it.[S26]

## Why this layer is needed

OECD's 2025 distributional wealth paper reports that wealth is concentrated in the top quintile, the bottom quintile often has **negative wealth**, inequality is driven by **financial assets**, and housing is the largest household asset, followed by deposits and equities/investment funds.[S16]

That matters because different assets do different political and social work:

- **housing and land** shape security, family formation, and rent extraction,
- **liquid assets** shape resilience and bargaining power,
- **retirement assets** shape old-age independence,
- **business equity and control rights** shape command over investment, labor, and media,
- **public/social wealth** shapes collective capacity and non-market provision.[S02][S13][S16]

## The composition rule

A moral distribution should satisfy **both**:

1. the archive's aggregate share bands, and
2. a **composition-and-control test**.

If the bands improve but key assets remain captured, the society has not yet reached the archive's target in substance.

## Working composition target by social position

### Bottom 50%

The lower half should not merely own a small statistical slice of wealth.
It should have a **durable independence package**:

- positive net wealth rather than debt-dominated balance sheets,
- thick enough equity that ordinary ownership is not mostly a leveraged residual claim,
- a liquid buffer for emergencies,
- retirement claims that are real and portable,
- meaningful access to secure housing or non-extractive tenure,
- some path into broader capital ownership over the life course.

A bottom half that technically owns 15-20% of wealth but mostly in illiquid or fragile forms still fails the bottom-security test.[S13][S16]

### Middle 40%

The middle should not be a housing-only bloc whose fortunes rise and fall with property cycles.
It should hold a **diversified civic portfolio**:

- owner-occupied housing equity where appropriate,
- pension/retirement wealth,
- liquid savings,
- some collective or direct exposure to productive capital,
- low dependence on predatory debt.

This matters because OECD evidence suggests housing can equalise wealth, but rising prices and access barriers can also deepen the divide between homeowners and renters.[S13][S16]

### Next 9%

The upper-middle should remain prosperous, but should not become a shadow oligarchy.
Its role in a broad-capital society is to widen expertise, enterprise, and savings capacity without fusing into a hereditary governing class.

### Top 1%

The top 1% may remain rich, but should **not** dominate control over the asset classes that structure mass life.
That means, at minimum, no routine top-tail dominance over:

- residential land and rental housing at scale,
- strategic infrastructure,
- core financial intermediation,
- agenda-setting media,
- concentrated voting control over large firms.

The archive therefore treats **control concentration** as an override condition: even if headline shares move in the right direction, heavy concentration in key assets can keep a society in correction or anti-oligarchy mode.[S15][S16]

## Public and social wealth

Positive public wealth should not be thought of only as a balance-sheet residual.
It is part of the archive's answer to private concentration.

Public and social wealth can hold strategic assets in non-oligarchic form:

- social housing and land banks,
- public development and venture funds,
- pension reserve funds governed for the public,
- utilities or infrastructure assets where natural-monopoly logic is strong,
- common funds that finance universal capabilities or social inheritance.[S02]

## Control red lines

The archive should treat the following as major warning signs even if aggregate shares appear to improve:

- extreme landlord concentration in tight housing markets,
- opaque beneficial ownership over major assets,
- high concentration of voting control in large firms,
- private capture of infrastructure or natural-monopoly bottlenecks without strong public discipline,
- media ownership concentrated enough to shape agenda-setting with weak accountability,
- bottom-half wealth growth driven mostly by asset-price inflation rather than broader asset access.[S13][S15]

## Practical use

When evaluating reforms, ask not only **who got richer**, but also:

- which asset classes changed hands,
- whether the bottom gained liquidity, housing security, retirement wealth, or only paper wealth,
- whether the top lost control or merely paid more tax while retaining command,
- whether public/social wealth expanded in strategically important sectors.

That is the main use of this document: it stops the archive from mistaking better-looking aggregates for a fully de-oligarchised ownership structure.
