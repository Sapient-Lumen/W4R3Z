# Encumbrance and debt quality

## Thesis

A morally acceptable wealth order is not only about **net wealth shares**.
It also depends on whether broad household ownership is sufficiently **unencumbered**.
If the bottom and middle distribution appear to own housing or other assets only through extreme leverage, high debt-service burdens, serial refinancing, or debt forms that keep households one shock away from distress, then nominal ownership can mask creditor dependence rather than genuine independence.[S13][S17][S24][S25]

This is not a sixth distributive axis in the same sense as shares or circulation.
It is a **cross-cutting encumbrance condition**.
A society can post a better net-wealth distribution while still failing if large parts of the broad base own only a thin equity slice sitting on heavy repayment obligations.

## Why this matters

The archive already tracks asset floor, composition, social protection, and reconcentration pressure.
The encumbrance question sharpens all four.

- **Asset-floor problem:** positive net wealth is weaker than it looks if modest income shocks, rate resets, or small price declines can wipe it out.[S17][S24]
- **Composition problem:** a middle class concentrated in one highly leveraged housing asset is less independent than a middle class with thicker equity, liquid buffers, pension claims, and safer tenure.[S13][S16]
- **Social-floor problem:** weak health, care, education, and housing systems often push households toward debt-financed self-protection or debt-financed entry.[S17][S21][S22]
- **Reconcentration problem:** creditor claims, rent extraction, and asset-price cycles can pull broad gains back upward even when households briefly appear wealthier on paper.[S10][S20][S24]

OECD defines household debt as liabilities that require future payments of principal or interest, mainly mortgages, consumer credit, and other accounts payable, measured against disposable income.[S24]
That is exactly why the archive should not treat all positive net wealth as equally emancipatory.
A household with a modest but durable claim is in a different political position from one with a similar net worth resting on high debt-service obligations and fragile collateral values.

## The encumbrance condition

When the archive judges a country's wealth order, it should ask:

1. **How leveraged is ordinary ownership?**
   - Are bottom-half and middle households accumulating assets with manageable leverage, or only by taking on obligations that dominate their future cash flow?
2. **How serviceable is the debt?**
   - Can households hold their assets through ordinary income interruptions and interest-rate changes, or does ownership depend on benign macro conditions at all times?[S17][S24]
3. **How entry-dependent is the system on family help?**
   - If safe entry requires parental transfers while non-family-backed entrants must accept dangerous leverage, the society is more dynastic than the headline shares suggest.[S13][S19]
4. **How diversified is the broad base?**
   - Is the middle anchored only in owner housing and mortgage debt, or also in liquid assets, pension rights, cooperative equity, or other durable claims?[S16]
5. **How extractive are the liabilities?**
   - Are debts mainly transitional and serviceable, or are fees, penalties, rate structures, and refinancing traps quietly converting broad ownership into creditor dependence?

## What should count as a pass

A country should count as closer to moral acceptability when broad household wealth is paired with:

- mostly positive bottom-half net wealth,
- liquid buffers large enough to reduce forced borrowing after routine shocks,[S17][S25]
- debt-to-income and debt-service burdens that do not dominate ordinary household cash flow,[S24]
- first-entry pathways into housing and asset ownership that do not require extreme leverage or wealthy parents,[S13][S19]
- declining negative-equity, arrears, delinquency, or forced-sale pressure,
- a middle class whose wealth is not almost entirely one leveraged housing bet.

## What should count as failure

The archive should treat the following as strong warning signs:

- headline wealth gains are driven mainly by house-price inflation while leverage stays high or rises,[S13][S24]
- homeownership expands but debt-service stress, arrears, or refinancing dependence also expand,
- the bottom half moves into nominal positive wealth but remains vulnerable to mild rate or price shocks,
- young adults can enter only by accepting extreme leverage or waiting for parental help,[S13][S19]
- education, health, care, or consumer debt regularly cancel out asset-building gains,[S17][S21]
- the political economy increasingly protects asset prices while socialising downside risk onto indebted households.

## Program surface

The encumbrance condition pushes the archive toward a somewhat tighter policy bundle:

- safer housing entry and tenure regimes,
- debt-relief or restructuring where legacy burdens are blocking family formation and asset acquisition,
- stronger liquid-buffer and emergency-support tools so shocks do not convert directly into debt spirals,[S17][S25]
- broader non-debt asset channels such as social inheritance, pension floors, public wealth, and cooperative claims,
- macroprudential and anti-speculation measures that keep broad ownership from depending on permanent asset inflation,
- stronger social-floor provision where private borrowing is compensating for weak public protection.[S21][S22]

## Archive rule

The archive should therefore add one compact question to every distributional judgment:

**Is broad ownership real enough to survive ordinary shocks, or is it mostly thin equity resting on heavy obligations to creditors?**

If the latter, the society should be treated as only partially compliant even when the net-wealth snapshot improves.
