# Social floor and private wealth

## Thesis

The archive should not judge private wealth distributions in the abstract.
The same private wealth pattern means very different things in two societies if one has strong universal provision of healthcare, pensions, education, childcare, disability support, and housing security while the other pushes those risks back onto households.

A morally acceptable wealth order therefore requires not only better **private shares**, a stronger **asset floor**, wider **circulation**, lower **reconcentration pressure**, and broader **control**.
It also requires a strong enough **social floor** that people do not need unusually large private balances merely to survive illness, unemployment, parenthood, disability, housing shocks, or old age.

This is a **conditioning rule**, not a sixth axis.
It tells the archive how to interpret the other five axes.

## Why the archive adds this layer

WIR 2026 emphasizes that private wealth has risen while public wealth has stagnated or weakened in many places.[S02]

OECD's 2025 health reporting adds that a lack of financial protection in healthcare can reduce access, undermine health, deepen poverty, and worsen socio-economic inequality. It reports that just under one-fifth of health spending across OECD countries comes directly from patients through out-of-pocket payments, and that catastrophic health spending affects more than 10% of households in some countries.[S21]

OECD's 2025 pension reporting adds that reducing inequality in old age is one of the objectives of pension systems and that high first-tier benefits and progressive pension formulas reduce old-age inequality.[S22]

Those facts point to a simple conclusion:
a society with weak public/social protection forces households to accumulate more precautionary private wealth just to avoid dependence.
The archive should not mistake that forced self-insurance for freedom.

## The conditioning rule

When judging whether a private wealth distribution is acceptable, always ask:

1. **How much basic security is already socialised?**
   - Healthcare, pensions, education, childcare, disability support, unemployment insurance, and housing security matter because they reduce the amount of private buffer a household must carry just to remain free.
2. **How much insecurity is being pushed back onto households?**
   - A country with high out-of-pocket health costs, thin pensions, weak tenant protection, and high education or care costs may require larger private balances merely to stand still.[S21][S22]
3. **Is the program reducing required private wealth, or merely telling households to save more?**
   - The archive should prefer reforms that lower the amount of private wealth needed for ordinary security, not only reforms that ask the bottom and middle to self-insure harder.

## What follows for the archive

### 1. The private target stays, but becomes conditional

The archive keeps the **20 / 50 / 20 / 10** attractor.
But that target should be read alongside the social floor.

- In a country with strong public services and secure social insurance, the bottom-half floor can be met with less precautionary private wealth than in a privatized-risk regime.
- In a country with weak services, the bottom and middle may temporarily need larger private buffers to remain independent.

That does **not** mean weak public provision morally licenses higher inequality.
It means the archive must read private wealth targets together with the public/social settlement that surrounds them.

### 2. Forced precautionary saving is not the same as freedom

A household that must accumulate large balances to pay for healthcare, education, care, or an inadequate old-age system is not necessarily more free than one with lower private balances in a society with strong universal provision.
The archive should treat these cases differently.

### 3. Public wealth is valuable when it is usable

Positive public wealth matters not only because it strengthens the state balance sheet.
It matters when it supports durable common provision and lowers household dependence on private fortune.[S02][S21][S22]

### 4. Social inheritance complements rather than replaces the social floor

Baby bonds, social wealth funds, and universal capital endowments are useful.
But the archive should not let them become substitutes for universal healthcare, pensions, housing security, or education access.
Otherwise social inheritance risks becoming a patch over privatized insecurity.

## Measurement implication

Use the five-axis scoreboard, but interpret it with a **social-floor calibration**.

At minimum, the archive should keep track of:

- health out-of-pocket burden and catastrophic health spending,[S21]
- pension adequacy / first-tier protection / old-age poverty risk,[S22]
- housing-cost overburden and secure-tenure access,[S07]
- whether child and care costs are forcing households into delayed family formation, debt, or asset depletion.

A country can improve private wealth shares and still fail if ordinary households remain too exposed to essential risks that should be partly socialised.

## What does not count as success

The archive should treat the following as false or weak progress:

- raising household private saving while leaving healthcare, housing, and care risks privatized,
- reducing top shares somewhat while families still need rich parents to fund education, entry into housing, or old-age security,
- building sovereign or public wealth that does not actually widen ordinary security,
- using one-time asset grants to offset structural under-provision of essential services.

## Summary rule

The archive's numerical target should be read as:

**broad private ownership inside a strong social floor**.

Without that social floor, the same private distribution can conceal forced dependence, arms-race self-insurance, and a higher private wealth requirement than a free society should demand.
