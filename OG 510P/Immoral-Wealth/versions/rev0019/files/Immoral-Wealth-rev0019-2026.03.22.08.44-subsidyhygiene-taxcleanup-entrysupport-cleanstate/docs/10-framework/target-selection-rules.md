# Target selection rules

## Thesis

An ideal wealth distribution should not be chosen by aesthetic preference alone.
It should be chosen by asking what distribution keeps a society **free, plural, innovative, non-oligarchic, open across generations, not organized around forced precautionary self-insurance, not quietly governed through an overly concentrated stewardship layer, not structured so that durable ownership arrives too late in life for ordinary adults, not flattered by household-level aggregation when person-level ownership remains badly skewed, and not made to look better than it is by rent-heavy or bottleneck-based wealth formation, and not quietly stabilized by public downside protection for concentrated private risk, and not passively enriched by land and development windfalls generated by collective action with too little common recapture**.

## The five tests

A proposed distribution is acceptable only if it passes all five tests.

### 1. Bottom-security test

The lower half of the population must hold enough wealth that mass life is not organized around permanent precarity. rev0005 makes this more concrete: bottom security means meaningful positive net wealth, liquid emergency resilience, secure housing, and at least some realistic route into retirement saving, cooperative equity, or enterprise.[S07][S16][S17]

### 2. Middle-plurality test

The middle 40% must be large enough in wealth terms to form an independent civic majority rather than a squeezed stratum between debtor masses and a thin owner elite.

### 3. Top non-rule test

The top tail must be rich without being able to casually purchase rulemaking, media agenda-setting, or de facto veto power over reform.

### 4. Public-capacity test

Public wealth must be positive enough that the state can fund universal capabilities, social inheritance, and counter-cyclical action without living as a balance-sheet pauper.[S02]

### 5. Transition test

The target must be demanding enough to matter, but not so detached from institutional reality that it collapses into rhetorical maximalism.

## The minimum-asset-floor constraint

rev0005 adds a clearer rule inside the bottom-security test: a distribution can look better in percentile terms while still failing if too many households remain **wealth-poor in function**.

OECD's 2025 distributional-wealth paper reports that the bottom quintile often has negative wealth.[S16] OECD policy work on economic insecurity reports that around 2017, 50% of people in middle-income households in OECD EU countries lacked enough liquid assets to stay above the poverty line for at least three months after a sudden income loss.[S17]

That means the archive cannot rely on bottom-share targets alone.
It also has to ask:

- are bottom-half households in positive net wealth?
- do they have liquid resilience, not only illiquid claims?
- is housing access secure enough that gains are not immediately reabsorbed by rent or fragile leverage?

For the fuller doctrine, see [`minimum-asset-floor.md`](minimum-asset-floor.md).

## The social-floor condition

rev0008 adds a fifth interpretive rule.
A distribution can look acceptable on **shares**, **floor**, **control**, **circulation**, and even **reconcentration**, yet still ask too much of households if the society has weak public/social protection.

OECD's 2025 health reporting says inadequate financial protection can deepen poverty and worsen socio-economic inequality, and reports that just under one-fifth of health spending across OECD countries comes directly from patients through out-of-pocket payments.[S21] OECD's 2025 pension reporting adds that pension systems reduce old-age inequality and that high first-tier benefits and progressive formulas lower inequality in retirement.[S22]

That means the archive also has to ask:

- how much precautionary private wealth are households forced to hold because health, care, education, or old-age risk is privatized?
- are better private balances reflecting greater freedom, or just higher self-insurance requirements?
- is public wealth being converted into usable common provision, or merely held while households stay exposed?

For the fuller doctrine, see [`social-floor-and-private-wealth.md`](social-floor-and-private-wealth.md).

## The encumbrance condition

rev0010 adds one more cross-cutting rule: a good distribution should not depend on **broad household leverage so high that nominal ownership masks fragility**.

OECD defines household debt as liabilities requiring future payments of principal or interest, primarily mortgages and consumer credit, measured against disposable income.[S24]
That means the archive should ask not only **who owns**, but **on what terms**.
A society does not fully pass just because more households appear in positive net wealth if their gains are thin, debt-service heavy, and vulnerable to ordinary rate or price shocks.[S13][S17][S25]

Questions to ask alongside the headline bands:

- how much of bottom-half and middle wealth is thin equity resting on large liabilities?
- can households retain their assets through a normal income interruption without immediate distress or forced sale?[S17][S25]
- does first entry into ownership require dangerous leverage for households without parental help?[S13][S19]
- is the broad base diversified, or mostly a single mortgage-backed housing position?[S16]

For the fuller doctrine, see [`encumbrance-and-debt-quality.md`](encumbrance-and-debt-quality.md).

## The non-extractive formation condition

rev0016 adds one more cross-cutting rule: a good distribution should not depend mainly on **scarcity rents, monopoly markups, fee extraction, or incumbent asset revaluation that makes entry harder for the next cohort**.

OECD's 2025 distributional-wealth paper says inequality is driven by financial assets and moderated by housing wealth, while housing remains the largest household asset.[S16]
OECD's 2025 economic-outlook work adds that rising market concentration, higher markups, and investment increasingly concentrated in a small group of highly profitable firms have coincided with weaker dynamism.[S20]

That means the archive should ask not only **who owns**, but **how broad gains are being made**.
A society should not fully pass if broad wealth looks better mainly because incumbents enjoy revaluation gains while new entrants face worse access, higher rents, or thinner margins for saving.

Questions to ask alongside the headline bands:

- are ordinary gains coming from better earnings, social inheritance, productive investment, and open access, or mainly from bottleneck ownership and revaluation?
- do policy gains widen entry into secure housing and capital ownership, or mainly lift incumbent asset prices?[S07][S13]
- are profits and capital gains linked to productive renewal, or to rising concentration, markups, and exclusionary scarcity?[S10][S20]
- would the same gains still look good when viewed from the entrant side rather than only the incumbent side?

For the fuller doctrine, see [`non-extractive-wealth-formation.md`](non-extractive-wealth-formation.md).


## The composition-and-control constraint

rev0004 added another interpretive rule: a distribution can pass the five tests **numerically** and still fail **structurally** if control over key asset classes remains too concentrated.

OECD's 2025 distributional-wealth paper reports that wealth is concentrated in the top quintile, the bottom quintile often has negative wealth, inequality is driven by financial assets, and housing is the largest household asset.[S16]

That means the archive also has to ask:

- does the bottom half have liquid resilience or mainly debt and thin paper claims?
- is the middle diversified or mostly a housing-only bloc?
- does the top retain commanding control over firms, land, finance, infrastructure, or media even if its share falls somewhat?

For the fuller doctrine, see [`asset-composition-and-control.md`](asset-composition-and-control.md).

## The stewardship split inside the control test

rev0011 adds one more refinement inside the archive's **control** test: broad financial claims do not automatically equal broad governance.

OECD's 2025 stewardship report says that, as of 2024, asset managers held **65%** of listed equity in the United States and **59%** in the United Kingdom. It also says the largest 20 asset managers held **USD 56 trillion**, or **38%** of global assets under management, and reviews pass-through voting as one way for asset owners and, in some circumstances, retail investors to influence how their underlying shares are voted.[S26]

That means the archive also has to ask:

- when households own capital through pensions, insurers, or pooled funds, who actually votes those shares?
- are stewardship mandates specific enough that managers act on beneficiary priorities rather than generic manager convenience?
- has broad capital ownership widened **voice**, or mostly widened **exposure**?

For the fuller doctrine, see [`intermediated-ownership-and-stewardship.md`](intermediated-ownership-and-stewardship.md).

## The intergenerational-circulation constraint

rev0006 adds a third interpretive rule: a distribution can pass **numerically**, can look better on **floor** and **control**, and still fail if the system is becoming too **dynastic**.

OECD's 2025 opportunity work reports that **over 25%** at least of total inequality in household market income across the OECD can be attributed to inherited circumstances, and that parental background contributes **over 60%** of inequality of opportunity in most countries.[S18] Companion OECD analysis reports that in OECD European countries in 2023 having homeowning parents increased the probability of owning a home by age 38-46 by **18.7%**, up from **12.7%** in 2011.[S19]

That means the archive also has to ask:

- how strongly does parental background predict adult asset position?
- can young adults without wealthy parents still enter ownership and durable security?
- are large transfers and delayed bequests reopening life chances, or mostly hardening them?

For the fuller doctrine, see [`intergenerational-circulation.md`](intergenerational-circulation.md).

## The life-course timing refinement inside circulation

rev0012 adds one more refinement inside the archive's **circulation** test: it matters not only **whether** adult asset position is inherited, but also **when** ordinary households can secure a first durable foothold.

OECD's 2025 opportunity work says wealth transfers are associated with a **larger wealth premium when they are received earlier in life**.[S19] OECD's inheritance-tax report adds that wealth is expected to remain concentrated among older cohorts for longer periods and that the age at which people inherit is likely to keep rising with longer life expectancy.[S05] OECD's recent housing and wealth work also says rising house prices have made homeownership less accessible for younger cohorts, especially those from lower-income or lower-wealth households.[S13][S19]

That means the archive also has to ask:

- do households without parental help reach secure tenure or positive durable wealth before midlife?
- is late inheritance being mistaken for open access to ownership?
- are reforms improving the timing of first footholds, or mostly protecting older incumbents while younger cohorts wait?

For the fuller doctrine, see [`life-course-timing-and-early-footholds.md`](life-course-timing-and-early-footholds.md).

## The person-level ownership condition

rev0013 adds one more cross-cutting rule: a good distribution should not be judged only at the **household** level when the moral question is whether **persons** have durable claims and credible exit.

OECD's 2025 opportunity work warns that the role of gender becomes much larger when inequality is measured using **individual earnings** rather than household market income.[S27]
OECD's 2025 household-wealth note adds that, on average across the OECD, **single men hold about USD 43 000 more wealth than single women**.[S13]
World Bank gender-asset work likewise warns that women often trail men in account and land ownership, and that household membership alone does not ensure equal participation in the benefits of property where formal rights are missing or weakly enforced.[S28][S29]
OECD's 2025 pension reporting adds a related late-life warning: among people aged 66 and over, average household disposable-income gaps are much smaller than gender pension gaps, so household pooling can hide large person-level inequality in retirement.[S30]

That means the archive also has to ask:

- who is actually named on housing, land, business, and major financial assets?
- who has direct pension entitlements and liquid account access?
- who can keep a fair claim in separation, widowhood, or household conflict?
- are ownership-spreading policies delivered to persons, or only to households and tax units?

For the fuller doctrine, see [`individual-ownership-and-intra-household-power.md`](individual-ownership-and-intra-household-power.md).

## The group-stratification condition

rev0014 adds one more cross-cutting rule: a good distribution should not be judged only on **aggregate**, **household**, or even **person-level** terms if durable ownership remains structurally closed across historically subordinated groups.

OECD's 2025 wealth paper says wealth gaps remain significant across demographic groups including gender, migrant status, and geographic location, reports that migrant households hold about **USD 136 000** less net wealth than native-born households across 20 OECD countries with available data even after ordinary controls, and warns that most countries still lack enough equality data on race/ethnicity, sexual orientation and gender identity, and disability to map wealth barriers cleanly.[S13]
Companion OECD opportunity work adds that financial-inclusion and asset-building policies can help support historically disadvantaged groups when they face persistent barriers to saving, credit, entrepreneurship, or housing entry.[S19]

That means the archive also has to ask:

- do broad gains reach groups long excluded from credit, land, housing, entrepreneurship, and inheritance?
- are headline improvements being driven mainly by already-advantaged groups while excluded groups remain stuck?
- can the state even see these gaps with disaggregated, privacy-protecting data?

For the fuller doctrine, see [`group-stratification-and-excluded-groups.md`](group-stratification-and-excluded-groups.md).



## The reconcentration-pressure constraint

rev0007 adds a fourth interpretive rule: a distribution can pass **numerically**, can look better on **floor**, **control**, and **circulation**, and still fail if its gains are too easy to reverse.

OECD's competition and economic-outlook work says evidence suggests competition has weakened across OECD countries over the last two decades, markets have become more concentrated and less dynamic, firms have higher markups on average, and investment has become more concentrated among a small group of highly profitable "superstar" firms.[S10][S20] OECD's 2024 tax report adds that taxing high-net-worth individuals poses specific policy and compliance challenges, some with cross-border dimensions.[S06]

That means the archive also has to ask:

- will rent, markup, and fee extraction simply pull ordinary gains back upward?
- can top fortunes reconstitute themselves faster than bottom and middle households can accumulate?
- can the public sector and broad household sector keep pace with private asset booms?

For the fuller doctrine, see [`reconcentration-pressure.md`](reconcentration-pressure.md).


## The downside-discipline condition

rev0017 adds one more cross-cutting rule: a good distribution should not depend on **concentrated private upside protected by repeatedly socialised downside**.

An IMF 2025 technical note says public solvency support to banks has often been used in systemic crises, but should remain a **last resort** and be designed to maximise burden sharing, minimise moral hazard, and protect taxpayers.[S32]
A companion IMF 2025 note says broad asset purchases may create moral hazard by providing a **broad, untargeted safety net for asset holders**, whereas more targeted, collateralised support can better limit risk transfer.[S33]

That means the archive should ask not only **who owns** and **how gains are made**, but also **who gets protected from losses**.
A society should not fully pass if large owners or major intermediaries can routinely keep upside while taxpayers, weaker public balance sheets, or later broad-middle retrenchment absorb the downside.

Questions to ask alongside the headline bands:

- when large institutions fail or markets seize up, who takes first loss: shareholders and unsecured creditors, or the public?[S32]
- is support aimed at protecting households and critical functions, or at protecting incumbent asset prices?[S33]
- are rescue tools temporary, priced, conditional, and recoverable through levies, equity, warrants, clawbacks, or similar devices?[S32][S33]
- would the same wealth order still look acceptable if public guarantees and untargeted backstops were removed?

For the fuller doctrine, see [`downside-discipline-and-loss-bearing.md`](downside-discipline-and-loss-bearing.md).

## The public-value-capture condition

rev0018 adds one more cross-cutting rule: a good distribution should not depend on **socially created land and development uplift being quietly privatised**.

OECD's global land-value-capture compendium says land-value-capture instruments allow governments to recover land-value uplifts generated by public interventions such as infrastructure investments or land-use changes.[S34]
OECD's 2025 housing-reform work on Czechia and Poland adds that affordable-housing requirements can be based on the increase in land value from development approvals and that public land can be managed strategically to recover the increase in land values generated by public investment or zoning changes.[S35]

That means the archive should ask not only **who owns**, **how gains are made**, and **who bears losses**, but also **whether collective action is manufacturing private windfalls that should partly return to common wealth and wider access**.
A society should not fully pass if infrastructure, rezoning, development permissions, or public-land decisions repeatedly create large private fortunes while the public balance sheet remains weak and affordable access remains constrained.

Questions to ask alongside the headline bands:

- when infrastructure or zoning raises site values, is part of that uplift recaptured for affordable housing, infrastructure, or public/social wealth?[S34][S35]
- are developer obligations, density gains, or comparable approvals tied to the size of the uplift rather than negotiated away as pure private windfall?[S35]
- is public land managed strategically, or sold off with weak public-purpose conditions and little claim on later appreciation?[S35]
- would the same wealth order still look acceptable if publicly created land and development windfalls were more fully socialised?

For the fuller doctrine, see [`publicly-created-value-and-common-asset-capture.md`](publicly-created-value-and-common-asset-capture.md).

## Why the archive lands near 20 / 50 / 20 / 10

The current attractor was chosen because it seems to satisfy the five tests better than both today's rich-country wealth concentrations and very flat equalisation schemes, once read together with the archive's conditioning rules on floor, control, stewardship, circulation, life-course timing, person-level ownership, reconcentration, social protection, non-extractive wealth formation, downside discipline, and public-value capture.

- It leaves meaningful rewards and room for savings differences.
- It creates a genuinely asset-owning middle.
- It keeps the top tail visible but not constitutionally dominant.
- It leaves space for positive public wealth.
- It can be paired with floor, control, stewardship, circulation, person-level, reconcentration, source-quality, loss-bearing, and public-value-capture constraints so success is not reduced to a single ratio or a short-lived phase.

## What the archive refuses

The archive refuses three temptations:

1. **Pure descriptivism** — treating whatever current rich countries look like as morally normal.
2. **Pure egalitarian aesthetics** — choosing a flat distribution because it looks tidy on paper.
3. **Single-metric closure** — pretending one percentile ratio can settle questions of dependence, control, concentrated stewardship, dynastic persistence, reconcentration risk, internal household dependence, wealth-source quality, downside asymmetry, whether public action is quietly being capitalised into private windfalls, or the amount of insecurity households are forced to self-insure.


## Implementation precondition — visible wealth

rev0009 adds one more selection rule, but it sits differently from the others.
This is not another distributive axis so much as a **governability test**:

- can the state identify ultimate owners of the major assets and vehicles that matter most?
- can it see large transfers and control shifts?
- can it link tax, company, land, and political-finance information well enough to enforce the rules it announces?[S04][S23]

A country should not be treated as safely inside the archive's target if the wealth that matters most is materially illegible.
In that case, the apparent distribution may be too flattering, the tax base too soft, and the anti-capture layer too porous.
The same caution now applies inside households as well: the headline distribution may also be too flattering if title and enforceable claim are materially more concentrated than household-level aggregates imply.[S27][S28][S29][S30]
