# Group stratification and excluded groups

## Thesis

A wealth distribution does not count as morally acceptable if it looks broad in national averages while durable ownership, safe credit, secure tenure, business assets, or inheritances remain systematically closed to **historically subordinated groups**.

This is not a separate sixth axis.
It is a cross-cutting condition on **shares**, **floor**, **circulation**, **timing**, and **person-level ownership**.

## Why averages can mislead

Recent OECD work says household-wealth gaps remain significant across different demographic groups, including gender, migrant status, and geographic location.[S13]
It also reports that, across 20 OECD countries with available data, migrant households hold about **USD 136 000** less net wealth than native-born households on average even after controlling for age, education, and number of adults in the household.[S13][S19]

The same OECD wealth paper warns that most OECD countries still do not systematically collect official equality data on race/ethnicity, sexual orientation and gender identity, and disability, even though such information would help reveal which groups face specific barriers to wealth building.[S13]
Companion OECD opportunity work adds that financial-inclusion and asset-building policies can help support historically disadvantaged groups, but that targeting matters when barriers to saving, credit, entrepreneurship, or housing entry are group-specific.[S19]

## What the archive should ask

- do wealth, homeownership, secure-tenure, savings, pension, land-title, or business-asset gaps remain large for historically subordinated groups after ordinary controls?
- are first footholds into ownership systematically slower, more debt-heavy, or more geographically blocked for those groups?
- are apparently broad national gains being driven mainly by already advantaged groups while excluded groups remain stuck?
- are high-opportunity neighborhoods, mortgage markets, procurement channels, or entrepreneurship pathways still partly closed?
- is the state even collecting enough disaggregated, privacy-protecting data to tell?

## Operational implications

A country should not fully pass if it cannot show credible progress on:

- disaggregated median and mean net wealth,
- positive-net-wealth and liquid-buffer rates by relevant group,
- homeownership and secure-tenure gaps,
- account, pension, land-title, and business-equity access,
- mortgage approval, credit-cost, or collateral disparities where data exist,
- inheritance or large-gift incidence by relevant group where measurable,
- anti-displacement outcomes in high-opportunity areas.[S13][S19]

## Program implications

The archive should favor a layered strategy:

1. **universal broad-ownership institutions** for everyone,
2. **disaggregated measurement with legal safeguards**,
3. **anti-discrimination enforcement** in lending, housing, land administration, entrepreneurship support, and procurement,
4. **stronger targeting** where universal systems are clearly missing specific groups,
5. **asset-building routes that do not require prior collateral, insider knowledge, or family backing**.[S13][S19]

## Bottom line

A society does not become morally acceptable merely because national wealth shares improve.
It has to become open enough that groups historically marked for exclusion can actually **build, keep, and use wealth on roughly civic-equal terms**.
