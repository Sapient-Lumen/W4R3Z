# Intergenerational circulation

## Thesis

A morally acceptable wealth order is not only widely held at a moment in time.
It must also remain **open across generations**.

If birth background strongly predicts who gets assets, housing access, business formation, or long-run security, then even a better-looking wealth snapshot can hide a society drifting toward softened caste.

## Why the archive adds this layer

OECD's 2025 opportunity work reports that **over 25%** at least of total inequality in household market income across the OECD can be attributed to inherited circumstances, and that parental background contributes **over 60%** of inequality of opportunity in most countries.[S18]

Companion OECD analysis reports that in OECD European countries in 2023 having homeowning parents increased the likelihood of owning a home by age 38-46 by **18.7%**, up from **12.7%** in 2011.[S19]

Those facts mean snapshot equalisation is not enough. A country can improve shares somewhat while still making ownership, stability, and life planning depend too heavily on who one's parents were. rev0012 adds a narrower timing warning: even when later-life wealth looks broader, a society still fails if secure ownership arrives too late to shape adulthood for people without wealthy parents.[S05][S19]

## The circulation test

The archive's current best judgment is that a morally acceptable society should pass four circulation tests:

1. **Adult asset access is not tightly predicted by parental position**
   - Parental wealth, education, and housing tenure should matter less than they do in today's inheritance-heavy regimes.[S18][S19]
2. **Large transfers do not dominate entry into ownership**
   - First-home access, entrepreneurship, and security should not depend mainly on inheritances or large gifts.
3. **Younger cohorts can build assets before they inherit**
   - People should not have to wait until midlife or later family transfer to gain their first durable stake.
4. **Timing matters, not only eventual receipt**
   - Earlier footholds count more than late ones; delayed inheritance should not be mistaken for open adulthood.[S05][S19]
5. **Public institutions reopen the future**
   - Social inheritance, public wealth, housing access, education, and anti-rent policy should widen the set of life paths available to people without rich parents.[S18]

## What does not count as success

The archive should treat the following as weak or false progress:

- reducing top shares somewhat while the path into ownership remains strongly inherited,
- giving young adults capital grants that are immediately capitalized into tuition or housing prices,
- celebrating an older broad-middle owner class while younger cohorts are locked out,
- relying on delayed bequests to solve youth assetlessness.

## How this changes the archive's target

rev0006 makes the archive's target explicitly **four-axis**:

1. **aggregate shares** — the 20 / 50 / 20 / 10 working target,
2. **asset floor** — whether ordinary households clear the floor bundle,
3. **control** — whether key assets remain too concentrated to reproduce dependence and rule,
4. **circulation** — whether the society remains open enough that adult asset position is not mainly inherited.

A country does not pass because it looks better on only one, two, or even three axes. For the narrower timing refinement inside circulation, see [`life-course-timing-and-early-footholds.md`](life-course-timing-and-early-footholds.md).

## Practical use

Use the circulation test when evaluating reforms:

- Did the reform reduce the importance of parental wealth for adult outcomes?
- Did it reopen entry into housing and saving for those without rich parents?
- Did it reduce the need to wait for inheritance?
- Did it broaden first-asset acquisition, or only reprice the same scarce assets?
