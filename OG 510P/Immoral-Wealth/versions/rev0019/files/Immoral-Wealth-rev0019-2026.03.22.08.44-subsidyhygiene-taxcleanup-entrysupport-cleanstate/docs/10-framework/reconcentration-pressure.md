# Reconcentration pressure

## Thesis

A wealth distribution is not morally acceptable if it can be improved for a few years and then rapidly **reconcentrates** under ordinary market pressures.
A good order must be judged not only by its snapshot, but by whether it is **durable** against rents, markups, asset-price booms, tax arbitrage, debt drag, and concentrated ownership of the channels through which gains compound.

rev0007 therefore adds a fifth axis: **reconcentration pressure**.

## Why the archive needs this axis

rev0004 established that shares can look better while control stays oligarchic.
rev0005 established that bottom households can own a slice on paper while still lacking a real floor.
rev0006 established that a society can look better cross-sectionally while still hardening into dynasty.

One more failure remains: a country can improve on all of those dimensions **temporarily** while the underlying flow regime still keeps pushing wealth upward.

Examples:

- wages rise, but rent and debt service rise faster,
- housing equity spreads, but access closes and price gains accrue mainly to incumbent owners,
- top shares fall briefly, but capital gains, private-market concentration, and tax leakage rebuild them,
- young adults get small endowments, but asset inflation outruns those grants.

A morally serious target must ask whether broad ownership can **hold**.

## The reconcentration test

A proposed wealth order passes only if it can answer yes to most of the following:

1. Do ordinary households retain a meaningful share of economy-wide gains rather than losing them back through rent, markups, fees, and forced indebtedness?[S07][S10][S17]
2. Are capital gains and business profits taxed, governed, and dispersed strongly enough that the top tail cannot routinely rebuild its command position after reform?[S04][S06]
3. Are competition, labor power, and housing policy strong enough that concentration pressures do not simply reappear in a new legal form?[S08][S10]
4. Can public/social wealth grow alongside private wealth rather than falling behind whenever asset prices rise?[S02]
5. Can young and non-inherited households enter ownership before asset inflation outruns them again?[S13][S19]

If the answer is repeatedly no, then the society has not solved immoral wealth inequality. It has only interrupted it.

## Main channels of reconcentration

### 1. Rent extraction

Housing scarcity, landlord power, monopoly pricing, and fee-heavy finance can quietly pull broad-based gains back upward.[S07][S10]

### 2. Asset-price inflation without entry

House-price or equity-price booms can make existing owners look richer while locking late entrants out. That is not broadening; it is closure with paper appreciation.[S13][S16][S19]

### 3. Tax and legal asymmetry at the top

If large fortunes can convert labor income into lightly taxed gains, use trusts and gifts to move wealth across generations, or arbitrage borders more easily than states can enforce tax rules, concentration will rebuild.[S04][S05][S06]

### 4. Weak labor claims on output

If labor's share keeps falling, broad households remain dependent on transfers or asset bubbles to catch up. That makes equalisation fragile rather than constitutional.[S02][S08][S14]

### 5. Common ownership and superstar concentration

When profits, markups, and investment are increasingly concentrated in a small set of leading firms or common owners, wealth tends to reconcentrate even if headline tax-and-transfer indicators improve for a while.[S10][S20]

## Operational implication

The archive should treat a society as failing the target if it produces only **one-cycle gains**.
A reform counts as durable only if, after a full business cycle or asset-price cycle, the bottom half and middle still hold more wealth, stronger buffers, and wider access than before.

That is why rev0007 makes reconcentration pressure part of the constitutional target rather than leaving it as a secondary implementation worry.
