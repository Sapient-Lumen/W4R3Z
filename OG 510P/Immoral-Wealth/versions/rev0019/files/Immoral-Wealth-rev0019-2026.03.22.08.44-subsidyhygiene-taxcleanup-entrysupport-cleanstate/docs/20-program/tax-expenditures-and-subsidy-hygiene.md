# Tax expenditures and subsidy hygiene

## Thesis

A wealth-equality program can fail even when its headline taxes and transfers look progressive if the state is quietly rebuilding incumbent wealth through **hidden spending in the tax code, credit policy, and asset-building subsidies**.

rev0019 adds a compact operating rule: treat major **tax expenditures and wealth-building subsidies** as distributional choices that belong inside the archive's wealth program, not as neutral background settings.[S31][S36][S37]

## Why this belongs in the archive

OECD's housing-taxation work says uncapped main-residence capital-gains exemptions provide vastly greater benefits to the wealthiest households, mortgage-interest relief has been found regressive and ineffective at raising homeownership rates, and housing tax relief can raise house prices when supply is fixed.[S36]

OECD's retirement-savings work says tax incentives mainly encourage participation and contributions among middle-to-high income earners, while low-income earners respond more to **matching contributions** and **fixed nominal subsidies**.[S37]

Those two findings matter together.
A state can say it supports broad ownership while still spending large sums on instruments that:

- reward households that already own appreciating assets,
- scale support with marginal tax rates or balance size,
- capitalise quickly into land or asset prices,
- or deliver less help to lower-wealth households than an equivalent capped, refundable, matched, or direct instrument would.

## Minimum operating rule

For major wealth-relevant tax expenditures or subsidies, ask five questions:

1. **Who receives the initial benefit?**
   - existing owners, new entrants, employers, financial intermediaries, or a broad base of households?
2. **How is the support delivered?**
   - deduction, exclusion, preferential rate, credit, matching contribution, fixed nominal subsidy, direct public provision, or supply-side investment?
3. **How much of the support capitalises into prices or fees?**
   - especially for housing, land, childcare-linked labor participation, and retirement products.
4. **Does the support reinforce existing balances?**
   - larger mortgages, larger taxable incomes, and larger account balances should not automatically imply larger public support.
5. **Would a capped, refundable, matched, universal-floor, or supply-side alternative do the job better?**

If an instrument fails those tests, the archive should treat it as a **wealth-inequality input**, not merely as a technical tax preference.

## Where the archive should look first

### 1. Owner-occupied housing tax preferences

Priority review items include:

- mortgage-interest deductions or credits,
- uncapped main-residence capital-gains exemptions,
- persistently undertaxed owner-occupied housing relative to other assets,
- first-buyer subsidies that primarily raise bids rather than access.

The archive should prefer reforms that widen entry and compress rents over reforms that simply enlarge the purchasing power of already-positioned buyers.[S36]

### 2. Retirement-saving support

Where the policy goal is to help lower- and middle-income households build long-run assets, the archive should be skeptical of support structures that deliver the largest subsidy through tax-rate progression or large deductible contributions.
It should consider:

- matching contributions,
- fixed nominal subsidies,
- refundable credits,
- simple defaults and automatic enrolment,
- and stronger public pensions where the private-incentive route is fiscally costly and weakly targeted.[S37]

### 3. Business, saving, or place-based subsidies with wealth effects

The archive should also review subsidies outside the classic housing/pension boxes when they materially affect wealth accumulation, land values, local monopoly, or incumbent advantage.
The relevant question is not just whether a subsidy raises investment, but whether it leaves behind **broader ownership, lower extraction, and stronger entry**.

## What this changes programmatically

rev0019 pushes the archive toward four habits:

- **count tax expenditures as spending** for distributional purposes,
- **publish beneficiary profiles** for major asset-related subsidies and tax preferences,
- **prefer capped / refundable / matched / fixed-amount support** over open-ended upside-down subsidies,
- **test capitalization** before calling a subsidy pro-ownership or pro-middle-class.[S31][S36][S37]

## Failure modes to avoid

- calling an owner subsidy "middle-class support" when most of the value flows to higher-income or already-owning households,
- measuring only statutory generosity rather than net benefit after price capitalization,
- leaving tax expenditures off the main distributional scorecard because they sit on the revenue side of the budget,
- assuming private retirement subsidies are progressive merely because participation is voluntary,
- and defending open-ended tax preferences after their original access rationale has plainly collapsed.

## Bottom line

A society should not celebrate broad ownership while financing it through **upside-down subsidies** that mainly reward incumbent wealth or bid up asset prices.
rev0019 therefore adds a simpler discipline:
**clean the hidden wealth state, not only the visible tax schedule.**
