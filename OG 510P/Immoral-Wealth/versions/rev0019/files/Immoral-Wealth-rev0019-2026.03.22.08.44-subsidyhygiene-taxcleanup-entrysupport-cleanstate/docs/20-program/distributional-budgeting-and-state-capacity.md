# Distributional budgeting and state capacity

## Thesis

A wealth-equality program that lives only in white papers is not yet a governing program.
It has to enter the **budget process**, the administrative data stack, and the routine machinery that decides what gets funded, delayed, or cut.

rev0015 adds a compact operating rule: countries should not rely only on good targets and good laws.
They should also build a **distributional budgeting discipline** that tests major measures early, across the archive's wealth scoreboard, and with enough disaggregation to catch person-level, cohort, and group-level failures that headline averages miss.[S13][S19][S31]

## Why this belongs in the archive

OECD's 2024 budgeting report says public expenditure, including transfers, plays a major role in reducing income inequality and argues for integrating **distributional impact analysis** directly into the budget process.[S31]
The same report also says countries use microsimulation models, results-based budgeting frameworks, linked administrative data, and ex ante plus ex post analysis to understand who gains and loses from spending choices.[S31]

That matters here because the archive's program is not a one-instrument tax plan.
It is a bundle touching labor, housing, inheritance, social insurance, public wealth, public investment, debt pressure, and anti-capture institutions.
Without a budgeting layer, governments can endorse the archive in principle while still passing budgets that quietly rebuild concentration through omissions, mistargeting, or austerity in the wrong places.

## Minimum operating rule

For major wealth-relevant packages, ask four questions **before** the budget is locked:

1. **Percentile effect** — what happens to bottom-half, middle-40, next-9, and top-tail wealth formation?
2. **Floor effect** — what happens to liquid buffers, debt stress, secure tenure, and first durable asset access?
3. **Closure effect** — what happens to inheritance channels, timing, person-level ownership, and excluded-group access?
4. **Durability effect** — does the package lower reconcentration pressure, or merely produce a temporary improvement that asset prices, markups, rents, or top-tail avoidance will later reverse?

A package that improves one surface while worsening the others should not be described as a successful wealth-equality measure.

## What to wire into the budget process

### 1. Ex ante distributional testing

Run distributional impact analysis early enough to shape policy design rather than merely justify it after the fact.[S31]
For this archive, the test should go beyond disposable income and include:

- housing affordability and secure-tenure effects,
- wealth-building and debt effects,
- inheritance and first-foothold effects,
- person-level title and pension-right effects where relevant,
- and disaggregated effects across historically subordinated groups where the legal and data framework allows.[S13][S19][S27][S28][S29][S30][S31]

### 2. Linked data and model capacity

A serious program needs connected administrative data, not only surveys.
OECD's budgeting report highlights the value of linking tax and expenditure administrative data with survey material and disaggregating as fully as possible while protecting confidentiality.[S31]

For this archive, the useful minimum is a state that can connect at least some of the following:

- tax records,
- transfer and pension data,
- land and property records,
- firm / beneficial-ownership information where available,
- education and labour-market data,
- and housing-cost or rent-support data.

The point is not surveillance for its own sake.
It is to stop governments from governing wealth inequality with instruments they cannot actually see.

### 3. Public distributional statements

Major budgets should publish a compact statement of likely distributional effects and the uncertainties around them.
That should include not just income-decile effects but, where feasible, the archive's wealth-relevant questions:
who gets a foothold, who stays overburdened, who remains debt-fragile, and which groups still miss the main access channels.[S31]

### 4. Ex post correction

Distributional analysis should not end at passage.
Governments should test whether the package actually delivered the forecasted reduction in closure, fragility, or concentration, then revise design when it did not.
A policy that only looks progressive in ex ante modelling but capitalises into land prices, fees, or markups is not a success.

## What this changes programmatically

This operating rule pushes the archive toward three implementation habits:

- **budget packages instead of isolated measures** — tax, housing, labor, social-floor, and public-wealth measures should be evaluated together;
- **wealth-aware analysis rather than income-only analysis** — because many failures in this archive arrive through assets, debt, timing, and control rather than through annual cash income alone;
- **disaggregated implementation** — because household averages and national averages can hide person-level and group-level failures.[S13][S27][S28][S29][S30][S31]

## Failure modes to avoid

- treating distributional analysis as a late public-relations appendix,
- modelling only income effects while ignoring wealth formation, debt, and tenure,
- using data too aggregated to detect timing, person-level, or group-level closure,
- passing asset-building subsidies without checking whether they capitalise into prices,
- and cutting public services or public investment in ways that force households back into precautionary saving, debt, or delayed independence.[S21][S22][S31]

## Bottom line

The archive still does not claim there is one magical metric or one magical policy.
rev0015 adds a simpler point:
**a good wealth-equality program needs a fiscal operating system.**
Otherwise even well-designed reforms remain too easy to dilute, mistarget, or reverse.


## rev0018 refinement — Budgeting for publicly created uplift

Infrastructure plans, rezoning packages, transport corridors, and public-land strategies should be treated as **distributional events**, not only as planning or capital-spending events.
A serious distributional-budgeting system should ask, before adoption:

- what uplift is likely to be created by the public decision,
- who is positioned to receive it,
- what share will be recaptured through levies, obligations, leasehold, land policy, or public ownership,
- and whether the recaptured share will widen access or strengthen long-run public/social wealth.[S31][S34][S35]

Without that discipline, a government can spend progressively in one line of the budget while quietly capitalising a large part of the gain into private land and development fortunes elsewhere.

## rev0019 refinement — Tax expenditures are spending in disguise

Asset-building deductions, exclusions, credits, concessional rates, and other tax expenditures should be routed through the same distributional discipline as direct spending.
A serious budgeting system should ask, before renewal or expansion:

- who receives the subsidy before any price effects,
- how much of it capitalises into land, house prices, fees, or larger account balances,
- whether the instrument scales its value with marginal tax rates or pre-existing wealth,
- and whether a capped, refundable, matched, fixed-amount, or direct-service alternative would widen access more cleanly.[S31][S36][S37]

Without that rule, governments can cut visible spending on equality with one hand while preserving a large hidden wealth state with the other.
