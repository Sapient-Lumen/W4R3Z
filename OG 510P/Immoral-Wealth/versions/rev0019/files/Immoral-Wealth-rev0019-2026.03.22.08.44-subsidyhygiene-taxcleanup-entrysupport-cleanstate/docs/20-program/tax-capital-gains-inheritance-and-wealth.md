# Tax capital gains, inheritance, and wealth

## Thesis

Tax policy should compress immoral concentration with the least avoidable distortion and the greatest anti-dynastic force.

## Current best judgment

The IMF's 2024 note argues that taxing **actual capital returns** is generally less distortive and more equitable than a recurrent net wealth tax, and recommends strengthening capital income taxation, especially capital gains, closing loopholes, and using inheritance taxation to address dynastic wealth.[S04]

The OECD's inheritance-tax work goes further: it argues there is a good case for greater use of inheritance taxation, especially a **recipient-based inheritance tax** with an exemption for low-value inheritances, and notes that inheritance taxes tend to have more limited effects on savings than other taxes on wealthy taxpayers. The same OECD work also warns that wealth is likely to remain concentrated among older cohorts for longer and that inheritance age is likely to rise.[S05]

rev0006 adds a tighter normative reason for this package. OECD's 2025 opportunity work argues that by taxing high-value transfers, inheritance taxation can enhance equality of opportunity and reduce the concentration of wealth. rev0012 adds a timing reason as well: if large transfers are arriving later in life, recipient-based taxation and lifetime-receipts logic help prevent late bequests from quietly carrying too much of the system's distributive weight.[S05][S19]

## Program order

### 1. Fix capital income taxation

- tax capital gains more consistently with labor income,
- reduce preferential treatment of large unrealized gains at transfer,
- strengthen information reporting and cross-border enforcement.

### 2. Strengthen inheritance and gift taxation

- recipient-based design,
- progressive lifetime receipts logic where administratively possible,
- strong anti-avoidance treatment of trusts and inter vivos gifts,
- exemptions for small inheritances, not for dynastic engineering.[S05]

### 3. Use recurrent net wealth taxes selectively

Recurrent net wealth taxes can still be useful, especially where elite wealth is extreme and asset records are strong. But this archive does not currently treat them as the first universal instrument. They are a possible complement, not the whole plan.[S04][S05]

## Principle

Tax **rents, windfalls, and dynastic transfers** harder than ordinary work. A society that taxes wages heavily but lightly taxes concentrated capital is financing equality with the wrong base.

rev0006 adds one more interpretive rule: tax design should be judged not only by revenue or top-share compression, but by whether it reduces the dependence of life chances on family wealth.


## Legibility and enforcement

rev0009 adds a practical warning: top-tail tax design is downstream of visible wealth.

The IMF's 2024 note argues that reforms aimed at wealthy households need anti-avoidance legislation, enforcement, and cross-border information sharing, while inheritance taxation needs integrated gift rules and attention to trusts and similar devices.[S04]
So the archive should treat these as part of tax design rather than administrative afterthoughts:

- trace beneficial owners rather than stopping at legal entities,
- integrate gift, inheritance, and trust reporting,
- build valuation and audit capacity for hard-to-price assets,
- use cross-border information channels as a normal part of top-tail enforcement,
- prefer taxes whose base can actually be seen and defended when state capacity is weak.

A nominally progressive system that cannot see the top tail clearly will often overtax the visible upper middle while undertaxing the truly rich.

## rev0019 refinement — Clean the hidden wealth state too

This archive should now read tax design together with **tax expenditures and subsidy design**.
Housing and retirement subsidies can become a back door through which governments rebuild incumbent wealth while claiming to support the middle class.
OECD's housing-taxation work says uncapped main-residence capital-gains exemptions provide vastly greater benefits to wealthier households and mortgage-interest relief has been found regressive and ineffective at raising homeownership rates.[S36]
OECD's retirement-savings work says tax incentives mainly move middle-to-high income earners, while low-income earners respond better to matching contributions and fixed nominal subsidies.[S37]

So the archive should prefer, where the policy goal is broad asset-building:

- capped and visible support over open-ended deductions,
- refundable credits, matching contributions, or fixed nominal subsidies over rate-driven upside-down relief,
- and direct public or supply-side measures where tax preferences mostly capitalise into prices.
