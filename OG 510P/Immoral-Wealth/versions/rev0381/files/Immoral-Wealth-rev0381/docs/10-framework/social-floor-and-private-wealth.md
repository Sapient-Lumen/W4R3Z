# Social floor and private wealth

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's core conditioning claims into a **stable reading rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the fuller target procedure in [`target-selection-rules.md`](target-selection-rules.md):
**how should the strength, claimability, and automaticity of the public/social floor change what private wealth distribution a society morally needs?**

The archive does **not** judge private wealth in the abstract.
The same private balance-sheet pattern means different things in two societies if one socialises major life risks while the other pushes them back onto households.
A society with stronger healthcare protection, pensions, unemployment insurance, disability support, childcare, education, housing security, and non-punitive access rules may need somewhat less **private** buffer wealth for ordinary freedom than a society that leaves those risks largely privatised.[S02][S17][S21][S22][S39][S139]

But this note is a **conditioning rule, not an unlimited substitution license**.
A stronger social floor can lower the amount of private wealth households need merely to stay safe.
It does **not** excuse floor failure, absent person-level title, weak footholds, late entry, gatekeeper dependence, or wealth-to-rule conversion.

## Minimum reading rule

Read private wealth **inside the surrounding security regime**.
A private distribution should be read more favorably when the social floor is strong, broadly accessible, timely, low-friction, and non-punitive in ordinary life.
Read it more harshly when households must self-insure against predictable essentials through large private balances, family backup, or defensive debt.
Formal provision should count for less when it is hard to claim, slow to arrive, easy to interrupt, or conditional on destructive spend-down.[S17][S21][S22][S39][S139]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**the better the common floor, the less private buffer wealth a free society should require merely to avoid humiliation, dependence, or preventable shocks.**
But the archive should never let that intuition collapse into: **"services are good, so weak private footholds no longer matter."**

## The five main false passes

### 1. Public promises are mistaken for delivered security

Statutory entitlements, nominal coverage, or positive public wealth do not count like lived security when access is late, fragmented, discretionary, or full of renewal friction.[S02][S39][S139]

### 2. High private saving is mistaken for freedom

Households may appear wealthier only because they are forced to carry large precautionary balances against healthcare, housing, care, unemployment, disability, or old-age risk that should be more socialised.[S17][S21][S22]

### 3. Positive public wealth is mistaken for household independence

A country does not pass this surface merely because the public balance sheet is positive.
Public/social wealth matters when it actually lowers household exposure, widens access, or strengthens non-market provision.
It counts for less when it sits far from ordinary people while bottom-half households still need large private buffers to survive ordinary life.[S02]

### 4. One-time assets are used to excuse structural underprovision

Capital grants, matched savings, or private account subsidies should count for less when they are asked to offset permanently weak healthcare, pensions, housing security, or income support.
The archive should not let a small foothold substitute for a missing common floor.[S17][S21][S22]

### 5. Stronger services are treated as an unlimited substitute for private footholds

Even an excellent social floor should not excuse negative or near-zero net wealth, absent liquid margin, weak tenure, lack of person-level title, or strong gatekeeper dependence.
This note conditions the private target.
It does not erase it.

## The six standing conditioning surfaces

### 1. Health and care financial protection

The social floor is stronger when illness, disability, or care need less often turns into debt distress, asset depletion, treatment avoidance, or coercive family dependence.[S21]

### 2. Old-age and disability security

The social floor is stronger when ordinary adults do not need extreme lifelong private accumulation merely to avoid poverty or humiliation in old age or disability.[S22]

### 3. Housing security and non-market shelter strength

The social floor is stronger when secure renting, social housing, anti-displacement rules, and affordable access reduce the need to chase ownership at any price merely to stabilise life.
This does not eliminate the case for broad private housing footholds.
It reduces how much private housing wealth households must hold just to escape chronic insecurity.[S17]

### 4. Income-shock protection and continuity

The social floor is stronger when temporary job loss or family shock does not immediately force distress borrowing, forced moves, or liquidation of modest footholds.[S17]

### 5. Claimability, automaticity, and low-friction access

The social floor is stronger when protection is easy to reach, easy to keep, and usable by ordinary people without repeated administrative combat, specialist navigation, or humiliating gatekeeping.[S39][S139]

### 6. Non-punitive interaction with modest wealth

The social floor is stronger when lower-income households can keep modest savings, early claims, or starter assets without being forced back into spend-down traps, sharp cliffs, or destructive eligibility rules.[S39]

## Fast use rule

Use this note in four steps:

1. **Read the private floor first.**  
   Ask whether the bottom half has real footholds, liquid margin, secure tenure, defensible claims, and debt that is not chronically extractive.
2. **Then read the surrounding social floor.**  
   Ask how much health, care, housing, income-shock, and old-age security is genuinely socialised and delivered in practice.
3. **Count only what is usable.**  
   Discount protection that is formal, delayed, fragmented, highly conditional, or clawed back through asset punishment.
4. **Refuse unlimited substitution.**  
   A stronger social floor can justify somewhat lighter private requirements near the archive's acceptable envelope, but it cannot excuse decisive failures in foothold quality, person-level independence, timing, visibility, or non-rule.

Default shortcut:

- **strong private floor + strong social floor** -> the case is read more favorably and can sit nearer the archive's lighter acceptable envelope;
- **weak private floor + weak social floor** -> the case should be read much more harshly because households are being asked to self-insure through insecurity;
- **mixed case** -> ask whether the social floor is genuinely reducing required private wealth or merely papering over weak household footholds with promises that are hard to use.

## Non-substitution rule

Do **not** let the following excuse weak private footholds:

- positive public wealth on paper,
- generous-looking statutory programs with low take-up or heavy friction,
- one-time capital grants,
- high measured private saving driven by forced self-insurance,
- or average service quality that still leaves major groups with weak access.

For the archive's narrower rule on acceptable nearby variation, see [`acceptable-envelopes-and-substitution-limits.md`](acceptable-envelopes-and-substitution-limits.md).
For the companion floor-side discount rules, see [`minimum-asset-floor.md`](minimum-asset-floor.md).

## Program implication

Once this surface is read clearly, two policy mistakes become easier to avoid.
First, the archive should stop mistaking harsh self-insurance regimes for morally stronger private ownership cultures.
Second, it should stop using public/social provision as an excuse to postpone broad private footholds indefinitely.
The right direction is **broad private ownership inside a strong, claimable, non-punitive social floor**.
That is why this note sits between the archive's floor doctrine, acceptable-envelope logic, and program portfolio.

## Bottom line

The archive should now read social floor and private wealth in this order:

1. **Ask how much ordinary security is really socialised and usable in practice.**
2. **Lower the required private buffer only to the extent that the common floor actually reduces household exposure.**
3. **Refuse to treat public/social strength as an unlimited substitute for person-level footholds, timing, title, visibility, or non-rule.**
4. **Prefer societies that combine a strong common floor with broad private wealth rather than forcing households to choose between the two.**

The point is not to pick one side of a public-versus-private argument.
It is to stop misreading a wealth order because the surrounding security regime has been ignored.[S02][S17][S21][S22][S39][S139]
