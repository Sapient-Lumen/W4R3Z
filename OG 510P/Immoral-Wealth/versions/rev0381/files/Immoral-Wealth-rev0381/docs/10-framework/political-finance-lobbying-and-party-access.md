# Political finance, lobbying, and party access

Political finance is a wealth-order surface because parties and campaigns decide which wealth reforms are even thinkable. Lobbying is a wealth-order surface because technical details decide whether a tax, housing, registry, labor, competition, or procurement reform survives contact with the actors it regulates.

The archive should distinguish legitimate participation from unequal constitutional voice. Businesses, unions, civil society groups, and experts may argue. The breach appears when large spenders can dominate candidate viability, policy calendars, technical drafting, or exemption design while weaker groups enter too late or too thinly to matter.[S187][S188][S189][S199][S200]

## The four-channel map

### 1. Candidate and party finance

Look for contribution limits, source bans, public funding, real-time reporting, independent audits, permissibility checks, and sanctions. The International IDEA database is the baseline comparative rail because it separates private-income bans and limits, public funding, spending regulation, reporting, oversight, and sanctions.[S187]

### 2. Independent spending and third-party campaigns

Formal contribution limits can coexist with unlimited independent expenditure, non-party campaigns, super PAC analogues, issue ads, digital ads, and transfers from donor-obscuring entities. In the United States, the FEC says independent expenditures are not contributions and are not subject to limits; the 2023-2024 cycle reported $4.4 billion in independent-expenditure disbursements.[S184][S185]

### 3. Dark money and donor-obscuring vehicles

The archive should treat nontransparent electoral spending as an opacity penalty, not a neutral unknown. Brennan Center analysis estimated more than $1.9 billion in dark money in 2024 federal races and described that as likely an underestimate because some categories cannot be reliably tracked.[S186]

### 4. Lobbying, advisory, and sponsored expertise

Lobbying can improve policy when interests and evidence are visible. It becomes a wealth-power rail when registers miss actors, clients, subjects, meetings, informal access, think-tank sponsorship, third-party campaigns, or foreign-state interests. OECD's revised standard explicitly treats the modern influence landscape as broader than classic lobbyist registration.[S188][S189]

## Minimum evidence pack

A case memo should not certify this surface without:

- campaign/party receipts, donor concentration, and public-funding structure;
- independent-expenditure and third-party spending rules;
- disclosure timing relative to votes and decisions;
- lobbying-register coverage, clients, subjects, meetings, and sanctions;
- treatment of nonprofits, shell entities, unincorporated associations, foundations, think tanks, and digital advertisers;
- source-of-funds checks for domestic, corporate, and foreign-linked donors;
- enforcement body independence, budget, investigatory powers, and sanction history.

Where a case lacks these data, mark hard evidence debt. Where opacity is a feature of the system, apply an opacity penalty.
