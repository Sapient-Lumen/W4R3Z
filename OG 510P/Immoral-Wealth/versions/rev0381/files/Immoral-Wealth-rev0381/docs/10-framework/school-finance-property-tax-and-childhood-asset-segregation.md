# School finance, property tax, and childhood asset segregation

Schools are not private assets, but they are part of a child’s wealth pathway. They shape later earnings, place access, social networks, college readiness, public safety, and the first threshold moments of entry. A wealth order therefore cannot certify childhood equality from household wealth data alone.[S238][S239][S241]

Property-tax school finance can be both useful and dangerous. It can give localities stable revenue and democratic accountability. It can also tie school quality, facilities, extracurriculars, and local tax effort to existing property wealth and residential segregation.[S240][S242]

## Core rule

A case cannot pass the early-foothold gate if school finance lets local property wealth buy systematically better childhood public assets while poor, racially subordinated, rural, or shrinking places face lower operating funds, weaker facilities, higher tax effort, or deteriorating local tax bases.[S238][S241][S242]

## What to score

The school-finance screen must separate:

1. **operating adequacy** — enough recurring money for staffing, support, instruction, and special needs;
2. **distribution progressivity** — more need receives more funding after cost adjustment;
3. **facilities adequacy** — safe, modern, maintained buildings and capital access;
4. **tax effort and tax base** — whether poor places must tax harder for less;
5. **race/place veto** — whether income-progressive formulas still leave racialized or segregated funding gaps;
6. **post-relief cliff** — whether one-time aid masked structural gaps.

## Policy consequence

The opening package should not simply transfer cash to households after childhood segregation has done its work. Where this screen fails, route to state-aid equalization, weighted student funding, facilities capital, poor-district tax relief, capital-match redesign, regional revenue sharing, and race/place outcome audits.[S238][S240][S241][S242]
