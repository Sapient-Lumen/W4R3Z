# Portability and non-forfeiture across ordinary transitions

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's most important continuity judgments into a **stable portability rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the full floor, timing, person-level ownership, claimability, gatekeeper, and defensibility notes:
**when should a foothold count less because ordinary movement through life can strand it, reset it, or force people to give it up?**

The archive already asks whether a foothold exists at all, whether it arrives early enough to shape adulthood, whether it belongs to the person rather than only to a household, whether people can actually claim it, whether one gatekeeper can choke it, and whether it can be cheaply proved and defended in conflict.[S28][S29][S30][S139][S41][S44]
But another question sits underneath those screens:
**can ordinary adults carry the foothold with them while changing jobs, sectors, providers, regions, households, or care patterns?**
A pension right, savings match, social-inheritance claim, housing protection, training credit, business entitlement, or long-run asset path counts less when the answer is no, even if headline enrolment or nominal vesting looks impressive.[S41]

This matters because a society can look more equal in long-run claims while still failing a basic independence test.
If keeping the foothold requires staying with one employer, one scheme, one place, one relationship, or one administrative channel long enough not to lose it, then the society is still rewarding immobility rather than securing freedom.[S41]
OECD's 2025 retirement-savings work gives the clearest operating example: mandatory defined-benefit schemes often require **10 to 25 years** of service before benefits vest, lack of portability across schemes can impede accrual across a full career, and mobile workers can miss minimum-service periods in multiple schemes and come away with much less than a stable worker with similar total effort.[S41]

This note therefore sits between the archive's timing, person-level ownership, claimability, gatekeeper, and implementation notes.
It asks whether a foothold survives ordinary transition strongly enough to count as part of an open life rather than as a reward for staying put.

## Minimum reading rule

Read a foothold through a **carry-it-with-you screen**.
A claim counts more when an ordinary holder can change employer, sector, region, household status, provider, or care arrangement without resetting the path, restarting the vesting clock, fragmenting the record, or accepting major loss just to preserve what is already supposed to be theirs.[S41]
It counts less when continuity depends on remaining attached to one sponsor, one scheme, one household configuration, one locality, or one administrative silo for unusually long periods.

The archive's compact intuition is:
**a foothold does not count fully when ordinary movement through life can quietly unmake it.**

Read this note more harshly when the apparent success of the lower half depends on:
- long uninterrupted tenure with one employer, sector, or scheme before meaningful security arrives,[S41]
- weak transfer or aggregation rails across providers, schemes, jurisdictions, or benefit channels,[S41]
- household or relationship arrangements that strand claims when adults separate, relocate, or leave unsafe settings,[S28][S29][S30]
- or mobility paths that require destructive liquidation, loss of matches, restart of waiting periods, or acceptance of large penalties.

## The six main false passes

### 1. Eventual vesting is mistaken for portable security

A society should count less success when a foothold technically exists but only after uninterrupted attachment long enough that normal mobility becomes punitive.
A right that survives only for stayers is weaker than it looks.[S41]

### 2. Coverage is mistaken for continuous accrual

The archive should count less success when many people are enrolled somewhere, but mobile workers still lose accumulation because records do not combine cleanly across schemes, employers, or sectors.[S41]

### 3. Sponsor portability is mistaken for person portability

A country should count less progress when the claim is tied mainly to one employer, scheme, provider, household unit, or local channel rather than traveling cleanly with the person.
The more the claim belongs to the sponsor, the less it belongs to the holder.

### 4. Geographic or household movement penalties are mistaken for neutral design

The archive should count less success when moving for work, safety, care, separation, widowhood, or family reorganisation predictably strands or dilutes the claim.
Mobility penalties are constitutional failures, not small frictions.

### 5. Transfer delay is mistaken for harmless administration

A society should count less success when rights are portable in principle but transfers, consolidations, or recognitions take so long that ordinary holders lose bargaining power in the meantime.
A claim that follows too late still leaves people stuck.

### 6. Asset broadening for the stable is mistaken for openness for the ordinary

The archive should count less success when wealth-building works mainly for people with stable careers, stable households, stable places, and stable paperwork, while ordinary movers repeatedly lose ground.[S41]

## The six standing portability surfaces

### 1. Vesting-horizon surface

The archive should ask how long uninterrupted attachment is required before the claim becomes materially secure.
The longer the holder must stay put, the less portable the foothold is.[S41]

### 2. Transfer-and-aggregation surface

A claim counts more when records, balances, service credits, and matched contributions can be combined or transferred across schemes, employers, or sectors without major leakage or dispute.[S41]
It counts less when movement fragments the path into partial or unusable pieces.

### 3. Person-versus-sponsor attachment surface

The archive should ask whether the foothold is really attached to the person or mainly to a sponsor, plan, provider, tax unit, or household configuration.
Person-attached continuity counts more than sponsor-attached continuity.

### 4. Place-and-household transition surface

A foothold counts more when moving locality, separating households, widowhood, caregiving shifts, or family reorganisation do not strand it.
It counts less when ordinary transitions trigger loss, re-qualification, or dependency on a previous arrangement.[S28][S29][S30]

### 5. Non-forfeiture-and-penalty surface

The archive should ask what must be surrendered in order to move.
A claim counts more when ordinary transition does not require large penalties, destructive liquidation, restart of waiting periods, or loss of prior matches.

### 6. Continuity-timeliness surface

A foothold counts more when consolidation, recognition, or transfer happens quickly enough that the holder can still act freely in ordinary time.
It counts less when portability exists only after delays long enough to weaken exit, planning, or bargaining now.

## Fast use rule

Use this note in four steps:

1. **Ask what happens at ordinary change.**  
   Score the foothold only after checking job change, sector change, provider change, move, separation, widowhood, and care interruption.
2. **Discount long uninterrupted-attachment requirements.**  
   Count less success when ordinary people must stay with one sponsor for unusually long periods before the claim becomes meaningfully real.[S41]
3. **Check whether the path follows the person without major loss.**  
   Ask whether records combine, balances transfer, and rights continue without penalties, resets, or destructive liquidation.
4. **Count low-loss continuity as real progress.**  
   Give strong credit when person-attached records, fast transfer rails, short vesting, and non-forfeiture let ordinary adults keep the foothold while moving through life.[S41]

Default shortcut:

- **person-attached claim plus quick transfer plus short vesting plus low-loss exit** -> count strongly because ordinary movement does not destroy the foothold;
- **sponsor-tied, fragmented, or restart-prone claims** -> discount because broadening still depends too much on staying put;
- **mixed case** -> ask whether an ordinary move for work, care, safety, or separation would materially reduce what is already supposed to be theirs.

## Program implication

Once portability is read clearly, the archive becomes stricter about one common false comfort: broadening that is real only for people who can stay in one lane long enough not to be reset.[S41]
That changes program judgment in three directions.
First, person-attached records, transfer defaults, aggregation rails, and non-forfeiture rules deserve more weight, because real freedom requires carrying footholds across work, place, and family change rather than only accumulating them under ideal stability.
Second, long vesting horizons and scheme fragmentation should be treated as structural dilution, not as small technical imperfections.
Third, measurement should stop treating enrolment, nominal vesting, or aggregate balances as morally complete when ordinary movers repeatedly lose ground for reasons built into the path itself.

This is why portability belongs inside the archive's constitutional reading of distribution rather than as a pension or benefits footnote.
A society is closer to justice when ordinary adults can move through work, care, geography, and household change **without leaving their footholds behind**.[S41]

## Bottom line

The archive should now read portability in this order:

1. **Ask what ordinary movement through life does to the foothold.**
2. **Discount claims that still depend too heavily on staying with one sponsor, scheme, place, or household arrangement.**
3. **Treat reset-prone or forfeiture-prone mobility as a real constitutional failure.**
4. **Count person-attached continuity, fast transfer rails, and low-loss exit as real equality gains.**
5. **Refuse to let broad enrolment or eventual vesting count like secure independence when ordinary movement can still quietly unmake the claim.**
