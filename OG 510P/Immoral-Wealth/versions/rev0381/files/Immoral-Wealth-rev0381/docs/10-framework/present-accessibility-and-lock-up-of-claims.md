# Present accessibility, lock-up, and usable claims

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's recurring but easy-to-misread judgments into a **stable present-accessibility rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the full floor, timing, claimability, and portability notes:
**when should a society count less success because a claim that exists on paper is too delayed, too restricted, too penalty-laden, or too contingency-bound to function as present independence?**

The archive already treats later-life security, protected long-run savings, and restricted-purpose capital as real goods.
But they are not the same thing as a foothold that can already change bargaining power in ordinary adulthood.[S13][S17][S19][S41]
A country can therefore look better on household balance sheets, pension participation, subsidised saving, or durable account balances while still leaving the lower half short of a usable present buffer.

This note sits between the archive's floor, timing, claimability, portability, gatekeeper, and forced-self-insurance notes.
Use it when the question is not whether a claim exists at all, but whether it is **usable enough, soon enough, and with little enough leakage** to count as present freedom rather than only deferred security.[S13][S17][S19][S41]

## Minimum reading rule

Read owned or promised claims through a **present-accessibility screen**.
A claim counts more when an ordinary holder can use it now or on a short, predictable timetable with low penalty, low discretion, and low need for sale, borrowing, or hardship pleading.
It counts less when value is technically there but remains locked until retirement, major hardship, sale of the primary foothold, employer continuity, age thresholds, or some other delayed contingency.[S19][S41]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**paper wealth should not count like present independence when the holder cannot actually mobilise it in ordinary life without heavy leakage, exceptional triggers, or future-only timing.**

Read this note more harshly when apparent lower-half progress depends on:
- retirement balances being used to excuse thin current liquid floors,
- child or starter accounts arriving too late to shape the key transition into adulthood,
- hardship withdrawal rules that make access formally possible but practically rare,
- home equity being treated as a current buffer even though access requires sale, refinancing, or major collateral risk,
- or restricted-purpose balances being used to stand in for general bargaining power.[S13][S17][S19][S41]

## The six main false passes

### 1. Paper wealth is mistaken for present bargaining power

A society should count less success when households are described as asset-bearing mainly because they hold balances that cannot be used in ordinary time without waiting, pleading, or paying large penalties.
Nominal possession is not the same thing as present operational freedom.[S19][S41]

### 2. Any withdrawal option is mistaken for low-friction access

The archive should count less success when a claim is called usable simply because a withdrawal channel exists somewhere in the rules.
Access that depends on hardship certification, employer separation, narrow exemptions, or large tax and fee leakage counts less than low-friction access.[S41]

### 3. Early title is mistaken for an early foothold

A society should count less success when a claim is created in childhood or early adulthood but cannot actually be used until much later.
Early bookkeeping is not the same thing as an early foothold if the value remains locked through the years when education, housing entry, migration, and family formation are decided.[S19]

### 4. Later-life security is mistaken for a present floor

Retirement protection can be highly valuable and still not substitute for current liquid resilience.
The archive should therefore count less success when strong long-run balances are used to excuse weak short-run buffers, debt fragility, or inability to meet ordinary shocks today.[S13][S17][S41]

### 5. Restricted-purpose capital is mistaken for general independence

A claim counts less as a general foothold when it can only be used for a narrow category, in narrow institutions, or under narrow life scripts.
Purpose-bound capital may still deserve credit, but not as if it were the same thing as broad present bargaining power.[S19][S41]

### 6. Sale-contingent access is mistaken for resilient ownership

The archive should count less success when households are said to have a buffer mainly because they could sell the dwelling, liquidate the business, or borrow against the core foothold that is supposed to keep them secure.
Access that requires destabilising the main asset should not count like ordinary usable wealth.[S13][S17]

## The six standing accessibility surfaces

### 1. Time-to-use surface

The archive should ask how soon the holder can use the claim for ordinary stabilisation, entry, or adjustment.
Short, predictable, low-discretion access counts more than claims that sit behind long age thresholds, vesting periods, or delayed maturity dates.[S19][S41]

### 2. Penalty and leakage surface

A claim counts less when taxes, fees, employer loss, forced liquidation discounts, refinancing spreads, or similar leakage materially shrink what can actually be used.
What matters is not only the face value but the usable remainder after ordinary access costs.[S41]

### 3. Hardship-gate and discretion surface

The archive should ask whether access depends on proving distress to an administrator, employer, provider, or other gatekeeper.
A claim that becomes usable only after hardship pleading or discretionary approval counts less as ordinary independence.
This surface should be read with the archive's claimability and gatekeeper notes.[S139][S41]

### 4. Purpose-binding surface

The archive should ask whether the claim can be used where real life pressure lands.
Balances restricted to schooling, housing purchase, retirement, or other narrow endpoints may still matter, but they should not be scored as if they solve general liquidity, mobility, or bargaining needs.[S19][S41]

### 5. Sale, collateral, and destabilisation surface

The archive should ask whether using the claim requires selling, borrowing against, or otherwise destabilising the very foothold being measured.
Home equity, business equity, or locked long-run balances count less as present access when mobilisation predictably endangers continuity.[S13][S17]

### 6. Measurement and scoring surface

The archive should ask whether the case is being judged with any distinction between **owned value** and **usable present value** at all.
Household net wealth, account participation, pension coverage, or durable-asset ownership should be read against liquidity, withdrawal conditions, penalty schedules, and timing rather than treated as self-sufficient evidence of current freedom.[S13][S17][S19][S41]

## Fast use rule

Use this note in four steps:

1. **Ask whether the holder can use the claim in ordinary time.**  
   Do not score a balance as present freedom before checking access timing, trigger conditions, and who decides.
2. **Check what is lost on the way to access.**  
   Ask what penalties, fees, taxes, collateral risks, or sale requirements shrink or endanger the claim.
3. **Separate later-life security from current footing.**  
   Give credit for protected long-run claims, but do not let them excuse weak present buffers or late footholds.
4. **Score restricted capital at its real function.**  
   Count restricted-purpose balances as what they are, not as general independence.[S19][S41]

Default shortcut:

- **low-friction, soon-usable, low-leakage claims** -> count strongly because paper value is translating into present bargaining power;
- **future-only, hardship-gated, or sale-contingent claims** -> discount because the holder is not yet secure in ordinary life;
- **mixed case** -> ask whether the claim can stabilise an ordinary shock or transition without destroying the foothold itself.

## Program implication

Once present accessibility is read clearly, the archive becomes stricter about one common false comfort: governments and systems that point to retirement balances, tax-favoured accounts, home equity, or other delayed claims while leaving ordinary households short of currently usable resilience.[S13][S17][S19][S41]
That changes program judgment in three directions.
First, the archive gives more credit to direct liquid floors and early, usable capital than to paper balances locked behind hardship or age walls.
Second, reforms that create or subsidise long-run saving should be paired with current liquid protection rather than being used as substitutes for it.
Third, scoreboards should distinguish between **future security gained** and **present freedom gained** rather than collapsing them into one wealth number.

This is why lock-up belongs inside the archive's constitutional reading of distribution rather than as a technical feature of account design.
A society is closer to justice when ordinary people do not need to wait for retirement, plead hardship, or destabilise their core foothold just to use wealth that supposedly already belongs to them.[S19][S41]

## Bottom line

The archive should now read present accessibility in this order:

1. **Ask whether the claim is usable in ordinary time, not only whether it exists on paper.**
2. **Discount balances that are future-only, hardship-gated, heavily penalised, or sale-contingent.**
3. **Distinguish later-life security and restricted-purpose capital from general present independence.**
4. **Count low-friction, soon-usable claims more strongly than delayed or leakage-heavy ones.**
5. **Refuse to let locked claims excuse thin current floors, late footholds, or weak bargaining power.**
