# Algorithmic eligibility, public benefits, and appeal rights

Digital benefit systems can widen access when they simplify enrollment, reduce paperwork, and make payments reliable. They can also convert public rights into surveillance, automated suspicion, and error-prone denials.

The UN Special Rapporteur's digital-welfare report remains the foundational warning for the cube: digital welfare systems can increase surveillance, privatize public functions, and intensify rights risks for poor claimants.[S296] The EU AI Act is a live comparative rail because it regulates AI on a risk basis, prohibits social scoring, and recognizes risks in public-benefit and other consequential decisions.[S295]

## Wealth-order risk

A benefit is not a floor if the claimant is scored out by a model, stuck in an identity-proofing loop, flagged for fraud, denied because of stale data, or forced to appeal after rent, food, health care, or utility stability has already collapsed.

## Required rails

- no fully automated final denial for subsistence or threshold benefits without meaningful human review;
- notice of data sources and reasons;
- pre-deprivation correction when feasible;
- emergency continuation when denial would create irreversible harm;
- audit of false positives by race, disability, language, household type, migration status, and digital access;
- public procurement transparency for vendors and model changes;
- appeal timing that preserves the claim.

## Certification rule

Digital delivery can support Gate 9 conversion, but it can fail Gate 17 when automation or risk scoring turns entitlement into suspicion and makes appeal too slow to protect wealth-forming stability.
