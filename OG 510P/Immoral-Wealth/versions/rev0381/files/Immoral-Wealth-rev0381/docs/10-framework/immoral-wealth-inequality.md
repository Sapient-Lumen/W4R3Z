# Immoral wealth inequality

## What this note is for

This note is the archive's **root moral screen**.
It answers the first question that comes before all the narrower comparison notes:
**when does unequal wealth stop being a tolerable difference in holdings and become an immoral social order?**

The archive's answer is not "whenever the ratio looks ugly" and not "only when perfect equality is absent."
It is also not a one-metric answer.
Wealth inequality becomes immoral when it starts acting as a durable operating system of **dependency, closure, and rule** rather than as a bounded variation inside an otherwise open and non-dominating society.[S02][S03][S04][S18][S20]

This note therefore sits **before** the target-selection procedure in [`target-selection-rules.md`](target-selection-rules.md).
It gives the archive's working definition of the phenomenon the rest of the project is trying to prevent and repair.

## Minimum reading rule

Read wealth inequality as **immoral** when the bottom is too asset-thin for ordinary independence, the top is powerful enough to bend social terms, and the main channels of entry, security, and future-building are too inherited, too closed, too extractive, or too publicly underwritten to count as a morally acceptable order.[S02][S03][S04][S13][S18][S19][S20][S23][S32][S33]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**wealth inequality becomes immoral when it stops being a dispersion of outcomes and starts functioning as a governing technology and a birth-lottery amplifier.**

That means the archive should not ask only whether some people are richer than others.
It should ask whether wealth inequality is now doing one or more of the following constitutional jobs:
- making a large share of people too buffer-thin to choose freely,
- converting large fortunes into political or institutional rule,
- locking adult life chances to family position,
- shifting ordinary security onto private savings and kin backing,
- hiding control behind opaque legal or financial wrappers,
- masking person-level or group-level exclusion behind flattering aggregates,
- or repeatedly socialising downside while leaving concentrated upside private.[S04][S13][S18][S19][S23][S26][S27][S28][S29][S30][S32][S33]

## Why wealth, not income alone

Income matters, but wealth does different moral work.
It compounds across time, survives short-term earnings drops, buys legal and geographic optionality, supports earlier family formation and mobility, changes bargaining power long before annual income tables fully register the change, and gives its holders claims on future income streams as well as on control rights attached to firms, land, credit, or institutions.[S03][S13][S20][S26]

The archive also has to ask **who really holds and controls the relevant claims**.
Household-level wealth can flatter the picture if one adult inside the household lacks direct title, liquid access, pension rights, or credible exit security.[S27][S28][S29][S30]
Intermediated wealth can flatter the picture if beneficiaries own the cash-flow claims but voting, stewardship, and agenda-setting remain bottlenecked in a narrow professional layer.[S20][S26]
And national averages can flatter the picture if wealth appears broad only because older, better-placed, or already advantaged groups are carrying the totals while younger adults, excluded groups, and weaker places remain shut out of the main compounding channels.[S13][S18][S19]

That is why the archive treats wealth inequality as a more constitutionally revealing object than income inequality alone.
The question is not only who gets what this year.
It is who gets **security, timing, voice, insulation from shocks, access to ownership, and leverage over the next round of rules**.[S04][S13][S18][S20][S26]

## The seven standing ways wealth inequality becomes immoral

### 1. Mass assetlessness and ordinary dependence

Wealth inequality becomes immoral when a large share of the population lacks the durable savings, tenure security, productive tools, or liquid buffers needed for ordinary independence.
That includes conditions in which people can survive only by relying on fragile debt, family rescue, forced self-insurance, delayed claiming, or near-assetlessness near the floor.[S07][S13][S17][S21][S22][S39][S139]

### 2. Wealth-to-rule conversion and stewardship concentration

The line is crossed when top-end wealth can routinely bend politics, lobbying, media, lawmaking, market structure, procurement, agenda-setting, or corporate governance in ways that help preserve its own position.
The archive also treats concentrated stewardship over pooled wealth as morally relevant when many people hold financial claims but a narrow layer still exercises the decisive governance rights.[S04][S20][S26]

### 3. Dynastic closure, inherited advantage, and late footholds

Inequality becomes immoral when parental wealth, inheritance timing, homeowning background, or family guarantees overwhelm contribution as determinants of adult security and entry.
A society also fails here when footholds arrive too late in life to shape housing access, independent adulthood, family formation, retraining, or ordinary long-horizon choice.[S13][S18][S19]

### 4. Extractive formation and blocked entry

The archive counts inequality as morally worse when the main channels of wealth accumulation are rents from land scarcity, monopoly position, financial tolls, incumbent revaluation, or other forms of extraction that tighten the next round of entry.
Broad gains that arrive mainly through higher asset prices, markups, or subsidy capitalisation do not count like the same moral gain as wider low-cost access and non-extractive formation.[S04][S07][S20][S31]

### 5. Public weakness, hidden subsidy, and inadequate common counterweight

A society crosses the line when public and social wealth are too weak to provide universal capability, countervailing infrastructure, or common claims that offset private concentration.
The failure is worse when the state quietly subsidises incumbent wealth through tax expenditures, capitalisation-friendly support, thin public alternatives, or budgeting patterns that look neutral while helping wealth harden upward.[S02][S142][S31]

### 6. Person-level, group-level, and place-level closure

Wealth inequality is immoral when flattering aggregate ownership hides internal household dependence, excluded-group barriers, or territorial closure.
The archive treats it as a constitutional failure when one adult inside the household lacks real claims, when historically subordinated groups remain durably excluded from safe credit, title, inheritances, land, entrepreneurship, or pension-building, or when the main opportunity-rich places stay closed from the entrant side.[S13][S18][S19][S27][S28][S29][S30]

### 7. Opacity, public downside, and automatic reconcentration

The archive reads inequality more harshly when major fortunes, transfers, and control rights are too opaque for democratic oversight and fair taxation, when major private downside is repeatedly shifted to taxpayers or public balance sheets, and when even after partial reform the normal accumulation channels keep washing wealth and control back upward.
That is not a temporary imperfection around an acceptable order.
It is evidence that the order is still structurally built to preserve hierarchy.[S23][S32][S33]

## Fast use rule

Use this note in five steps:

1. **Ask whether the bottom half has real independence or mainly exposed survival.**  
   If ordinary adults still lack buffers, secure tenure, or timely footholds, start from suspicion rather than neutrality.
2. **Ask whether the top tail can translate wealth into rule.**  
   Check political influence, controller opacity, market power, and stewardship bottlenecks rather than looking only at formal ownership counts.
3. **Ask whether entry is genuinely open.**  
   Look for inherited advantage, group closure, place closure, and late footholds before giving moral credit for averages.
4. **Ask how wealth is being formed and protected.**  
   Discount gains driven mainly by extraction, subsidy capitalisation, public downside absorption, or other incumbent-favoring channels.
5. **Ask whether the system tends to self-correct or self-reconcentrate.**  
   If apparent gains are thin, fragile, opaque, or quickly washed back upward, read the inequality as structurally immoral rather than merely high.[S04][S13][S18][S19][S20][S23][S31][S32][S33]

Default shortcut:

- **broad floor plus bounded top plus open entry plus strong public counterweight** -> inequality may still exist, but it is less likely to count as immoral by the archive's standard;
- **thin floor plus dynastic closure plus wealth-to-rule conversion plus public downside** -> treat the inequality as immoral even before every exact target ratio is settled;
- **mixed case** -> move immediately to [`target-selection-rules.md`](target-selection-rules.md) and the companion discount-rule notes instead of arguing from one flattering headline.

## Current empirical backdrop

The archive's backdrop remains stark.
World Inequality Report 2026 describes wealth as extremely concentrated at the top in every world region: the bottom 50% holds only a very small share of total wealth, while the top deciles and especially the top 1% continue to command very large shares.[S02]
OECD reporting likewise stresses that wealth is much more unequally distributed than income, that top shares remain very large across member countries, and that bottom and younger cohorts often face much weaker positions than all-age averages imply.[S03][S13][S142]

The opportunity picture is also warning-heavy.
OECD's 2025 opportunity work says inherited circumstances still explain a large share of inequality of opportunity and that parental background continues to shape education, earnings, and housing access.[S18][S19]
That means the archive is not dealing with a world where wealth merely differs.
It is dealing with a world where wealth often arrives bundled with earlier timing, better insurance, safer housing access, and stronger political insulation for some groups than for others.[S13][S18][S19]

## Program implication

This note should change the archive's operational posture in three ways.

1. It stops the archive from treating immoral wealth inequality as a one-number problem.
   The program has to weaken domination, reopen entry, strengthen the floor, build public/social counterweight, and prevent washback at the same time.
2. It clarifies why tax reform alone is not enough.
   If insecurity, housing closure, opacity, group exclusion, weak public wealth, or public downside absorption remain intact, the wealth order can stay immoral even after partial top-tail compression.
3. It gives the archive a cleaner trigger for escalation.
   Once several of the standing failure families are present together, the correct response is not cosmetic redistribution but a fuller prevention-and-response package with stronger anti-capture, floor-building, housing, entry, legibility, and burden-sharing measures.

## Bottom line

The archive should now read immoral wealth inequality in this order:

1. **Start with the bottom:** ask whether ordinary adults have real, durable, timely footholds.
2. **Then read the top:** ask whether concentrated wealth can convert into rule, opacity, or crisis privilege.
3. **Then read the channels:** ask whether wealth is being formed through open entry or through extraction, inheritance, closure, and subsidy.
4. **Then read the masks:** ask whether households, national averages, or pooled ownership are hiding person-level, group-level, or place-level exclusion.
5. **Then read the dynamics:** ask whether the system is self-opening or self-reconcentrating.

A society can therefore be unequal without being fully morally intolerable.
But once inequality becomes a durable machine for dependence below, command above, and closure across generations and groups, the archive should name it plainly:
**the wealth inequality is no longer merely descriptive; it is immoral.**
