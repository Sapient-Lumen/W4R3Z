# State capacity, capture, and enforcement discretion

A wealth order can fail because the state is too weak to enforce equality-enhancing rules, or because it is strong but captured. The archive needs both risks in the same frame.

## Two failures

1. **Capacity failure:** the state cannot identify, value, audit, deliver, litigate, collect, maintain, or repair.
2. **Capture failure:** the state can do those things but allocates capacity toward insiders, enemies, favored contractors, or politically safe targets.

Either failure can turn a nominal reform into dependency, closure, or rule.

## Discretion screen

Discretion is not always bad. Complex valuation, hardship relief, enforcement prioritization, and emergency delivery require judgment. The danger is unreviewable discretion.

A discretionary rail needs:

- published criteria;
- recordkeeping;
- conflict-of-interest controls;
- appeal or supervisory review;
- representative standing or ombud access;
- distributional monitoring;
- periodic independent audit.

The World Justice Project's 2025 rule-of-law evidence is a warning that rule-of-law decline can happen quickly, with weakening in judicial independence, civil justice, open government, and checks on power.[S177]

## Capacity indicators

Minimum case indicators:

- staff and skill adequacy;
- case-selection model quality;
- data access and interoperability;
- audit/investigation cycle time;
- appeal and court backlog;
- collection rate after assessment;
- sanctions that exceed expected avoidance gains;
- insulation from appropriations whiplash or political defunding;
- independent oversight.

The IRS high-income/high-wealth audit case shows the distinction. Recoveries after renewed funding show latent enforceability; GAO's staffing, training, research, and central-management findings show that the rail remains capacity-sensitive.[S163][S164]
