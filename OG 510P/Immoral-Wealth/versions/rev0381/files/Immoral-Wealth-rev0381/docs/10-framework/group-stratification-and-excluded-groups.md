# Group stratification and excluded groups

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's core anti-averaging judgments into a **stable group-closure rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the fuller target procedure in [`target-selection-rules.md`](target-selection-rules.md):
**can a society count as broadly acceptable when national wealth conditions improve, but historically subordinated groups are still systematically blocked from the main channels of durable ownership, secure tenure, and ordinary economic independence?**

The archive does **not** treat group exclusion as a soft side issue.
A country can post a better national snapshot, a higher bottom-half share, or a somewhat stronger median position while still failing because ownership, housing entry, liquid buffers, pension claims, land title, business assets, and inheritance remain patterned by durable group hierarchy.[S13][S18][S19]

That means this note is not adding a separate sixth axis.
It is a **cross-cutting constitutional condition** on the archive's existing judgments about floor, circulation, timing, person-level ownership, and anti-averaging.
If historically subordinated groups remain fenced out of the main asset-building routes, the order still counts as too closed even when headline shares soften.[S13][S18][S19]

## Minimum reading rule

Read group stratification as a **constitutional inclusion condition**, not as a fairness footnote.
A society should count as more just only when historically subordinated groups are gaining materially more equal access to durable ownership, housing security, liquidity, pension and savings channels, business entry, and inheritance-adjacent life chances rather than merely sharing in a national average that still leaves the main asset routes group-gated.[S13][S18][S19]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**a wealth order is not morally acceptable if it remains predictably safer, earlier, and more durable for the already advantaged groups while excluded groups stay on thinner, slower, or more reversible ownership paths.**

This note therefore reads group closure as a serious downgrade.
It should be read more harshly when the apparent broadening of the order depends mainly on:
- household or national averages hiding large within-country group gaps,
- older or already advantaged cohorts benefiting while excluded groups remain blocked at entry,
- universal-looking systems that still require collateral, insider navigation, documented tenure history, or protected geography that excluded groups are less likely to have,
- weak disaggregated data that makes exclusion hard to see,
- or person-level weakness being masked by household-level ownership reporting.[S13][S18][S19]

## The six main false passes

### 1. National broadening is mistaken for civic-equal access

A society should count less progress when overall wealth or middle-wealth positions improve while historically subordinated groups still hold markedly thinner buffers, weaker title, lower homeownership or secure-tenure rates, and lower access to business or pension assets than dominant groups.[S13][S19]

### 2. Formal universalism is mistaken for equal entry

The archive should count less success when systems are nominally universal but in practice still reward prior collateral, stable documentation, inherited geography, informal sponsorship, or insider familiarity that excluded groups are less likely to possess.[S13][S19]

### 3. Small average gap narrowing is mistaken for the end of exclusion

A society should count less progress when mean or median gaps narrow modestly while the main pathways into secure housing, land title, safe credit, entrepreneurship, or inheritance-backed advancement remain sharply segmented by group.[S13][S18][S19]

### 4. One cohort or subgroup success is mistaken for broad group inclusion

The archive should count less success when a better-positioned slice within an excluded group improves while poorer, younger, migrant, rural, racialised, disabled, or otherwise multiply burdened members remain far more closed out of the main wealth channels.[S13][S19]

### 5. Household-level improvement is mistaken for person-level group equality

A society should count less progress when group comparisons rely mainly on households even though title, control, exit security, or defensible claims within households remain badly skewed by gender, legal status, or household power.[S13]

### 6. Missing disaggregated data is mistaken for a neutral case

The archive should count less success when a state cannot or will not collect privacy-protecting evidence on wealth, tenure, and access gaps across historically subordinated groups.
Opacity should not be read as inclusion simply because exclusion becomes harder to measure.[S13]

## The six standing group-closure surfaces

### 1. Wealth and buffer distribution by group

The archive should ask whether relevant groups are converging not only in mean or median net wealth but also in positive-net-wealth rates, liquid-buffer position, and exposure to negative or near-zero wealth.
A narrower national gap still counts for less if excluded groups remain heavily concentrated near zero or below it.[S13]

### 2. Early foothold and timing surface

Group closure is weaker when historically subordinated groups can reach secure adult footholds at roughly civic-equal times rather than much later, with more waiting, more debt, more family dependence, or more fallback to insecure tenure.
This surface should be read alongside the archive's timing and circulation notes.[S18][S19]

### 3. Housing, land, and place-access surface

The archive should ask whether excluded groups can enter secure housing, defend tenure, remain in high-opportunity places, and obtain land or title on reasonably equal terms.
Housing and place remain among the main channels through which group hierarchy becomes wealth hierarchy across generations.[S13][S19]

### 4. Credit, business, and asset-channel surface

A society counts more favorably when excluded groups can access safe accounts, pensions, mortgages, business credit, procurement ladders, and starter capital without unusually harsh collateral, pricing, or gatekeeping conditions.
The archive should read this surface more harshly when entrepreneurship or saving routes still presume prior family wealth or protected status.[S13][S19]

### 5. Measurement, visibility, and legal-safeguard surface

Group closure is harder to excuse when the state has the capacity to collect disaggregated, privacy-protecting data and use it to identify exclusion in wealth-building pathways.
A society counts less favorably when it keeps group closure politically invisible by not measuring it well enough to govern.[S13]

### 6. Retention, displacement, and equal staying-power surface

The archive should ask whether excluded groups can not only enter asset channels but keep their footholds through ordinary shocks, neighborhood upgrading, administrative friction, and intergenerational transfer.
Entry without staying power still leaves the order too stratified.[S13][S19]

## Fast use rule

Use this note in four steps:

1. **Ask who the national gains are really reaching.**  
   Look past the overall share story and ask whether historically subordinated groups are gaining person-level footholds on roughly civic-equal terms.
2. **Discount flattering averages.**  
   Count less progress when national medians, household measures, or one subgroup success hide durable exclusion in housing, liquidity, pension, title, or business channels.
3. **Treat missing group visibility as a non-pass.**  
   Do not upgrade a case merely because the state lacks privacy-protecting disaggregated data on wealth pathways.
4. **Prefer universal-plus-targeted repair.**  
   Count more progress when universal broad-ownership institutions are paired with the measurement, enforcement, and targeted repair needed to reopen the specific channels that remain group-gated.[S13][S18][S19]

Default shortcut:

- **better national shares plus persistent excluded-group closure** -> read the case as still morally defective, because the ownership order remains civically unequal in who can build and keep wealth;
- **formal universalism plus harsh group-specific entry barriers** -> read the case more harshly, because equal rules are not equal access when background conditions remain stratified;
- **mixed case** -> ask whether excluded groups are reaching earlier, safer, and more durable footholds, not just whether the national average looks broader.

## Program implication

Once this group-closure surface is read clearly, three policy mistakes become easier to avoid.
First, the archive should stop mistaking universal-looking rules for equal access when historically subordinated groups still face sharper documentation, geography, credit, discrimination, or title barriers.
Second, it should stop treating disaggregated measurement and enforcement as optional optics rather than part of what makes broadening real.
Third, it should stop reading national gains as morally sufficient when excluded groups still enter later, with weaker retention, and through narrower channels than others.

That is why this note sits between target choice and the reopening lanes.
A society moves closer to justice when historically subordinated groups can build, keep, and use wealth on roughly civic-equal terms rather than remaining permanent latecomers to the ownership order.

## Bottom line

The archive should now read excluded-group closure as a constitutional defect in its own right, not as a side statistic.
National broadening counts less when the main asset-building channels remain group-gated, and it counts more when excluded groups are gaining earlier, safer, more defensible, and more durable footholds across the very channels that once kept them out.[S13][S18][S19]
