# Forced self-insurance and private buffer coercion

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's core floor-conditioning claims into a **stable forced-self-insurance rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the fuller target procedure in [`target-selection-rules.md`](target-selection-rules.md):
**how much apparent household wealth should count when ordinary adults still need unusually large private buffers just to survive predictable basic risks?**

The archive does **not** treat this as a side question about personal finance culture.
A society can report higher saving, thicker household balance sheets, broader retirement holdings, or stronger precautionary reserves while still organizing ordinary life around missing common insurance.[S17][S21][S22]
That is not simple prudence.
It is a constitutional way of forcing non-rich households to carry risks that a decent society would partly socialise.

This note therefore focuses on a specific failure:
**private wealth is doing too much defensive work because the common floor is too weak.**
When that is true, the archive should count less apparent progress even if savings rise, retirement balances spread, or owner-occupation becomes more central to family survival.[S07][S17][S21][S22]

## Minimum reading rule

Read large precautionary buffer demands as a **discount on apparent wealth success**, not as neutral evidence of freedom.
A society should count as more just only when ordinary households can remain safe against predictable illness, care burdens, old-age insecurity, housing instability, earnings interruption, and comparable routine shocks **without needing unusually large private war chests merely to stand still**.[S07][S17][S21][S22]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**wealth accumulated because the common floor is too weak should count less like freedom and more like a tax imposed through insecurity.**

This note therefore downgrades apparent progress when the ordinary answer to major predictable risks is still some combination of:
- hold much larger cash buffers,
- buy safety through high-cost housing tenure,
- rely on family wealth or parent backing,
- self-fund care, illness, or retraining shocks,
- or over-accumulate retirement wealth simply to avoid humiliation or precarity in old age.[S07][S17][S21][S22]

## The six main false passes

### 1. Higher household wealth is mistaken for more freedom

A society should count less progress when larger balances mainly reflect the need to self-insure against predictable essentials rather than a stronger common floor or earlier open footholds.[S17][S21][S22]

### 2. Responsibility rhetoric is mistaken for justice

The archive should discount praise of thrift or prudence when institutions first push common risks onto households and then celebrate the households that manage to hoard enough to survive them.[S21][S22]

### 3. Broad retirement balances are mistaken for good old-age security

A regime counts less favorably when apparently thicker retirement wealth mainly reflects weak first-tier public provision, thin disability protection, or strong pressure on households to over-save simply to avoid poverty later.[S22]

### 4. Housing buffers are mistaken for healthy wealth-building

Apparent broadening should count less when families chase leveraged ownership, parental guarantees, or oversized home-equity buffers because secure renting, non-market housing, and anti-displacement protection are too weak to make ordinary life stable otherwise.[S07][S13]

### 5. Emergency reserves are mistaken for a real floor

A society should count less progress when households need unusually large liquid reserves just to survive ordinary income interruption, illness, or care shocks because public protection arrives late, thinly, or conditionally.[S17][S21]

### 6. Subsidising larger buffers is mistaken for solving insecurity

The archive should treat top-heavy tax preferences or broad saving subsidies as an incomplete answer when they enlarge private defensive accumulation more than they reduce the underlying risks households are being forced to carry.[S31][S36][S37]

## The six standing forced-self-insurance surfaces

### 1. Health, disability, and care exposure

The archive should ask how much private wealth ordinary adults need to avoid debt, delayed treatment, family collapse, or care-driven destitution when illness, disability, or care needs arrive.[S21]

### 2. Old-age security and retirement coercion

Forced self-insurance counts as worse when households need extreme lifelong private accumulation merely to avoid poverty, dependency, or downward mobility in old age, rather than building assets above a strong first-tier guarantee.[S22]

### 3. Housing and tenure security

The archive should read regimes more harshly when home equity has to do the work that secure tenure, lower rent extraction, safer renting, and anti-displacement protections should partly do.[S07][S13]

### 4. Earnings interruption and ordinary shock cover

A society counts less favorably when one short spell of unemployment, reduced hours, or family shock quickly turns into forced borrowing, asset liquidation, or kin dependence unless households have unusually large private buffers.[S17]

### 5. Early-adulthood and family-backed entry pressure

Forced self-insurance is also a timing problem when young adults need parent-backed reserves, unusually high down-payment buffers, or family insurance against training and mobility risk before open adulthood becomes safe.[S07][S17][S21]

### 6. Budget and scoreboard treatment

The archive should treat buffer-load reduction as a real gain.
A regime should count more favorably when budgeting and scoreboarding ask whether reforms lower the amount of private wealth non-rich households must hold merely to remain safe, rather than only whether those households are saving more.[S17][S21][S22][S31]

## Fast use rule

Use this note in four steps:

1. **Ask what risks households are being told to privately absorb.**  
   How much illness, care, housing, employment, and old-age insecurity is still being managed mainly through household reserves rather than through a reliable common floor?
2. **Discount defensive accumulation.**  
   Count less apparent progress when savings, equity, or retirement balances are rising mainly because households must privately insure predictable essentials.
3. **Check timing and entry pressure.**  
   Read the case more harshly when young adults or low-wealth households need parental backing, large deposits, or unusually thick emergency funds just to enter stable adulthood.
4. **Prefer risk reduction over buffer inflation.**  
   Count more progress when reforms directly lower exposure to predictable shocks instead of merely subsidising larger private war chests.[S07][S17][S21][S22][S31]

Default shortcut:

- **strong common insurance plus moderate household reserves** -> read the floor more favorably because private wealth can do more developmental and discretionary work;
- **weak common insurance plus large required buffers** -> read the distribution more harshly because apparent wealth is doing defensive work that should not be privately demanded at that scale;
- **mixed case** -> ask whether the missing public protection still forces ordinary households to save, borrow, or family-insure far more than would otherwise be necessary.

## Program implication

Once this surface is read clearly, two policy mistakes become easier to avoid.
First, the archive should stop counting thicker household balance sheets as straightforward success when those balances are being built under conditions of weak health protection, weak care support, insecure housing, thin income insurance, or inadequate first-tier pensions.
Second, it should stop mistaking subsidies for saving, ownership, or retirement accumulation for a complete answer when the deeper task is to lower the amount of private wealth ordinary households must hold merely to remain safe.

That is why this note sits between the social-floor notes and the program stack.
A society moves closer to justice when non-rich households can still build meaningful assets, but do not have to organize their whole lives around fear-driven buffer accumulation just to survive predictable risks.

## Bottom line

The archive should now read forced self-insurance in this order:

1. **Ask how much private wealth ordinary households need merely to stay safe against predictable basics.**
2. **Discount balance-sheet progress when larger buffers mainly compensate for weak common insurance.**
3. **Treat family-backed entry, large emergency reserves, and over-saving for old age as warning signs when they are institutionally compelled.**
4. **Prefer reforms that directly reduce ordinary exposure over policies that merely subsidise defensive hoarding.**
5. **Refuse to let insecurity-driven accumulation count like the same moral gain as freely usable, developmental, and less fear-conditioned wealth.**
