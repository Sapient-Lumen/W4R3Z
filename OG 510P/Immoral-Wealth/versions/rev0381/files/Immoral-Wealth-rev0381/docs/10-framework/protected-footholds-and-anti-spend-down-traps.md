# Protected footholds and anti-spend-down traps

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's core floor-conditioning claims into a **stable protected-foothold rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the fuller target procedure in [`target-selection-rules.md`](target-selection-rules.md):
**how much apparent floor protection should count when households near the bottom still have to remain almost assetless, liquidate first footholds, or fear visible saving in order to receive basic support?**

The archive does **not** treat spend-down traps as a small administrative detail.
A society can talk about resilience, savings, ownership, and dignity while still organizing essential protection around anti-asset rules that require families to run down modest reserves before help appears.[S17][S39][S139]
That is not neutral targeting.
It is a constitutional way of reproducing bottom-half wealthlessness while claiming to protect need.

This note therefore focuses on a specific failure:
**the floor punishes modest footholds instead of shielding them.**
When that is true, the archive should count less apparent progress even if transfer budgets rise, formal eligibility expands, or statutory poverty protection looks better on paper.[S17][S31][S39][S139]

## Minimum reading rule

Read punitive asset tests, forced liquidation, and sharp benefit cliffs as a **direct attack on the floor**, not as a neutral refinement of means-testing.
A society should count as more just only when ordinary households can build and keep modest emergency reserves, child accounts, simple productive tools, safer transport, starter housing positions, or other first footholds **without that progress itself triggering loss, delay, or heavy dilution of essential support**.[S17][S39][S139]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**a decent wealth order should not require people to prove need by remaining nearly assetless.**

This note therefore downgrades apparent progress when access to basic support still depends on conditions like:
- very low asset thresholds near the floor,
- liquidation of modest liquid buffers before help arrives,
- harsh treatment of developmental or productive assets,
- abrupt cliffs rather than smooth tapers,
- interaction rules that neutralize baby bonds, matched savings, or debt relief,
- or administrative regimes that teach households visible saving is dangerous.[S17][S31][S39][S139]

## The six main false passes

### 1. More anti-poverty spending is mistaken for floor-building

A society should count less progress when it spends more on hardship relief but still requires households to remain nearly assetless in order to receive that help.[S17][S39]

### 2. Excluding comfortable wealth is mistaken for punishing modest footholds

The archive should distinguish between refusing to subsidise clearly comfortable wealth and penalising the first small buffers or starter assets of households near the floor.
Conflating those is a constitutional mistake.[S39]

### 3. Relief after liquidation is mistaken for protected independence

A regime counts less favorably when households can reach help only after running down the very reserves, tools, or early claims that would otherwise support later stability and autonomy.[S17][S39]

### 4. Precision targeting is mistaken for very low ceilings and sharp cliffs

Apparent targeting quality should count less when rules are so strict that small gains in savings or assets cause disproportionate losses of essential support, making first-foothold accumulation irrational.[S39][S139]

### 5. Asset-building programs are mistaken for real broadening when adjacent rules claw them back

A state should count less progress when it advertises matched savings, baby bonds, debt relief, or small-asset promotion while nearby support rules still erase their value through withdrawal, delay, liquidation pressure, or non-counting interaction rules.[S17][S31][S39]

### 6. The hidden wealth constitution is ignored

A regime looks worse, not better, when it lightly subsidises incumbent wealth at the top while tightly policing tiny buffers at the bottom.
The archive should read these together as one distributional constitution rather than two separate policy worlds.[S31][S36][S37][S39]

## The six standing protected-foothold surfaces

### 1. Baseline asset disregards and protected small reserves

The archive should ask whether households can hold modest emergency savings, child accounts, simple retirement balances, essential tools, safer vehicles, or other first footholds without immediate disqualification from basic support.[S39]

### 2. Tapers, grace periods, and transition rules

Protected footholds count more when movement upward is gradual.
Grace periods, partial disregards, and smoother tapers are constitutionally better than abrupt cliffs that punish small gains.[S39][S139]

### 3. Treatment of productive, developmental, and early-capital assets

The archive should read regimes more harshly when they force liquidation of assets that help later independence, such as work tools, education-linked savings, child accounts, safer transport, or other modest productive claims.[S17][S39]

### 4. Interaction with claiming, disclosure, and visible saving

Spend-down traps are worse when households must repeatedly disclose and justify tiny balances through complex administrative channels, or when program design teaches families that remaining informal, unbanked, or unrecorded is safer than visible saving.[S39][S139]

### 5. Interaction with social inheritance and public-capital policy

Protected footholds count less as a public commitment when baby bonds, matched savings, debt relief, or public-capital grants are formally offered but then rapidly neutralized by nearby means-tested withdrawal rules.[S17][S31][S39]

### 6. Budget and scoreboard treatment

The archive should treat anti-spend-down design as part of package quality.
A regime should count more favorably when budgeting, implementation, and scoreboarding ask whether reforms let households **retain** early gains while receiving essential protection, rather than only whether benefits exist in statute.[S31][S39][S139]

## Fast use rule

Use this note in four steps:

1. **Ask what modest footholds trigger trouble.**  
   Which small balances, tools, child accounts, vehicles, or starter claims make a household lose, delay, or fear claiming basic support?
2. **Separate substantial wealth exclusion from first-foothold punishment.**  
   Refusing to subsidise clearly comfortable asset holders is not the same as requiring near-assetlessness from people at the floor.
3. **Check whether movement upward is gradual or punitive.**  
   Grace periods, disregards, and tapers count better than cliffs, immediate liquidation demands, or rules that erase the value of visible saving.
4. **Count retained gains, not announced gains.**  
   A floor counts more only when households can keep modest early gains while still remaining protected through ordinary hardship.[S17][S31][S39][S139]

Default shortcut:

- **protected small buffers plus smooth tapers** -> read the floor more favorably because early gains can survive alongside support;
- **low asset ceilings plus liquidation before help** -> read the floor more harshly because the system still demands near-assetlessness;
- **mixed case** -> ask whether ordinary households learn that visible saving is safe or dangerous.

## Program implication

Once this surface is read clearly, two policy mistakes become easier to avoid.
First, the archive should stop treating stricter asset tests as obviously more just when they mainly force low-wealth households to liquidate first footholds before protection begins.
Second, it should stop treating asset-building policy as fully real when nearby means-tested rules still claw back or neutralize the resulting gains.

That is why this note sits between the floor notes and the program stack.
A society moves closer to justice when ordinary people can still qualify for essential protection **while keeping** modest emergency reserves, developmental assets, and early capital claims that help them stop being permanently assetless.

## Bottom line

The archive should now read protected footholds in this order:

1. **Ask whether basic support still requires near-assetlessness.**
2. **Distinguish substantial-wealth exclusion from punishment of modest first footholds.**
3. **Count grace periods, disregards, and tapers better than liquidation demands and cliffs.**
4. **Treat visible-saving fear as evidence that the floor is still badly designed.**
5. **Refuse to let asset-building rhetoric count fully when adjacent support rules still force people to run down the very gains the archive wants them to keep.**
