# Claimability, take-up, and administrative friction

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's core floor-conditioning claims into a **stable claimability rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the fuller target procedure in [`target-selection-rules.md`](target-selection-rules.md):
**how much of the public/social floor should count when eligible people predictably fail to reach, keep, or use support because the claiming path is too hard, too slow, too fragmented, or too discretionary?**

The archive does **not** treat claimability as a service-delivery footnote.
A society can announce strong benefits, protect large nominal entitlements, or rely heavily on public provision while still failing because ordinary households must fight through paperwork, recertification, opaque rules, office scarcity, digital exclusion, or adversarial screening just to reach the floor that supposedly substitutes for private buffer wealth.[S39][S139]

That means this note is not just about whether support exists in law.
It is about whether support is **straightforwardly reachable in ordinary life**: discoverable before crisis escalates, claimable without heroic skill, timely enough to matter, stable enough to plan around, and maintainable without repeated humiliation or attrition.[S39][S139]

## Minimum reading rule

Read claimability as a **constitutional part of the floor**, not as a secondary operational detail.
A society should count as more just only when a large majority of eligible people can actually discover, claim, maintain, and use basic support without repeated expert help, repeated crisis escalation, or repeated submission to degrading gatekeeping.[S39][S139]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**rights that exist mostly on paper should be discounted when judging how much private wealth households really need to remain free.**

This note therefore reads weak claimability as a serious downgrade.
It should be read more harshly when the apparent strength of the public/social floor depends mainly on:
- benefits with low take-up or high churn,
- repeated documentary burdens for facts the state already knows,
- digital-only or office-scarce channels,
- slow or unpredictable processing,
- short certification windows and constant renewal,
- or adversarial design that makes eligible households rationally avoid claiming at all.[S39][S139]

## The six main false passes

### 1. Statutory entitlement is mistaken for real support

A society should count less progress when legal eligibility looks strong but realized access is much thinner because ordinary people cannot navigate the claiming process reliably.[S139]

### 2. Low take-up is mistaken for low need

A regime should count less favorably when non-claiming is read as lack of demand even though the real causes are confusion, hassle costs, stigma, delay, mistrust, or thin expected value after administrative burden.[S139]

### 3. Initial enrolment is mistaken for durable access

A society should count less success when households can get onto a program once but are then lost through recertification churn, documentary repetition, channel failure, or unstable case management.[S139]

### 4. Digitalisation is mistaken for simplification

Online claiming can help, but the archive should count less success when digital channels simply shift burden onto claimants while phone, paper, outreach, interpretation, and in-person help remain weak.[S139]

### 5. Anti-fraud burden is mistaken for neutral administration

Verification can be legitimate, but the archive should count less progress when screening logic predictably deters or ejects eligible people, especially near the floor where small delays or false negatives are highly coercive.[S39][S139]

### 6. Formal public provision is mistaken for full substitution for private wealth

A social floor should count only partially when households still need extra savings, family fallback, or private debt simply to survive the claiming maze, the waiting period, or the renewal cliff.[S39][S139]

## The six standing claimability surfaces

### 1. Discovery and presumptive reach

The archive should ask whether likely-eligible households are easy to identify and reach, whether information is intelligible, and whether systems do enough to surface likely entitlement rather than waiting for perfect self-advocacy.[S139]

### 2. Documentary burden and state reuse of known facts

Claimability is stronger when the state reuses information it already holds, proportionates evidence demands, and avoids forcing claimants to repeatedly prove stable facts through hard-to-obtain records.[S139]

### 3. Timing and continuity

Support counts more when it arrives fast enough to matter for rent, food, childcare, medicine, or utility stress, and when continuation is predictable enough for households to plan around it rather than treat it as contingent luck.[S139]

### 4. Channel pluralism and live support

Claimability is stronger when digital access is paired with phone, paper, in-person help, interpretation, outreach, and competent human support for households facing complex situations or low digital confidence.[S139]

### 5. Renewal, churn, and maintenance burden

The archive should ask how much work is required not just to enter support, but to stay on it.
Frequent recertification, repeated office visits, and unstable documentation demands should count as part of floor weakness rather than as morally neutral maintenance.[S139]

### 6. Asset-test and rule complexity near the floor

Claimability is weaker when eligibility depends on confusing asset rules, spend-down expectations, or interacting thresholds that make households unsure whether modest savings, family support, or irregular work will disqualify them or trigger repayment risk.[S39]

## Fast use rule

Use this note in four steps:

1. **Ask how much of the floor is really reachable.**  
   What share of nominal protection is actually discoverable, claimable, and maintainable by ordinary eligible households?
2. **Discount paper protection.**  
   Count less public/social substitution when access is slow, confusing, repetitive, or heavily gatekept.
3. **Check continuity, not just entry.**  
   Read the case more harshly when churn, recertification, and channel failure strip people out after initial approval.
4. **Prefer low-friction delivery.**  
   Count more progress when systems reuse data, simplify proofs, presumptively enrol where appropriate, and pair automation with real human support.[S139]

Default shortcut:

- **high take-up, low-friction continuity** -> read the floor more favorably because social protection genuinely substitutes for some private buffer need;
- **nominal rights plus high burden** -> read the floor more harshly because households still need private wealth, family fallback, or debt to survive access friction;
- **mixed case** -> ask whether the missing claimability still forces ordinary people into avoidable insecurity even if legal design looks generous.

## Program implication

Once this claimability surface is read clearly, two policy mistakes become easier to avoid.
First, the archive should stop counting announced protection as fully real when ordinary people still need money, time, literacy, transport, and luck just to reach it.
Second, it should stop treating delivery friction as morally separate from inequality when that friction directly raises the private buffer wealth households must hold.

That is why this note sits between the social-floor companion notes and the implementation stack.
A society moves closer to justice when protection is not only legally promised but also straightforward to reach, straightforward to keep, and fast enough to matter under ordinary stress.

## Bottom line

The archive should now read claimability in this order:

1. **Ask how much of the nominal floor ordinary eligible households can actually reach and keep.**
2. **Discount legal generosity when take-up, timing, continuity, or channel access is weak.**
3. **Treat churn, recertification burden, and asset-rule confusion as part of floor weakness, not neutral administration.**
4. **Count low-friction, multi-channel, timely delivery as real equality infrastructure.**
5. **Refuse to let paper rights stand in for a real reduction in coercive private buffer need.**
