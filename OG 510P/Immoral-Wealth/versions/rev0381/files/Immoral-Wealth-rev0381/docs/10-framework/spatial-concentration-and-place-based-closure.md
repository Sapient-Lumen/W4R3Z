# Spatial concentration and place-based closure

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's most important **territorial-closure claims** into a stable reading rule.
It answers a narrower question than the full housing and land program in [`../20-program/housing-land-and-anti-monopoly.md`](../20-program/housing-land-and-anti-monopoly.md):
**when should a society count national wealth broadening as morally weaker because the places where opportunity, services, bargaining power, and appreciation compound fastest are still practically closed to ordinary entrants, weaker-balance households, or excluded groups?**

The archive already treats housing, land, and public-value capture as central rather than peripheral.
What this note adds is a compact rule for judging the **geography of access**.
A country can look better in national wealth shares while still reproducing a harsh territorial order if the main compounding metros, school catchments, transit-linked corridors, and job-rich neighbourhoods remain open mainly to incumbent owners, high earners, or families with prior wealth.[S07][S13][S19][S34][S35][S48]

This matters because place is not a decorative background condition.
Where people can stably live shapes what jobs they can reach, which schools and services they can use, how much time and money commuting absorbs, whether public investment turns into common gain or private windfall, and whether the next generation grows up near the main engines of mobility.
Recent OECD work also stresses that opportunities vary significantly within countries by place, that metropolitan and higher-income areas tend to provide better access to jobs and services, that smaller geographic units reveal disparities hidden by larger averages, and that many people cannot simply move to better places because mobility has real financial and care costs.[S07][S13][S19][S34][S35][S48]

## Minimum reading rule

Do **not** treat footholds in all places as territorially interchangeable.
A society should get more moral credit when ordinary households can obtain secure tenure, first ownership, or durable non-owner stability **in the places where opportunity actually compounds**, or within realistic access distance on stable terms.
It should get less credit when national improvement depends on pushing later entrants farther out, farther away, or into weaker-tenure positions while the main opportunity-rich places remain guarded by scarcity, incumbent veto, land-value capitalisation, or displacement.[S07][S13][S19][S34][S35][S48]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**cheap footholds in weak-opportunity places do not fully substitute for secure access to the main compounding places.**

That means the archive should read territorial structure more harshly when apparent progress depends mainly on:
- national gains that are bought by geographic downgrading,
- housing supply that expands mostly where opportunity is thinner,
- public investment that raises incumbent site values faster than it widens access,
- long commutes or weak transport links being treated as equivalent to real access,
- excluded groups being filtered out of high-opportunity places even while national averages improve,
- or local fiscal and political systems that reward exclusion by protecting incumbent asset values and screening out entrants.[S07][S13][S19][S34][S35][S48]

## The six main false passes

### 1. National broadening is mistaken for territorial opening

A society should count less success when national wealth or homeownership looks broader while the main compounding places remain effectively reserved for already-positioned households.[S13][S19][S48]

### 2. Any foothold is mistaken for a good foothold

The archive should count less progress when ordinary households can only secure tenure in distant, weak-opportunity, weak-service, or weak-connection places and that is treated as equivalent to access in job-rich or service-rich areas.[S07][S19][S48]

### 3. New supply is mistaken for open access

A country should count less progress when supply rises but mostly in locations that do not materially open access to the main labor, school, service, or transport advantages, or when supply near those advantages remains blocked by incumbent vetoes, land scarcity, or exclusionary rules.[S07][S35][S48]

### 4. Public uplift is mistaken for public opening

The archive should discount transport, rezoning, infrastructure, and redevelopment wins that mostly capitalise into incumbent land values, rents, or speculative margins while access, tenure security, and common capture lag behind.[S34][S35]

### 5. Formal mobility is mistaken for practical mobility

A society should count less success when people are told they can simply move to opportunity even though moving costs, care obligations, social ties, school disruption, or weak transport make that instruction unrealistic for many households.[S19][S48]

### 6. Group equality is read without place

The archive should count less progress when excluded groups technically gain on average while still being disproportionately screened out, displaced from, or unable to remain in high-opportunity neighbourhoods and metros.[S13][S19][S48]

## The six standing place-closure surfaces

### 1. Opportunity-density surface

Ask where the main opportunity bundles actually sit.
A path counts more when secure tenure and footholds open in places where jobs, schools, care, transport, safety, civic influence, and future appreciation are genuinely clustered.
It counts less when ordinary access is pushed toward places with thinner opportunity bundles.[S07][S19][S48]

### 2. Entry-versus-displacement surface

Ask whether the main compounding places are becoming easier to enter **and** easier to stay in.
A path counts less when new entrants face higher deposits, more screening, thinner tenancy security, or faster displacement even if formal production rises.[S07][S13][S19]

### 3. Distance-and-time surface

Ask whether access is realistic in daily life.
A foothold counts less when reaching the main labor, education, care, or public-service nodes requires punishing travel times, high transport costs, or brittle commuting chains that effectively ration opportunity by time and exhaustion rather than by sticker price alone.[S07][S19][S48]

### 4. Public-uplift-capture surface

Ask what happens after public action raises local value.
A path counts less when transit, infrastructure, upzoning, and public-land strategy mostly raise private site values or rents instead of widening durable access, lowering household cost, or building common/public wealth.[S34][S35]

### 5. Local-veto-and-fiscal-incentive surface

Ask whether local institutions are rewarded for openness or rewarded for exclusion.
A path counts less when incumbent homeowners, local fiscal structures, or fragmented local veto points can preserve scarcity, protect revaluation, and shift lower-income households elsewhere.[S07][S35]

### 6. Within-place stratification surface

Ask who actually gets the good locations inside the broader place.
A country counts less progress when headline metro access improves while poorer households or excluded groups are still concentrated in the worse schools, worse transport access, worse environmental exposure, or more precarious tenures within that same metro.[S13][S19][S48]

## Fast use rule

Use this note in four steps:

1. **Locate the main compounding places.**  
   Do not begin from national averages; begin from the metros, neighbourhoods, corridors, and catchments where opportunity and value are densest.[S07][S19][S48]
2. **Ask whether ordinary households can enter and remain there on stable terms.**  
   Count tenure security, deposit burden, transport time, and displacement risk rather than only asking whether units exist somewhere.[S07][S13][S19]
3. **Ask what public action is doing to local values.**  
   Discount the case when infrastructure, rezoning, or redevelopment mainly produces private incumbent uplift instead of broader access or common capture.[S34][S35]
4. **Ask whether the same groups keep being sorted outward.**  
   Count less success when excluded groups or weaker-balance households are still being screened out of the best locations even as national averages soften.[S13][S19][S48]

Default shortcut:
- **national gains + closed opportunity-rich places** -> likely territorial false pass;
- **more supply mostly away from main opportunity nodes** -> likely partial pass at best;
- **public uplift without access widening or value capture** -> likely incumbent-side gain flow;
- **cheaper peripheral footholds sold as full equivalence** -> likely overstatement.

## What this changes in classification

This note changes three ordinary readings.

First, it tells the archive to classify some apparent national broadening as **territorially incomplete** rather than fully broad.
A country can improve on aggregate while leaving the decisive geography of mobility and security too closed.

Second, it tells the archive to treat **distance, commuting burden, and practical accessibility** as part of wealth judgment rather than as a side topic in transport or urban policy.
A foothold that comes with large daily access penalties is often a weaker foothold than national statistics suggest.[S07][S19][S48]

Third, it tells the archive to read housing, land, transport, redevelopment, and anti-displacement policy together rather than separately.
Territorial openness is usually produced by a package, and territorial closure is usually reproduced by a package too.[S07][S34][S35]

Read this note especially when a case sounds like:
- “ownership is cheaper farther out, so access is open enough,”
- “new infrastructure proves the place is becoming fairer,”
- “national gains show that the geography is taking care of itself,”
- or “displacement is regrettable but secondary to production.”

The archive's answer is that these claims require much stronger proof than they are usually given.
A wealth order is not close to just if the main compounding places remain materially closed from the entrant side.
