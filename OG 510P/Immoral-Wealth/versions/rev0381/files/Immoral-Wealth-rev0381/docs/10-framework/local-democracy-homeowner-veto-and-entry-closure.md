# Local democracy, homeowner veto, and entry closure

Local democracy can protect communities. It can also convert incumbent property wealth into a veto over who may enter opportunity-rich places. This is a wealth-order problem when existing owners can preserve asset values by excluding renters, lower-wealth households, young households, migrants, or future residents from housing supply, transport access, schools, and job-rich locations.[S195][S196][S202]

## Entrant-voice rule

A land-use decision counts as democratically thin when the main affected people are not in the electorate or the meeting room. Would-be residents, renters, future children, lower-wealth movers, care workers, and displaced households often bear the cost of exclusion without having an equal procedural route to contest it.

The archive should therefore score local land-use governance not only by procedural legality but by entrant voice.

## Owner-veto indicators

- discretionary approvals that give small incumbent groups repeated veto points;
- meeting and notice structures that favor high-time, high-property-stake residents;
- fiscal or tax structures that make incumbent municipalities prefer scarcity;
- exclusionary rules such as minimum lot sizes, multifamily bans, parking mandates, height limits, or delay-heavy review;
- legal challenge structures that let high-resource opponents outlast affordable-housing proponents;
- missing regional or state review when local rules create spillover exclusion;
- affordable-housing programs that add demand without enough supply or siting power.

The CEA's 2024 housing chapter states the land-use incentive problem plainly: zoning and land-use rules can enrich existing property owners at the expense of renters and would-be owners, and locations with more homeowners than renters tend to have stricter supply regulations as homeowner political influence grows.[S195]

## Folk-economics complication

This is not only naked self-interest. Research on opposition to new housing finds that many urban and suburban voters do not believe increased supply lowers prices, even while they understand supply and demand in other markets. The archive should therefore separate corrupt owner veto from mistaken but politically powerful supply skepticism.[S202]

## Program implication

Where this surface is live, opening packages should consider:

- regional or state-level housing-supply targets;
- by-right approvals for compliant projects;
- inclusive upzoning near transit and job-rich areas;
- anti-displacement and tenant protections during supply transitions;
- fiscal incentives that reduce local scarcity rewards;
- representation or weighting mechanisms for renters and would-be residents;
- judicial or administrative review when local decisions export exclusion to outsiders.

Do not let "local control" function as a magic word. The question is whether local power protects ordinary residents or preserves an asset-holder perimeter.
