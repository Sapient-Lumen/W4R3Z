# Care costs, time poverty, and family formation

Care is a wealth-order issue because it determines whether people can work, save, train, move, form families, leave bad relationships, care for elders, and keep assets through illness and aging. A private childcare or eldercare market can become a cost-of-life tollgate even when wages and public benefits look adequate on paper.[S273][S274][S276][S277]

## Childcare test

A childcare system blocks wealth formation when net costs consume the surplus available for savings, when waiting lists or hours prevent work, when quality/safety requires private premiums, when subsidy cliffs punish earnings, or when care gaps force parents—often mothers—to reduce hours, quit jobs, decline promotions, or abandon training.[S273][S274][S275][S276]

## Time-poverty test

The cube should count time as part of the wealth floor. A household with formal income but no reliable care time can be unable to claim benefits, attend school, pursue work, manage repairs, contest debts, or build a business. Care time is a conversion rail for nearly every other claim.

## Family-formation test

Do not treat family formation as private preference only. When childcare, housing, medical, and debt costs make children unaffordable or make caregiving economically punitive, the wealth order is rationing ordinary life-course choices through inherited resources and partner/family backing.[S274][S275]

## Routing

Care-cost failures route through Gate 16 first, then Gate 14 workplace power, Gate 2 floor reality, and Gate 4 household/person-level power when exit, safety, or bargaining depends on partner or family resources.
