# Claimant load and per-person adequacy

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's quieter but highly consequential housing judgments into a **stable claimant-load rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the full floor and housing notes:
**when should a household foothold count less because too many adults or dependents must rely on it at once for it to provide person-adequate security, privacy, and ordinary independence?**

The archive does **not** require atomised living or one dwelling per adult.
Shared households can count strongly.
But the archive also refuses to treat a single thin household foothold as fully equivalent to several person-adequate footholds when ordinary entry is blocked and too many people are forced to pool around one scarce base.[S13][S19][S27][S43]

That is why this note belongs between the floor, person-level ownership, housing-entry, and timing notes.
A society can look better on household wealth, homeownership, or secure-tenure measures while still failing a more basic question:
**does the foothold actually give the people relying on it enough room, exit margin, and independent life space to function as free adults rather than compressed dependents?**[S13][S19][S27][S43]

## Minimum reading rule

Read household footholds against **claimant load**, not just against legal tenure or gross household asset position.
A modest dwelling, savings bundle, or other shared foothold should count strongly only when it is secure enough, spacious enough, and person-adequate enough for the actual people depending on it.
When too many adults or dependents must live off one thin foothold, the archive should discount that foothold rather than treating it as clean evidence of broad independence.[S13][S19][S27][S43]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**a household foothold counts less when its apparent adequacy depends mainly on compression, delayed exit, or claimant dilution rather than on enough real security per person.**

Read this note more harshly when the apparent success of the lower half depends on:
- household-level counting that hides very low per-person margin,
- overcrowded or near-overcrowded living arrangements,
- adult children or separated adults remaining pooled because independent entry is blocked,
- one modest foothold being scored as if it delivered several direct footholds,
- or person-level title and exit security remaining weak inside the shared arrangement.[S13][S19][S27][S43]

## The six main false passes

### 1. Household ownership is mistaken for person-adequate independence

A society should count less success when a household is owner-occupied or asset-positive on paper but the actual people inside it still lack enough space, privacy, margin, or exit capacity for ordinary independent life.[S13][S27][S43]

### 2. Voluntary sharing is mistaken for all observed compression

Some multigenerational or extended-family living is chosen and can be good.
But the archive should count less success when co-residence is driven mainly by blocked entry, high rents, thin wages, or weak early footholds rather than by stable preference.[S19][S43]

### 3. One modest foothold is mistaken for several footholds

The archive should count less success when one thin dwelling or household asset package is implicitly scored as if it delivered full security to multiple adults who would ordinarily need distinct footholds or stronger direct claims.

### 4. Household wealth improvement is mistaken for per-person resilience

A country should count less progress when household net wealth, tenure security, or housing quality improve while additional adults within the same household still have little direct liquidity, little exit margin, and little ability to absorb conflict or life transitions on their own.[S13][S27][S30]

### 5. De-crowding failure is mistaken for a small detail

The archive should count less success when reforms improve housing counts, ownership counts, or aggregate wealth metrics while leaving overcrowding, claimant load, or forced compression largely unchanged.[S43]

### 6. Household aggregation is mistaken for a complete reading

The archive should count less success when official reading relies heavily on household-level success categories without asking how many people depend on each foothold and what that means for per-person adequacy, household formation timing, and real autonomy.[S13][S19][S43]

## The six standing claimant-load surfaces

### 1. Occupancy and space adequacy

The archive should ask whether the relevant dwelling or foothold provides adequate living space relative to the people relying on it.
This is not just a room count question; adequacy depends on household composition, privacy, and the practical livability of the arrangement.[S43]

### 2. Adult household formation and delayed exit

Claimant-load failure is worse when younger adults, separated adults, or other ordinary entrants remain involuntarily pooled into existing households because independent entry has become too expensive or too risky.
This surface should be read with the archive's timing note.[S19][S43]

### 3. Person-level title, control, and exit inside the shared foothold

A shared dwelling counts less when title, retirement claims, liquid buffers, or decision rights are concentrated in one household member while other adults depend on that foothold without secure claim, credible say, or realistic exit options.[S13][S27][S30]

### 4. Direct claims alongside pooled housing

The archive should ask whether adults inside a shared household also hold direct liquid, pension, savings, or other durable claims of their own.
A shared home counts more when it sits alongside direct person-level claims rather than substituting for them entirely.[S13][S27][S30]

### 5. Shock survivability under shared dependence

Claimant load is more serious when one illness, breakup, eviction risk, job loss, or family conflict can destabilise several adults at once because too many lives are tied to one thin foothold.
One fragile household anchor should not be read as broad resilience simply because it keeps many people barely attached.[S13][S27]

### 6. Measurement and scoring surface

The archive should ask whether the case is being judged with any per-person adequacy lens at all.
Household tenure, wealth, or owner counts should be read against claimant load, overcrowding, delayed household formation, and the scale of direct claims per adult rather than taken as self-sufficient evidence of broad independence.[S13][S19][S43]

## Fast use rule

Use this note in four steps:

1. **Ask how many people a foothold is carrying.**  
   Do not score a household asset package before asking how many adults or dependents actually rely on it.
2. **Check whether the arrangement is person-adequate.**  
   Ask about space, privacy, exit margin, direct claims, and ordinary independent life rather than tenure alone.
3. **Discount claimant dilution.**  
   Count less success when one thin foothold is doing the work of several missing footholds.
4. **Count de-crowding and independent entry as real progress.**  
   Credit reforms that reduce forced co-residence, improve person-level adequacy, and widen ordinary entry into housing and direct claims.[S19][S43]

Default shortcut:

- **secure shared foothold plus person-adequate scale** -> count it strongly because pooling is not doing hidden constitutional damage;
- **thin foothold carrying too many lives** -> discount it because household success is being bought through compression rather than broad independence;
- **mixed case** -> ask whether the arrangement is chosen and resilient or instead reflects blocked entry and weak person-level alternatives.

## Program implication

Once claimant load is read clearly, the archive becomes stricter about one common false comfort: household broadening that is really just many lives clustering around too little independent security.
That changes program judgment in two directions.
First, housing, wage, and early-capital reforms deserve more credit when they reduce forced pooling and reopen ordinary household formation.
Second, measurement should stop treating household owner or asset counts as morally equivalent across very different claimant loads.

This is why claimant load belongs inside the archive's constitutional reading of distribution rather than as a technical housing footnote.
A society is closer to justice when shared footholds are roomy, secure, and genuinely supportive of the people inside them—not when bottom-half survival depends on packing more claimants onto one scarce foothold.[S19][S27][S43]

## Bottom line

The archive should now read claimant load in this order:

1. **Ask whether the household foothold is large and secure enough for the people depending on it.**
2. **Discount household-level success when it depends on overcrowding, delayed exit, or claimant dilution.**
3. **Read one modest foothold carrying several adults as less than several person-adequate footholds.**
4. **Count de-crowding, direct person-level claims, and easier independent household formation as real equality gains.**
5. **Refuse to let household aggregation hide per-person inadequacy near the floor.**
