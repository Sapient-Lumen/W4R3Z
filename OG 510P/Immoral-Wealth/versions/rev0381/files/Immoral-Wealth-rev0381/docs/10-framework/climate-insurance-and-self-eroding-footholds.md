# Climate, insurance, and self-eroding footholds

## What this note is for

This note adds a climate/insurance surface to the archive's floor, housing, and washout screens. It is not a general climate-policy note. It asks a narrower wealth-order question:

**When does an asset that looks like a foothold become self-eroding because hazard exposure, insurance premiums, deductibles, repair costs, utility costs, credit terms, or exit limits make it too expensive or risky to keep?**

Use this note with [`carrying-costs-and-self-eroding-footholds.md`](carrying-costs-and-self-eroding-footholds.md), [`stress-tested-footholds-and-ordinary-shock-survivability.md`](stress-tested-footholds-and-ordinary-shock-survivability.md), [`spatial-concentration-and-place-based-closure.md`](spatial-concentration-and-place-based-closure.md), and [`../20-program/housing-land-and-anti-monopoly.md`](../20-program/housing-land-and-anti-monopoly.md).

## Minimum rule

A climate-exposed housing or land foothold does not count fully unless the holder can **insure, repair, remain, or exit** on non-destructive terms. Home equity that survives only while premiums stay low, claims are paid, disasters do not recur, public repair is discretionary, or buyers ignore risk is a **self-eroding** and **shock-fragile** foothold.[S59]

The archive should treat climate/insurance fragility as part of wealth inequality because it changes who can hold assets safely, who must self-insure, who is trapped in risky places, and who can move before losses are capitalized into prices.

## Four false passes

### 1. Nominal home equity is mistaken for secure wealth

A household may appear wealthier because it owns a home, but the asset counts less when insurance, repairs, taxes, utilities, mitigation costs, or deductibles absorb the gain. A foothold that cannot be kept under foreseeable climate stress is not a clean floor pass.[S59]

### 2. Insured status is mistaken for protected status

Insurance can be partial, expensive, unavailable, claim-fragile, or dependent on public backstops. The scoreboard should ask whether coverage is affordable, comprehensive enough, and durable through repeat events.

### 3. Place attachment is mistaken for real choice

A household may remain in a high-risk place because moving would crystallize losses, sever work/care networks, or require a deposit it does not have. That is not voluntary stability; it may be **place-closed** wealth exposure.

### 4. Public disaster aid is mistaken for a social floor

After-the-fact relief does not count as a durable public counterweight unless access is rule-bound, timely, equitable, and sufficient to prevent debt spirals or forced displacement.

## Scoreboard fields

Add these fields when a case has material hazard exposure:

| Field | Ask |
|---|---|
| hazard_exposure_by_income_and_group | Are lower-wealth or excluded groups concentrated in higher-risk places? |
| insurance_affordability | Are premiums and deductibles affordable relative to income and liquid buffers? |
| insurance_availability | Are insurers withdrawing, limiting coverage, or excluding key risks? |
| repair_cost_burden | Can ordinary owners repair without destructive debt or forced sale? |
| residual_public_backstop | Is public help rule-bound and timely, or discretionary and late? |
| exit_feasibility | Can households move without losing all foothold value? |
| municipal_fiscal_risk | Are local tax bases and services deteriorating in ways that compound wealth loss? |

## Routing consequence

Climate/insurance pressure can move four lanes:

1. **Floor lane:** when households need oversized private buffers to survive predictable hazards.
2. **Housing lane:** when risk-adjusted entry, repair, or insurance costs block secure tenure.
3. **Public/common lane:** when public insurance, mitigation, relocation, or infrastructure is needed to prevent private self-insurance from becoming coercive.
4. **Top-tail/rule lane:** when insurers, lenders, developers, or asset owners can shift losses onto households or the public while preserving upside.

## Program implications

A climate-exposed case should not be told simply to expand homeownership. It should first ask whether homeownership is still a safe foothold in the relevant places. The package may need resilient public housing, social insurance, relocation grants, land-use reform, infrastructure repair, insurer/lender rules, anti-displacement protections, and public-value capture from resilience investments.

The archive should prefer **risk-open footholds**: assets that remain usable under foreseeable hazard conditions, can be repaired without ruin, and preserve exit options.
