# Democratic non-domination and wealth power

The archive already treats wealth-to-rule conversion as a constitutional problem. rev0310 sharpens the democratic test: a wealth order cannot pass when large fortunes, asset-holder blocs, or intermediated controllers can make ordinary people live under rules they cannot contest on remotely equal terms.

This is not a demand that every citizen have identical influence. It is a non-domination rule. A society fails this surface when concentrated wealth can repeatedly buy agenda access, policy delay, exemption, intimidation, information control, or veto power over the ordinary routes by which people protect floors, footholds, housing, labor power, tax fairness, public assets, or remedies.[S15][S188][S189][S198]

## Minimum rule

A wealth order must show that the anti-closure program can survive democratic contest rather than being quietly neutralized by concentrated money. The test has five questions:

1. **voice equality:** can ordinary citizens and claimant groups be heard before rules are locked in;
2. **influence legibility:** can voters, journalists, researchers, regulators, and opponents see who is paying, lobbying, sponsoring, litigating, or controlling relevant institutions;
3. **agenda pluralism:** can wealth concentration shape the information order so strongly that alternatives become politically invisible;
4. **veto prevention:** can owner blocs, large donors, local incumbents, regulated firms, or dynastic vehicles block entry-opening reforms without carrying the public burden of doing so openly;
5. **survival under countermobilization:** when reform threatens high-value rents, are the legal, administrative, media, and political rails strong enough to hold.

If the answer is no, the case cannot receive a comfort verdict even when shares, taxes, or public programs improve.

## False passes

### 1. Disclosure without usable timing

Campaign, lobbying, media, or beneficial-owner records count less when they appear after the election, after the zoning decision, after the procurement award, after the litigation campaign, or after the rescue package. Late disclosure helps historians. It does not let citizens contest rule while it is being made.[S184][S186][S188]

### 2. Formal one-person-one-vote with one-dollar-many-vetoes

A jurisdiction can hold regular elections yet let concentrated money dominate candidate viability, issue advertising, ballot campaigns, litigation threats, lobbying capacity, or policy expertise. The archive should not treat that as ordinary pluralism merely because the final vote is formally equal.[S185][S186][S187][S199]

### 3. Media plurality on paper, agenda concentration in practice

Multiple outlets do not solve the problem when ownership, state advertising, covert sponsorship, platform reach, or politically connected owners produce one-sided agenda power. The relevant question is whether ordinary people can tell whose voice is speaking and whether rival information channels can survive.[S190][S191]

### 4. Expert advice without funding transparency

Think tanks, advisory panels, sponsored research, professional associations, and litigation shops can be legitimate. They become wealth-power channels when funding, clients, beneficial owners, or conflict interests are hard to see and when the same actors repeatedly define the evidence environment in their own fiscal or regulatory interest.[S188][S189][S199]

### 5. Local democracy as entrant exclusion

Local control is not automatically democratic when only current asset holders show up, when renters and would-be residents are absent by design, or when the affected future population has no vote. Land-use democracy can become an owner-veto device that converts existing housing wealth into exclusionary control over entry.[S195][S202]

## Trigger rule

Treat democratic non-domination as failing or under serious veto pressure when at least two of these are true:

- large-donor, independent-expenditure, or dark-money channels are material and poorly constrained;
- lobbying and advisory influence are weakly registered or reported late;
- media ownership or state-advertising dependence threatens pluralism;
- local asset-holder vetoes block supply, entry, or mobility while outsiders have weak voice;
- strategic litigation can exhaust public/claimant capacity faster than remedies can preserve the claim;
- foreign or cross-border money can enter political finance through weak permissibility or source-of-funds checks;
- enforcement bodies lack independence, investigative powers, data access, sanctions, or stable budgets.

The trigger is deliberately earlier than formal autocracy. Wealth-rule harm begins when money reliably changes the feasible reform set.

## Program implication

Once this surface is live, the archive should route through the democratic-power stack:

- political-finance caps, public matching, timely disclosure, donor-source checks, and enforcement powers;
- lobbying, advisory, and third-party-campaign transparency;
- media-ownership, state-advertising, and concentration safeguards;
- public-interest access to ownership and influence records;
- local-land-use reform that gives affected outsiders representation or higher-level review;
- reform-survival monitoring after enactment, not only during passage.
