# Downside discipline and loss-bearing

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's cross-cutting crisis and hierarchy judgments into a **stable loss-bearing rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the fuller emergency, anti-capture, and measurement notes:
**when should apparently improved wealth distribution still count as morally weaker because large owners, major intermediaries, or systemically important actors keep much of the upside while the public repeatedly absorbs or backstops a large share of the downside?**

The archive does **not** claim that every public backstop is illegitimate.
Sometimes the state really does need to protect deposits, payrolls, payments, basic credit channels, or broader household security under severe stress.
But those episodes are not distributively neutral.
An order in which owners, managers, and junior claimants bear real losses before the public steps in is not morally equivalent to one in which crisis language is repeatedly used to protect incumbent valuations, cheapen funding for dominant actors, and leave later fiscal repair to ordinary households or the public balance sheet.[S15][S23][S31][S32][S33][S49]

That matters because wealth concentration is partly a question of **who gets to compound under protection**.
Recent IMF guidance says public solvency support to banks should be a **last resort**, should maximise burden sharing, minimise moral hazard, and protect taxpayers; the same note also points to industry-funded resolution tools and recovery of support costs where possible.[S32]
A companion IMF note says broad asset purchases in NBFI stress can create moral hazard by operating as a **broad, untargeted safety net for asset holders**, whereas more targeted, collateralised support can better limit risk transfer.[S33]
Recent OECD work also stresses that state support and expectations of public support can entrench incumbents, lower their funding costs, and weaken entry or expansion by rivals, which means crisis support can alter market structure as well as immediate loss allocation.[S49]

## Minimum reading rule

Read crisis architecture and public backstops through a **loss-bearing symmetry screen**.
A wealth order counts more when private gains are matched by real private downside, when emergency action is aimed first at households and critical functions rather than incumbent paper wealth, and when any unavoidable public support is temporary, conditional, priced, recoverable, and paired with public upside capture or comparable restructuring leverage.[S31][S32][S33][S49]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**wealth counts less as morally acceptable when large private gains are being quietly underwritten by recurrent public downside.**

That means the archive should read apparent broadening more harshly when:
- crisis support repeatedly protects asset prices or incumbent balance sheets more than household security or critical functions,[S32][S33]
- first losses are not being borne mainly by shareholders, managers, and junior private claimants where feasible,[S32]
- public support arrives on soft terms with weak recovery, weak restructuring, or quick restoration of private extraction,
- expectations of support operate like an implicit subsidy that lowers funding costs for dominant incumbents and entrenches them against later entry or competition,[S49]
- or later fiscal repair is pushed onto weaker public services, weaker public wealth, or delayed broad-based capital formation.[S31][S32][S33]

## The six main false passes

### 1. Market calm is mistaken for fair loss allocation

A society should count less success when spreads tighten, funding markets reopen, or asset prices recover while the underlying distribution of losses remains tilted toward taxpayers, workers, tenants, or future public retrenchment.
Stability can be bought in ways that preserve hierarchy rather than discipline it.[S31][S32][S33]

### 2. “Last resort” language is mistaken for real discipline

A public backstop should count for less as a clean exception when the legal or rhetorical language is strict but the practical package still bypasses meaningful burden sharing, management accountability, restructuring, or recovery.[S32]

### 3. Support for critical functions is mistaken for support for incumbent wealth

Protecting payments, deposits, payrolls, and indispensable credit channels is not the same thing as preserving all incumbent paper claims on favorable terms.
The archive should discount cases where those two tasks are quietly fused.[S32][S33]

### 4. Broad household asset exposure is mistaken for shared downside treatment

The fact that households also own pensions or fund claims does **not** make every rescue distributively neutral.
If control rights, concentrated fortunes, or large intermediaries receive systematically softer downside than ordinary households do, the hierarchy problem remains live.[S26][S32][S33]

### 5. One rescue without recovery is mistaken for a temporary exception

A society should count less success when public risk absorption is treated as self-cleaning even though warrants, levies, clawbacks, dilution, payout restrictions, or equivalent recovery devices are absent or weak.[S32][S33]

### 6. Crisis support that entrenches incumbents is mistaken for neutral stabilisation

The archive should count less success when state support lowers incumbent funding costs, protects dominant scale, or discourages entry and expansion by weaker rivals.
A crisis package can stabilize today while worsening tomorrow's concentration map.[S49]

## The six standing loss-bearing surfaces

### 1. First-loss-allocation surface

Ask who actually absorbs the first serious losses when major actors fail.
A society counts more when shareholders, controlling owners, managers, and junior private claimants are diluted, restructured, or wiped before broad taxpayer exposure arrives except where strict function-protection reasons make that impossible.[S32]

### 2. Instrument-targeting surface

Ask what the instrument is designed to save.
A package counts more when it protects households and critical functions with targeted tools; it counts less when it relies on broad asset purchases, generalized valuation support, or other measures that spill a large untargeted benefit toward incumbent asset holders.[S33]

### 3. Term-and-pricing surface

Ask whether support is meaningfully temporary and costly enough to discourage routine dependence.
A package counts less when public guarantees, liquidity, capital, or insurance arrive on terms so soft that they behave like cheap standing protection rather than emergency discipline.[S32][S33]

### 4. Recovery-and-public-upside surface

Ask what the public gets back if it must absorb unusual downside.
A package counts more when the state receives equity, warrants, fees, levies, senior claims, clawbacks, payout restrictions, restructuring authority, or comparable mechanisms that convert emergency exposure into public recovery rather than one-way socialisation.[S32][S33]

### 5. Competitive-neutrality-and-entrenchment surface

Ask what the package does to market structure.
A society counts less progress when support creates or deepens implicit-subsidy advantages for dominant incumbents, weakens contestability, protects TBTF scale, or discourages entry by smaller rivals.
The archive should treat this as part of loss-bearing symmetry because support that changes future funding conditions is itself a long-run distributive intervention.[S49]

### 6. Post-crisis-repair surface

Ask what is left thinner after the panic passes.
A package counts less when household scars, public-balance-sheet damage, weaker public wealth, or delayed broad-based asset building are treated as unrelated after-effects rather than as part of the same loss-bearing event.[S17][S21][S22][S31][S32][S33]

## Fast use rule

Use this note in four steps:

1. **Ask what is actually being stabilised.**  
   Separate households and critical functions from incumbent valuations, portfolios, or management structures.[S32][S33]
2. **Ask who bears first losses and what private extraction is paused.**  
   Discount the case when shareholders, managers, and junior claimants escape serious loss while dividends, bonuses, buybacks, or analogous extraction resume quickly.[S32]
3. **Ask whether the support is temporary, priced, recoverable, and coupled to public upside or restructuring leverage.**  
   Count less progress when the public takes unusual risk without strong recovery devices.[S32][S33]
4. **Ask what the package does to future concentration.**  
   Discount the case when support entrenches incumbents, lowers their funding costs, protects TBTF status, or leaves later repair to weaker groups and future budgets.[S31][S49]

Default shortcut:
- **critical-function protection + real private loss + temporary terms + public recovery + low entrenchment** -> count as disciplined stabilisation rather than hierarchy maintenance;
- **broad incumbent-side support + weak burden sharing + soft pricing + no meaningful recovery** -> discount strongly because private gain remains publicly underwritten;
- **mixed case** -> ask whether the public is mainly buying time for fair restructuring or mainly preserving incumbent wealth on favorable terms.

## What this changes in classification

This note changes three ordinary readings.

First, it makes the archive stricter about calling a society **improved** just because top shares soften somewhat or because one crisis passed without collapse.
If the top still compounds under unusually soft downside discipline, the moral improvement is narrower than the snapshot suggests.[S31][S32][S33]

Second, it moves crisis architecture out of the emergency footnote lane and into the main constitutional reading of distribution.
A society that repeatedly socialises concentrated downside is not just managing stress badly; it is helping reproduce the very concentration it later claims to regret.[S31][S32][S33][S49]

Third, it links rescue design to anti-capture and anti-monopoly judgment.
When support expectations act like implicit subsidies, they become part of the hidden wealth state, part of wealth-to-rule conversion, and part of the long-run reconcentration mechanism rather than merely a technical central-banking issue.[S23][S31][S32][S33][S49]

Read this note especially when a case sounds like:
- “the rescue was necessary, so its distributive side effects do not matter,”
- “ordinary households also own capital, so the support was neutral,”
- “the package was temporary, therefore it does not belong in the inequality story,”
- or “market stabilisation itself proves the losses were allocated fairly.”

The archive's answer is that these claims require much stronger proof than they are usually given.
A wealth order is not close to just when concentrated private upside continues to rest on recurrent public downside delivered on privileged terms.

## Bottom line

The archive should now read downside discipline in this order:

1. **Ask what is being protected: households and critical functions, or incumbent wealth.**
2. **Ask who bears first losses before public risk is allowed to spread.**
3. **Discount packages that are soft, broad, weakly recoverable, or quickly permissive of renewed private extraction.**
4. **Treat support that entrenches dominant incumbents or TBTF expectations as a distributive failure, not just a competition footnote.**
5. **Refuse to count a society as morally improved when large private gains are still being quietly underwritten by recurrent public downside.**
