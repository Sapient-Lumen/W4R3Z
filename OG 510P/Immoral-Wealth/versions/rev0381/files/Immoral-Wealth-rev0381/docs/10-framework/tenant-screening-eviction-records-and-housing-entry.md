# Tenant screening, eviction records, and housing entry

Housing entry is a threshold moment. Tenant screening converts credit history, rental records, eviction filings, criminal records, income estimates, proprietary risk scores, and automated recommendations into access or denial.[S286][S287]

The CFPB tenant-screening market report identifies three central challenge areas: public records including eviction and criminal records; credit reports and credit scores; and customized rental risk scores with automated screening solutions.[S286] HUD's 2024 guidance says the Fair Housing Act applies to tenant screening and housing advertising even when AI, algorithms, or third-party screening companies are used.[S287]

## Wealth-order risk

Tenant screening is dangerous to wealth formation because it blocks the move before the household can stabilize. A family may have income, a voucher, or a deposit and still lose the unit if a screen uses stale records, sealed/expunged records, non-disposition records, wildcard matches, overbroad criminal categories, low credit, or an opaque rental-risk score.

## Required rails

- applicant sees the exact record and score before final denial;
- eviction filings are distinguished from judgments and outcomes;
- sealed, expunged, dismissed, satisfied, and domestic-violence-related records are suppressed or context-tested;
- credit scores designed for loan default are not blindly repurposed as rent-risk scores;
- landlords cannot outsource discretion to an off-the-shelf denial recommendation;
- applicants have a real chance to dispute accuracy and relevance before the unit is lost;
- disparate-impact monitoring is conducted for protected groups.

## Certification rule

Housing supply or rent subsidy cannot comfort-certify when tenant-screening records prevent eligible households from converting affordability into actual occupancy.
