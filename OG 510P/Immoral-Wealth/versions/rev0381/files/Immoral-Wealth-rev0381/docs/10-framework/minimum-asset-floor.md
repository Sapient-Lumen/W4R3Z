# Minimum asset floor

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's core judgments into a **stable floor rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the fuller target procedure in [`target-selection-rules.md`](target-selection-rules.md):
**what must ordinary households actually clear before a society can claim that the bottom half has a real foothold rather than a flattering paper share?**

The archive does not treat the floor as a single balance-sheet threshold.
A society can raise the bottom-half share somewhat and still leave households too fragile, too indebted, too illiquid, too exposed to carrying costs, too dependent on household controllers or gatekeepers, or too easy to strip back down after one ordinary shock.[S02][S142][S17][S19][S24][S25][S28][S29][S30][S39][S139][S41][S42][S43][S44][S45]

So this note keeps the floor **compact but functional**.
It treats the floor as a seven-part spine, then uses companion notes to discount weak, reversible, or maze-dependent passes.
That is meant to prevent two opposite mistakes at once:

1. reading the floor as a loose moral vibe with no operational content, and
2. reducing the floor to one gross net-wealth number that flatters insecure households.

## Minimum reading rule

Read the floor as a **functional pass condition**, not a flattering average.
A society should count the bottom half as having a real floor only when most ordinary households clear a compact package of positive position, liquid resilience, secure and defensible shelter, holdable claims, protected retention, and debt quality strong enough that one mild ordinary shock does not immediately reset them into renewed dependence.[S142][S17][S19][S24][S25][S39][S139][S42][S43][S44][S45]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**the bottom half should not merely own a little more on paper; it should be hard to knock back into humiliation, forced deference, or total restart through ordinary life.**

This note therefore treats the floor as a constitutional screen.
A society should read it more harshly when the apparent foothold is mainly:
- temporary price inflation,
- overleveraged housing entry,
- locked-up claims that arrive too late,
- household-level masking of person-level weakness,
- weak retention under spend-down or garnishment,
- high recurring carrying costs,
- or debt structures that continue extracting the gain away.

## The seven main false passes

### 1. Bottom-half shares rise but the foothold is still mostly paper

A society should count less progress when the bottom half owns a somewhat larger share only because asset prices rose, while households still lack liquid margin, secure tenure, or durable person-level claims.[S02][S142][S17][S19]

### 2. Positive net wealth is mistaken for usable resilience

A bottom-half household may be technically above zero while still having too little liquid room to survive short interruptions, predictable expenses, or ordinary administrative delay without distress borrowing, missed bills, or forced dependence.[S17][S39][S139]

### 3. Ownership is achieved through fragile leverage and housing overburden

Owner occupation or formal asset acquisition should count for less when the package relies on thin equity, high recurring housing costs, weak title defensibility, or rate and price sensitivity strong enough that a mild shock quickly wipes out the gain.[S07][S13][S24][S42][S44]

### 4. Long-run claims are mistaken for present independence

Retirement claims, matched accounts, cooperative equity, or other long-run assets should count for less when they are hard to access, easy to forfeit across ordinary transitions, or too dependent on one employer, administrator, household controller, or specialist intermediary.[S19][S28][S29][S30][S139][S41][S44][S45]

### 5. One thin household foothold is mistaken for several adequate person-level footholds

A household can look asset-secure while too many adults or dependents are compressed into one fragile dwelling, one account structure, or one controller-mediated claim, leaving inadequate person-level independence and diluted adequacy.[S43][S44]

### 6. Modest footholds are still easy to strip back down near the floor

A society should count less success when early gains are washed out by spend-down tests, sharp benefit cliffs, creditor pressure, weak anti-seizure protection, or routine compulsory depletion near the bottom.[S24][S25][S31][S39]

### 7. Calm-weather footholds are mistaken for durable footholds

A floor pass should count for less when buffers and claims survive only in benign periods and quietly fail under mild income interruption, local housing stress, moderate recurring carrying costs, or ordinary debt servicing pressure.[S17][S24][S25][S42]

## The seven standing floor surfaces

### 1. Positive balance-sheet position

Persistent negative net wealth should be unusual rather than normal.
The archive does not require identical bundles for every household, but it does require that chronic net-negative position not be the default condition of ordinary adults.[S142]

### 2. Liquidity and ordinary stress margin

Households should have enough liquid margin to absorb a short interruption or predictable expense without immediate debt distress, arrears, or eviction pressure.
This note therefore counts liquid resilience as part of the floor itself, not as a luxury add-on.[S17]

### 3. Housing security, adequacy, and defensibility

Ordinary households should usually have secure and adequate shelter, whether through renting or ownership.
The relevant pass condition is not ownership romance but stable tenure, affordable carrying burden, adequate living space relative to household composition, and the practical ability to prove and defend occupancy, title, or co-title rights without prohibitive friction.[S07][S13][S24][S43][S44]

### 4. Net holdability

A modest foothold should be cheap enough to keep.
Insurance, fees, taxes, maintenance, servicing costs, or similar recurring charges should not quietly self-erode the holding until the household owns something in gross terms but retains very little security in net terms.[S42]

### 5. Person-level long-run claim quality

Long-run claims should complement liquid buffers and housing rather than substitute for them.
They count more when they survive ordinary movement across jobs, sectors, households, and places; when they are not easy to interrupt unilaterally; when they are understandable to ordinary users; and when they do not depend too heavily on one gatekeeper.[S19][S28][S29][S30][S139][S41][S44][S45]

### 6. Retention and shielding near the floor

The floor is stronger when modest reserves and early claims are protected against rapid stripping, forced liquidation, or compulsory depletion near the bottom.
The archive should count less progress when households must repeatedly run back through asset depletion just to access ordinary protection.[S24][S25][S31][S39]

### 7. Debt quality and extraction discipline

Debt should not routinely neutralise the gains from foothold formation.
The floor is stronger when debt service is more compatible with retention, lower exploitation, and ordinary shock survival rather than acting as a standing extraction channel that quickly undoes modest assets.[S24][S25]

## Fast use rule

Use the floor this way in quick comparisons:

1. ask whether most bottom-half households clear the seven-part spine in function, not merely on paper,
2. discount the pass immediately when the apparent gain depends on leverage, lock-up, gatekeepers, claimant dilution, weak title, spend-down exposure, or self-eroding carrying costs,
3. read the floor more favorably when the social floor reduces forced self-insurance and lowers how much private buffer is required,
4. but do not let strong services excuse absent liquid margin, weak person-level claims, late footholds, or easy downward reset.

A society should therefore count as closer to justice when the bottom half is becoming genuinely harder to dispossess, harder to knock backward, and less dependent on household hierarchy, expert mazes, and shock luck for ordinary security.
