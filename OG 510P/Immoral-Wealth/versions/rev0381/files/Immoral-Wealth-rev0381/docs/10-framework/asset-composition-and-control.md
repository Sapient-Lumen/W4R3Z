# Asset composition and control constraints

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's older cross-cutting claims into a **stable constitutional reading rule**.
It answers a narrower question than the fuller target procedure in [`target-selection-rules.md`](target-selection-rules.md):
**why can a society still fail morally even when the headline shares improve, if the wrong people still control the wrong asset classes in the wrong forms?**

The archive does not judge wealth distribution only by percentile shares.
It also asks **what kinds of assets** are widely held, **which claims are liquid or durable enough to matter**, **which holdings remain mostly debt-fragile or purely paper**, and **who actually controls the strategic choke points of social life**.
Housing, land, liquid savings, retirement claims, business equity, infrastructure, finance, and media do different social and political work.
So a society can look better on the scoreboard while still failing in substance if the bottom gains mainly thin or illiquid claims, the middle is reduced to a housing-only bloc, or the top continues to dominate the assets that structure rent, investment, information, and governance.[S02][S13][S15][S142][S20][S26]

## Minimum reading rule

Read the asset mix through a **composition-and-control** rule.
A society should count as closer to justice only when better shares are arriving in forms that widen ordinary independence **and** reduce top-tail command over key asset classes.
That means bottom and middle gains should not be read generously when they are mostly:

- debt-heavy housing exposure with weak retention,
- illiquid or late-arriving claims that do little for ordinary bargaining power,
- household- or intermediary-mediated claims with weak person-level control,
- incumbent asset inflation without reopened entry,
- or broad financial exposure paired with unchanged stewardship bottlenecks at the top.[S13][S142][S20][S26]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**a wealth order is not morally repaired when the claim side broadens a little but the control side remains oligarchic.**

## The six main false passes

### 1. Better shares are mistaken for better asset position

A society should count less progress when the bottom half owns a somewhat larger slice of wealth but still does so mainly through fragile housing equity, debt-offset balances, or other thin claims rather than through a sturdier independence package that includes liquidity, secure tenure, and holdable long-run claims.[S13][S142]

### 2. Housing broadening is mistaken for a full asset mix

Owner occupation can matter a great deal, but the archive should count less success when the middle is basically a property bloc with weak liquid buffers, weak productive-capital exposure, and high vulnerability to price, rate, and local rent cycles.
A housing-heavy middle can still be civically thin and politically defensive.[S13][S142]

### 3. Broad capital claims are mistaken for broad control

Retirement funds, mutual funds, and similar vehicles can widen return claims without dispersing stewardship.
The archive should count less success when votes, engagement priorities, and governance norms remain concentrated in a narrow manager layer even though many households are nominally invested.[S20][S26]

### 4. Taxing the top is mistaken for reducing command over choke points

A top tail can pay more tax and still retain heavy control over land, rental housing, finance, infrastructure, media, or concentrated voting blocs in major firms.
Where command survives, the case should still be read harshly on the control surface.[S15][S20]

### 5. Public weakness is mistaken for private broadening success

A society should count less success when private ownership broadens somewhat while public/social wealth, land reserves, strategic funds, or other common counterweights remain too weak to discipline scarcity rents and reconcentration.[S02][S20]

### 6. Price gains are mistaken for de-oligarchised ownership

The archive should discount episodes where incumbent wealth rises through asset-price inflation, zoning scarcity, subsidy capitalisation, or platform/market-power revaluation while real entry, claim quality, or strategic control barely changes.[S13][S142][S20]

## The six standing asset-and-control surfaces

### 1. Bottom-half independence mix

The lower half should not merely own a token share.
It should hold a usable bundle:
positive or at least non-chronic net wealth, some liquid resilience, secure and defensible housing or tenure, portable long-run claims, and pathways into broader capital ownership that are not wholly backloaded or intermediary-dependent.[S13][S142]

### 2. Middle-class diversification rather than housing monoculture

The middle 40% should be prosperous in a **diversified civic** sense, not only through owner-occupied housing.
A stronger case includes housing equity where appropriate, but also liquid savings, retirement wealth, lower dependence on extractive debt, and some meaningful exposure to productive capital or member-governed common wealth.[S13][S142][S20]

### 3. Housing and land without landlord-rule

A society should read asset composition more favorably when housing security is broad, land appreciation is not mainly captured by incumbent owners, and large-scale landlord power does not dominate entry, local bargaining, or household vulnerability.
This is why housing composition and housing control have to be read together.[S13][S20]

### 4. Productive-capital claims with dispersed stewardship

Broadening is stronger when households, workers, public funds, cooperatives, or other member-governed institutions hold meaningful claims on productive capital **and** when stewardship is not bottlenecked in a few private asset managers or controlling blocs.
Claim dispersion without stewardship dispersion is only a partial pass.[S20][S26]

### 5. Strategic common wealth and public counterweight

The archive reads a case more favorably when public/social wealth holds enough land, infrastructure, utilities, housing capacity, or strategic investment power to offset private concentration and finance broadening over time.
This is not just a balance-sheet side issue; it is part of what makes the ownership order governable.[S02][S20]

### 6. Anti-oligarchic control of key choke points

The top tail should not routinely dominate the assets that structure mass life and rule formation:
residential land at scale, rental housing bottlenecks, strategic infrastructure, major finance channels, agenda-setting media, or concentrated voting control over large firms.
Where those choke points remain captured, the archive should treat the case as still in correction or anti-oligarchy territory even if the aggregate shares are moving in the right direction.[S15][S20][S26]

## Fast use rule

Use the composition note in this order:

1. ask whether bottom and middle gains are landing in **functionally different assets** or only in prettier paper balances,
2. ask whether households are gaining liquidity, tenure security, portable long-run claims, and some access to productive capital,
3. ask whether the top is losing **control** over key choke points or only surrendering some tax payments,
4. ask whether public/social wealth is strengthening as a counterweight,
5. discount the case immediately when broadened claims sit on top of unchanged landlord power, stewardship bottlenecks, or strategic private command.

Default shortcut:

- **better shares + same control map** -> only partial success;
- **better shares + broader household asset mix + same stewardship bottleneck** -> still partial success;
- **better shares + broader asset mix + weaker choke-point capture + stronger public counterweight** -> much stronger pass.[S02][S13][S20][S26]

## Program handoff

This note should make the archive stricter in four places.

First, it should stop the target discussion from treating percentile shares as if they were the whole ownership story.
See [`target-selection-rules.md`](target-selection-rules.md), [`ideal-wealth-distribution-bands.md`](ideal-wealth-distribution-bands.md), and [`acceptable-envelopes-and-substitution-limits.md`](acceptable-envelopes-and-substitution-limits.md).

Second, it should harden the archive's housing reading against cases where owner wealth rises but entry closes, landlord power persists, or public uplift keeps capitalising into incumbent asset values.
See [`../20-program/housing-land-and-anti-monopoly.md`](../20-program/housing-land-and-anti-monopoly.md) and [`publicly-created-value-and-common-asset-capture.md`](publicly-created-value-and-common-asset-capture.md).

Third, it should keep the archive from mistaking broad retirement or fund claims for fully dispersed capitalism when stewardship remains concentrated.
See [`intermediated-ownership-and-stewardship.md`](intermediated-ownership-and-stewardship.md).

Fourth, it should make anti-capture and common-wealth strategy easier to read as ownership-structure questions rather than as after-the-fact add-ons.

## Bottom line

The archive should now read asset composition this way:

1. **Do not stop at the shares.**
2. **Ask what assets ordinary households actually hold and how usable those claims are.**
3. **Ask who controls the strategic choke points even after reform.**
4. **Count broadened claims less when stewardship, land, infrastructure, finance, or media remain concentrated.**
5. **Treat stronger public/social wealth as part of the ownership answer, not a side issue.**

The point is not to demand identical portfolios for every household.
It is to stop calling a society broadly owned when the bottom gets thin claims, the middle gets housing monoculture, and the top keeps the levers that matter most.[S02][S13][S15][S142][S20][S26]
