# Upside-down subsidies and the hidden wealth state

## What this note is for

This note turns one of the archive's most important **state-made path-quality claims** into a stable reading rule.
It answers a narrower question than the full policy package in [`../20-program/tax-expenditures-and-subsidy-hygiene.md`](../20-program/tax-expenditures-and-subsidy-hygiene.md):
**when should a society count public support for wealth-building as morally weaker because the support mainly rewards already-positioned owners, scales with existing balance-sheet strength, or capitalises into prices rather than widening first footholds or common wealth?**

The archive already treats wealth distribution as a matter of **stocks, control, timing, floor integrity, and path quality**.
This note sharpens one especially easy false pass inside that wider picture:
a society can look reformist on the visible side of taxes and transfers while quietly running a **hidden wealth state** underneath through tax expenditures, credit privileges, guarantees, exclusions, and subsidy architecture that refill incumbent advantage.[S31][S36][S37][S46]

That means this note is not a separate doctrine so much as a **cross-cutting subsidy-reading rule**.
It tells the archive how to read housing support, retirement-saving support, credit support, and similar wealth-facing policies once incidence, capitalisation, visibility, and entrant-side effects are taken seriously.
The compact intuition is simple:
**public support for wealth-building should count less when it mainly strengthens incumbent position rather than widening durable entry.**[S31][S36][S37][S46]

## Minimum reading rule

Read wealth-relevant subsidies **net of incidence, net of capitalisation, and net of visibility**.
A policy should count more when its first durable effects widen entry, thicken lower-balance claims, reduce forced private burden, or build common/public wealth.
It should count less when its practical effect is to increase bids, inflate scarce asset values, reward larger contributions or higher marginal tax rates, or reinforce existing ownership position while appearing distributively benign in gross statutory form.[S31][S36][S37][S46]

The archive's compact intuition is:
**a subsidy is not pro-ownership just because it is labeled pro-ownership; it must actually widen durable footholds after leakages are counted.**

This note therefore reads policy architecture more harshly when the apparent support depends mainly on:
- tax relief that scales with income-tax rate, contribution size, or mortgage size,
- owner supports that mostly raise house prices, rents, seller gains, or land values,
- retirement-saving incentives that deliver larger advantages to higher earners or already-secure savers,
- off-budget guarantees, exclusions, or credit structures that are politically hidden but economically large,
- or fiscal effort that could have been delivered more cleanly through capped, refundable, matched, fixed-amount, direct, or supply-linked designs.[S31][S36][S37][S46]

## The six main false passes

### 1. Tax expenditures are mistaken for neutral background

A society should count less progress when major wealth-facing support sits in the tax code or guarantee architecture and therefore escapes the same scrutiny applied to visible spending.[S31]

### 2. Gross statutory generosity is mistaken for real household gain

The archive should discount the pass when the headline size of a subsidy flatters reality because much of the benefit leaks into prices, fees, spreads, or incumbent seller gains before ordinary households keep it.[S36]

### 3. Owner support is mistaken for entry support

A society should count less success when homeownership support mainly improves the position of existing owners, repeat buyers, or higher-balance households while first entrants still face later entry, higher leverage, or exclusion from high-opportunity places.[S07][S13][S36]

### 4. Retirement-saving support is mistaken for broad asset building

The archive should count less progress when retirement incentives mainly reward those already able to make larger contributions or benefit more from tax relief, while lower-income households receive weaker or less usable support than they would under flatter or direct designs.[S37][S46]

### 5. Hidden subsidy architecture is mistaken for equal citizenship support

A society should count less progress when large public support reaches households only indirectly through lenders, providers, tax intermediaries, or asset markets rather than through visible footholds, visible lower costs, or visible common wealth.[S31][S36][S37]

### 6. Fiscal effort is mistaken for entrant-friendly path quality

The archive should count less success when a politically expensive subsidy package still leaves the next cohort thinner, later, or more indebted because its effects are absorbed upward rather than delivered as genuine opening.[S13][S19][S31][S36]

## The six standing hidden-wealth-state surfaces

### 1. Beneficiary-incidence surface

The archive should ask who gets the first-order durable benefit.
A subsidy counts more when lower-wealth households, first entrants, younger adults, or ordinary savers receive the main practical gain.
It counts less when advantage scales with pre-existing ownership, larger balances, or higher tax liability.[S31][S37][S46]

### 2. Capitalisation-and-price-leakage surface

The archive should ask how much support is being absorbed into asset prices, rents, fees, or provider margins.
Support that mainly raises bids should count less than support that survives as real access or real retained position.[S34][S35][S36]

### 3. Visibility-and-budget-discipline surface

The archive should ask whether the measure is visible enough to be distributionally audited.
When comparable fiscal support would look regressive as direct spending but hides more easily as a tax expenditure, exclusion, deduction, or guarantee, the archive should read the policy more skeptically.[S31]

### 4. Design-gradient surface

The archive should ask whether the instrument is open-ended and balance-weighted or whether it is capped, flat, refundable, matched, or otherwise structured to avoid mechanically magnifying prior advantage.
Flatter gradients should count more than designs that rise automatically with existing position.[S37][S46]

### 5. Entrant-versus-incumbent surface

The archive should ask whether the policy widens first footholds or mainly deepens incumbent holdings.
A subsidy that enlarges incumbent paper wealth without making the next entrant safer, earlier, or less indebted should count less as morally valuable broadening.[S07][S13][S36]

### 6. Common-wealth-versus-private-refill surface

The archive should ask whether the fiscal effort is building durable shared capacity — public wealth, lower-cost common provision, infrastructure access, or protected minima — or whether it is simply refilling private balance sheets.
Where the same fiscal effort could build common wealth but instead hardens private advantage, the hidden wealth state should be read as a constitutional negative.[S31][S34][S35][S36]

## Fast use rule

Use this note in four steps:

1. **Ask where the money really lands.**  
   Who keeps the durable benefit after incidence and capitalisation are counted?[S31][S36]
2. **Ask whether the design scales with prior advantage.**  
   Does support rise with tax rate, contribution size, loan size, or pre-existing balance?[S37][S46]
3. **Ask whether entrants are genuinely opening.**  
   Are first footholds becoming easier, earlier, and less debt-heavy — or are incumbents simply being topped up?[S07][S13][S36]
4. **Ask whether the same fiscal effort could have been delivered more flatly and visibly.**  
   Prefer capped, refundable, matched, fixed-amount, direct, or supply-aware designs when they would produce better entrant-side incidence and clearer budget control.[S31][S36][S37][S46]

Default shortcut:

- **visible, flatter, entrant-weighted, low-capitalisation support** -> count more favorably because the public effort is actually widening footholds;
- **hidden, open-ended, incumbent-weighted, price-capitalising support** -> discount because the public effort is quietly rebuilding hierarchy;
- **mixed case** -> ask where the next cohort ends up after the support works through prices, balances, and gatekeepers.

## Program implication

Once subsidy architecture is read clearly, the archive becomes stricter about a common evasion: the idea that any asset-facing support should count as inclusive because it is formally universal or politically popular.
That changes program judgment in three directions.
First, the archive should prefer **entrant-weighted and lower-gradient support** over open-ended designs that scale with prior advantage.[S37][S46]
Second, housing-side subsidies should be read together with land supply, land-value capture, and price-capitalisation risks rather than assumed to work through clean household incidence.[S34][S35][S36]
Third, distributional budgeting should bring tax expenditures, guarantees, and hidden supports into the same evaluative field as visible spending, because the wealth constitution can be rebuilt through the revenue side just as easily as through the expenditure side.[S31]

This is why the hidden wealth state belongs inside the archive's constitutional reading of inequality rather than as a later implementation footnote.
A society is closer to justice when public support is becoming **more visible, flatter, more entrant-opening, less price-capitalising, and less dependent on already owning more to receive more**.[S31][S36][S37][S46]

## Bottom line

The archive should now read wealth-facing subsidies in this order:

1. **Ask who keeps the durable benefit after incidence and capitalisation are counted.**
2. **Discount support that scales mainly with prior advantage or existing balance-sheet strength.**
3. **Treat hidden tax-code and guarantee architecture as part of the wealth constitution, not neutral background.**
4. **Count flatter, visible, entrant-opening designs as real progress.**
5. **Refuse to let public support count like justice when it mainly refills incumbent wealth.**
