# Insurance scoring, surveillance pricing, and risk segmentation

Insurance can be a foothold protector or a foothold destroyer. It protects households from loss only when coverage is available, affordable, understandable, and not priced through opaque proxies that punish low-wealth status.

NAIC says credit-based insurance scores are used primarily in underwriting and rating, and insurers may group consumers by assigned risk category to adjust premiums.[S291] New York DFS and Colorado DOI now provide live governance anchors for AI systems, external consumer data, algorithms, and predictive models in insurance underwriting and pricing.[S292][S293]

## Surveillance-pricing bridge

The FTC's surveillance-pricing study shows a broader market risk: firms can use precise location, browser history, demographics, shopping behavior, and even granular web interactions to set individualized prices for the same goods and services.[S290]

That makes risk pricing a general wealth-order surface, not just an insurance issue.

## Required questions

- Are credit-based insurance scores allowed, restricted, or banned in the jurisdiction?
- Does the insurer use external consumer data or model features that proxy race, income, disability, neighborhood, medical vulnerability, or prior hardship?
- Are consumers told which factor caused the adverse price or denial?
- Can they correct data or challenge a model outcome before losing legally or practically necessary coverage?
- Are regulators able to test disparate impact, data quality, model drift, and vendor systems?
- Are individualized prices, discounts, or offers hiding a higher effective price for low-exit households?

## Certification rule

Do not count insurance availability as a buffer if pricing or underwriting converts credit, location, behavior, or opaque external data into unaffordable coverage for the households most exposed to loss.
