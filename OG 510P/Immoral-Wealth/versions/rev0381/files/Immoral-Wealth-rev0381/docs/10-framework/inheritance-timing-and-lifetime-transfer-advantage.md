# Inheritance timing and lifetime-transfer advantage

The cube should not treat inheritance only as a death-time bequest. The morally decisive transfers often happen earlier: rent-free housing, tuition, down payments, informal loans, job-entry subsidy, family business access, emergency help, and guarantees.

## Timing test

A transfer is **early-entry wealth** when it arrives before or during a threshold moment. It is **late compensation** when it arrives after the recipient already missed the threshold. The same dollar amount can therefore have different moral weight depending on timing.

Examples:

- a parental down payment before age 30 can substitute for years of savings and alter housing, school, partner, commute, and job options;
- a family guarantee can lower borrowing cost and reduce underwriting denial even when no cash transfer occurs;
- tuition support can reduce debt and allow unpaid internships, graduate education, or geographic mobility;
- a late bequest after retirement may improve comfort but cannot undo missed compounding, nonownership, or earlier debt.[S321][S324]

## Scoreboard consequence

Do not ask only “how many inheritances?” Ask:

1. what share of households receive lifetime transfers before major entry moments;
2. how large the transfer is relative to deposit, tuition, business-entry, repair, or care costs;
3. whether transfers are cash, housing, guarantees, business stakes, or education;
4. whether nonrecipients face equivalent public/social substitutes;
5. whether the timing gap is racialized, gendered, caste-marked, immigration-marked, or regionally stratified.[S322][S323]

## False passes

- **Bequest-only pass:** ignores lifetime gifts and guarantees.
- **Amount-only pass:** ignores timing and threshold relevance.
- **Expectation pass:** treats expected future inheritance as current security.
- **Household pass:** ignores person-level control when the transfer is mediated by parents, spouse, family firm, or trust.
