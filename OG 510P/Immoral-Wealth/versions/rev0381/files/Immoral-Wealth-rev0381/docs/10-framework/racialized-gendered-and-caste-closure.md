# Racialized, gendered, caste, Indigenous, and migrant closure

## What this note is for

This note sharpens the archive's group-closure screen. The companion note [`group-stratification-and-excluded-groups.md`](group-stratification-and-excluded-groups.md) sets the general rule: national averages cannot certify a wealth order when excluded groups remain on thinner, later, or less defensible ownership paths. This note names the case surfaces that should be checked before the archive lets a country use a national average as moral cover.

## Minimum rule

A wealth order is not acceptable when racialized, caste, Indigenous, gendered, migrant, regional, religious, disability, or other historically subordinated groups remain structurally later to first footholds, weaker in title/control, more exposed to displacement and debt, or less able to transmit secure claims. This is a constitutional veto surface, not a diversity appendix.[S28][S29][S30][S57][S58][S66][S67]

## Why the screen must be concrete

The U.S. Survey of Consumer Finances illustrates the anti-averaging problem: 2022 median wealth was around $285k for White families, $44.9k for Black families, $61.6k for Hispanic families, and $536k for Asian families.[S57] A national wealth gain can therefore coexist with very different degrees of security, entry, and staying power by group.

The gender/title surface is equally important. World Bank land-rights work emphasizes that women's ownership and control of housing, land, and other productive assets remain constrained by legal, administrative, inheritance, and registration barriers in many countries.[S28][S29][S58]

## Required surfaces

| Surface | Case question |
|---|---|
| median and lower-tail wealth by group | Are excluded groups still clustered near zero or negative wealth? |
| liquid buffers by group | Can excluded groups survive ordinary shocks without destructive debt? |
| title and control | Who is named on deeds, accounts, business assets, pensions, and land records? |
| early foothold timing | Do excluded groups reach secure adult footholds later or only through debt? |
| housing and place | Are safe, high-opportunity places open, or are groups displaced/segregated? |
| land and inheritance | Are inheritance, marital property, land registration, and customary systems equal in practice? |
| credit and business access | Are safe credit and procurement ladders open without harsher collateral or pricing? |
| carceral/legal-status debt | Do fines, fees, immigration status, or criminal-legal systems strip wealth or block claims? |
| data visibility | Are data privacy and measurement good enough to detect closure? |

## False passes

### 1. Universal eligibility is mistaken for equal access

A rule can be formally universal and still require collateral, documentation, legal status, inherited geography, or expert navigation that excluded groups are less likely to possess.

### 2. Homeownership is mistaken for equal housing security

A higher ownership rate does not pass if the homes are in higher-risk places, carry worse credit terms, have weaker title, face greater environmental risk, or are more likely to be lost in shocks.

### 3. Household wealth is mistaken for person-level claim

A household may own an asset while women, migrants, disabled people, younger adults, or other members lack title, control, exit rights, or practical bargaining power.

### 4. Small gap narrowing is mistaken for repair

A median gap can narrow while first entry, inheritance, business ownership, land title, and safe credit remain sharply group-gated.

### 5. Missing data is mistaken for neutrality

If the state cannot see group-specific wealth pathways, the archive should not infer inclusion. It should record proof debt and block comforting certification.

## Program implications

Group closure usually requires **universal-plus-targeted repair**. Universal floors and broad asset accounts matter, but they must be paired with the specific repairs needed to reopen blocked channels: title reform, inheritance enforcement, anti-discrimination, land and housing registration, credit-pricing oversight, debt relief, procurement access, local anti-displacement, and privacy-safe disaggregated measurement.

A package that raises average wealth while leaving excluded groups later, thinner, or more exposed should be scored as **group-closed**, not as broadly successful.
