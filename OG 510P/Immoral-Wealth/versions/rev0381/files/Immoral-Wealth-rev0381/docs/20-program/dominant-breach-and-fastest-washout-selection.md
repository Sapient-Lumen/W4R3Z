# Dominant breach and fastest washout selection

## What this note is for

The archive now has a minimum evidence pack, a case-memo workflow, an opening-package router, package tie-break rules, monitoring rules, and confidence labels.
But future case work still faced one thin judgment problem too often:
**when several serious failures are live at once, which one should count as the dominant breach, and which channel should count as the fastest washout?**

Without a compact rule here, case memos will keep drifting toward three bad habits:
- choosing the most rhetorical breach rather than the most constitutionally decisive one,
- confusing the main breach with the main reabsorption channel,
- or naming too many co-equal failures and then using that diagnostic sprawl to justify a policy shopping list.[S07][S15][S18][S19][S20][S21][S22][S23][S31][S32][S33]

This note gives the archive a **thin selection rule** so future country, city, sector, and cohort memos can move from evidence to routing without guesswork.

## Minimum operating rule

Every serious real-case memo should try to leave the reader with:
1. **one dominant breach**,
2. **one fastest washout channel**,
3. and at most **one secondary breach** that materially changes the package or the governing mode.

The archive's standing rule is:

**choose the dominant breach by asking which failure is doing the most mass constitutional damage now, and choose the fastest washout by asking which channel would erase visible gains fastest if left open.**

Those are often related, but they are not the same question.
A society can have **floor failure** as the dominant breach while **housing and debt washout** are the fastest reabsorption channels.
A society can have **top-tail rule** as the dominant breach while **opacity, procurement favoritism, rescue privilege, or media leverage** are the washout / protection channels that would undo weaker reforms first.[S07][S10][S15][S17][S20][S23][S31][S32][S33][S36]

So the note's main discipline is simple:
**do not let the breach and the washout collapse into one blur, and do not let ten live problems pretend to be equally first.**

## The two questions to ask first

### 1. Which failure is doing the most constitutional damage now?

This is the **dominant breach** question.
It is about what makes the case most morally unacceptable at present.
The archive usually expects the answer to come from one of six families:
- floor failure,
- late footholds / dynastic closure,
- control concentration,
- rent-washout and debt fragility,
- thin public/common counterweight,
- or wealth-to-rule conversion.[S13][S15][S18][S19][S20][S21][S22][S23][S31][S32][S33]

### 2. Which channel would erase visible gains fastest if left open?

This is the **fastest washout** question.
It is about what would reabsorb or neutralize improvement first.
The archive usually expects the answer to come from one of these families:
- housing and land capitalization,
- debt service and refinancing pressure,
- monopoly, fee, or markup extraction,
- subsidy capture or capitalisation,
- opacity and controller invisibility,
- procurement / rescue asymmetry,
- or some other channel by which gains are privately reclaimed faster than they are socially secured.[S07][S10][S17][S20][S23][S31][S32][S33][S36]

The first question chooses the **lead lane**.
The second chooses the **mandatory closure lane**.
That is why the archive needs both answers separately.[S07][S15][S18][S19][S20][S21][S22][S23][S31][S32][S33]

## The dominant-breach selection rule

When several serious failures are live, choose the dominant breach by reading them through **four screens**.

### 1. Mass reach

Which breach is exposing the largest share of ordinary people, entrants, or excluded groups to real constitutional harm right now?
A breach counts more when it is not just dramatic at the top but widely structuring ordinary insecurity, blocked entry, weak bargaining power, or thin footholds below.[S13][S18][S19][S20][S21][S22]

### 2. Depth and irreversibility

Which breach is producing the hardest-to-reverse damage if it stays open for another durability window?
Late footholds, repeated forced debt, housing lockout, concentrated control over strategic assets, or a strong money-into-rule channel usually count more than softer, more easily reversible distributional drift.[S15][S18][S19][S20][S23][S31][S32][S33]

### 3. Reinforcement power

Which breach is amplifying the others?
A breach counts more when it is the hinge that keeps several other failures alive at once.
Floor failure that keeps people exposed to debt and rent extraction, housing closure that nullifies wage gains, or top-tail rule that blocks corrective government should usually rise in the ranking for exactly this reason.[S07][S10][S15][S17][S20][S21][S22][S23][S31][S32][S33][S36]

### 4. Package-determining power

Which breach most changes what a serious opening package must look like?
A failure counts more when naming it correctly would materially alter the lead lane, the mode, or the pace of action.
This screen exists to stop the archive from choosing a breach that sounds important but does not actually steer the program answer.[S15][S20][S23][S31][S32][S33]

### Fast rule

If one breach wins clearly on **mass reach + reinforcement**, it should usually be the dominant breach.
If one breach wins clearly on **governability-blocking power** because wealth is already buying rule, vetoing correction, or blinding the state, it can outrank a wider but more governable mass breach and push the case into harder mode.[S15][S23][S31][S32][S33]

## The fastest-washout selection rule

Choose the fastest washout by reading candidate channels through **four different screens**.

### 1. Speed of reabsorption

Which channel can take back the gain first once reform starts moving?
Housing capitalization, rent passthrough, debt-service absorption, and fee extraction often score high here because they can move faster than slower household asset accumulation or public build-out.[S07][S10][S17][S20][S31][S36]

### 2. Breadth of erosion

Which channel can neutralize gains across the widest part of the intended population?
A washout counts more when it can eat gains for ordinary tenants, debtors, new savers, entrants, younger adults, or low-bargaining-power workers all at once rather than only for a narrow slice.[S07][S17][S20][S21][S22][S36]

### 3. Reliability of sabotage

Which channel most reliably turns a nominally progressive package into weaker real incidence?
The archive counts less seriousness when a reform depends on households outrunning a reabsorption path that predictably capitalizes support upward or charges it back out through debt, prices, or weakly visible extraction.[S07][S10][S17][S20][S23][S31][S36]

### 4. Closure importance

Which channel most clearly has to be closed in the opening package rather than later?
A washout counts more when leaving it open would make the rest of the package flatter itself almost immediately.
This is the archive's reason for forcing a mandatory closure lane into the opening package.[S07][S10][S17][S20][S31][S36]

### Fast rule

If one channel is the clearest **rapid reabsorption path**, treat it as the fastest washout even when some other channel is morally ugly but slower.
The washout question is about **what undoes improvement first**, not about which pathology is most rhetorically offensive.[S07][S10][S17][S20][S31][S36]

## The six main false passes

### 1. The most visible top-line failure is automatically called dominant

A large top share, one striking billionaire cluster, or one ugly sector headline does not automatically name the dominant breach.
The archive still asks which failure is doing the most constitutional damage to the many and which one most changes the governing posture.[S15][S18][S19][S20][S23]

### 2. The breach and the washout are collapsed into the same sentence

A memo counts less when it says only something like “housing is the problem” without clarifying whether housing is the dominant breach, the fastest washout, or both.
Those are different questions and they route differently.

### 3. The memo names too many co-equal breaches

A memo counts less when it lists four or five main breaches and then treats them as equally first.
That usually means the memo has not actually chosen a lead lane or closure lane yet.
The archive wants one dominant breach, one fastest washout, and at most one genuine secondary breach.

### 4. A morally ugly but slower channel is treated as the fastest washout

Opacity, capture, and dynastic insulation matter enormously.
But if rent capitalization or debt extraction would obviously reclaim gains sooner in the live case, the archive should still name those as the fastest washout and treat opacity or capture as durability / anti-capture issues unless they are already blocking ordinary correction outright.[S07][S15][S17][S20][S23][S31][S32][S33][S36]

### 5. A package preference is chosen first and the breach is reverse-engineered later

A memo counts less when the writer prefers a tax instrument, a public fund, or a labor reform in advance and then names the dominant breach to fit the instrument.
The archive wants the diagnosis to route the package, not the package to retrofit the diagnosis.[S15][S20][S23][S31]

### 6. Evidence thinness is used to treat everything as equally first

Thin evidence does not justify diagnostic sprawl.
It just lowers confidence.
Under proof debt, the memo should still name the best current dominant breach and fastest washout, then state its confidence honestly using [`confidence-labels-and-action-under-proof-debt.md`](confidence-labels-and-action-under-proof-debt.md).[S15][S23][S31][S32][S33]

## Default tie-breaks when the case is close

### A. Mass floor failure versus top-tail rule

If wealth-to-rule conversion is already materially blocking ordinary correction, blinding enforcement, or vetoing the package, it can become the dominant breach.
Otherwise, when both are serious, the archive will often treat **mass floor failure** as the dominant breach and **top-tail rule** as the durability / escalation condition that hardens the mode and the package.[S15][S20][S21][S22][S23][S31][S32][S33]

### B. Late entry versus current floor failure

If ordinary people are failing now because the floor is too thin to survive, **current floor failure** should usually dominate.
If the current floor is somewhat passable but the case is still reproducing closure because footholds come too late and too parent-dependently, **late entry / dynastic closure** can dominate.[S18][S19][S21][S22][S52]

### C. Rent-washout versus weak earnings

If earnings gains would quickly be eaten by rents, debt, fees, or markups, the archive should often treat **rent-washout** as the fastest washout and may even treat it as the dominant breach when it is the hinge keeping floor and wage repair from sticking.
If the main failure is that households simply have no saveable room even before washout, **weak earnings** may remain the dominant breach while washout stays the mandatory closure lane.[S07][S08][S09][S10][S17][S20][S21][S22][S31][S36]

### D. Thin public/common counterweight versus direct lower-half breach

Weak public/common wealth matters because it lowers durability and raises forced private dependence.
But unless its absence is the main reason ordinary households are presently failing, the archive will often treat it as a durability problem that must be added to the package rather than as the dominant breach itself.[S02][S12][S20][S21][S22][S23][S31]

## Minimal memo add-on

A future case memo can usually stay thin by adding one short block after the target-distance section:

- **Dominant breach:** one phrase + one sentence on why it outranks the others
- **Fastest washout:** one phrase + one sentence on how it would reabsorb visible gains
- **Secondary breach, if any:** one phrase + one sentence on why it materially changes the package or the mode
- **Confidence in this selection:** certification-grade / action-grade / directional / too thin

That is usually enough.
The archive does not need long diagnostic essays every time.
It needs a stable shorthand that makes routing choices legible and contestable.

## Fast use rule

Use this note in six steps:

1. **List the live serious failures.** Usually no more than three or four candidates should survive this first pass.
2. **Choose the dominant breach by mass reach, depth, reinforcement, and package-determining power.**
3. **Choose the fastest washout by speed, breadth, reliability of reabsorption, and closure importance.**
4. **Keep them separate unless the same channel really wins both tests.**
5. **Name only one genuine secondary breach.** Add it only if it changes mode or materially changes the opening package.
6. **Then route the package.** Use the dominant breach for the lead lane and the fastest washout for the mandatory closure lane.

## Bottom line

The archive now has a cleaner answer to a recurring case-work problem:
**when several failures are live, what actually comes first?**
Choose the dominant breach by which failure is doing the most mass constitutional damage now.
Choose the fastest washout by which channel would erase visible gains fastest if left open.
Keep those judgments separate, keep the list short, and let them steer the package rather than the other way around.[S07][S15][S18][S19][S20][S21][S22][S23][S31][S32][S33][S36]
