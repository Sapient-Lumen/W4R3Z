# Monetary policy, asset prices, and wealth

## What this note is for

This note adds a macro-financial channel to the archive's washout and top-tail screens. It is not a claim that central banks are the primary cause of wealth inequality, and it is not a recommendation that stabilization should be withheld from a collapsing economy. It asks a narrower question:

**When do monetary and credit conditions protect broad security, and when do they reflate asset-holder advantage without enough repair for non-owners, renters, new entrants, and the bottom half?**

Use this note with [`downside-discipline-and-loss-bearing.md`](../10-framework/downside-discipline-and-loss-bearing.md), [`emergency-exceptions-sunsets-and-repair-duties.md`](emergency-exceptions-sunsets-and-repair-duties.md), [`housing-land-and-anti-monopoly.md`](housing-land-and-anti-monopoly.md), and [`measurement-and-scoreboard.md`](measurement-and-scoreboard.md).

## Minimum rule

A case should not treat macro-financial stabilization as distributionally neutral. Asset purchases, low rates, credit easing, lender backstops, and emergency liquidity can support employment and prevent collapse, but they can also raise the prices of financial assets and housing held disproportionately by asset owners. The archive should therefore ask whether stabilization is paired with repair for renters, first entrants, lower-wealth households, and public balance sheets.[S61][S62][S70]

## Core reading

The ECB has noted that euro-area residential property prices increased nearly 50% from the start of QE in 2015 to their 2022 peak, and that asset-price effects can exacerbate wealth inequality through portfolio-rebalancing channels.[S61] Recent cross-country research on asset-purchase programs finds wealth-inequality effects more pronounced and persistent than income-inequality effects.[S62]

The archive should use those findings as a warning surface, not as a one-size-fits-all verdict. The right question is case-specific: who held the assets, who received the credit, who bore the downside, and what repair followed?

## False passes

### 1. Stabilization is mistaken for broad repair

Preventing collapse is valuable. But if asset owners recover quickly while renters, young entrants, and the bottom half face higher prices, thinner buffers, and more debt, stabilization has not produced a wealth-order pass.

### 2. Lower mortgage rates are mistaken for housing access

Lower rates can help some buyers, but they can also capitalize into prices when supply is constrained. The scoreboard should look at entry-adjusted affordability, not rates alone.

### 3. Aggregate net worth is mistaken for broad wealth

Rising aggregate wealth can mask a distributional shift toward those who already owned financial and housing assets.

### 4. Emergency discretion is mistaken for public wealth

Public balance-sheet support counts less when gains are privatized, losses socialized, and repair duties are weak or unenforced.[S32][S33]

## Scoreboard fields

| Field | Ask |
|---|---|
| asset_price_incidence | Which wealth groups owned the assets whose prices rose? |
| entry_adjusted_housing_affordability | Did lower rates improve entry, or mostly lift prices? |
| renter_nonowner_repair | Did renters/non-owners receive offsetting support or footholds? |
| credit_allocation | Did credit easing reach small firms and households, or mainly incumbents? |
| public_loss_recovery | Were rescue costs recouped from beneficiaries? |
| distributional_monitoring | Did authorities publish distributional analysis and repair plans? |
| unwind_risk | Does tightening later strip fragile entrants while incumbents keep prior gains? |

## Routing consequence

This surface can move three lanes:

1. **Washout lane:** when asset-price inflation erodes affordability and reabsorbs wage or transfer gains.
2. **Top-tail lane:** when financial-asset revaluation and private-market gains thicken top shares.
3. **Public/common lane:** when public backstops create repair debt, warranting clawbacks, public equity, recovery levies, or broad social wealth claims.

## Program implication

A serious wealth program should not leave asset-price channels outside the scoreboard. Where stabilization lifts asset values, the archive should ask what compensating policy protects first entrants and non-owners: social housing, land-value capture, public equity stakes, renter support, progressive taxation of windfalls, debt relief, or universal/social inheritance.
