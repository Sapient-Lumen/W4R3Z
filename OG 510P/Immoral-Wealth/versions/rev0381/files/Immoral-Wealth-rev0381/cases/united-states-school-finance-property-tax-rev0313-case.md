# United States school finance and property-tax place closure case — rev0355

## Verdict

**Correction required.** The United States cannot certify childhood early-entry equality while school finance and facilities remain materially tied to local property wealth, state formula quality, and residential segregation. NCES shows local sources remain a very large share of K–12 public-school revenue, while current school-funding fairness work continues to find persistent adequacy and distribution gaps.[S238][S239]

## Dominant breach

The dominant breach is **childhood public-asset segregation through place finance**. Housing markets and local tax bases can convert family wealth into better public childhood assets even before inheritance, parental deposits, or college financing enter the story.

## Core reading

The school-finance screen does not say all property taxation is immoral. The Lincoln Institute source is useful because it shows why property tax is stable and locally accountable while still creating the school-funding dilemma. The cube’s rule is narrower: local finance fails when state aid, regional sharing, capital support, and anti-segregation safeguards do not neutralize property-wealth advantage.[S240]

The facilities layer matters separately. A district can look partially equalized on operating revenue while school buildings, HVAC, lead, broadband, labs, libraries, and safe transport remain capital-wealth artifacts. The facilities case therefore routes to Gate 15 and Gate 3 at the same time.[S242]

## Gate 15 finding

Gate 15 is **blocked/watch**. State reform can reduce income-based gaps, but a comfort verdict needs direct evidence on race/place gaps, facilities capital, tax effort, and post-relief sustainability.[S241]

## Opening package

State-aid equalization, facilities capital, low-wealth district match relief, regional tax-base sharing where feasible, local tax relief that does not starve schools, and race/place outcome audits.

## Evidence debt

District-level cost-adjusted operating funds, facilities condition, tax effort, racial/ethnic exposure, special education costs, transportation, and the post-ESSER fiscal cliff.
