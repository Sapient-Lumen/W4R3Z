# U.S. climate-insurance stress case — rev0355

## Case header

- **Case:** U.S. climate-insurance / homeowners-insurance stress surface
- **Memo ID:** `us-climate-insurance-rev0304-case`
- **Status:** active stress-surface case
- **Date:** 2026-05-21
- **Case type:** climate-insurance self-eroding foothold
- **Dominant breach:** nominal housing wealth becomes self-eroding when insurance, deductibles, repairs, nonrenewals, and uninsurable risks rise faster than household buffers
- **Fastest washout:** premium shock, deductible shock, repair shock, mortgage escrow shock, forced sale, underinsurance, and property-value repricing
- **Verdict:** **emergency repair in exposed local markets; national certification blocker where hazard exposure is material**

## Why this case matters

The archive's housing doctrine cannot treat home equity as automatically good lower-half wealth. Treasury's 2025 Federal Insurance Office release states that homeowners insurance costs are rising and availability is declining as climate-related events take their toll.[S374] NOAA's billion-dollar disaster database records major U.S. weather and climate disaster exposure and provides a hazard-cost anchor for the stress surface.[S84]

This is not a climate-policy note. It is a wealth-order note. When insurance becomes unaffordable, unavailable, unreliable, or too deductible-heavy, a household's main foothold can turn into a liability. That makes homeownership a false pass unless the case tests the net holdability of the asset.

## Threshold-moment diagnosis

The decisive moments are:

1. renewal notice or nonrenewal;
2. premium and escrow jump;
3. named-storm, wildfire, hail, or flood deductible;
4. denial, delay, or partial claim payment;
5. repair-cost inflation;
6. mortgage lender insurance requirement;
7. property-value repricing after risk becomes legible;
8. choice between relocation, underinsurance, or debt.

The archive should treat these as **asset survivability tests**, not merely environmental risk indicators.

## Opening package

1. **Measure net holdability.** A housing foothold passes only after premiums, deductibles, taxes, utilities, repairs, and mitigation costs are included.[S374]
2. **Protect low-wealth homeowners and renters without subsidizing avoidable risk.** Means-tested mitigation, relocation, repair, and insurance support should be paired with land-use and disclosure rules.
3. **Make climate risk legible.** Buyers, renters, lenders, insurers, and local governments need accessible hazard, premium, claims, and nonrenewal data.[S374][S84]
4. **Avoid public-loss privatized-gain.** Public backstops need clawbacks, pricing discipline, or adaptation requirements where they preserve asset values.
5. **Do not certify housing-led wealth programs in exposed markets without this screen.** A homeownership subsidy can become a self-eroding foothold subsidy if climate insurance is ignored.

## What would change the verdict

The stress case improves when premiums and coverage stabilize, mitigation reduces risk for low-wealth households, claims are reliable, public supports are rule-bound and non-regressive, and exposed households have real relocation or adaptation choices. It worsens when nonrenewals, deductibles, and premiums rise while low-wealth households are trapped in declining or uninsurable assets.

## Required next evidence

- county/ZIP premiums, nonrenewals, deductibles, claims, and coverage gaps by income/race/age;
- underinsurance and forced-sale incidence;
- mortgage delinquency and escrow shocks linked to insurance;
- public residual-market and backstop exposure;
- adaptation/mitigation support by household wealth;
- property-value repricing in hazard-exposed markets.
