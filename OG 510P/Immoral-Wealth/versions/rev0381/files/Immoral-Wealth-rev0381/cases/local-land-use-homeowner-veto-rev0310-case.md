# Local land-use homeowner-veto case — rev0355

## Verdict

**Correction required where entry depends on incumbent-owner consent.** The case treats local land-use governance as a democratic-power surface. A place can have legal meetings, public notice, and local votes while still letting incumbent owners turn property wealth into an entry veto over renters and would-be residents.

## Dominant breach

The dominant breach is **entrant exclusion by incumbent asset-holder veto**. The moral problem is not every neighborhood objection; it is the systematic mismatch between who benefits from scarcity, who attends and votes, and who pays through rent, commuting, delayed family formation, lost opportunity access, and parent-backed threshold dependence.[S195][S196][S202]

## Why this case exists

This case links the democratic-power layer back to the cube's housing, foothold, place-closure, and family-gate surfaces.

## Core reading

The 2024 CEA housing chapter describes zoning and land-use regulation as generally enriching existing property owners at the expense of renters and would-be owners, and says locations with more homeowners tend to have stricter housing-supply regulations as homeowner political influence grows. OECD housing sources provide cross-country affordability and policy indicators, while the Netherlands 2025 survey chapter shows how permitting, land speculation, tenure imbalances, and municipal incentives matter in a concrete country case.[S195][S196][S197]

## Gate 12 finding

Gate 12 is **blocked** in jurisdictions where local control lacks entrant voice, regional review, and supply responsiveness while housing scarcity is a dominant breach.

## Opening package

Use local-land-use democracy router: map veto points, add entrant representation, regional/state review, by-right approvals, anti-displacement, fiscal incentives, and public-evidence design where supply skepticism rather than narrow self-interest is the binding obstacle.

## Evidence debt

Evidence debt remains on local meeting participation, owner/renter turnout and voice, discretionary approval timelines, litigation delays, per-jurisdiction supply elasticity, and distributional effects by age, race/caste/ethnicity, income, and disability.
