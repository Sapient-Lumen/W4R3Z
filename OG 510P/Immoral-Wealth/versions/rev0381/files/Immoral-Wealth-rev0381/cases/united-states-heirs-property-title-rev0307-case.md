# United States heirs-property and title-finality case — rev0355

## Verdict

**Verdict:** `correction_required` as a title-security subsystem case.  
**Mode:** `correction` / rails-first.  
**Dominant breach:** occupied or inherited property can fail as wealth when title is cloudy, fractionalized, undocumented, or difficult to prove.  
**Fastest washout:** partition pressure, inability to finance repairs, disaster-aid proof failures, legal costs, and predatory acquisition.

## Why this case was added

The archive has strong housing and climate-insurance doctrine, but it still needed a clearer title-finality surface. Heirs property shows that “homeownership” can be overstated when ownership is not legally usable.

USDA’s Heirs’ Property Relending Program explicitly targets inherited land without clear title or documented legal ownership.[S130] HAC/Fannie Mae research identifies potential residential heirs-property properties and emphasizes legal aid, mediation, and financial support for title clearing.[S131] FEMA’s damaged-home documentation guidance shows how ownership and occupancy proof remain live gates for disaster aid.[S132]

## Current reading

This is a correction-required subsystem because the affected asset may be real in use but weak in law. A household can have shelter, family history, and land attachment but still lack the title finality needed to:

- get a repair loan;
- insure or rebuild;
- sell at fair value;
- resist partition;
- access disaster recovery;
- consolidate ownership;
- use equity for education, business, relocation, or safe exit.

## Threshold moments

1. death of owner without clear succession;
2. tax, maintenance, or repair bill;
3. co-owner dispute or partition petition;
4. attempt to borrow against equity;
5. disaster-damage application;
6. insurance claim;
7. sale or buyout offer;
8. intergenerational transfer.

## Opening package

Lead with title rails:

1. legal-aid and mediation capacity;
2. low-cost title search and succession planning;
3. partition-sale protections;
4. funds for buying out fractional interests;
5. alternative disaster-aid documentation;
6. anti-predatory buyer rules;
7. repair/climate-adaptation grants for cloudy-title households;
8. data on heirs-property risk by race, place, tenure, age, and disaster exposure.

## Doctrine consequence

A homeownership statistic cannot certify a wealth order if ownership is less defensible for some groups. The relevant unit is not just “has a home.” It is “has a home that can be kept, repaired, insured, financed, transferred, and defended.”
