# United States claim-conversion case — rev0355

## Verdict

**Verdict:** `correction_required` as a subsystem case.  
**Mode:** `mixed` / floor-repair plus rails.  
**Dominant breach:** public and market claims exist, but take-up gaps, paperwork churn, account/fee leakage, and tax/benefit complexity prevent full conversion into usable lower-half security.  
**Fastest washout:** administrative delay, non-take-up, paid intermediation, overdraft/NSF/account fees, and renewal interruption.

## Why this case was added

The United States already appears in the portfolio as a rich asset-price democracy with housing, parent-backed entry, racial closure, liability-stack, and top-tail problems. rev0307 adds a narrower question: **can people convert public and quasi-public claims into usable money at the threshold moment?**

This matters because the U.S. relies heavily on refundable tax credits, tax filing, means-tested benefits, fragmented agencies, private bank accounts, private tax preparation, and private payment rails. A system can spend large amounts and still fail conversion.

## Current reading

The U.S. does not fail every conversion surface. The EITC reaches millions of households and pays large annual amounts.[S123] Banking access has improved: the FDIC reports a historically low unbanked rate in 2023, though millions remained unbanked and a much larger group remained underbanked.[S263]

The problem is that the conversion layer remains brittle:

- the IRS/Census EITC participation estimate shows that a substantial eligible minority still does not receive the credit;[S122]
- FDIC underbanked data show that mainstream banking access is not universal and that nonbank transaction/credit channels remain distributionally relevant;[S263]
- CFPB overdraft/NSF evidence shows that account systems can drain small balances even after reforms;[S262][S129]
- benefit programs can be interrupted by paperwork churn, as shown by SNAP recertification evidence;[S140]
- direct payment and free-filing experiments are policy-dependent rather than yet stable infrastructure.[S123]

## Threshold moments

1. first tax season after low earnings;
2. child birth or custody change;
3. rent or utility arrears;
4. SNAP or Medicaid renewal;
5. new bank account or overdraft event;
6. first refund or refundable credit;
7. identity mismatch or fraud flag;
8. emergency application after job loss, illness, or disaster.

## Proof debt

The case needs direct evidence on:

- net usable value after paid filing, refund delays, offsets, bank fees, and account penalties;
- claim outcomes by race, language, disability, immigration status, rurality, banking status, and household form;
- denial/recertification/appeal data across major benefits;
- what share of public transfers arrive in safe, low-cost, person-controlled accounts;
- how many low-income households use paid help to claim supposedly public benefits.

## Opening package

Lead with a conversion package:

1. stabilize free or public filing and prefilled claims where feasible;
2. expand automatic enrollment and no-wrong-door benefit access;
3. protect benefits and refunds from avoidable fees, offsets, and garnishment;
4. ensure safe low-cost account/card/cash-out options;
5. reduce renewal churn;
6. measure conversion failures by subgroup;
7. create fast appeal and error-correction paths.

This is not a small administrative appendix. It is the difference between a paper floor and a usable one.
