NAME: Jim Withers / Operation Safety Net / Street Medicine Institute — street medicine house-call-to-the-unsheltered office
LOCATION: Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, USA; translocal through the Street Medicine Institute field network
OFFICE: Street medicine / house-call-to-the-unsheltered / clinical threshold where the clinic door does not reach
STATUS: KEEP; OFFICE-CANON CANDIDATE FOR STREET MEDICINE; PRIVACY, ENCAMPMENT-SAFETY, LAW-ENFORCEMENT-PROXIMITY, AND HOUSING-SUBSTITUTION FLAGS

SUMMARY:
Jim Withers and Operation Safety Net are admitted as a street-medicine office: direct clinical care offered to people living outside, under bridges, in camps, in alleys, and in other places they call home. Street Medicine Institute's own history begins the office in 1992, when Withers, with a formerly homeless guide and liaison, began medical visits to people living on Pittsburgh streets. Pittsburgh Mercy's current Operation Safety Net pages corroborate the Pittsburgh origin and the house-call shape. SMI is now treated as a field-building institution rather than a single heroic doctor story.

WHY THIS FITS THE CUBE:
The threshold is not a hotline, shelter, grave, or law. It is the place where a sick person cannot cross the clinic's threshold, and the clinician must decide whether the clinic's threshold was morally located in the wrong place all along. The office is not “bringing services” as spectacle. It is repeated return, relationship, scope-limited care, records, navigation, warm handoff, and the humility to treat a tent, bridge, alley, or sidewalk as the patient’s current clinical site without pretending it is a home in the sense justice requires.

OFFICE SHAPE:
- Clinical care, relationship-building, and navigation for unsheltered people where they live.
- Use of people with lived experience and street knowledge as guides, liaisons, and trust-bridges.
- Hospital interface: emergency department, inpatient consult, respite care, continuity clinic, and navigation through a larger health system.
- Field-building: training, program support, clinical guidelines, and a global street-medicine community.
- Boundary discipline: privacy, consent, scope of practice, confidentiality, encampment-location safety, and refusal to let street medicine become a substitute for housing.

EVIDENCE:
1. Street Medicine Institute says that in 1992 Withers dressed in tattered clothes and began medical visits to people living on Pittsburgh streets with a formerly homeless guide and liaison, motivated by people excluded from mainstream care.
2. Pittsburgh Mercy says Withers partnered with formerly homeless street-savvy individuals in 1992, made house calls to people living on Pittsburgh streets, and that Operation Safety Net and street medicine were born from those rounds.
3. Pittsburgh Mercy's current street medicine page describes teams making house calls to people experiencing homelessness in Allegheny County, including camps, alleys, under bridges, overpasses, and other places people call home.
4. UPMC Mercy's fellowship page shows the hospital-interface office: ED interface, inpatient consult, outpatient referral, continuity clinic, respite care, and navigation.
5. SMI describes itself as facilitating and enhancing direct healthcare to unsheltered homeless people where they live and as supporting programs in many cities, countries, and continents.
6. Field-guidance and ethics sources support the boundary: outreach is outside clinic walls and relationship-based; care must attend privacy, safety, confidentiality, law-enforcement proximity, and the fact that permanent housing remains necessary for justice.

DOOR-ETHICS FLAGS:
- Outreach is not housing. The office must not let the medical visit become the moral substitute for a safe, permanent place to live.
- A tent, camp, sidewalk, alley, bridge, or overpass is a clinical site only because care has failed to reach the person elsewhere. Do not romanticise it as “home” without the scare quotes the sources themselves require.
- Mapping, geocoding, outreach routing, and encampment knowledge can help care; they can also expose people to sweeps, policing, surveillance, stigma, or predatory attention. The cube must not record operational locations, routines, staff patterns, or patient identifiers.
- Trust-bridge roles held by people with lived experience must not be flattened into inspirational scenery or unpaid extraction.
- Field-building claims are not quality claims for every program using the label “street medicine.”

BOUNDARY NOTE:
Street medicine is admitted strongly because it answers a real threshold failure: the clinic waits in the wrong place. It is also held under high evidence debt because outreach can become surveillance, clinical care can become housing theater, media can turn a patient encounter into a scene, and medicine can be asked to launder a city's refusal to house people.
