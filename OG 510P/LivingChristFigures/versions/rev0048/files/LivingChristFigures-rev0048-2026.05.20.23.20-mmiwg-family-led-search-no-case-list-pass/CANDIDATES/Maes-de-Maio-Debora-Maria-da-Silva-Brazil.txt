Débora Maria da Silva / Mães de Maio (Mothers of May) — Brazil

Status: KEEP WITH FAMILY-OF-VICTIM AND STATE-PERPETRATOR FLAGS; OFFICE-CANON FOR THE BRAZILIAN VARIANT OF SEARCH-FOR-THE-DISAPPEARED
Location: Baixada Santista and São Paulo state, Brazil; translocal voice through national civil-society networks (Conectas Direitos Humanos)
Office: Mothers' refusal of impunity / memory of state-perpetrated killings / search-for-truth-and-named-victim where the killer wore the uniform

Work:
Between 12 and 21 May 2006, in retaliation for coordinated attacks by the PCC (Primeiro Comando da Capital) on São Paulo's police and prison system, the São Paulo Military Police and allied extermination groups killed more than 500 civilians — overwhelmingly young, Black, and from the city's peripheries — in what came to be called the Crimes of May. Débora Maria da Silva's son, Edson Rogério Silva dos Santos, a 29-year-old street sweeper (gari), was shot dead on 15 May 2006 at a gas station in the Baixada Santista, a day after the family's Mother's Day celebration. From shared grief Débora and other bereaved mothers built Mães de Maio, a network commemorating the dead, demanding inquiry and accountability, and reframing the official "PCC violence" narrative as state-led killing. Twenty years on (May 2026 anniversary marked with extensive Brazilian press coverage), one police officer is imprisoned for three of the deaths; no other agents have been held criminally accountable. The movement has worked with Conectas Direitos Humanos to bring the case before the UN.

Why it belongs / might belong:
The rev0011 SEARCH-FOR-THE-DISAPPEARED card was written around Mexican searcher-mothers, where the typical killer is a cartel and the state typically fails to investigate. The Brazilian case sharpens that card: here the killers were directly uniformed agents of the state and allied extermination squads. The office becomes search-for-truth and refusal-of-the-official-narrative; the bodies were not missing but the cause was. The mothers' work converts grief into a public record the state will not produce.

Flags:
Family-of-victim founder; the cube does not romanticise grief as virtue. The political stakes are sharp — Brazilian police violence is structural and ongoing, and any account that suggests the Crimes of May were exceptional rather than typical understates the problem. Débora Maria da Silva has received public honours (Dandara dos Palmares, Cidadã Paulistana, Medalha Chico Mendes); this is not unknown-canon work, but the office is too important to omit. The cube follows the network's own framing — "Crimes of May" rather than "police response" — and uses the names the mothers have themselves carried forward: Edson Rogério Silva dos Santos (Débora's son), and the cluster of three men shot at a Jardim Brasil corner for which the police officer Alexandre André Pereira da Silva was eventually imprisoned: Murilo de Moraes Ferreira, Felipe Vasti Santos de Oliveira, and Marcelo Heyd Meres.
