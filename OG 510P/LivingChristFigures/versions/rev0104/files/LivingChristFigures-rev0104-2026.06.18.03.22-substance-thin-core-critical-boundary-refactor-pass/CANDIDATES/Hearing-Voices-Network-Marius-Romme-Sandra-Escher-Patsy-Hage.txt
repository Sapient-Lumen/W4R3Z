Marius Romme, Sandra Escher, Patsy Hage — Hearing Voices Movement / Intervoice — translocal peer-led psychiatric office

Status: OFFICE-CANON; FOUNDER-PLUS-VOICE-HEARER NETWORK CANDIDATE; KEEP WITH DECEASED-PROFESSOR, ACADEMIC-PROXIMITY, AND MEDICATION-DEBATE FLAGS
Location: Origin Maastricht, Netherlands (1987); now Intervoice / national Hearing Voices Networks in roughly 29 countries including the UK (Manchester, 1988), US, Canada, Australia, Brazil, the Czech Republic, Ireland, and many others
Office: Peer-led psychiatric crisis support / non-pathologising frame for voice-hearing / refusal of the medical model as the only model

Work:
In 1987 the Dutch social psychiatrist Marius Romme (1934–2021), the journalist and child psychologist Sandra Escher, and the voice-hearer Patsy Hage formed an unprecedented therapeutic partnership: Hage challenged Romme's assumption that her voices were merely pathological, and the three together issued a Dutch television appeal to other voice-hearers. The hundreds of responses formed the basis of the Hearing Voices Movement. The movement's central premises: voice-hearing is a meaningful human experience (an estimated 2-4% of the general population hears voices without psychiatric contact); voices are best understood in the context of a person's life and trauma; coping is improved when peers — not only clinicians — work with the voice-hearer. Hearing Voices Groups now exist in over 20 countries, federated under Intervoice (incorporated as a not-for-profit in the UK in 2007, with Romme as president until his death). Successor leadership includes Jacqui Dillon (England), Eleanor Longden, Dirk Corstens, Mark Morris, and many voice-hearer leaders. The work received SAMHSA's "promising practice" designation for family caregivers in the United States.

Why it belongs / might belong:
The rev0012 SearchTrail named "psychiatric-hospital discharge soteria / peer-support / Open Dialogue" as a gap. Open Dialogue is structurally a state mental-health system (Finnish public services in Western Lapland) rather than a costly-mercy office — important and humane, but the wrong shape for the cube. The Hearing Voices Movement is the right shape: peer-led, often refused by the psychiatric mainstream, building groups in living rooms and church halls before earning a place in mental-health policy. The threshold is the moment a person hearing voices realises they are not alone, not necessarily sick, and not required to take medication to be heard.

Flags:
Marius Romme was a psychiatry professor; the office is not strictly "unknown canon" though Hearing Voices Groups themselves are peer-led. The movement has had an uneasy relationship with mainstream psychiatric research methods, and some clinicians remain critical of the framework; the cube does not adjudicate the clinical dispute. The movement does not require non-medication, but its history has at times read as anti-medication. Some Hearing Voices Groups operate within hospitals (good integration); others operate explicitly outside (clearer office boundary); both forms exist. The published voice-hearer literature includes named lived-experience accounts by Patsy Hage, Eleanor Longden, Jacqui Dillon, Debra Lampshire, Joan McNamara and many others; these accounts were chosen by their authors and the cube treats them as such.
