NAME: Melinda Hunt / The Hart Island Project — public cemetery access and anonymity-clock office
LOCATION: New York City, New York, USA; Hart Island / Bronx / public cemetery system
OFFICE: Public-cemetery record access, family/chosen-family visitation, and the conversion of anonymous state burial into searchable memory
STATUS: KEEP; OFFICE-CANON CANDIDATE FOR PUBLIC-CEMETERY RECORD ACCESS; PRIVACY, ART-MEDIATION, STATE-BURIAL, AND FAMILY-ACCESS FLAGS

SUMMARY:
The Hart Island Project, founded and shaped by artist Melinda Hunt, works at the threshold where public burial becomes public disappearance. Its own mission says it assists families and individuals with limited resources in accessing history and public information concerning Hart Island burials, maintains an online database for burials since 1980, maps grave locations, and uses the Traveling Cloud Museum as a storytelling and visualization system. The office is neither funeral service nor rescue. It is record access, family access, and the refusal to let a city burial become a permanent anonymity sentence.

WHY THIS FITS THE CUBE:
The cube already has name-after-death, disconnected-dead, migrant-grave, and unclaimed-child burial offices. Hart Island adds a different civic threshold: the mass public cemetery where the person may have a name in a ledger, no ordinary grave marker, restricted visiting history, family who did not know where to go, chosen family whose standing could be invisible, and a city agency controlling the door. The mercy-shape is searchable record, map, visit, story, advocacy, and the slow conversion of penal/secrecy infrastructure into mournable public space.

OFFICE SHAPE:
- Maintaining or advocating access to burial records and maps for people interred in a public cemetery.
- Building a story layer without pretending story equals consent or return.
- Helping families locate and visit burial places, while city policy and authority remain the actual gate.
- Pressuring the city toward transparency, visitor access, and removal of penal control over the cemetery.
- Interpreting Hart Island as a civic threshold of poverty, AIDS stigma, fetal-remains privacy, public burial, mass graves, and chosen-family grief.

EVIDENCE:
1. The Hart Island Project says its mission is to assist families and people with limited resources in accessing history and public information concerning burials, and that it maintains an online database and grave-location maps.
2. The Hart Island Project's about page describes Hart Island as New York's City Cemetery, notes the long use of prison labor for unclaimed and unidentified burials in mass graves, and credits the project with database, storytelling, and access advocacy.
3. NYC's Hart Island pages now point members of the public to CMTS records dating back to 1977 and describe gravesite visits for family, chosen family, close friends, and partners.
4. NYC separately flags record gaps and fetal-remains privacy redaction, which directly supports the cube's boundary treatment.
5. Reed Magazine's profile describes Melinda Hunt's long role in changing family access and public awareness around Hart Island, while retaining the story's AIDS-stigma and family-grief context.
6. NYC Archives records the longer public-cemetery ledger history: city purchase/designation, indigent/unclaimed burial, archival transfer, gaps, and the evidentiary value of ledgers.
7. Current public planning around Hart Island is still moving: the 2025 concept-plan reporting points toward visitor amenities, chapel/remembrance use, and climate resilience, but the cube treats this as future planning rather than achieved care.

DOOR-ETHICS FLAGS:
- Searchable memory is not return. A database can help a family find a burial place, but it cannot restore the person or prove consent to public narration.
- Public-record access is not public-record extraction. The cube may name the existence and boundary of the system without copying individual records from it.
- A story platform can become an art-mediated claim over someone else's dead; the cube must distinguish family-submitted story, public record, artist interface, and civic advocacy.
- Public tours are not the same as family access. Family, chosen-family, partner, and close-friend gravesite visits must remain morally primary.
- Fetal-remains records, child records, Medical Examiner numbers, plot numbers, exact burial locations, and family stories should not be repeated in cube prose unless directly necessary to an audited claim.
- Natural/green burial and affordability language must not imply that people buried through poverty, stigma, administrative failure, or family-unawareness chose the cemetery as a positive preference.

CURRENT CAPACITY / SAFETY:
Public record access and scheduled visit structures are supported by current NYC pages, but the cube is not a search tool, visit guide, legal guide, burial-assistance guide, cemetery map, or public-record reproduction layer. Rev0043 adds a no-record-extraction gate: public database visibility may support system-shape and record-integrity claims, but it does not authorize repeating names, plot/section/grave identifiers, permit or Medical Examiner metadata, dates or places of death tied to individuals, fetal-remains details, family-submitted stories, maps, contact fields, or scheduling instructions. Policies, schedules, city systems, record completeness, and eligibility rules can change. `live_referral_safe=false` remains mandatory.

BOUNDARY NOTE:
Hart Island is a gift to the cube because it makes the threshold civic rather than private. The door is not simply a saintly helper. It is a city, an artist interface, a database, a ferry, a cemetery, a public archive, a privacy policy, a family request, a chosen-family claim, and an unresolved history of poverty and stigma. The entry is admitted strongly; it is also held under high privacy and narrative-ownership debt.

REV0043 NO-RECORD-EXTRACTION ADDENDUM:
The Hart Island Project search surface, NYC's CMTS-facing pages, Data.gov metadata, and NYC Council material make the office more legible and more dangerous. They show why record access matters, but they also expose exactly the fields the public cube must not reproduce: individual names, plot/section/grave identifiers, permit or Medical Examiner metadata, places of death, institutional death-location lists, family stories, images, map/visit/search surfaces, and public-tour logistics. Rev0043 therefore strengthens this candidate as an admitted office while narrowing public use: searchable memory can be evidence of a civic threshold, not a data source for narrative extraction.
