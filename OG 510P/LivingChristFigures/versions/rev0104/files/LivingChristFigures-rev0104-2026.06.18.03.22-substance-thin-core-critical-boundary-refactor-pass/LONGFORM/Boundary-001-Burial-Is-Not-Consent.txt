# Boundary Essay 001 — Burial Is Not Consent

Status: seeded rev0026
Track: longform_failure_and_boundary_essays
Seed candidate: `cand_elissa_davey_garden_of_innocence_unclaimed_children`
Seed office: `office_unclaimed_child_burial_and_naming`

A dignified burial can still be a false door if it smuggles consent in after the person can no longer refuse.

The unclaimed-child burial office is among the easiest mercy-shapes to love. A child dies, no family can or will carry the burial, and a community steps forward with a name, grave, casket, blanket, poem, procession, prayer, song, and memory. The cube should not pretend this is small. Refusing disposal logic at the edge of a child's death is real threshold mercy.

But this is exactly why the office must be read with suspicion. The child cannot consent to the name. The child cannot consent to the prayer. The child cannot consent to the public service, the photograph, the donor story, the handmade object, the press report, the volunteer's consolation, or the sentence that explains what the child's death means. Where the dead cannot refuse, the living must carry a higher burden of restraint.

The cube therefore separates dignity from ownership. A grave is not ownership. A name is not ownership. A poem is not ownership. But any of them can become ownership when they make the helper, the church, the donor, the agency, or the audience the final interpreter of the child.

The first boundary is category truth. "Abandoned," "unidentified," "unclaimed," "stillborn," "indigent," "family unable," and "family unwilling" are not the same condition. They may all lead to burial need, but they do not carry the same moral meaning. Calling them by one name may comfort the ceremony while wronging the absent family, the child, or the record.

The second boundary is family knowledge. An infant may be unclaimed because no kin exists, because kin cannot be found, because a parent is incapacitated, because a family is poor, because a bureaucracy is opaque, because grief is complicated, because the death is attached to violence, addiction, illness, shame, or mental health crisis. The office must not use the absence of a visible family to invent a simple story about abandonment.

The third boundary is religious framing. Religious ritual may be the community's way of carrying the dead with honor. It may also be a script the dead never chose and the family might not have chosen. The cube does not reject ritual. It asks whether ritual is held as gift or imposed as possession.

The fourth boundary is publicity. Public attendance can repair anonymity. Publicity can also turn a child into a purity object. The safest public edition should not repeat names, images, measurements, case facts, or ceremonial details unless they are load-bearing to an audited claim.

The office survives these questions only if it can say: we did not save this child from death, and we do not own what remains. We are here to keep disposal from being the last act.

That is enough to matter. It is not enough to end the audit.
