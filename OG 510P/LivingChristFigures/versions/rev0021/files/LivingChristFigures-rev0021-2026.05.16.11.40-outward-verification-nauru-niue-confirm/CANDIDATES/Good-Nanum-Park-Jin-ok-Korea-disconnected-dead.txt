Good Nanum (좋은 나눔) / Park Jin-ok — and Nanum and Nanum (나눔과 나눔) — South Korean lonely-death funeral offices

Status: PROVISIONAL KEEP / OFFICE-CANON FOR THE KOREAN VARIANT OF DISCONNECTED-DEAD-NO-KIN
Location: Goyang, Seoul, and other South Korean cities; Nanum and Nanum operates Seoul's Public Funeral consultation hotline
Office: Disconnected-dead-no-kin in a working-age, economically-collapsed-male demographic / godoksa (고독사) funeral and naming

Work:
Good Nanum is a Korean civic group/charity directed by Park Jin-ok that holds one-day funerals — with portrait, name tablet, Buddhist or Christian rites depending on what the deceased or family permits — for people whose bodies go unclaimed at hospitals, flophouses, and police morgues. Bodies are typically held about a month while details are posted to local government websites in case family members come forward; if no one does, Good Nanum staff and a volunteer Buddhist nun perform a cremation ceremony, carry name tablets to the crematorium, and arrange charnel-house interment. Nanum and Nanum is a parallel, more recently institutionalised nonprofit that, since a 2019 MOU with the Seoul Metropolitan Government, operates the 24/7 Public Funeral consultation hotline (1668-3412) under Seoul's 2018 Public Funeral Ordinance.

Why it belongs / might belong:
The Korean phenomenon of godoksa (lonely death) differs from Japanese kodokushi in demographic: in 2021 Korea recorded 3,378 such deaths, 60% of which were men in their 50s and 60s, often after job loss, disability, or post-divorce social collapse. By 2024 the count of unclaimed dead nationally had nearly doubled to 6,139. The office is the same shape the cube already names for Japan (DISCONNECTED-DEAD-NO-KIN), but the threshold population is working-age men in an economically failing safety net. The Seoul Public Funeral system shows the office becoming partly state-supported through civic-NGO partnership — a different model from Japanese temple practice.

Flags:
Park Jin-ok's interviews include emotionally taxing details (including a mother found with a newborn) that the cube notes but will not repeat as proof of office — these are private deaths and any iteration of the cube must be careful with whose grief is named. The Seoul model's increasing state involvement is a positive efficiency but raises the same DOOR-ETHICS question as elsewhere: does state co-option dilute the relational character of the office? Reporting is mostly Korean-language; English coverage relies heavily on photos taken in 2016 at Goyang crematorium by Jean Chung for Getty Images, which the cube acknowledges as the primary visual record without endorsing image-based fundraising practices.
