Isshinji temple (one-spirit-per-urn practice) and Ending Center (NPO "grave-friends" model) — Japanese muenbotoke offices

Status: OFFICE-CANON; INSTITUTION-CANDIDATES, NOT SINGLE-FOUNDER CANON
Location: Isshinji — Tennoji ward, Osaka, Japan (head priest commissioned the first bone-Buddha in 1887; the practice is roughly decadal). Ending Center — Tokyo periphery, founded by sociologist Inoue Haruyo; the model is an NPO-run burial ground inside, though not affiliated with, a Buddhist cemetery.
Office: Disconnected-dead-no-kin / muenbotoke / interment without a legal successor

Work:
Japanese law and conventional Buddhist temple practice require a legal successor to maintain a family grave through ongoing fees. A person who outlives kin, never married, has estranged family, or whose family cannot or will not pay fees becomes muenbotoke (無縁仏) — "buddha without bonds." Isshinji is the most famous of the temples that respond: cremated remains donated to the temple are eventually combined with resin and cast into a Buddha statue (Okotsubutsu), one every roughly ten years. Acceptance is regardless of prior religious affiliation. Donation fees are modest by Japanese funeral standards (¥20,000–¥50,000). Ending Center is a more recent NPO model: members in life pay a one-time fee, gather for workshops and "grave-friend" (haka tomo) meetings, and are interred together rather than with biological kin.

Why it belongs / might belong:
The cube needed a non-US/UK case of the no-family / no-successor threshold, and a wealthy-aging-society counterpart to the migrant-grave office. In Japan, kodokushi (lonely death) and post-Hanshin earthquake displacement made the problem visible; the unconnected dead are also produced by household size collapse, urban migration, and the cost of memorialisation. Isshinji and Ending Center answer the same question Sonia Bermúdez answers in La Guajira, but with different machinery: not poverty but successor-absence; not unclaimed migrant but unaccompanied citizen.

Flags:
Buddhist temples in Japan have a structural revenue interest in graves (the danka parish system); the Isshinji and Ending Center models are partly reform of that system, not pure mercy. The "grave-friend" model has aesthetic appeal — cherry trees, ceremony — and the cube should not let cherry-tree imagery substitute for analysis of what these models do for the very poor, the disabled, or the foreign-born deceased. The phenomenon of kodokushi has produced a private cleaning industry (e.g., Japan Association of Memento Organization) that is paid commercial service, not the kind of costly mercy the cube studies, though their workers describe the work in language much closer to it than to industry copy.
