Sally J. Finn / International Overdose Awareness Day (and subsequent stewardship by the Penington Institute)

Status: OFFICE-CANON FOR OVERDOSE MEMORIAL; KEEP WITH RELIGIOUS-MOVEMENT AND FAITH-FRAMING FLAGS FOR FOUNDER ORIGIN
Location: St Kilda, Melbourne, Australia (founding 2001); since 2012 coordinated by Penington Institute, Melbourne; now observed in 40+ countries on 31 August
Office: Overdose-memorial / name-after-overdose-death / un-stigmatising grief

Work:
Sally J. Finn, manager of a Salvation Army needle-exchange in St Kilda, began International Overdose Awareness Day in 2001 as a small BBQ ribbon event after working with a person who had lost seventeen friends to overdose and noticing that families could not openly grieve because of the stigma attached to drug-related death. Roughly six thousand silver ribbons were distributed on the first day. From 2012 the campaign has been coordinated by the Penington Institute, a Melbourne-based public-health non-profit, and is now the world's largest annual overdose-awareness campaign — vigils, name-readings, candles, and remembrance events in over forty countries on 31 August.

Why it belongs / might belong:
The cube's existing NAME-AFTER-DEATH office covers the homeless dead. This is the parallel office for the overdose dead, who carry an additional layer of stigma: an illegal substance turned a death into a moral verdict. The threshold is from "died of an illicit cause" to "remembered by name without apology." It pairs naturally with the cube's existing ADDICTION-THRESHOLD office, but operates after death rather than during use.

Flags:
Originating inside a Salvation Army programme means the office began with an evangelical organisation that has its own contested record around LGBTQ+ inclusion. Penington Institute is now the steward and is secular; the campaign's framing has been carefully kept secular and harm-reduction-aligned. As with all named-death work, the cube should not publish family names as proof of office, and should remain alert when memorial events are co-opted into rhetoric ("war on drugs") that increases stigma rather than reducing it. "Stigma reduction" is also a claim the cube should not accept at face value — measure by what bereaved families and people who use drugs say, not by donor copy.
