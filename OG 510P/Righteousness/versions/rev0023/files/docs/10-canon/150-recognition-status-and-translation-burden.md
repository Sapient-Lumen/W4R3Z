# Recognition Status and Translation Burden

## The remaining pressure

The archive's portable-core move solved one problem and exposed another.

It is now easier to say what structure survives comparison. But it is still too easy to speak as if every successful translation had the **same status**.

That is not believable.

A tradition may say any of the following about the archive's portable core:
- yes, that is righteousness proper;
- yes, but only as a thin and incomplete slice of righteousness;
- no, that is a nearby moral analogue, not righteousness itself;
- or no, the translation distorts the concept too badly to count.

Comparative philosophy warns against exactly the mistakes that show up here: oversimplification, illicit assimilation, and false confidence about commensurability.[S22] Work on translating Chinese philosophy adds a second warning: translation is not just swapping words, because philosophical meaning rides on broader inferential structures, not isolated vocabulary items.[S23]

## Current move

The archive now adds a second portability rule:

> every claim that a structure is **portable** must distinguish **recognition status** from **public-translation status**.

This is now the cleanest way to keep portability honest.

## Recognition status

Recognition status asks:

> **if a tradition encountered the portable core on its own terms, what would it count as?**

The archive now uses four statuses.

### 1. Identity

The portable core counts as righteousness proper, even if stated at a high level of abstraction.

### 2. Incomplete identity

The portable core counts as genuine righteousness, but only as a thinned or partial rendering. A fuller account still requires the tradition's own richer grounding, diagnosis, hope, or repair-language.

### 3. Analogue

The portable core tracks a neighboring moral structure — perhaps justice, uprightness, fidelity, or public duty — but not righteousness itself.

### 4. Rejection

The translation fails badly enough that the archive should stop claiming portability at that point.

## Public-translation status

Public-translation status asks a different question:

> **can this translated structure function as a shared justificatory module for common institutions?**

This question matters especially for law and office in pluralist settings. Rawls's overlapping-consensus model treats a political conception as a freestanding module that citizens can affirm for different reasons from within their own comprehensive doctrines.[S13] That means a formulation may be publicly usable even when traditions disagree about whether it is righteousness proper. Public reason extends the same point: coercive decisions should be justifiable in terms others may reasonably accept, not only by appeal to one contested doctrine.[S18]

So public-translation status is **not** the same thing as recognition status.

A tradition may:
- reject the portable core as full righteousness,
- yet still accept its public translation for shared institutions;
- or accept it as righteousness proper,
- yet still deny that the portable version exhausts its deepest truth.

## Translation burden

Because portability can succeed in more than one way, the archive now imposes a translation burden.

Any new portability claim should answer five questions:

1. **Preservation:** what inferential structure actually survives translation?
2. **Loss:** what grounding, motivation, diagnosis, or repair-language gets thinner or disappears?
3. **Recognition:** is the claim identity, incomplete identity, analogue, or rejection?
4. **Public use:** is the translated form being offered as a public module for shared institutions, or as a full theory?
5. **Internal resistance:** who inside the source tradition would reasonably object, and why?

If these are not answered, the portability claim is underdescribed.

## Why this improves the archive

This move does three useful things.

### 1. It blocks forced yes/no answers

The archive no longer has to pretend the only options are:
- the portable core simply **is** righteousness everywhere; or
- the comparative program fails.

There is a real middle space: incomplete identity and analogue.

### 2. It protects sacred thickness without surrendering public reason

Traditions can insist that righteousness finally answers to God, covenant, sacred order, or another thick frame, while still participating in shared public justification through a translated module.[S18][S21]

### 3. It makes future comparison more disciplined

Comparative work becomes less theatrical once it has to say not merely that two ideas are "similar," but **how** they are similar, how much is lost, and what kind of recognition is actually being claimed.[S22][S23]

## Archive rule

The archive now adopts the following working-canon rule:

> never call a theory of righteousness "portable" without typing both its **recognition status** and its **public-translation status**.

## What remains open

The archive still does not know, tradition by tradition, which status assignments are most defensible.

That mapping remains a live research task rather than a settled result.
