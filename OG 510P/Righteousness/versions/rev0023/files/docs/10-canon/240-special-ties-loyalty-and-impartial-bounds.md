# Special Ties, Loyalty, and Impartial Bounds

## Why this surface matters

A portable theory of righteousness cannot live only on two extremes:
- **flat impartiality**, where family, friendship, promise, membership, and representation never thicken what is due;
- **unbounded loyalty**, where proximity, kinship, faction, or constituency can swallow the claims of outsiders almost whole.

Ordinary moral life resists the first extreme. Public ethics resists the second. The archive therefore needs a compact rule for how special ties matter without letting them become a solvent for due.[S17][S68]

## Starter thesis

> **Righteousness is neither flat impartiality nor unrestricted loyalty. Special ties may intensify what is due where relationship, promise, dependency, or entrusted representation creates thicker obligations; but they do not erase baseline due to outsiders, and in offices ordered to adjudication, allocation, or public trust they often trigger restraint, recusal, or anti-nepotism barriers rather than privileged service.**[S17][S32][S45][S68][S69]

## Three different sites of partiality

### 1. Intimate and promissory life

Special obligations are morally familiar because some relations change what is owed. Promisees, friends, children, parents, spouses, colleagues, and dependents are not just any persons to us. Partiality here is often part of the due rather than a betrayal of it.[S17]

So the archive should not act as though righteousness requires giving identical concern to one's child and to every stranger in every moment. Some near claims become thicker because reliance, vulnerability, shared history, or explicit undertaking has made them thicker.[S17][S68]

### 2. Representative life

Some roles are not adjudicative but representational. A legislator, union delegate, guardian ad litem, ombuds role, or advocate may be charged with making a person's or constituency's concerns present in a public setting rather than hovering above all sides in judge-like neutrality.[S45]

This means the archive should not collapse all public righteousness into one model of impartial distance. Some offices are rightly structured around accountable partiality **for** those represented. But that partiality is still bounded: it may not falsify facts, erase the rights of nonconstituents, convert office into spoil, or pretend that authorization by some dissolves public reasons owed to others.[S18][S19][S45]

### 3. Adjudicative, allocative, and disciplinary life

Other roles work differently. Judges, procurement officers, hiring officials, regulators, inspectors, grant-makers, prosecutors, police, and similar bearers of public power are not licensed to privilege friends, family, donors, or faction under the name of loyalty. Here special ties often operate as **standing defects** or as triggers for recusal and anti-preferential-treatment rules rather than as reasons for righteous preference.[S32][S68][S69]

So the same relationship can play opposite roles in different settings. Caring more for one's child at home may be part of righteousness; steering a public job or permit toward one's child is not.[S32][S69]

## Office bright lines

Current federal ethics rules are useful here because they make the archive's point sharply. Executive-branch employees are told to act impartially and not give preferential treatment to any private organization or individual, and they may not use public office for the private gain of friends or relatives.[S32]

The anti-nepotism statute sharpens the same lesson into a bright line: a public official may not appoint, employ, promote, advance, or advocate the appointment or advancement of a relative in an agency the official serves or controls.[S69]

The archive does **not** infer that all righteous judgment must therefore be relationless. It infers something narrower and more useful: once the role is structured around public trust, fair allocation, adjudication, or coercive power, private tie-based preference is presumptively disqualifying unless the role itself is one of accountable representation rather than impartial administration.[S32][S45][S69]

## Working rule

When a righteousness claim relies on a special tie, the archive should now ask five questions:
1. **What relation is doing the work?** Family, friendship, promise, office assurance, constituency, covenantal membership, dependence, or something else?[S17]
2. **What baseline due remains to outsiders or nonfavored parties?** The tie may thicken some obligations without canceling others.[S17][S68]
3. **What kind of role is this?** Intimate care, entrusted representation, adjudication, distribution, discipline, or coercive office are not all alike.[S32][S45]
4. **Would the same rationale survive public articulation across like cases?** If not, the tie is probably laundering favoritism or capture rather than expressing righteous loyalty.[S18][S19][S32]
5. **If the role is supposed to be impartial, what restraint followed?** Recusal, abstention, disclosure, anti-nepotism compliance, or some other visible limit should do the work rather than private reassurance.[S32][S69]

## Compression sentence

> **Honor relationship-thickened due without letting special ties become a solvent for baseline due, and treat private ties inside impartial public office as presumptive reasons for restraint rather than privileged service.**[S17][S32][S45][S68][S69]
