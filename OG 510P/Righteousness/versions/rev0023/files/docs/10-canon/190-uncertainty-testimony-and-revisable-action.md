# Uncertainty, Testimony, and Revisable Action

## Why this surface now

The archive already knows how to talk about due, breach, contestability, conflict, and time. But it still lacked a compact rule for **epistemic burden**.

Without such a rule, two opposite errors stay too easy:
- **overconfidence** — acting as if righteousness were compatible with thin inquiry, prejudicial disbelief, or hidden uncertainty;
- **paralysis** — treating uncertainty as if it canceled responsibility to act, protect, or decide.

The archive now rejects both errors.

## Working-canon thesis

> **Under material uncertainty, righteousness requires proportionate inquiry, fair uptake of testimony, explicit acknowledgment of uncertainty, burden-sensitive caution where harms may be grave or hard to repair, and action designed to remain answerable to revision, reversal, or repair as evidence improves.**[S34][S40][S41][S42]

This is not a new moral substance beside due-fidelity. It is a discipline for how due-fidelity must proceed when knowledge is incomplete, contested, socially distorted, or still emerging.

## 1. Uncertainty does not excuse thin inquiry

Righteousness under uncertainty is not a permission slip for intuition, convenience, or official guesswork.

Where stakes are serious, the first righteous question is often not only *what is due?* but also *what inquiry is now due before acting, and to whom must that inquiry be answerable?*

The rule-of-law and public-justification materials already in the archive support this: hearing, evidence, reason-giving, and review are not administrative luxuries, but protections against arbitrary power.[S34][S35]

So the archive now adds a new negative rule:

> **do not call a decision righteous if the relevant uncertainty was handled by haste, prejudice, convenience, or sealed self-trust when better inquiry was owed.**

## 2. Testimony is part of the burden of righteousness

Uncertainty is rarely solved by private reflection alone. Much of what agents and institutions know comes through testimony, records, expertise, and the speech of affected parties.[S40][S42]

That matters because social epistemology also tracks a recurrent danger: some people are disbelieved, unheard, or conceptually blocked not because their testimony is weak, but because social power distorts the economy of credibility. Testimonial and hermeneutical injustice therefore matter to righteousness, not just to epistemology.[S40]

So the archive now adds another rule:

> **where a case depends on who is heard, righteousness requires fair uptake of testimony, especially from those who bear the risk, burden, or injury.**

This does **not** mean automatic belief. It means that prejudicial dismissal, credibility asymmetry, and strategically cultivated ignorance are themselves standing defects in judgment.

## 3. Caution is asymmetrical when harms are grave or hard to repair

The archive does **not** adopt a single algorithm for uncertainty. But it now admits one portable cue.

When there is scientifically or evidentially reasonable suspicion of serious danger, and the harm would be grave, hard to repair, or loaded onto the more vulnerable party, righteousness may require protective action even before certainty is available. The precautionary-principle literature is helpful here: incomplete evidence does not always license waiting, especially where delay itself pushes risk onto others.[S41]

This is a burden-sensitive rather than panic-driven rule.

It does **not** say:
- every low-probability danger justifies maximal restraint;
- every uncertain allegation should be treated as proven;
- or that caution outranks every other due in every case.

It says only that uncertainty is morally structured. The location of possible harm, the reversibility of error, and the vulnerability of those exposed all matter.

## 4. Moral uncertainty requires humility, not fetishized certainty

Some uncertainty concerns facts; some concerns which moral principle or ranking should govern the case. The literature on moral decision-making under uncertainty matters here because it rejects the fiction that agents can simply wait until first-order moral truth is obvious.[S42]

The archive does **not** adopt a full decision theory here. But it does adopt a humility rule:

> **when rival serious moral considerations remain live, righteousness should not present a contested judgment as if it were self-evidently pure.**

This strengthens several earlier archive moves at once:
- conflict cases should still carry remainder;
- secrecy cases should disclose uncertainty where possible;
- requalification cases should not overclaim what has been restored;
- office should avoid irreversible burden-shifting where a revisable path exists.

## 5. Revisability is part of righteous design

When uncertainty cannot be eliminated before action, righteousness should prefer forms of action that remain answerable to correction.

That can include:
- preserving record;
- staging intervention rather than leaping to the most irreversible step;
- building review triggers;
- creating appeal paths;
- leaving room for compensation, reversal, or repair if the judgment proves mistaken.[S26][S28][S34]

This is especially important for office. A righteous office under uncertainty does not merely say "trust us." It structures action so that the costs of possible error are not simply dumped onto the less powerful without later answerability.

## 6. More exact verdicts

This surface improves verdict language again.

More exact judgments now include:
- **substantively serious but evidentially underdisciplined**;
- **protectively cautious under uncertainty**;
- **prematurely certain and standing-damaged**;
- **review-capable under uncertainty**;
- **testimonial uptake failed, so righteousness claim defeated**.

These are often better than a flat yes-or-no.

## Office-facing note

For office, the epistemic burden is heavier.

Public power should not convert its own uncertainty into other people's irreversible burden while withholding both reasons and recourse. Where facts are incomplete, righteous office normally widens inquiry, protects dissenting and affected testimony, names uncertainty honestly, and prefers narrower or more revisable burdens when stronger evidence is not yet available.[S19][S34][S35][S40][S41]

## What this does not settle

This surface does **not** settle:
- how much inquiry is enough before action in highly time-compressed emergencies;
- when testimony should outweigh background statistical evidence or institutional expertise;
- how precaution should differ across office types;
- or which moral-uncertainty decision rules are best in theory.

Those remain live.

## Compressed rule

> **Do not let uncertainty become either arrogant certainty or moral paralysis. Inquire proportionately, hear testimony fairly, name uncertainty honestly, protect the more exposed where risk is grave, and act in ways that remain revisable or repairable.**
