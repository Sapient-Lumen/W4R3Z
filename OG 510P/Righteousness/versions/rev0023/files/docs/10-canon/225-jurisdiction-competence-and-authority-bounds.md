# Jurisdiction, Competence, and Authority Bounds

## Why this surface is now needed

The archive has become stronger on entrusted power, delegation, contestability, and nonownership of office. But it still lacked a compact answer to a recurrent pathology: **doing a substantively attractive thing through the wrong bearer, beyond the bearer's remit, or without the competence required to burden others rightly**.

Without a clearer answer, the archive risks calling some acts righteous merely because the aim sounded good, even where the act involved office usurpation, unauthorized coercion, or confidence beyond the actor's warranted competence.[S18][S19][S32][S59][S60][S61]

## Starter thesis

> **Righteous office is not only due-facing in aim. It is also bearer-disciplined. A public act cannot count as righteous office merely because its end was attractive; it must also be undertaken by a bearer with the right jurisdiction, adequate competence, and a genuinely authorized way of acting. Good ends do not purify office usurpation.**[S18][S19][S32][S59][S60][S61]

This is a clarification, not a new moral universe. It tightens what the archive already means by entrusted power, public proof, and nondelegable remainder.

## Three distinct questions

### 1. Jurisdiction

Who is the proper bearer of this decision, burden, permission, or coercive act? Some roles may advise, some may investigate, some may adjudicate, some may authorize, and some may only object or disclose. The rule-of-law literature is useful here because it treats public power as constrained by established public norms rather than by ad hoc preference, and because it stresses that officials act under a different presumption than ordinary persons: they need authorization rather than a general liberty to act however they wish.[S59]

### 2. Competence

Even if a role formally touches the matter, is this actor or office competently situated to decide it? Competence here includes not only skill or technical knowledge, but fit process, evidentiary posture, hearing structure, and the capacity to carry the decision without reckless ignorance or procedural distortion.[S34][S40][S42][S59]

### 3. Authority / authorization

Has the office actually been empowered to do this as office? Political-authority theory is useful because authority is a normative power to alter others' duties or permissions by specified procedures; it is not merely strong preference backed by position or force.[S60] Official public-law materials are useful too because they repeatedly state that agencies and officers act only to the extent authorized by law.[S61]

These three questions often travel together, but they should not be collapsed. One may have information without authority, title without competence, or urgency without jurisdiction.

## No clean office-righteousness for unauthorized power

The archive now adopts a narrow but strong rule:

> **If a public actor claims to be acting *as office* while lacking the jurisdiction, competence, or authorization required for that exercise of public power, the act is presumptively disqualified as righteous office.**[S18][S32][S59][S60][S61]

The point is not fetishistic legality. It is anti-usurpation. Offices are held powers, not floating moral licenses. When an actor claims office force, office secrecy, office immunity, or office channels, the archive now asks whether the office was actually entitled and equipped to do what it did.

## Why good ends are not enough

A familiar temptation now needs a direct refusal: “yes, this exceeded the role, but the goal was righteous.” Sometimes the goal may indeed have been morally serious. But if the act used public power without the proper bearer, process, or grant, the better verdict is usually one of the following:

- morally urgent but **not righteous office**;
- perhaps understandable, but **standing-defective**;
- at best a form of **burdened interruption or witness** rather than clean performance of office.[S29][S32][S52][S59][S60]

This preserves a crucial distinction. The archive can still say that extraordinary interruption, whistleblowing, or conscientious breach may sometimes be righteous. But when that happens, it should not be misdescribed as ordinary righteous office-performance. Otherwise “necessity” becomes a solvent for entrusted limits.

## Competence is itself a standing burden

The archive's earlier uncertainty rule now sharpens into a competence rule. A sincere official, committee, or expert may still act unrighteously by deciding beyond the inquiry, hearing, evidentiary basis, or technical grasp needed for the burden imposed. Competence is therefore not a prestige marker; it is part of what those burdened are owed.[S34][S40][S42]

This also matters for delegation. Outsourcing to experts or technical systems may improve competence, but it cannot cure a basic mismatch in authority or accountability. Delegation can supply information or implementation; it cannot magically create jurisdiction where none exists, nor can it erase the office's duty to know when to defer, escalate, recuse, or stop.[S43][S44][S45][S46]

## Emergency and gap cases

The archive should not become naive about genuine emergencies. Sometimes danger develops faster than ordinary ratification or channeling can keep pace. But emergency does not cancel bearer-discipline; it changes the burden profile.

Where officials act under pressure near or beyond ordinary boundaries, the righteousness claim now owes at least four extra showings:

1. why ordinary jurisdiction or authorization was genuinely unavailable or too slow for the danger at hand;
2. why the act stayed as narrow, temporary, and burden-minimizing as possible;
3. what immediate path of ratification, review, disclosure, or sunset followed;
4. what remainder, remorse, or repair remained due if the emergency move overreached.[S29][S34][S36][S59][S61]

That is a burden-increase rule, not a purifier.

## Refusal is not usurpation

This surface also clarifies a nearby confusion. An official who refuses an unlawful or captured directive, discloses abuse through protected channels, or resigns rather than become a vehicle of wrong is not thereby claiming wider office authority. In many such cases the act is better understood as **righteous interruption** rather than as self-authorized expansion of office power.[S32][S52][S53]

So the archive now distinguishes two very different postures:

- **refusal / witness** — declining to perform or conceal a wrong one is not entitled to carry out;
- **usurpation** — seizing a power, process, or burden-allocation one is not entitled to wield.

The first may sometimes be righteous. The second is presumptively standing-defective even where its announced goal sounds morally elevated.

## Office-facing tests

When a public righteousness claim is made, now ask:

- who had jurisdiction over this act;
- what made that bearer competent enough to decide or carry it;
- what legal, procedural, or constitutional authorization empowered the act;
- whether the actor was using office powers, office information, office secrecy, or office channels outside their proper remit;
- whether the better classification is ordinary office-performance, delegated implementation, recusal, interruption, emergency overreach, or public witness.[S18][S32][S34][S43][S52][S59][S60][S61][S62]

## What this tightens elsewhere

This new surface sharpens several earlier archive rules at once:

- **public trust** now includes nonusurpation, not only anti-appropriation;[S32][S57]
- **contestability** now asks not only whether reasons were offered, but whether the right bearer was acting;[S18][S34][S59]
- **delegation discipline** now distinguishes supplying competence from laundering authority;[S43][S44][S46]
- **righteous interruption** is clarified as refusal or witness rather than self-certifying office expansion;[S52][S53]
- **uncertainty discipline** now includes knowing when one lacks the warranted competence to impose the burden one is about to impose.[S40][S42]

## Short formula

> **Do not call it righteous office merely because it aimed at the right thing. Ask whether the right bearer, with adequate competence and real authorization, was the one acting — and if not, whether the act belongs under interruption, emergency overreach, or witness rather than righteous office at all.**[S18][S32][S59][S60][S61]
