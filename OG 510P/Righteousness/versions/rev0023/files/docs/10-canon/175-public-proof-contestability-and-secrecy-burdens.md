# Public Proof, Contestability, and Secrecy Burdens

## Why this surface now

The archive now has thresholds, defeaters, repair, and requalification. What it still lacked was a compact rule for a different question:

> **when may an office or institution *claim* righteousness in public, rather than merely hope that it acted rightly?**

Without such a rule, the archive risks a serious confusion.

It may begin to treat righteousness as if it were self-certifying: something an office can announce about itself on the basis of inaccessible motives, sealed process, insider assurance, or post hoc moral confidence.

That is not a stable view of public righteousness.

## Working-canon thesis

> **For persons, righteousness may sometimes remain partly hidden. For offices and institutions, however, a righteousness claim normally requires contestable justification: shareable reasons or criteria, preserved record, avenues of challenge or review, and evidence not wholly locked inside the claimant's own conscience or closed circle. When secrecy is necessary, the burden rises rather than disappears.**[S18][S32][S34][S35][S36]

This is a rule about the **public standing** of righteousness claims, not a claim that all moral truth must always be fully visible.

## 1. Not all righteousness is equally public

A private person may do something righteous without public recognition. Hidden generosity, quiet promise-keeping, or unadvertised restraint can still be righteous.

But offices and institutions are different. They exercise power, allocate burdens, interpret rules, and sometimes coerce. Once righteousness is claimed for those acts, the issue is no longer only whether someone had a pure intention. The issue is whether those subject to the power are owed reasons, notice, record, and some path of contestation.

Public reason and public-justification traditions help clarify why. Where coercion or burden-allocation is involved, justification cannot rest only on the claimant's private certainty; it must be justifiable for or to those under the power on terms they can in some sense recognize.[S18][S35]

## 2. Four marks of publicly contestable righteousness

The archive now treats four marks as especially important for office and institutions.

### A. Publicly offerable reasons

The reasons for action should be fit to be offered in public terms, especially where coercion, adjudication, exclusion, or public burden-allocation is involved.[S18][S35]

### B. Legible norms and preserved record

Rules, standards, and decisions should be public, accessible, and intelligible enough to guide conduct and to show people the basis on which they are being judged or burdened. Record matters because later review without record is often theater.[S34]

### C. Challenge, hearing, or review

Serious loss, stigma, or penalty should not be imposed without some avenue for evidence, argument, response, and reason-giving. The archive therefore treats reviewability as part of righteous public standing, not merely as an administrative extra.[S34]

### D. Transparency, accountability, and participation fit to the domain

Open-government and integrity frameworks are useful here because they join transparency, accountability, integrity, and stakeholder participation rather than treating any one of them as sufficient on its own.[S36]

These marks are not identical in every office. A court, legislature, regulator, police agency, intelligence service, and school system will satisfy them differently. But some functional equivalent is normally required if public righteousness is being claimed.

## 3. Contestability is not total visibility

This rule is stricter than private moral sincerity, but it is not a demand for perfect publicity.

The archive does **not** say that every reason, source, witness, or operational detail must always be disclosed immediately to everyone. That would be naive in many domains.

Instead, the rule is narrower:

> **public righteousness requires that those burdened are not asked to rely only on inaccessible virtue.**

Some combination of public reason-giving, preserved record, independent scrutiny, appeal, representation, adversarial testing, or later disclosure must exist if the righteousness claim is to stand.

## 4. Secrecy is burdened, not self-vindicating

A secrecy claim may sometimes be real. But secrecy does not lighten the burden of righteousness; it increases it.

So the archive now adopts a burdened-secrecy rule. Where immediate publicity is impossible, a serious righteousness claim normally requires stronger substitutes such as:
- narrowing principle and clear scope limits;
- contemporaneous internal record;
- independent oversight or review;
- recusal / conflict-management where standing is impaired;
- sunset, reopening, or later disclosure where possible;
- and reparative answerability if secrecy wrongly shifted burdens or blocked redress.[S29][S32][S34][S36]

This is also why the archive rejects a familiar office temptation:

> **"trust us; the reasons are good, but cannot be examined" is not yet a righteousness claim.**

At best it may be a claim of necessity, prudence, or temporary opacity under burden.

## 5. What this changes in verdicts

This surface improves the archive's verdict vocabulary again.

More exact verdicts now include:
- **substantively plausible but contestability-defective**;
- **temporarily secret and oversight-burdened**;
- **possibly justified but not yet publicly righteous**;
- **righteously reviewable even under partial secrecy**.

This matters because office and institutions can otherwise launder themselves through confidence, classification, or technical opacity.

## Compressed rule

> **No righteous office without pathways of contestation; no secrecy without burden-shifting into record, review, limit, and later answerability.**
