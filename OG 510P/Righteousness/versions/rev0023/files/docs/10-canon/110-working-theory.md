# Working Theory

## Narrow-waist proposal

**Righteousness is steadfast practical fidelity to valid claims, right order, and breach-responsive repair across relations, roles, and institutions — especially under pressure from gain, fear, status, or ritual substitution.**

This is the current archive-level synthesis. It is not treated as timeless final doctrine; it is treated as the smallest serious theory that presently compresses the strongest recurrences.

The archive now pairs this with two further claims: first, this narrow waist is a **portable core**, not a denial that many traditions thicken righteousness through covenant, divine command, sacred order, repentance, grace, or worship; second, portability claims must be typed by **recognition status** and **public-translation status** rather than spoken of as if all translation succeeded in the same way. See `docs/10-canon/145-portable-core-and-sacred-thickening.md` and `docs/10-canon/150-recognition-status-and-translation-burden.md`.

## Why these terms are in the definition

### 1. Steadfast

Righteousness is not a lucky isolated right act. It names a durable posture or disposition that survives incentives and adversity.[S03][S04][S09]

### 2. Practical fidelity

Righteousness is enacted, not merely admired. Aristotle treats justice as virtue in relation to others; the biblical and Qur'anic materials likewise press toward enacted judgment, aid, promise-keeping, and endurance rather than verbal orthodoxy alone.[S01][S09][S11][S12]

### 3. Valid claims

A central recurring structure is *due-ness*: what another person, community, covenant, office, or moral order may rightly demand. Public office matters especially here, because entrusted power intensifies what is owed and narrows the authority of merely private reasons. This is the strongest shared bridge between ancient virtue accounts, legal-moral traditions, and modern justice theory.[S01][S02][S06][S07][S13][S17][S18]

### 4. Right order

Righteousness is not only dyadic fairness between individuals. It also concerns whether conduct helps preserve a morally acceptable order rather than a corrupt one. This matters in Aristotle, prophetic justice language, Rawlsian institutional thought, and Confucian role ethics.[S01][S04][S10][S11][S13]

### 5. Breach-responsive repair

A serious theory of righteousness cannot stop at “do not wrong.” Once order is breached, or when an agent stands inside wrongful benefit or damaged civic standing, righteousness includes truthful acknowledgment, responsibility uptake, restitution or other reparative measures where due, and repair-oriented response. This does **not** mean that every righteous act is always already reparative; it means that repair becomes constitutive once breach is in view.[S26][S27][S28]

## Five operational dimensions

### A. Discernment
Can the agent tell what is actually due here, rather than what is flattering, profitable, or customary?

### B. Restraint
Can the agent refuse wrongful gain, domination, or self-excusing loopholes?[S04][S07]

### C. Positive duty
Can the agent render what is owed — protection, honesty, care, office-duty, fair dealing, or aid to the wronged?[S10][S12]

### D. Covenant / pledge fidelity
Can the agent keep promises, honor entrusted roles, and remain accountable over time?[S09]

### E. Repair
When failure occurs, or when wrongful benefit and damaged standing are already present, can the agent move toward acknowledgment, restitution or other reparative measures, and truth instead of camouflage?[S26][S27][S28]

## Carriers and priority

This archive no longer treats person and institution as flat co-equals.

Its current view is:

- **relations and claims are explanatorily primary;**
- **persons are the primary accountable carriers;**
- **institutions are the major shaping and scaling structures.**

That architecture explains why righteousness can be said of both persons and institutions without reducing one to the other.[S01][S08][S13][S15][S16]

## Threshold clarification

The archive now states more explicitly that righteousness occupies a middle band between two distortions:
- **below it:** minimal nonwrongdoing, excuse, or procedural cleanliness without active due-fidelity;
- **above it:** heroic, saintly, or otherwise supererogatory excess that is admirable but not universally required.[S24][S25]

So the working theory is not a maximizing view. It asks for steadfast due-rendering under pressure, not the best or most sacrificial act in every imaginable comparison set.

## Defeater clarification

The archive now adds a further rule: righteousness is **standing-sensitive**, not merely outcome-sensitive. Some defects presumptively defeat the righteousness claim even when some valuable result is achieved.

The clearest defeater families are:
- capture or complicity that bends judgment toward private, factional, or role-distorting ends;
- hypocritical self-exemption that demands of others what one refuses for oneself or one’s own side;
- laundering that uses legality, ritual, ideology, or administrative surface to disguise abandonment or domination;
- emergency necessity-talk that purports to purify a grave act into righteousness instead of treating it as tragic, burdened, and accountable.[S19][S29][S30][S31]

So the archive no longer asks only “was some due rendered?” It also asks “what is the standing of the actor, office, or institution making the righteousness claim?”

## Requalification clarification

A further rule now matters. When a defeater has been exposed, the archive distinguishes:
- the status of the original act or exercise of office;
- the possibility of a **righteous response to the exposure**;
- and the longer question of whether standing can be re-earned.

Its present answer is disciplined but non-permanentist: some defeaters block the present righteousness claim without fixing a person or institution in permanent unrighteousness. But any serious requalification path normally requires four things: avowal of the defect, interruption of the corrupted posture, reparative action where due, and time-bound re-earning of standing. Later honesty can change present standing; it cannot retroactively make the earlier tainted act righteous after all.[S26][S27][S30][S32][S33]

## Contestability clarification

A further rule now matters for office and institutions. Private moral seriousness can sometimes be quiet or hidden. But once righteousness is claimed for public power, inaccessible virtue is not enough. The archive now treats public righteousness as normally requiring contestable reasons, preserved record, some avenue of challenge or review, and stronger burdens where secrecy blocks ordinary transparency.[S18][S32][S34][S35][S36]

This does **not** mean that every righteous public act must be fully visible in real time. It means that offices cannot ask those they burden to rely only on sealed confidence. Where secrecy is necessary, righteousness becomes more demanding: scope limits, independent scrutiny, record, reopening, later disclosure where possible, and repair if opacity shifted burdens wrongly.[S29][S34][S36]

## Absent-claimant clarification

A further rule now matters once the archive admits affected standing seriously. Some valid claimants cannot straightforwardly appear: children, persons needing decision-support, diffuse publics, and future persons. The archive therefore now refuses two opposite shortcuts. It will not treat direct present voice as the only form of moral presence; but neither will it let proxies simply overwrite the claimant and call that care. Its current rule is support before substitution, accountable proxy voice where substitution is necessary, and stewardship rather than self-authorized voice for future or diffuse claimants who cannot yet answer back.[S45][S63][S65][S66][S67]

## Special-tie clarification

A further rule now matters between intimacy and office. The archive now rejects two opposite errors: treating morality as though all righteous judgment had to be equally impartial across every relationship, and treating family, friendship, faction, or constituency as though proximity could simply override the due. Some ties genuinely thicken obligation — through promise, dependence, entrusted representation, or sustained relationship — but thickened due is not the same thing as permission to abandon baseline due to others.[S17][S45][S68]

This matters especially for office because not all public roles are structured the same way. Some roles are representational and therefore allow bounded, accountable partiality for those represented. Other roles are adjudicative, allocative, disciplinary, or coercive, and there private ties become standing defects or triggers for recusal rather than reasons for righteous preference. Anti-preferential-treatment and anti-nepotism rules help the archive say this cleanly: office does not become righteous merely by helping one's own people.[S32][S45][S69]

## Conflict clarification

A further rule now matters for hard cases. Valid claims can collide. In such cases, righteousness is not the fantasy of satisfying every due at once, nor the excuse that tragedy suspends moral judgment altogether. The archive now treats righteous conflict-response as disciplined priority among valid claims together with honest carriage of the unmet remainder: naming which claim pressed harder, why, who bore the loss, and what later explanation, mitigation, or repair became due.[S17][S26][S29][S37][S38]

This keeps the archive from collapsing into either clean maximization or helpless lament.

A further rule now matters inside conflict itself. The archive still rejects a timeless ranking algorithm, but it now admits a starter family of **portable priority cues**: serious harm and urgent need over gain or convenience; dependency and vulnerability over easy downward burden-shifting; voluntarily induced reliance over casually disappointed expectation; creator-burden over dumping residue on the already exposed; and reversibility / repairability over hard-to-correct imposition where evidence or agreement remains uncertain.[S17][S38][S41][S47][S48][S49]

## Equity clarification

A further rule now matters for rules and standards. Public norms matter because they reduce arbitrariness, preserve like-treatment, and make power contestable. But ethical and legal judgment cannot be exhausted by mechanical rule application, because many generalizations hold only for the most part and some particular cases underfit the rule's language or paradigm. The archive now treats equity as disciplined particular correction: a righteousness claim may depart from strict rule-form only when the departure is publicly articulable, tied to the rule's purpose, responsive to morally relevant differences, consistent with how like cases will be handled, and preserved in record and review rather than hidden as insider privilege.[S01][S34][S50][S51]

This guards against two opposite failures: rigid formalism that misrenders the due by pretending the case is ordinary when it is not, and favoritist exception that renames ad hoc partiality as humane discretion.

## Temporal clarification

A further rule now matters for judgments through time. The archive now distinguishes three temporal levels:
- **episode** — whether a particular act or omission rendered the due in that case;
- **standing** — whether the actor, office, or institution is presently well-placed to claim righteousness more generally;
- **trajectory** — whether the lived pattern is bending toward or away from trustworthy righteous standing.

This keeps the archive from two opposite mistakes: reading one bright act as proof of settled righteousness, or reading one grave failure as a final essence verdict that makes present standing and later repair unintelligible. In office especially, standing is prospective and trust-facing: others are asked to rely on competence, willingness, self-application, and review-capable conduct over time.[S26][S30][S32][S33][S39]

## Uncertainty clarification

A further rule now matters for inquiry and action under imperfect knowledge. The archive now treats righteousness under uncertainty as requiring proportionate inquiry, fair uptake of testimony, explicit acknowledgment of uncertainty, and a preference for revisable or repairable action where evidence is incomplete and harms may be grave or hard to reverse.[S34][S40][S41][S42]

This guards against two opposite failures: arrogant certainty built on thin or prejudicial evidence, and moral paralysis that pretends uncertainty cancels the burden to protect, decide, or answer for foreseeable risk. In office especially, the question is not only whether the choice was substantively serious, but whether uncertainty was handled in a way that preserved later review, correction, and fair hearing for those asked to bear the burden.[S19][S34][S35][S40]


## Delegation clarification

A further rule now matters for mediated action. Righteousness may use delegates, committees, administrative routines, contractors, and technical systems, but it cannot simply hand off its standing burden with them. Delegation may distribute labor or expertise; it does not automatically transfer away answerability for claim-setting, guardrails, contestability, and repair.[S17][S19][S34][S43][S44][S45][S46]

This guards against a familiar laundering move: when something goes wrong, the office says the vendor built it, the committee approved it, the process required it, or the system decided it. The archive now treats many-hands complexity as a tracing problem, not a moral solvent. Public power may delegate tasks; it may not make answerability disappear into proxy structure.[S19][S34][S43][S44]

## Mercy and sanction clarification

A further rule now matters after wrongdoing has been named and some penalty, pardon, or clemency is in view. The archive now rejects two opposite failures: punitive righteousness theater, which treats severity as proof of seriousness, and mercy laundering, which renames selective indulgence or victim-cost-shifting leniency as humane righteousness. Sanction can be righteous when it publicly names the wrong, protects against recurrence, and keeps repair in view without turning suffering into a moral end in itself.[S26][S34][S54]

Mercy, meanwhile, is not the same thing as forgiveness. Forgiveness belongs primarily to the wronged party, whereas mercy and pardon are authority-side powers connected to the remission or reduction of an available penalty.[S55] The archive therefore treats mercy as righteous only when it preserves truth about the wrong, does not demand victim absolution, remains compatible with protection and repair, and can be publicly explained without favoritist residue. Seneca is useful here because clemency is framed as the restraint of a superior who could otherwise lash out, something closer to disciplined public restraint than to sentimental softness.[S56]

## Public-trust clarification

A further rule now matters for office itself. The archive has repeatedly described office as entrusted power, but it now sharpens that idea into a nonownership rule: office powers are held under public terms and may not be possessed as personal, factional, familial, or future-career property. Existing government-ethics materials are helpful here because they explicitly frame public service as a public trust and bar conflicting interests, misuse of nonpublic information, and other private conversions of office.[S32]

The archive uses fiduciary language here as a **qualified cue**, not as a total reduction of public office to private fiduciary law. The fiduciary-political-theory literature is helpful because it highlights loyalty, care, deliberation, conscientiousness, robustness, and anti-appropriation as central features of officeholding; it is equally helpful because it warns that not every public-law domain fits private-law fiduciary structure cleanly.[S57][S58]

This clarification tightens several earlier rules at once: anti-capture now includes office appropriation, contestability now looks like monitoring of a held trust rather than scrutiny of someone’s personal property, delegation cannot privatize answerability, and mercy or equity cannot be treated as spoils generated by the officeholder’s ownership of office opportunities.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S57][S58]

## Authority-bounds clarification

A further rule now matters for bearer and remit. Righteous office cannot be identified only by attractive ends or sincere motivation. Public acts that impose burdens, permissions, or coercive effects now also have to be judged by **who** was acting, **what** they were entitled to do as office, and whether they were competently situated to do it. Good ends do not purify office usurpation; lacking jurisdiction, adequate competence, or real authorization presumptively defeats a claim of righteous office-performance, even where morally serious interruption or witness might still be available under a different description.[S18][S32][S34][S52][S59][S60][S61][S62]

## Affected-standing clarification

A further rule now matters for who is actually made present in public judgment. The archive already requires public reasons, record, review, fair testimony uptake, and bearer-discipline. But those conditions are still too thin if the people most burdened by a decision remain functionally absent from the process that framed, justified, or imposed it.

So the archive now adds an affected-standing rule: where public power materially burdens identifiable persons or communities, righteousness normally requires some domain-fit way of making the burdened present — direct hearing, meaningful participation, accountable representation, or a justified emergency substitute that remains open to later challenge and repair.[S35][S40][S45][S63][S64]

This does **not** mean that every righteous public act requires unanimous consent, town-hall governance, or direct assembly. Scale, incapacity, confidentiality, and tempo matter. But those constraints raise the burden for justified surrogates rather than licensing benevolent over-the-head rule. A process may be legible and still unrighteous if it treated the burdened mainly as objects of administration instead of as subjects owed uptake, advocacy, or answerable representation.[S34][S45][S63][S64]

## Interruption clarification

A further rule now matters when a role, directive, or institutional channel is itself compromised. The archive now treats righteousness as compatible with interruption rather than continued compliant participation when obedience would materially launder abuse, false standing, or blocked answerability. Its preferred order is disciplined rather than theatrical: objection and internal correction where still live, recusal or targeted refusal where a specific task would make one a direct vehicle of wrong, protected disclosure where hidden abuse will otherwise persist, resignation where continued occupancy would lend false legitimacy, and burdened public disobedient witness only when narrower lawful channels are blocked or corrupted.[S16][S19][S30][S32][S34][S52][S53]

This guards against two opposite failures: obedient complicity that confuses role fidelity with righteousness, and resistance romanticism that treats every leak, exit, or breach as morally self-certifying. Interruption can itself be righteous, but only under truth, evidence, proportionality, and later answerability for the burdens it shifted or the procedures it bypassed.[S29][S34][S52][S53]

## Short formula

When in doubt, test righteousness as:

> seeing the due, resisting corrupt gain, rendering what is owed, carrying conflict remainder honestly when not all dues can be met, keeping public power contestable where power is public, judging episodes, standing, and trajectory without laundering either bright acts or bad history, handling uncertainty through inquiry, fair testimony uptake, and revisable action, and keeping delegated action visibly owned rather than proxy-laundered, treating office itself as held rather than owned, refusing to call an exercise of public power righteous when the wrong bearer, inadequate competence, or missing authorization is doing the work, making the materially burdened present through hearing, accountable representation, or justified substitute rather than ruling over their heads, correcting rule underfit openly without favoritist exception, interrupting compromised roles without laundering either obedience or rebellion, sanctioning wrong without cruelty theater, showing mercy only under public and non-favoritist restraint, answering breach reparatively when breach is present, and refusing self-exemption — without confusing that with either mere blamelessness or heroism.
