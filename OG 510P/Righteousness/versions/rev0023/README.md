# Righteousness — rev0023

## When loyalty changes what is owed

Caring especially for your child and steering a public job toward your child are not two instances of the same moral permission. This edition asks why a relationship can deepen a duty in one setting and demand restraint in another. Its answer is neither equal distance from everyone nor loyalty without limits: the relation matters, the role matters, and the claims of people outside the favored circle still matter.

**Begin with [Special Ties, Loyalty, and Impartial Bounds](files/docs/10-canon/240-special-ties-loyalty-and-impartial-bounds.md).** It is a short, concrete entrance to the edition's defining argument. Then read the [Working Theory](files/docs/10-canon/110-working-theory.md) for the larger account of fidelity, public power and repair. The [collection introduction](../../README.md) connects this early synthesis to the later institutional essays.

## Three different kinds of partiality

The special-ties essay separates settings that a blanket demand to “be impartial” would obscure:

- **Intimate and promissory relationships:** dependence, shared history and undertakings can make a claim more demanding. Caring for a child or keeping a promise is not necessarily a departure from what is owed; the relationship can help constitute what is owed.
- **Entrusted representation:** an advocate or representative may be responsible for bringing particular people's concerns into a public decision. Judge-like neutrality toward every side would sometimes fail that responsibility. But representation does not permit falsehood, the erasure of outsiders' rights, or treating an office as a personal possession.
- **Adjudication, allocation and discipline:** private ties can instead compromise the position from which someone decides. In these settings the essay looks for disclosure, recusal or other visible limits, rather than reassurance that affection will somehow improve the decision.

This is the significance of the child-at-home/public-job contrast. It is not an argument against close relationships, nor an exception allowing favoritism whenever someone can name a relationship. The task is to explain what a particular tie warrants **in this role**, while retaining what remains due to everyone else. Public representation and impartial administration therefore cannot be collapsed into one model of office.

The essay turns this distinction into five questions: which relation is doing the justificatory work; what remains owed to outsiders; what kind of role is involved; whether the rationale can be articulated consistently across like cases; and what restraint actually followed when impartiality was required. A reader can use these questions to examine a promise, a constituency claim or a hiring decision without treating closeness itself as either proof of duty or proof of corruption. The legal examples are part of the March 2026 source argument, not a statement of current law.

## The theory this distinction develops

The [Working Theory](files/docs/10-canon/110-working-theory.md) proposes righteousness as steadfast practical fidelity to valid claims, right order and repair after breach. “Steadfast” matters because a lucky good act is not yet a dependable disposition; “practical” matters because admiration and declarations do not discharge obligations. Its account begins from relations and claims, treats persons as accountable agents, and asks how institutions shape and scale their conduct.

Two limits prevent this becoming either a demand for universal sacrifice or a certificate of procedural cleanliness. The theory places righteousness above merely avoiding blame but below always choosing the most heroic action. And it allows valid claims to conflict: priority for one claim need not extinguish the other. Explanation, mitigation or repair may still be owed to those who lost out.

That remainder connects loyalty to the rest of the work. A near claim may press harder, but its priority does not make outsiders disappear. Likewise, a good outcome does not automatically vindicate the authority that produced it. The theory asks whether power was held within its remit, whether burdened people had meaningful hearing or representation, and whether reasons and decisions could be challenged. Acknowledgment, interruption of the wrong, repair and changed conduct over time can rebuild present standing without retrospectively making an earlier wrong righteous.

The comparative ambition is a portable account that does not simply strip away covenant, sacred order, repentance or grace. The theory distinguishes recognition within a tradition from translation into shared public terms. Its proposed common structure is an argument to examine, not a claim that every tradition already means the same thing.

## Read the development, then test the boundary

A short route through this snapshot is:

1. [Special ties](files/docs/10-canon/240-special-ties-loyalty-and-impartial-bounds.md): the three settings and the five questions.
2. [Working Theory](files/docs/10-canon/110-working-theory.md): how this distinction sits beside conflict, repair, nonownership of office, affected standing and uncertainty.
3. [Changelog](files/CHANGELOG.md): the sequence from entrusted office and conflict remainder through hearing, absent claimants and finally bounded partiality. The revision entries show how each clarification was introduced, rather than presenting the synthesis as a single finished insight.
4. [Open questions](files/OPEN_QUESTIONS.json), especially OQ-0018: when does a special tie create an obligation, when does it merely permit a preference, and when must a public role suppress that preference? OQ-0016 and OQ-0017 keep representation and proxy authority open to scrutiny.
5. [Source record](files/SOURCES.md) and [original reading route](files/START_HERE.md): follow the source identifiers and widen the inquiry.

The unresolved obligation/permission distinction is consequential. Showing that it can be permissible to favor someone does not establish that favoring them is required; showing that a relationship creates duties does not settle their weight against other valid claims. The edition supplies a useful structure for asking these questions, while its own open-question record acknowledges that a tradition- and role-sensitive map is still missing.

A further question runs through the whole proposal: how are the claims counted as “valid” justified when traditions or affected parties disagree? The compact theory helps expose the dispute and reject shortcuts such as gain, legality or loyalty alone. It does not supply a universal ranking algorithm that removes the need for moral argument.
