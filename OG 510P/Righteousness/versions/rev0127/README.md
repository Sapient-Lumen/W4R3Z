# Righteousness — rev0127

## What does participation require you to surrender?

A service can offer a choice and still make refusal costly: accept behavioural tracking, or pay to avoid it while keeping access to the people, information or work already gathered there. This edition asks when that arrangement is a legitimate way to fund a service and when it makes privacy something people must buy back.

**Start with [Consent or Pay, Tracking Walls, and No-Privacy-by-Tribute](files/docs/10-canon/760-consent-or-pay-tracking-walls-and-no-privacy-by-tribute.md).** Its target is not payment or advertising as such. It asks whether meaningful participation depends on tracking or a fee for escaping tracking, instead of a genuinely equivalent, less-intrusive and nonpunitive alternative. The [collection introduction](../../README.md) gives the broader philosophical setting; the [earlier edition](../rev0023/README.md) offers a more compact entrance to the account of claims, roles and repair.

## From a visible choice to the conditions of choosing

The tracking-wall essay changes the object of attention. A button labeled “consent” does not by itself explain what makes the decision free. A subscription price does not by itself explain what the payment purchases. The relevant object is the whole service relation:

- **What is being processed?** Behavioural advertising, cross-service combination and tracking are not interchangeable with every use of data or every kind of advertising.
- **What can the person do instead?** The essay distinguishes contextual advertising, a less-personalised equivalent service, a paid private option, and a bare tracking-or-payment binary.
- **What would refusal cost in practice?** A stored social graph, professional reliance, accumulated content or educational dependence can make departure costly even when closing the account is technically possible.
- **What remains equivalent?** A nominal alternative may protect privacy while degrading the core service, reach or functionality enough to make refusal materially costly.
- **What happens after consent?** Withdrawal matters as well as the initial choice. An unfair penalty for changing one's mind can preserve the same dependency under a different interface.

These distinctions make the phrase “privacy by tribute” more than a complaint about expensive subscriptions. The proposed wrong is that the institution uses the service relation to make surveillance surrender or payment for relief govern access. Its strongest cases combine dependence, costly exit, unaffordable refusal or unequal alternatives. The essay explicitly leaves room for paid services, premium tiers and bounded advertising.

A useful way to read it is to compare an ordinary charge for a service with a fee demanded for escaping a tracking-heavy arrangement. The amount alone cannot settle the difference. One must ask what baseline of privacy is owed, which processing the service can justify, and whether the alternatives preserve meaningful participation. Those are moral questions the edition raises, not conclusions that follow merely from labeling one option “free.”

## The wider inquiry: what actually controls access?

The later Righteousness essays repeatedly ask whether an institution's stated purpose still governs its treatment of people, or whether its machinery has become the effective condition for being admitted, trusted or heard. This is a development of the earlier concern with valid claims and answerable power: the wrong can lie in the arrangement through which a claim must pass, not only in an openly hostile decision.

Two neighboring essays give that question different, concrete forms:

- [Age Assurance and Age Verification](files/docs/10-canon/745-age-assurance-age-verification-and-no-child-safety-by-compulsory-identification.md) distinguishes establishing the age fact a setting needs from collecting more identity than it needs. The argument joins a protective aim to limits on collection, reuse, cross-service linkage and unchallengeable error. It does not reject every age check.
- [Student Monitoring and Remote Proctoring](files/docs/10-canon/755-student-monitoring-remote-proctoring-and-no-learning-by-panopticon.md) distinguishes bounded invigilation or device administration from making students continuously watchable in order to count as trustworthy. It follows recordings and flags into retention, disciplinary use, provider side-use and the student's ability to explain context or challenge a mistake. It does not reject every educational record or safety measure.

The mechanisms differ. An age gate can demand excessive identity; a proctoring system can demand continuous visibility; a tracking wall can demand consent or money. Reading them together reveals the shared question without erasing those differences: **what must a person submit to before the institution will recognize their participation, and what justifies that demand?**

The [later Working Theory](files/docs/10-canon/110-working-theory.md) gathers these and many other applications. It is a large accumulated synthesis, so the individual essays are the easier entrance. The [changelog](files/CHANGELOG.md) identifies the successive additions and how they were threaded into the theory and supporting records.

## Evidence, argument and the open boundary

The tracking-wall essay draws on four entries in the [source record](files/SOURCES.md): S522–S525, covering EDPB, ICO and European Commission materials. These are its historical regulatory supports, not a single interchangeable rule for every service or jurisdiction. The essay uses them to argue for a broader moral distinction between bounded funding and privacy made into a luxury tier. This guide describes that argument; it does not update the March 2026 edition's legal claims.

The strongest question for the reader is where that distinction becomes decisive. A service does need resources. A less-intrusive option may have a cost, and meaningful equivalence does not tell us by itself which differences are acceptable or who should bear that cost. Conversely, a funding need does not establish permission to exploit dependence or demand any profitable data use.

The edition's [OQ-0122](files/OPEN_QUESTIONS.json) leaves the domain-sensitive boundary open: subscriptions, contextual advertising and privacy-preserving funding must be distinguished from arrangements in which inappropriate fees or non-equivalent options make ordinary privacy a premium purchase. OQ-0119 and OQ-0121 retain the corresponding unresolved boundaries for age assurance and educational monitoring. The seven questions in each essay help identify the facts that matter; they are not a calibrated test that automatically returns a moral verdict.

Read this snapshot as an expanding philosophical investigation with source-linked cases, not as an implemented privacy tool or a completed institutional design. Its value is in making the structure of a demand inspectable: purpose, means, dependence, alternatives, burdens and the possibility of contesting the result.
