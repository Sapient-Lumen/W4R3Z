# Water, Sanitation, and No-Thirst-by-Administration

## Why this surface matters

The archive already rejects subsistence abandonment, housing precarity, punitive noncare, collective deprivation, and no-destitution-by-sanction. What it still lacked was a compact rule for **what happens when basic life is governed through water and sanitation systems themselves** — through shutoff, contamination, inaccessible collection, unsafe or degrading toilets, missing hygiene support, or wastewater neglect that predictably makes some people drink less, wash less, travel farther, get sicker, or live more publicly and humiliatingly than others.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S93][S163][S164][S166][S167][S261][S166][S263][S264][S265][S266][S267][S268]

That gap matters because unrighteousness here often hides inside infrastructure language. Rate design, arrears collection, utility governance, tanker distribution, school-facility design, public-toilet policy, wastewater treatment, drought restrictions, and settlement servicing can all be described as neutral management even when the practical result is thirst, disease exposure, time theft, gendered danger, disability exclusion, family stress, school absence, or neighborhood hierarchy carried through pipes, queues, and shutoff notices rather than through openly punitive speech. The archive needed a cleaner rule against that slide.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S66][S67][S93][S153][S154][S232][S261][S166][S263][S264][S265][S266][S267][S268]

## Working-canon thesis

> **Righteousness is burdened, and often defeated, when a person, office, or institution governs access to safe drinking water, sanitation, hygiene, or wastewater protection through shutoff, unaffordability, contamination, discriminatory siting, inaccessible design, humiliating dependence, or neglected infrastructure mainly in order to discipline arrears, externalize cost, rank neighborhoods, simplify administration, or treat a dignity-bearing floor as a mere consumer service. Water and sanitation are not exhausted by nominal network presence or a toilet somewhere on the map. They require sufficient and continuous availability, safety, physical accessibility, affordability, acceptability, privacy, and real priority to those most in need. Scarcity, maintenance, and service regulation can justify triage or bounded interruption, but not denial to people who cannot pay, not governance through thirst or filth, and not service designs that predictably force people into unsafe sources, open defecation, disease exposure, or humiliating dependency without live review, emergency protection, and remedy.**[S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S93][S163][S164][S166][S167][S261][S166][S263][S264][S265][S266][S267][S268]

## 1. Water and sanitation are rights-bearing infrastructure, not optional utilities

Current OHCHR materials are useful because they do not describe water and sanitation as ordinary commodities that matter only after market allocation. They treat them as basic human rights indispensable to dignity and healthy livelihoods, and they tie implementation to universal access without discrimination while prioritizing those most in need.[S261][S166] The General Assembly recognition matters for the archive because it makes explicit what the rest of the Covenant structure was already pressing toward: these are baseline conditions for the enjoyment of life and many other rights, not luxuries to be addressed only after politics becomes generous.[S261][S166]

So the archive should now reject a familiar laundering move: because the relevant actor runs a utility, owns a building, manages a camp, contracts a provider, or balances a budget, water and sanitation are treated as if they were only service lines and invoices rather than standing-bearing conditions. Institutions may still charge, maintain, ration, and regulate. But once the system decides who will live with chronic thirst, unsafe water, unusable toilets, or degrading dependence because that is easier than protecting a floor, the claim of righteousness is already badly burdened.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S93][S261][S166][S268]

## 2. Availability, safety, accessibility, affordability, and acceptability are constitutive

General Comment No. 15 and OHCHR's compact guidance are morally revealing because they refuse the shallow view that any technical connection or distant facility answers what is owed. They treat the right to water as requiring sufficient and continuous supply for personal and domestic uses, safety, physical accessibility, and affordability; OHCHR's current materials extend the same basic discipline to sanitation and hygiene while foregrounding accessibility, safety, acceptability, affordability, and non-discrimination.[S261][S166][S263][S264]

That matters because deprivation often hides in sub-threshold design. A standpipe that is too far, a toilet unsafe at night, a school without menstrual-hygiene support, a public facility without privacy, a service point unusable by wheelchair users, a queue that consumes hours of labor, or a supply that is technically present but unsafe to drink may all leave the right formally acknowledged but practically defeated. The archive should therefore now ask not only whether infrastructure exists, but whether ordinary persons can actually use it in ways consistent with health, privacy, dignity, and daily life.[S34][S35][S41][S66][S67][S93][S145][S146][S147][S261][S166][S263][S264][S267]

## 3. Poverty does not make shutoff righteous

Affordability materials are especially useful because they state the point with unusual clarity: payment for water and sanitation must not force people to sacrifice other basic goods such as food, housing, and health care, and no individual or group should be denied access to safe drinking water because they cannot afford to pay.[S166][S266] The Detroit statements are morally revealing for the archive because they sharpen the same structure into a governance warning: disconnection for genuine inability to pay is not a morally neutral collection tactic but a rights violation that can cascade into housing loss, family stress, and discriminatory burden.[S166][S266]

This does **not** mean every bill is unjust or every interruption forbidden. Leakage, drought, maintenance, wasteful use, and strategic non-payment can be real problems. But those problems raise the duty to use hardship protection, public subsidy, debt restructuring, targeted collection against those able to pay, emergency minimum guarantees, and reviewable exceptions rather than letting poverty itself trigger thirst or unusable sanitation. Where the system's real logic is that the poor must carry infrastructural failure in their bodies, the archive should now treat that as no-thirst-by-administration rather than as ordinary fiscal discipline.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S93][S166][S166][S266][S268]

## 4. Sanitation is not a side issue and wastewater is not morally invisible

The archive also needed a sharper sanitation rule. Current OHCHR materials are useful because they describe sanitation as available, accessible, affordable, safe, acceptable, private, and dignified, explicitly include hygiene, and make clear that wastewater treatment infrastructure and toxic contamination sit inside the rights question rather than outside it.[S263][S267] That matters because institutions often behave as though water has a rights vocabulary while sanitation can remain an afterthought delegated to stigma, gender norms, or invisible downstream disposal.

So the archive should now reject another laundering move: because toilets exist somewhere, or because waste disappears from elite sight, the sanitation burden is treated as solved. In practice, missing privacy, unsafe or absent facilities for women and girls, inaccessible toilets, lack of handwashing, untreated sewage, and exposure to pollutants can decide whether people attend school, work safely, preserve bodily dignity, or face disease and public shame. A righteous claim about sanitation therefore has to ask who can use facilities safely and privately, what happens to waste after use, and whose bodies or neighborhoods are being asked to absorb the contamination cost.[S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S66][S67][S93][S167][S261][S263][S267][S268]

## 5. Common-good governance and remedy matter because infrastructure can launder domination

The common-violations report matters because it frames breaches of water and sanitation rights as rights violations properly so called and says that all components of those rights are justiciable and must be subject to effective remedies.[S265] The 2025 OHCHR expert statement is useful for a second reason: it warns that market-driven and financialized water governance can undermine human rights and argues for governing water as a common good with equality, participation, accountability, sustainability, and targeted subsidies rather than as a business opportunity extracted from the exposed.[S268]

That helps the archive name the standing defect more precisely. Water and sanitation systems are easy places to diffuse responsibility: the provider blames the regulator, the city blames the utility, the owner blames the tenant, the school blames the budget, the camp manager blames scarcity, and the upstream polluter blames complex infrastructure. But where no one owns hardship protection, contamination prevention, minimum supply, safe sanitation, or emergency redress, the system is not merely complicated. It is organized so that basic bodily dependency becomes governable without answerability.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S43][S44][S45][S46][S93][S153][S154][S265][S268]

## 6. Working rule

When righteousness is claimed in a water or sanitation case, ask seven questions:
1. **What interest was actually at stake?** Drinking water, domestic water, handwashing, menstrual hygiene, toilet access, wastewater safety, school attendance, health protection, or another basic bodily condition?[S261][S166][S263][S264][S267]
2. **Which element of the right was failing?** Availability, continuity, safety, physical accessibility, affordability, acceptability, privacy, dignity, or non-discrimination?[S261][S166][S263][S264][S267]
3. **What device was doing the real work?** Shutoff, rate design, debt collection, contamination, poor siting, inaccessible design, hours restrictions, document demands, privatized neglect, or wastewater dumping?[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S153][S154][S261][S166][S265][S266][S267][S268]
4. **What hardship protections or alternatives were seriously used first?** Subsidy, minimum essential supply, emergency reconnection, debt restructuring, tankering, accessibility retrofit, safe temporary facilities, pollution control, or targeted enforcement against actors who could actually bear the cost?[S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S166][S166][S266][S268]
5. **Who carried the burden in practice?** Poor households, children, women and girls, persons with disabilities, informal settlements, displaced people, detainees, rural communities, or neighborhoods already ranked low in public standing?[S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S66][S67][S93][S145][S146][S147][S232][S261][S166][S263][S267]
6. **What review and remedy path remained before decisive harm hardened?** Could the person contest a shutoff, correct records, obtain urgent reconnection, challenge contamination, or force repair before thirst, disease, school loss, or housing breakdown became irreversible?[S26][S34][S35][S41][S93][S111][S112][S265]
7. **Was the system really preserving shared infrastructure, or was it using infrastructure to rank whose dignity counted?** Drought management, maintenance, and regulation may be necessary, but were they structured through public reason and equal concern, or through no-thirst-by-administration?[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S93][S261][S166][S266][S268]

## Compression sentence

> **Do not govern people through thirst, contamination, unsafe sanitation, or humiliating dependence on service mercy.** Righteousness may justify bounded rationing, repair work, pricing, and regulation, but it is burdened when water and sanitation are administered so that some people must live through shutoff, unaffordability, dangerous facilities, or wastewater exposure because protecting their dignity was treated as optional.[S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S93][S163][S164][S166][S167][S261][S166][S263][S264][S265][S266][S267][S268]
