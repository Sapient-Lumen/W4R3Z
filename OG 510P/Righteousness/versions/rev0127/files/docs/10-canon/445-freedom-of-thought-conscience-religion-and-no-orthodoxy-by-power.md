# Freedom of Thought, Conscience, Religion, and No-Orthodoxy-by-Power

## Why this surface matters

The archive already rejects compelled creed, no-silencing-by-power, no-association-by-sufferance, and no-rule-by-exclusion. What it still lacked was a compact rule for **what happens when power reaches more broadly into whether people may have, change, keep, manifest, or publicly live a religion or belief at all**: anti-conversion pressure, registration systems that decide which communities may exist, coercive pressure to recant or adhere, exclusion from schools or public office because of belief, suppression of worship or observance, or state management of orthodoxy through fear, dependency, or selective permission.[S34][S35][S41][S93][S127][S132][S227][S228][S229]

That missing rule matters because Article 18 protects freedom of thought, conscience, and religion, including the freedom to have or adopt a religion or belief of one's choice and the freedom to manifest religion or belief individually or in community, publicly or privately, in worship, observance, practice, and teaching.[S127][S228] General Comment No. 22 sharpens the moral architecture: freedom to have or adopt a religion or belief admits no limitation whatsoever, the article protects theistic, non-theistic, and atheistic beliefs, and coercive policies or practices impairing freedom to have or adopt a religion or belief are inconsistent with the Covenant.[S132] OHCHR's current topic guidance compresses the same structure by describing freedom of religion as the right to choose what religion to follow and to worship without undue interference, while the religion-or-belief discrimination materials add that states must refrain from discrimination, prevent it, and take steps so everyone enjoys rights in practice without discrimination on religion-or-belief grounds.[S227][S229] So the archive should now treat coercive orthodoxy, forced conformity, and religion-or-belief discrimination as a distinct standing defect: **public order may sometimes justify narrow limits on manifestation, but power may not rightfully treat inward conviction, conversion, nonbelief, worship, or community belonging as objects of managerial possession**.[S127][S132][S227][S228][S229]

## Working-canon thesis

> **Righteousness is burdened, and often defeated, when a person, office, or institution governs by coercing belief, punishing conversion or nonbelief, suppressing worship or observance beyond narrow necessity, or distributing standing, safety, education, livelihood, custody conditions, or public office on religion-or-belief terms it cannot publicly justify. Freedom of thought and conscience are not permissions held on sufferance. Manifestation of religion or belief may sometimes be limited by law for genuinely protective reasons, but the burden stays steep: the inward forum is not for public capture, coercion is prohibited, discrimination on religion-or-belief grounds is standing-defective, and plural public order must be built through coexistence and narrow necessity rather than through orthodoxy management.**[S127][S132][S227][S228][S229]

## 1. The inward forum is not public property

Article 18 and General Comment No. 22 matter because they distinguish holding or adopting a religion or belief from manifesting it. The freedom of thought and conscience, and the freedom to have or adopt a religion or belief of one's choice, admit no limitation whatsoever; by contrast, manifestations may be subject only to narrow lawful limits.[S127][S132] GC22 is also morally useful because it protects theistic, non-theistic, and atheistic beliefs alike and treats coercive pressure to adhere, recant, or convert as inconsistent with the right.[S132]

That matters because power often claims it is regulating conduct when it is really trying to settle what convictions may be lived at all. Registration rules, loyalty rituals, conversion bans, apostasy pressure, compulsory religious instruction, anti-blasphemy enforcement, or ideological vetting can all be framed as public order, tradition, or cultural protection. But once what is being managed is not merely a concrete act with public effects but the person's freedom to have, reject, change, or keep a conviction, the archive should presume a standing defect. The state may punish assault, fraud, coercion, or incitement; it may not own the soul, the conscience, or the permission to believe otherwise.[S18][S19][S34][S35][S127][S132][S227][S228]

## 2. Manifestation is protected even though it is not absolute

The declaration text is useful because it spells out what manifestation includes: worship, observance, practice, and teaching, individually or in community, in public or private; it also lists assembling for religion or belief, maintaining places for those purposes, training leaders, publishing, teaching, observing days of rest, and celebrating holidays and ceremonies.[S228] Article 18(3) and the declaration also sharpen the limit structure: manifestation may be limited only by law and only where necessary to protect public safety, order, health or morals, or the fundamental rights and freedoms of others.[S127][S228]

This matters because offices often say religion or belief remains free while confining it to interiority, invisibility, or administrative discretion. But a freedom that may be held only silently, alone, or at the pleasure of the stronger party is already being narrowed into managed permission. The archive should therefore now ask a sharper question whenever clothing, symbols, worship, diet, assembly, holy-day observance, burial rites, publication, charity, leadership, or conscientious nonparticipation is burdened: **what concrete protected interest required the burden, and why could a narrower route not preserve both plural coexistence and the claimant's practice?** Where the real problem is public visibility, minority persistence, or conversion away from favored orthodoxy, righteousness is already drifting toward orthodoxy management.[S18][S19][S34][S35][S127][S132][S227][S228]

## 3. Discrimination on religion-or-belief grounds is not cleaned by administrative form

The 1981 Declaration and OHCHR's discrimination guidance matter because they make the anti-discrimination point explicit. No one should be subject to discrimination by any state, institution, group, or person on religion-or-belief grounds, and states must refrain from such discrimination, prevent it, and take steps so all persons in their territory enjoy human rights in practice without discrimination of any kind.[S228][S229]

That matters because power can burden religion or belief without openly banning it. Schools may exclude dress, calendar, or dietary practices in ways that predictably sort out minorities. Public offices may impose facially neutral eligibility rules that quietly filter on faith identity. Police, prisons, migration systems, or welfare systems may allocate suspicion, search intensity, or access to services by sectarian stereotype. The archive should therefore now reject the thought that freedom of religion or belief is preserved whenever there is no explicit creed test on the books. If ordinary standing, safety, schooling, work, or civic access predictably turns on religion-or-belief identity or nonidentity, the defect is already live even when it travels under neutral-looking administration.[S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S66][S127][S228][S229]

## 4. Power may not cure pluralism by deciding orthodoxy for everyone

The archive already knew from the compelled-creed surface that offices may require duty-fit commitments without owning inward allegiance. Religion-or-belief materials widen that lesson. OHCHR's current topic page links freedom of religion or belief to diversity, while the Special Rapporteur's mandate exists because states continue to face recurring obstacles to enjoyment of that right and need recommendations on how to overcome them.[S227][S229] The declaration is useful because it insists that intolerance and discrimination on religion-or-belief grounds should be eliminated, not normalized as tools of social peace.[S228]

So the archive now rejects two familiar laundering moves. The first is **orthodoxy by favoritism**: one religion, denomination, or official secularism receives public comfort while rivals bear permit friction, surveillance, stigma, or disabled access. The second is **orthodoxy by fear**: minorities, dissenters, converts, atheists, or heterodox members are left formally free but practically exposed to violence, mob pressure, detention, or public exclusion without meaningful protection. In both cases, public power is not simply preserving social peace. It is deciding whose conscience counts as ordinary and whose must remain precarious.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S227][S228][S229]

## 5. Plural public order requires facilitation and protection, not forced sameness

OHCHR's current materials are useful because they connect freedom of religion or belief to other rights and to the management of diversity in society rather than to a private indulgence that can be withdrawn when social friction appears.[S227] That helps the archive resist a shallow theory of peace in which the easiest way to reduce conflict is to flatten public difference, suppress minority rites, punish conversion, or privatize all contested conviction. A peaceful order is not righteous merely because it is quiet. If quiet was purchased by making belief invisible, by sorting access through majority comfort, or by forcing vulnerable people to choose between safety and conviction, then peace-talk is covering a standing defect.[S18][S19][S34][S35][S41][S227][S229]

## 6. Working rule

When religion-or-belief practice, identity, conversion, or nonbelief is burdened under pressure, ask six questions:
1. **Was the burden really about conduct with public effects, or about inward conviction itself?** The power to regulate conduct does not include the power to own belief, conversion, or conscience.[S127][S132][S228]
2. **What manifestation was burdened?** Worship, observance, teaching, assembly, dress, symbols, holidays, diet, leadership, burial, publication, charity, and conscientious refusal all require specific rather than generic analysis.[S127][S228]
3. **What concrete protected interest required the burden, and why was a narrower route unavailable?** Public order cannot do all the work by itself.[S18][S34][S35][S127][S228]
4. **Who predictably bore the burden?** Did the rule fall mainly on minorities, converts, dissenters, atheists, migrants, prisoners, schoolchildren, workers, or persons already structurally dependent?[S34][S35][S41][S66][S229]
5. **Was discrimination or selective permission doing the practical work?** A system can preserve formal liberty while deciding ordinary safety, access, or public visibility by religion-or-belief identity.[S32][S34][S35][S228][S229]
6. **What protection and repair existed?** Were there complaint, review, anti-violence protection, accommodation, and correction channels strong enough to keep plural belonging real in practice?[S34][S35][S41][S227][S229]

## Compact maxim

> **Do not govern conscience by deciding whose belief may exist comfortably in public.** Righteous regulation may burden manifestation narrowly for genuine protection of others, but righteousness is heavily burdened when power coerces adherence, punishes conversion or nonbelief, disables worship, or distributes ordinary standing through favored orthodoxy and managed precarity.[S127][S132][S227][S228][S229]
