# Consent or pay, tracking walls, and no-privacy-by-tribute

> **Righteousness is burdened, and often defeated, when access to a meaningful digital service is made to depend on either surrendering behavioural-tracking consent or paying a fee, so that ordinary participation is governed by surveillance or tribute rather than by a genuine less-intrusive alternative, bounded data use, and freely withdrawable choice.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S522][S523][S524][S525]

## 1. The missing moral question is not only whether a service may charge money, but whether privacy has been turned into a premium feature

The archive already rejected no-agency-by-stealth, no-obligation-by-friction, no-price-by-profile, and no-price-by-ambush. But it still lacked a direct rule for a different defect: **a service making the practical price of ordinary access either behavioural advertising consent or a fee paid to avoid that consent.**[S522][S523][S524][S525]

That gap matters because the wrong is not exhausted by saying that platforms need funding, that advertising exists, or that premium subscriptions are allowed. A service can still be standing-defective when the real structure is: participate under tracking, or buy back a more private relation at a price the user cannot realistically refuse. In that case, privacy is no longer treated as a normal baseline constraint on governance. It is treated as a luxury tier or a toll good.[S522][S523][S524][S525]

So the archive now needs a canon surface that is not reducible either to generic online-tracking law or to generic pricing law. No-privacy-by-tribute asks when a service stops merely choosing a business model and starts making the person purchase relief from surveillance or accept tracking as the price of ordinary social, informational, or civic participation.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S522][S523][S524][S525]

## 2. Current official materials now name this family clearly enough to justify canon admission

Current official materials converge unusually sharply here. The EDPB's 2024 opinion on large online platforms says that, in most cases, it will not be possible to obtain valid consent where users are confronted only with a choice between consenting to behavioural-advertising processing and paying a fee.[S522] The Board says large online platforms should not treat a paid alternative as the default way forward and should consider an equivalent alternative that does not entail a fee and uses less or no personal data for advertising.[S522]

The ICO's 2025 consent-or-pay guidance keeps the same structure while speaking in a more implementation-facing register. It says consent-or-pay models can comply only if organisations can demonstrate freely given consent and document that assessment, and it organizes the key questions around power imbalance, appropriate fee, equivalence, and privacy by design.[S523] The ICO also says that where the only alternative to consent is paying a single price that combines service access with a fee for avoiding personalised advertising, it can be difficult to demonstrate freely given consent.[S523] Its 2025 online-tracking strategy says the regulator is taking action on cookie compliance and has issued consent-or-pay guidance to clarify how organisations must give users meaningful control.[S524]

The European Commission's current DMA Q&A adds the competition-and-gatekeeper side. It says gatekeepers must offer end users who do not accept the combination or cross-use of personal data a less personalised but equivalent alternative, and it says the Commission found Meta's binary pay-or-consent model non-compliant because it did not provide the required less-personalised equivalent alternative and did not allow users to exercise their right to freely consent to the combination of personal data.[S525]

Together these materials show that consent-or-pay is not just a niche advertising debate. It is a live governance seam about whether privacy becomes something people must purchase back from institutions that otherwise demand tracking as the ordinary condition of access.[S522][S523][S524][S525]

## 3. The righteousness issue is not charging as such or advertising as such; it is making surveillance surrender or tribute do the real work of access

Righteousness should not treat every paid service, every premium tier, or every contextual-advertising system as automatically unrighteous. Nor should it assume that every subscription model is a privacy violation. The narrower defect appears where the practical choice architecture is: accept behavioural tracking, or pay to escape it, with no genuinely equivalent less-intrusive route.[S522][S523][S525]

That defect is clearest where a service has strong lock-in, network effects, content dependency, professional reliance, educational importance, or social centrality; where the fee is high enough to make refusal unrealistic; where the paid and unpaid versions are not broadly equivalent in their core service; where existing users risk losing content, contacts, reach, or accumulated standing; where withdrawal from tracking carries detriment; or where the model is presented through harmful design that obscures what is being traded away.[S522][S523][S524][S525]

In those cases the wrong is not simply that money changes hands or that ads exist. It is that the institution has arranged access so that the person must either become behaviourally mineable or pay tribute for a more ordinary level of privacy. That is why the archive now needs a no-privacy-by-tribute rule rather than leaving the issue scattered across privacy, manipulation, and pricing materials.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S522][S523][S524][S525]

## 4. Questions righteous judgment should now ask

When an actor claims righteousness while using consent-or-pay or similar tracking-wall arrangements, ask seven questions:

1. **What exact service relation is at stake?** Casual entertainment, a core social network, messaging, publishing access, professional networking, news, education, or another service with stronger dependency or public relevance?[S522][S523][S525]
2. **What exact data processing is being demanded?** Behavioural advertising, cross-service combination, cross-use of personal data, tracking across products, or some narrower use?[S522][S523][S525]
3. **What alternatives actually exist?** A free contextual-advertising option, a less personalised but equivalent alternative, a paid private option, or only the binary choice between tracking and payment?[S522][S523][S525]
4. **Is the fee realistically avoidable?** Does the amount make refusal unrealistic for ordinary users or for users already dependent on the service?[S522][S523]
5. **Are the options genuinely equivalent in their core service?** Or does the privacy-protective route degrade reach, functionality, access, or social standing in ways that make the choice formal rather than real?[S522][S523][S525]
6. **What power imbalance or lock-in exists?** Market position, network effects, professional dependence, existing social graph, stored content, or other conditions that make exit or nonuse unusually costly?[S522][S523]
7. **Can tracking consent be refused or withdrawn without unfair detriment?** If not, has the system effectively turned privacy into a tribute rather than preserving it as a normal governance constraint?[S522][S523][S524][S525]

## 5. Canonical compression

> **Do not make people buy back ordinary privacy from tracking-heavy access arrangements.** Paid services and bounded advertising can be legitimate, but righteousness is heavily burdened when behavioural-tracking consent or a fee paid to avoid that tracking does the real work of access rather than leaving people a genuinely equivalent, less-intrusive, and nonpunitive alternative.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S522][S523][S524][S525]
