# Episodic Acts, Settled Standing, and Trajectory

## Why this surface now

The archive has been saying from the start that righteousness is **steadfast**. But it still lacked a compact temporal rule.

Without such a rule, two opposite errors stay too easy:
- treating one bright act as proof that a person, office, or institution is righteous full stop;
- treating one grave failure as proof of permanent unrighteous essence, as if no distinction remained between the tainted episode, the present standing, and a possible trajectory of repair.

The archive now rejects both errors.

## Working-canon thesis

> **Righteousness should be judged at three temporal levels: episode, standing, and trajectory. A single act may be righteous without establishing settled righteous standing; damaged standing may improve without retroactively purifying the earlier act; and public claims to righteousness become stronger only where trustworthy performance is carried across time under review.**[S03][S26][S30][S32][S33][S39]

This is a temporal discipline, not a new moral substance. It tells the archive how to speak more honestly about time.

## 1. Episode is not standing

An **episode verdict** asks whether a particular act, decision, or omission rendered the due in that case.

A **standing verdict** asks whether an agent, office, or institution is presently well-placed to claim righteousness more broadly.

Those questions are related but not identical.

A person may do one thing that is righteous in the episode while still lacking settled righteous standing because the wider pattern remains captured, hypocritical, or review-blocking. Conversely, a person or institution with a generally strong record may commit an unrighteous act that should not be hidden inside prior credit.[S03][S30]

So the archive now rejects **episode inflation**: do not infer enduring righteous standing from one right move.

## 2. Standing is prospective and trust-facing

Standing matters because righteousness is not only retrospective praise. It is also prospective answerability.

This is clearest in office. Public roles ask others to become vulnerable to one's judgment, competence, discretion, and restraint. The trust literature is useful here: trustworthiness requires at least competence and willingness, not only good-sounding motive.[S39]

So a serious standing claim normally needs more than one clean episode. It needs enough durable performance, self-application, and review-capable conduct that others are not simply being asked to gamble on private sincerity.

That is why recusal, disqualification, preserved record, and oversight matter so much in compromised offices. They are not merely symbolic. They acknowledge that righteous standing is partly about whether others may reasonably rely on you now.[S32]

## 3. Trajectory is how requalification becomes real

The archive already states that requalification cannot be instant. This surface now clarifies the temporal form of that claim.

A **trajectory verdict** asks whether the pattern is bending toward or away from righteous standing.

After exposed failure, a credible improving trajectory normally includes:
- avowal rather than excuse-management;
- interruption of the corrupted posture;
- reparative action where due;
- role limits, recusal, or restructuring where trust is not yet warranted;
- repeated, non-laundered performance over time.[S26][S32][S33][S39]

This is why apology without changed pattern remains thin. The point is not merely that words are insufficient; it is that time is part of the evidence.

## 4. More exact verdicts

This surface widens the archive's verdict vocabulary again.

More exact judgments now include:
- **episodically righteous, standing-limited**;
- **substantively correct this time, but not yet trust-restoring**;
- **historically damaged, trajectory-improving**;
- **one bright act inside an otherwise unrighteous pattern**;
- **reparatively serious, but still under temporal probation**.

These verdicts are often more truthful than a flat yes-or-no use of *righteous*.

## 5. Office-facing note

For office and institutions, temporal discipline is stricter.

An office may do the right thing in one decision and still not yet be a righteous office if the larger pattern remains captured, opaque, retaliatory, or structurally unfair. Likewise, reform cannot be inferred from a single apology, single disclosure, or single well-publicized act.

Where public trust has been damaged, righteous trajectory normally requires not just better intent but repeatable safeguards, observed compliance, and enough time for affected parties to test whether the new pattern is real.[S19][S32][S34][S36][S39]

## 6. What this does not settle

This surface does **not** settle the exact evidentiary horizon. The archive still lacks a role-sensitive answer to questions like:
- how many episodes count as a pattern;
- how much time is enough for standing to be re-earned;
- when one grave act should outweigh a long clean record;
- how institutional reforms should be timed and verified.

Those remain live.

## Compressed rule

> **Do not infer settled righteousness from one act, and do not infer permanent unrighteousness from one failure. Judge episodes, present standing, and lived trajectory separately.**
