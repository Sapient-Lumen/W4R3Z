# Business, Privatization, and No-Rights-by-Outsourcing

## Why this surface matters

The archive already rejects delegation as a moral solvent, public-trust privatization, no-office-by-sale, no-rights-by-austerity, no-rights-without-redress, and no-rightlessness-by-model. What it still lacked was a compact rule for **what happens when essential services, protections, or rights-bearing conditions are governed through contractors, concessionaires, platforms, school chains, landlords, insurers, utilities, franchisors, or supply chains while each actor treats the decisive burden as somebody else's problem**.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S43][S44][S45][S46][S49][S66][S67][S87][S93][S145][S146][S147][S164][S294][S325][S326][S327][S328][S329]

That gap matters because outsourcing is one of the cleanest laundering surfaces in modern governance. Offices say the vendor failed, the platform moderated, the landlord priced, the contractor cut corners, the subsidiary handled the site, the supplier hired the labour, or the concessionaire operated the service. Lead firms say the state set the rules; states say the market delivered the outcome. But if safe water, housing, care, work, schooling, identity, movement, speech, or ordinary safety are being shaped through privatized chains that make oversight thinner and remedy harder, the archive needs a cleaner name for the wrong. Righteousness is not preserved merely because the burden travelled through contract form, corporate layering, or market language before it landed on the person who had to live with it.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S43][S44][S45][S46][S49][S66][S67][S87][S93][S145][S146][S147][S164][S294][S325][S326][S327][S328][S329]

## Working-canon thesis

> **Righteousness is burdened, and often defeated, when a State, office, lead enterprise, or institutional actor governs essential services, coercive functions, public infrastructures, or other rights-bearing conditions through privatization, contracting, concessions, platforms, supply chains, or layered business relationships in ways that diffuse oversight, due diligence, public reason, and remedy while leaving affected people to absorb the burden. Private delivery, procurement, commercial partnership, and multi-layered production are not automatically unrighteous. But once contract form, ownership distance, or vendorization is doing the practical work of making housing, water, food, care, work, schooling, data systems, movement, or ordinary safety harder to protect and harder to repair, righteousness fails unless strong oversight, contract discipline, due diligence, contestability, and real remedy remain live across the chain.**[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S43][S44][S45][S46][S49][S66][S67][S87][S93][S145][S146][S147][S164][S294][S325][S326][S327][S328][S329]

## 1. States do not shed duties by privatizing delivery

The central moral clarification is now direct rather than inferential. OHCHR's current business-and-human-rights materials say States must protect against human-rights abuse by business enterprises, that all business enterprises are expected to respect human rights, and that victims must have access to effective remedy.[S325] The UN Guiding Principles remain the globally endorsed baseline for this field.[S325][S326] The original report matters especially because it states the point that many governance systems still try to evade: **States do not relinquish their international human-rights-law obligations when they privatize the delivery of services that may impact the enjoyment of human rights**; relevant contracts and enabling legislation should clarify human-rights expectations, and States should ensure effective oversight, including independent monitoring and accountability mechanisms.[S327]

That is morally revealing for this archive because it blocks a common innocence story. A government does not become less responsible for rights-bearing burdens merely because the school is chartered, the prison privately run, the welfare portal vendor-built, the water system concessioned, the housing stock financialized, or the care home outsourced. The form can change. The duty cannot simply evaporate into the form.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S43][S44][S45][S46][S49][S66][S67][S93][S145][S146][S147][S164][S294][S325][S326][S327][S328][S329]

## 2. Business respect duties run through relationships, not only direct acts

The archive also needed the enterprise-facing half of the rule. The current OHCHR overview page says business enterprises are expected to respect human rights and to address adverse human-rights impacts with which they are involved.[S325] The due-diligence materials sharpen what that means in practice: enterprises are expected to identify and assess impacts they may cause or contribute to through their own activities, and also impacts directly linked to their operations, products, or services by their business relationships; then they must integrate findings, act, track effectiveness, and communicate how impacts are being addressed.[S87] The point is not merely that firms should be nice. It is that they should not treat supplier distance, subsidiary structure, franchise form, or service-contract layering as a solvent for rights-bearing responsibility.[S43][S87][S326]

That matters because many of the archive's most serious surfaces are now often mediated through business relationships rather than direct state action alone. Work can be governed through labour brokers and subcontractors, education through chains and public-private partnerships, water through utilities and concessions, care through private providers and insurers, identity and welfare through digital vendors, housing through landlords and financial intermediaries, and public voice through platforms and infrastructure providers. The archive needed a way to say that relationship distance can change the mode of duty without nullifying the duty itself.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S43][S44][S45][S46][S49][S66][S67][S87][S93][S145][S146][S147][S164][S294][S325][S326]

## 3. The State-business nexus is a morally load-bearing site

The Working Group's current page on **the State as an economic actor** is useful because it names the zones where public power and business structure meet: State-owned enterprises, export credit, investment insurance, and public procurement, all under a broader demand for policy coherence across departments that shape business practice.[S328] The original Guiding Principles report sharpens the same point by saying States should exercise adequate oversight when they contract with, or legislate for, business enterprises that provide services affecting human rights, and that States have unique opportunities through procurement to promote awareness of and respect for human rights by enterprises with which they conduct commercial transactions.[S327]

This gives the archive a portable way to read a wide range of cases that otherwise look unrelated. A procurement decision is not just a purchasing event if it structures who builds the shelter, who runs the detention facility, who scores the welfare claim, who moderates the civic platform, who supplies the hospital, or who manages the utility. A concession is not just a commercial technique if it decides whose water gets shut off, whose home gets cleared, whose labour becomes cheapest to ignore, or whose child is educated under extractive rather than rights-bearing conditions. Outsourcing can therefore be a genuine division of labour; but it can also be a device for thinning public reason while thickening private leverage.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S43][S44][S45][S46][S49][S66][S67][S93][S145][S146][S147][S164][S294][S325][S326][S327][S328]

## 4. Remedy has to travel through the chain

The archive also needed a sharper rule about remedy in multi-actor settings. OHCHR's current business-and-human-rights materials say that if abuses occur, victims must have access to effective remedy through judicial and non-judicial grievance mechanisms.[S325] The Working Group's access-to-remedy page adds that Guiding Principle 1 requires States to prevent, investigate, punish, and redress business-related human-rights abuse; Guiding Principle 22 requires businesses that caused or contributed to adverse impacts to provide for or cooperate in remediation through legitimate processes; and Guiding Principle 25 requires States to ensure that those affected have access to effective remedy. It also distinguishes three mechanism types: state-based judicial mechanisms, state-based non-judicial mechanisms, and non-state-based grievance mechanisms.[S294]

That is morally clarifying for the archive because outsourced wrongs often become everyone's responsibility in theory and no one's remedy in practice. The call centre sends the person to the agency; the agency sends them to the vendor; the lead firm points to the supplier code; the supplier says it complied with the contract; the grievance portal records the complaint but cannot change the outcome; the parent company says the subsidiary is separate; the state says the business decision was private. No-rights-by-outsourcing names the moment when diffusion of actors becomes diffusion of remedy.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S43][S44][S45][S46][S49][S66][S67][S93][S145][S146][S147][S164][S294][S325][S327]

## 5. This is not a ban on all private provision

The archive needed a narrow rule here. Not every contract, concession, supplier chain, or partnership defeats righteousness. States can purchase services, regulate markets, and sometimes rely on private provision. Businesses can supply goods, build systems, hire subcontractors, and operate at scale. General Comment No. 24 is useful because it does not collapse the whole field into one blanket prohibition; instead it says action plans and governance in this area should incorporate meaningful participation, non-discrimination, gender equality, accountability, and transparency.[S329] The current OHCHR overview and Working Group pages likewise emphasize standards, due diligence, policy coherence, and remedy rather than simple anti-business rhetoric.[S325][S328]

So the archive does **not** say that every privatization or business relationship is automatically unrighteous. It says the justificatory burden becomes steep once contract form, concession design, supplier layering, or corporate structure is doing the practical work of making a rights-bearing service or protection less supervised, less contestable, or less repairable. The relevant question is not public-versus-private in the abstract. It is whether the people at the burdened end still have identifiable protectors, reachable reasons, live oversight, and a real path to correction when things go wrong.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S43][S44][S45][S46][S49][S66][S67][S87][S93][S145][S146][S147][S164][S294][S325][S326][S327][S328][S329]

## 6. Working rule

When an actor claims righteousness while service delivery, infrastructure, or governance runs through private actors, ask seven questions:
1. **What rights-bearing thing was actually at stake?** Water, housing, food, care, work, schooling, movement, public voice, identity, safety, or another standing-bearing condition?[S164][S294][S325][S327]
2. **Which relationship did the real burden work?** Procurement, concession, platform governance, landlord structure, insurer gatekeeping, school chain, labour broker, supplier chain, parent-subsidiary separation, or contractor hierarchy?[S43][S44][S45][S46][S87][S327][S328]
3. **Who still had leverage?** The ministry, municipality, regulator, parent company, lead firm, public buyer, State-owned enterprise, lender, or platform owner may still have had power to change the conditions.[S327][S328]
4. **What due diligence existed?** Were impacts assessed, integrated into decisions, tracked, and communicated in a way affected people could actually test?[S87][S325][S326]
5. **What oversight remained live?** Contract terms, audits, inspections, regulatory supervision, public reporting, independent monitoring, and complaint routes should still have been visible and usable.[S327][S328][S329]
6. **What remedy remained live?** Could affected people reach a mechanism with power to correct, restore, compensate, or stop the harm before it hardened?[S294][S325]
7. **Was market form doing the moral laundering?** If every actor could point elsewhere while the burdened person still had to live through the unsafe house, missing medicine, exploitative job, denied service, broken system, or contaminated utility, the righteousness claim is presumptively corrupted.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S43][S44][S45][S46][S49][S66][S67][S87][S93][S145][S146][S147][S164][S294][S325][S326][S327][S328][S329]

## Compression sentence

> **Do not let contract form, supplier distance, or privatized delivery dissolve the due: where services or protections run through private actors, righteousness requires owned oversight, live due diligence, and reachable remedy across the chain rather than rights lost in the gap between contractor and office.**[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S43][S44][S45][S46][S49][S66][S67][S87][S93][S145][S146][S147][S164][S294][S325][S326][S327][S328][S329]
