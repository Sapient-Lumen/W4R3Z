# Permits, licenses, and no-rights-by-permission

## Core claim

> **Righteousness is burdened, and often defeated, when permits, licenses, prior authorization, registration status, or comparable approval systems make official permission do the real work of deciding whether people may assemble, associate, organize, speak, worship, move, work, or otherwise exercise standing-bearing rights, rather than using narrowly bounded coordination, safety, or legal-personality tools that remain reviewable, non-discriminatory, and subordinate to the right itself.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S217][S421][S422][S423][S424]

## 1. The missing moral question is when administration turns a right into a favor

The archive already had sectoral surfaces on peaceful assembly, freedom of association, freedom of expression, freedom of thought and religion, movement, corruption, and rightlessness by paper. But it still lacked a direct rule for an earlier and more portable institutional move: **treating a right as something one may exercise only after the authority says yes**. That gap matters because offices often describe permits, licenses, registrations, approvals, and prior authorization as if they were morally small matters of coordination. The current official materials show why that description is often false. ICCPR articles 21 and 22 recognize assembly and association as rights and allow restrictions only when they satisfy the familiar steep tests of legality, necessity, and democratic justification.[S93] General comment No. 37 adds that assemblies are presumed protected, that notification may sometimes be used, and that failure to notify does not itself make participation unlawful.[S217]

That matters for righteousness because permission systems can quietly reverse the moral grammar of public life. Instead of the institution bearing the burden to justify a restriction on a right, the burden shifts to the person to secure indulgence before acting. Once that reversal becomes ordinary, office is no longer merely coordinating. It is allocating standing by approval.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S217][S421][S422][S423][S424]

## 2. Assembly materials make the inversion easiest to see

The assembly sources are especially revealing because they say the point unusually plainly. The 2012 Special Rapporteur report says the organization of a protest should not be subject to prior authorization from administrative authorities, but at most to prior notification.[S421] The 2012 statement repeats the same rule and adds that spontaneous assemblies should be recognized in law and exempt from notification.[S422] The 2015 Chile statement sharpens the moral diagnosis: requiring authorization turns the right to freedom of peaceful assembly into a privilege.[S423]

The archive therefore needed a more direct surface. A permit or authorization requirement is not automatically trivial just because it arrives before the event rather than after it. If the system lets officials deny, narrow, postpone, or dissolve collective action because the right was first converted into an application, then the office may be claiming to regulate assembly while actually deciding whose public presence must ask leave to exist.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S217][S421][S423]

## 3. Association materials show that legal personality may be optional while freedom remains primary

Association materials sharpen a second distinction the archive needed. The 2013 Special Rapporteur report says the formation of associations should not be subject to prior authorization but rather to a notification system that is simple, easily accessible, non-discriminatory, and non-onerous or free of charge.[S422] The 2024 Joint Declaration goes further: associations, both registered and unregistered, may seek and use resources without prior authorization or undue impediments; states should guarantee a simple and accessible route to legal personality; and states should not compel associations to register in order to exist and operate freely.[S424]

That matters because offices often defend registration and licensing regimes by blending two very different questions. One question is whether a body wants the additional incidents of legal personality, tax status, or formal representation. Another is whether people may exist, meet, organize, and act together at all. Righteousness fails when those questions are fused so that the State’s decision to recognize a legal shell becomes the gate through which collective life itself must pass.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S422][S424]

## 4. The difference between facilitation and permission is morally load-bearing

The archive therefore now distinguishes **facilitative administration** from **rights-by-permission governance**. Facilitative administration may ask for bounded information so that authorities can protect participants, allocate limited technical infrastructure, prevent concrete collisions of use, or furnish a legal personality that some groups may want.[S217][S421][S422][S424] Rights-by-permission governance is different. There the form, permit, license, or authorization becomes the practical source of the right, and lack of approval becomes the ordinary reason why assembly, association, organizing, or another standing-bearing activity may be interrupted, dissolved, or penalized.

This is morally load-bearing because the same paperwork can play two different roles. A notification system that helps an authority route traffic, protect marchers, or arrange a venue is not equivalent to a discretionary approval system that lets officials decide whether the event may happen at all. A registration route that allows access to legal personality is not equivalent to a system that treats unregistered organization as presumptively illicit. The archive now needs to name that difference directly because institutions routinely hide domination inside procedural form.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S217][S421][S422][S423][S424]

## 5. Permission systems are especially dangerous where discretion, stigma, or dependency already run high

The 2024 Joint Declaration is useful here because it says restrictions on association must be case-specific, evidence-based, non-discriminatory, and justified by real rather than hypothetical risk, while the least intrusive means should be prioritized and independent review should remain available.[S424] That is morally revealing for the archive because permission systems are most dangerous where vague risk language, ideological suspicion, or dependency already define who is easy to delay or refuse. Once approval is needed first, the stronger party acquires a low-visibility power to thin civic space, force organizational domestication, or make ordinary participation costly for the unfavored while still claiming everything happened “according to procedure.”[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S423][S424]

So the archive’s new rule is **not** that every permit, license, or registration mechanism is forbidden. It is that righteousness is heavily burdened once permission rather than right becomes the operative baseline. Where an office cannot explain why a narrower notice, safety, recordkeeping, or legal-personality route would not suffice, and where non-approval predictably disables ordinary civic exercise, the system is presumptively drifting into no-rights-by-permission.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S217][S421][S422][S423][S424]

## 6. Working rule

When an actor claims righteousness while relying on permits, licenses, prior authorization, or registration gates, ask seven questions:
1. **What standing-bearing activity was being gated?** Assembly, association, organizing, speech, worship, movement, work, service access, or something else?[S93][S217][S421][S422][S424]
2. **What was the administrative tool actually for?** Coordination, safety, legal personality, scarce technical allocation, or discretionary control over whether the activity could happen at all?[S217][S421][S422][S424]
3. **Could the activity exist without prior approval?** Were unregistered or unnotified forms still protected, or did lack of permission itself become the practical wrong-trigger?[S217][S421][S422][S423][S424]
4. **How discretionary was the gate?** Clear criteria, narrow timelines, reasons, appeal, and review, or broad official latitude to delay, deny, dissolve, or stigmatize?[S34][S35][S41][S45][S49][S421][S423][S424]
5. **Who predictably failed the gate?** Minorities, dissidents, migrants, poorer groups, informal workers, new associations, or groups already marked as inconvenient or suspect?[S41][S45][S47][S49][S424]
6. **What lesser route was available?** Notification, ex post accountability for concrete harms, safety planning, neutral infrastructure rules, or optional legal-personality registration rather than permission to exist?[S217][S421][S422][S424]
7. **What happened after refusal or noncompliance?** Fines, dispersal, deregistration, stigma, funding exclusion, police action, or effective civil death for the activity at issue?[S34][S35][S41][S45][S49][S423][S424]

## Compression sentence

> **Do not let permission become the source of a right: where permits, licenses, prior authorization, or registration status do the real work of deciding whether people may assemble, organize, or otherwise exercise standing-bearing freedoms, righteousness fails unless the gate is genuinely narrow, facilitative rather than discretionary, non-discriminatory, reviewable, and clearly subordinate to the right it claims only to administer.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S217][S421][S422][S423][S424]
