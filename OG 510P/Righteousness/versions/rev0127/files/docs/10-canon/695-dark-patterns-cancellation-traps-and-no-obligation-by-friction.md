# Dark patterns, cancellation traps, and no-obligation-by-friction

> **Righteousness is burdened, and often defeated, when public or private actors make signup, assent, disclosure, or payment easy while making refusal, comparison, privacy protection, or cancellation materially harder, so that interface friction does the real work of manufacturing continued obligation, data surrender, or recurring extraction rather than leaving consent, continuation, and exit meaningfully symmetric, intelligible, and reachable.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S460][S461][S462][S463]

## 1. The missing moral question is when obligation is being sustained by burden asymmetry rather than by fair reason

The archive already rejected nondeception failures, burdened consent, no-agency-by-stealth, and no-attention-by-hook. But it still lacked a direct rule for a narrower and highly reusable defect: **the stronger actor does not need to lie outright or keep someone endlessly returning if it can instead make entry simple and reversal costly.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S460][S461][S462][S463]

That gap matters because many systems are defended by pointing to a technically available choice. The consumer could find the cancel page, the worker could navigate the portal, the user could untick the box, the customer could compare plans, the parent could locate the privacy controls, the patient could withdraw, the tenant could dispute the fee, the member could stop renewal. But righteousness has to ask whether those possibilities were real enough to do legitimating work, or whether they mainly functioned as a surface story covering a path intentionally built to be harder to traverse than the path into commitment.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S460][S461][S462][S463]

So the archive needs a surface that is not reducible either to generic deception or to compulsion-loop design. Deception can happen in one message; compulsion engineering can work through repeated return over time. **No-obligation-by-friction asks a different question: when is an ongoing obligation, waiver, payment stream, or data exposure being kept alive because the interface itself makes reversal or refusal materially harder than assent?**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S460][S461][S462][S463]

## 2. Current official materials now name this family clearly enough to justify canon admission

The Digital Services Act is morally revealing here because current Commission materials say dark patterns such as aggressive pop-ups and confusing or misleading consent buttons are prohibited.[S460] That matters because the DSA does not treat manipulative interface structure as an ordinary market flourish. It treats certain choice architectures as governance defects in their own right.[S460]

FTC materials sharpen the point from another angle. The Commission's current click-to-cancel materials say sellers must make cancellation as easy as enrollment, must not misrepresent material facts, and must disclose material terms before obtaining billing information and charging consumers.[S461] That is a powerful compact moral lesson for the archive: a commitment path that is easier to enter than to leave is not automatically righteous merely because the downstream charges follow the formal terms.[S461]

OECD materials widen the family beyond one statute by saying dark commercial patterns subvert decision-making through the way choices are presented and can include forcing personal-information disclosure and making it difficult to cancel services.[S462] CFPB materials complement that by saying negative-option practices can violate the law where sellers fail to clearly disclose material terms, fail to obtain informed consent, or erect unreasonable barriers to cancellation.[S463] Together these materials show that this is no longer just scattered user-experience criticism. It is a live governance seam about how design can manufacture practical obligation.[S460][S461][S462][S463]

## 3. The righteousness issue is not annoyance; it is manufactured continuation

Righteousness should not treat every extra click, every reminder screen, or every continuity mechanism as morally decisive. Some friction is legitimate: fraud checks, cooling-off clarifications, confirmation for destructive actions, accessibility options, child-safety safeguards, anti-impersonation checks, or steps genuinely needed to prevent accidental cancellation or harmful interruption. The narrower question is what the friction is *for*.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S460][S461][S462][S463]

Where the design preserves clear terms, symmetry between joining and leaving, reachable privacy controls, realistic comparison, and a short path to reversal, bounded continuity support may remain righteous. But where the service gains its practical advantage by hiding terms until late, preselecting the profitable path, obstructing refusal, forcing repeated screens, requiring unnecessary disclosure, shunting people through maze-like settings, or making cancellation substantially harder than signup, the actor is no longer mainly relying on fair uptake. It is governing through no-obligation-by-friction.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S460][S461][S462][S463]

This matters well beyond retail subscriptions. Employment portals, school platforms, public-benefit applications, landlord or utility dashboards, insurance systems, health apps, donation flows, privacy notices, and civic participation tools can all be arranged so that the weaker party formally retains choice while practically bearing the heavier navigational burden. In those cases the moral defect is not only bad usability. It is a standing defect because the actor has made burden asymmetry part of the title by which it keeps money, data, waiver, or compliance flowing.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S460][S461][S462][S463]

## 4. Questions righteous judgment should now ask

When an actor claims righteousness while relying on continuity flows, consent interfaces, privacy settings, or cancellation design, ask seven questions:

1. **What was being kept alive?** A subscription, recurring charge, consent flag, data flow, waiver, obligation, or dependence relation?[S460][S461][S462][S463]
2. **Was assent materially easier than refusal or exit?** Was signup one step while cancellation, privacy protection, or comparison took many more or required navigation through confusing routes?[S460][S461][S462][S463]
3. **What terms were disclosed when they mattered?** Were charges, renewal logic, deadlines, data consequences, and cancellation terms clear before commitment?[S461][S463]
4. **What burden sat on the weaker party?** Time, confusion, disclosure, emotional exhaustion, literacy demands, device access, language barriers, disability barriers, or child-specific vulnerability?[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S462][S463]
5. **Was the friction genuinely protective or mainly extractive?** Did it prevent accidental harm, fraud, or impersonation, or mainly keep payment, waiver, or data exposure in place?[S460][S461][S462][S463]
6. **What narrower route could have served the legitimate aim?** Could the actor have used clear disclosure, one-step cancellation, balanced defaults, or a short confirmation rather than a maze?[S460][S461][S462][S463]
7. **Was friction doing the real work?** If the design had made exit, refusal, privacy protection, or comparison as reachable as entry, would the actor still have secured the same commitment?[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S460][S461][S462][S463]

## 5. Canonical compression

> **Do not let formal click-through, buried settings, or technically available cancellation masquerade as righteous consent or fair continuation: where interface friction does the real work of keeping a person subscribed, paying, disclosed, waived, or locked in, righteousness fails unless assent and exit remain meaningfully symmetric, intelligible, reachable, and reviewable.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S460][S461][S462][S463]
