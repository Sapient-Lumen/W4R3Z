# Peaceful Assembly, Protest, and No-Order-by-Suppression

## Why this surface matters

The archive already rejects arbitrary detention, lethal-force-by-convenience, conviction-by-opacity, reprisal, and surveillance overreach. What it still lacked was a compact rule for **what happens when power treats public dissent, gathering, marching, vigiling, striking, or visible collective presence as a problem to be neutralized rather than a claim-bearing practice to be facilitated and protected**: permit systems used to suppress criticism, blanket protest bans justified by vague order language, aggressive dispersal of non-violent crowds, mass arrest for administrative ease, internet or route control used to dissolve assembly capacity, or crowd-management that quietly treats visibility itself as the threat.[S34][S35][S41][S93][S202][S217][S218]

That missing rule matters because Article 21 does not describe peaceful assembly as a favor. It recognizes the right and permits restrictions only when they are lawful and necessary in a democratic society for the listed ends.[S93] General Comment No. 37 sharpens the point by requiring States to allow assemblies to take place without unwarranted interference, to facilitate them, and to protect participants; by presuming assemblies peaceful; by protecting spontaneous and even remote or online participation; and by treating blanket restrictions as presumptively disproportionate.[S217] OHCHR's current topic guidance compresses the same moral structure by treating peaceful assembly and association as essential components of democracy and by explicitly including meetings, sit-ins, strikes, rallies, events, and protests, both offline and online.[S218] So the archive should now treat suppression of peaceful assembly as a distinct standing defect: **public order may sometimes justify narrow limits, but it may not become a standing excuse to erase dissent, visibility, or collective claim-making**.[S93][S217][S218]

## Working-canon thesis

> **Righteousness is burdened, and often defeated, when a person, office, or institution suppresses peaceful assembly mainly to avoid criticism, disperse visibility, punish dissent, simplify control, or protect official comfort. Peaceful assembly may be regulated for lawful, necessary, and proportionate reasons tied to public safety, the rights of others, and comparable protected interests, but the burden stays steep: assemblies are presumptively peaceful, unauthorized or spontaneous gatherings remain protected if non-violent, facilitation and protection duties remain live, and force or dispersal must stay exceptional, minimized, and answerable. Protest management that turns inconvenience, embarrassment, or oppositional speech into grounds for mass force, mass arrest, blanket prohibition, or digital disablement is not righteous order but order-through-suppression.**[S93][S202][S217][S218]

## 1. Public order is not a permission slip to erase dissent

The archive already knew that emergency, security, and administrative convenience can launder coercion. Assembly materials sharpen that lesson for public gathering. Article 21 permits restrictions only when lawful and necessary in a democratic society for specified grounds such as national security, public safety, public order, public health or morals, or the rights and freedoms of others.[S93] General Comment No. 37 adds that restrictions must be narrowly drawn, necessary and proportionate, and the least intrusive measures capable of serving the relevant protective function; blanket restrictions on peaceful assemblies are presumptively disproportionate.[S217]

That matters because institutions often redescribe protest as a generic threat to order: traffic disruption, noise, reputational embarrassment, administrative burden, possible future disorder, or discomfort with oppositional speech. But GC37 is morally useful precisely because it refuses to let a vague idea of order do all the work. Public order must itself be understood consistently with human rights, and peaceful assemblies can be inherently or even deliberately disruptive without thereby losing protection.[S217] So the archive should now ask a sharper question whenever officials cite order: **what concrete protected interest was at stake, and why could it not be served through a narrower, assembly-preserving response?** Where inconvenience or criticism is doing the real work, righteousness is already drifting toward suppression.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S93][S217]

## 2. Peaceful does not mean permitted, silent, or frictionless

General Comment No. 37 matters because it protects more than choreographed, pre-approved civic ritual. Spontaneous assemblies are protected; remote and online participation can fall within article 21; and failure to comply with domestic legal requirements does not by itself place participants outside the protection of the right if the assembly remains non-violent.[S217] It also says that pushing, shoving, or disruption of vehicular or pedestrian movement or daily activities do not by themselves amount to violence.[S217]

That helps the archive resist a familiar laundering move. Authorities often treat lack of authorization, route deviation, blocked traffic, or loud oppositional presence as proof that a gathering has ceased to be peaceable in the morally relevant sense. But that collapses friction into violence and bureaucracy into righteousness. The portable point is narrower: non-violent assemblies do not lose protection merely because they are uncomfortable, inconvenient, unauthorized, or difficult to manage.[S93][S217][S218]

## 3. States owe facilitation and protection, not merely abstention

The archive already learned from hearing and accommodation surfaces that institutions can wrong people by designing the channel badly, not only by openly prohibiting entry. Assembly materials make the same point for public gathering. General Comment No. 37 states that States have positive duties to facilitate peaceful assemblies and make it possible for participants to achieve their objectives, sometimes by blocking streets, redirecting traffic, or providing security, and must protect participants against abuse by non-State actors and counterdemonstrators.[S217] OHCHR's current topic page likewise frames protest management around facilitation, protection, and dialogue rather than mere permission to intervene.[S218]

So the archive should now reject a recurrent office temptation: claiming neutrality while designing the ground so that disfavored assemblies predictably fail. If a city forces protest into invisible zones, withholds safety planning, declines to protect participants from violent opposition, or structures the permit system so that only bland or friendly assemblies can comply, the defect is not cured by the absence of a formal ban. Public power may be suppressing assembly by under-facilitating it.[S19][S32][S34][S35][S217][S218]

## 4. Force and dispersal are last-resort tools, not crowd-management defaults

The archive already treated lethal force as exceptional. Protest settings sharpen why that exceptionality must remain live even under disorder. The Basic Principles on the Use of Force and Firearms require law-enforcement officials to apply non-violent means as far as possible before resorting to force; where force is unavoidable, they must exercise restraint, minimize injury, preserve life, provide medical aid, and report the incident. For unlawful but non-violent assemblies, force should be avoided or restricted to the minimum extent necessary. For violent assemblies, firearms remain tightly limited and may not be used except under the strict life-protective conditions already governing lethal force generally.[S202]

This matters because crowd control is one of the places where institutions most readily convert urgency into permission. Mass arrest becomes easier than differentiated response. Kettling becomes easier than safe routing. Chemical agents or impact projectiles become easier than patience and dialogue. Live fire becomes thinkable because the crowd is described as a single threat rather than as many persons with different conduct. The archive should now resist that compression. Protest management that reaches first for pain, panic, sweep, or lethal risk is often not preserving order under burden but governing through suppression by force.[S34][S35][S41][S93][S202][S217]

## 5. Some violence does not dissolve everyone else's protection

General Comment No. 37 is morally revealing here because it refuses guilt-by-crowd. There is a presumption in favour of considering assemblies peaceful, isolated acts of violence by some participants should not be attributed to others, and violence against participants by authorities, counterdemonstrators, or agents provocateurs does not render the assembly non-peaceful.[S217] It also protects monitors and journalists or others observing or reporting on assemblies against undue limitation, reprisals, or confiscation of equipment.[S217]

That matters because institutions often defend indiscriminate crowd burdens by narrating the entire gathering as contaminated: a few threw objects, some lane was blocked, one faction anticipated disorder, police intelligence predicted escalation, or counter-protesters attacked. But where the response ceases to distinguish organizers, peaceful participants, observers, medics, legal monitors, and genuinely violent actors, the state is often no longer tracing answerability. It is replacing differentiated public order with collective suppression.[S19][S32][S34][S35][S217]

## 6. Working rule

When a protest, march, rally, vigil, strike, sit-in, encampment, counterdemonstration, or online assembly is restricted or dispersed under pressure, ask six questions:
1. **What concrete interest justified restriction?** Was it real public safety, rights of others, and a pressing social need, or mainly embarrassment, criticism, administrative inconvenience, or anticipated opposition?[S93][S217]
2. **Was the assembly actually non-violent?** Did authorities distinguish disruption, noise, route conflict, or unauthorized status from violence in the stricter sense?[S217]
3. **What facilitation burden was carried first?** Traffic redirection, communication, negotiated route, separation from hostile countergroups, medical access, and space management matter before prohibition or force.[S217][S218]
4. **What force logic was used?** Were non-violent means tried first, was force minimized, and were firearms excluded except under the strict life-protective conditions that apply generally?[S202]
5. **Who was treated as answerable?** Did isolated violence by some swallow protection for peaceful participants, journalists, medics, monitors, and bystanders?[S217]
6. **What review stayed live afterward?** Reporting, preserved evidence, complaint channels, medical aid, and later challenge matter because suppression is often laundered after the street is cleared.[S34][S35][S202][S217]

## Compact maxim

> **Do not govern by suppressing peaceful assembly.** Order may justify narrow, lawful, and proportionate limits, but not the conversion of public gathering, protest, or visible dissent into a target for blanket prohibition, indiscriminate crowd burden, or force-first management.[S93][S202][S217][S218]
