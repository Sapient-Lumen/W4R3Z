# Avowal, Requalification, and No Instant Purity

## Why this surface now

The archive now has a defeater model. What it still lacked was a compact answer to the next question:

> **if a righteousness claim is defeated, can standing ever be regained — and if so, how?**

Without a rule here, the archive risks two further mistakes:
- treating defeaters as permanent identity verdicts with no path back; or
- letting apology, confession, or rhetorical humility instantly wash a defect into renewed righteousness.

The current archive rejects both.

## Working-canon thesis

> **Some standing defeaters block the present righteousness claim without fixing agents or institutions in permanent unrighteousness. But requalification, where possible, requires costly self-implicating avowal, interruption of the conflicted posture, reparative action where due, and time-bound re-earning of standing. It never retroactively purifies the tainted act itself.**

This thesis narrows rather than softens rev0008. The point is not that defeaters were overstated. The point is that the archive must distinguish three questions:
1. **Was the act / office / institution righteous in that moment?**
2. **Can the actor now respond righteously to the exposure of the defect?**
3. **Can standing later be partially regained?**

The answers may be: **no**, then **yes**, then **sometimes**.

## 1. Requalification is not retroactive laundering

A defeated righteousness claim does not become true just because the agent later confessed, resigned, repaired, or improved.

If an office was captured, a norm was hypocritically weaponized, or a necessary-but-tainted act was falsely purified, later honesty does not make the original act righteous after all. It may only mean that a new, more righteous response began after the exposure.

So the archive now adopts a strict anti-laundering rule:

> **later repentance can change present standing; it cannot rewrite the moral status of the earlier act.**

This matters especially in public life, where apology is often used as a recredentialing device rather than as an avowal of disqualification.[S19][S26][S30]

## 2. Four gates of requalification

The archive now treats requalification as gated. When a standing defeater has been exposed, the path back — if there is one — normally requires all four of the following.

### A. Avowal

The defect must be named in first-person or first-institution terms. Not merely “mistakes were made,” but some form of:
- what was done,
- what norm it violated,
- who was burdened,
- and how the speaker or institution stood inside the wrong.[S26][S33]

Atonement and reconciliation materials help here because they center acknowledgment, responsibility, and making amends rather than mere image repair.[S26][S33]

This revision adds a further gate on avowal itself: avowal must be free enough not to be manufactured by the stronger party. Once confession, apology, or recantation is extracted by custody, humiliative pressure, dependency, legal threat, or ideological scripting, its moral weight as proof of truth or restored standing is sharply burdened.[S91][S93][S127][S128][S129][S130][S131]

### B. Interruption of the corrupted position

The actor must stop claiming clean standing while the conflict or camouflage is still live. Depending on the case, this may require:
- recusal or disqualification,
- disclosure,
- divestment,
- relinquishment of decision power,
- reopening blocked review,
- or sunset / narrowing of emergency powers.[S19][S32]

For office, this is especially important. Federal ethics rules are explicit that impartiality concerns call for recusal from participation in particular matters, and that financial-conflict rules likewise use disqualification as a central remedy.[S32]

### C. Repair where due

Standing is not restored by self-description alone. Where others were harmed, misled, excluded, dominated, or burdened, requalification requires reparative action proportionate to the case: apology, restitution, compensation, correction of record, institutional reform, or other forms of redress.[S26][S27][S33]

### D. Time-bound re-earning

Even after avowal, interruption, and repair, standing is not automatically back. The archive now treats righteous standing as something that may need to be re-earned over time through non-recurrence, constraint acceptance, and visible willingness to remain under review.[S19][S30]

This is why “I admitted it” is not yet enough.

## 3. The first righteous act may be recusal

For officeholders, the first genuinely righteous response to a standing defect may be **not to continue deciding well**, but to stop deciding while conflicted.

This is a useful correction to a familiar temptation. Captured or implicated officials often argue:
- that they can remain objective,
- that their expertise is indispensable,
- or that stepping aside would itself harm the public.

Sometimes that may be true as a matter of consequences. But the archive now states a stricter standing rule:

> **when impartial standing is itself compromised, persistence in power is usually not the righteous way to prove righteousness.**

The more appropriate act may be recusal, temporary withdrawal, delegated review, or exposure to independent scrutiny.[S18][S19][S32]

## 4. No instant purity through confession

This revision also adds a negative rule. Confession, apology, contrition, or repentance can be morally serious, but they do not instantly restore full righteous standing.

Why not?
- because the original defect may still be materially active;
- because harmed parties may still be carrying unrepaired costs;
- because institutions often use confession as a substitute for redistribution of burden;
- because confession itself may have been managed, extracted, or scripted under asymmetric pressure;
- and because standing depends partly on whether one accepts consequences, not only whether one narrates regret.[S26][S27][S30][S33][S91][S93][S127][S128]

So the archive now rejects **instant purity claims**.

## 5. What this changes in verdicts

This surface improves the archive's verdict vocabulary again.

When defeaters have been exposed, more exact verdicts now include:
- **unrighteous act followed by righteous avowal**;
- **captured office beginning a real requalification path**;
- **apology without requalification because conflict remains live**;
- **reparative seriousness without restored standing yet**;
- **tainted necessity followed by owned burden rather than purification**.

This lets the archive describe moral recovery without laundering the original defect.

## Compressed rule

> Defeat is not always permanent. But where requalification is possible, it runs through avowal, interruption, repair, and time — never through instant self-purification.
