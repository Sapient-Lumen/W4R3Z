# Freedom of Expression, Press, and No-Silencing-by-Power

## Why this surface matters

The archive already rejects cover-up, reprisal, conviction-by-opacity, surveillance overreach, and order-through-suppression. What it still lacked was a compact rule for **what happens when power treats criticism, reporting, witness-bearing, publication, or access to public-interest information as something to be thinned, delayed, intimidated, or monopolized rather than answered**: censorship justified by vague order language, licensing or accreditation used to tame criticism, information blackouts that make public challenge impossible, violence or impunity directed at journalists, or legal and administrative burdens designed less to protect rights than to make speaking and knowing too costly.[S34][S35][S93][S127][S219][S220][S221]

That missing rule matters because Article 19 does not protect only a ruler-approved zone of polite expression. It protects the freedom to hold opinions without interference and the freedom to seek, receive, and impart information and ideas through any media regardless of frontiers.[S93][S127] General Comment No. 34 sharpens the point by describing freedom of opinion and expression as indispensable conditions for the full development of the person and as foundation stones for every free and democratic society; it also treats transparency and accountability as dependent on expression and explicitly includes a right of access to information held by public bodies.[S219] OHCHR's current topic guidance compresses the same structure by tying freedom of expression to media freedom, journalist protection, and anti-incitement work that must not become a pretext for strangling speech.[S220] UNESCO's current journalist-safety work adds the practical lesson: attacks on journalists and impunity for those attacks do not merely harm a profession; they degrade the public's capacity to know, witness, and hold power to account.[S221] So the archive should now treat expression and press freedom as a distinct standing surface: **public order, reputation, security, and anti-hatred concerns can justify some narrow burdens, but they may not become a standing excuse to monopolize narration, punish criticism, or make public knowledge structurally unsafe**.[S93][S127][S219][S220][S221]

## Working-canon thesis

> **Righteousness is burdened, and often defeated, when a person, office, or institution suppresses criticism, reporting, witness-bearing, or access to public-interest information mainly to avoid scrutiny, monopolize narrative, chill dissent, or make answerability practically unusable. Freedom of opinion may not be interfered with, and freedom of expression includes seeking, receiving, and imparting information and ideas; regulation remains possible only under steep burdens of legality, necessity, proportionality, and rights-protective fit. Media freedom, journalist safety, and usable access to public information are therefore not side issues but part of whether power is willing to live under truthful public challenge.**[S93][S127][S219][S220][S221]

## 1. Opinion and criticism are not permissions granted by the powerful

General Comment No. 34 matters because it begins from a strong premise: freedom of opinion and freedom of expression are indispensable conditions for human development and for any free and democratic society, and freedom of opinion itself may not be interfered with.[S219] The ICCPR text likewise protects holding opinions without interference and protects expression through any media regardless of frontiers.[S93][S127]

That matters because institutions often treat criticism as a tolerated luxury whose scope contracts whenever authority feels threatened, embarrassed, or politically exposed. But once criticism is redescribed as disloyalty, reputational injury, instability, or disrespect merely because it is unwelcome, righteousness has already drifted. The archive should therefore treat public criticism not as an indulgence granted by confident power, but as one of the ordinary conditions under which power proves whether it can remain answerable while being opposed.[S18][S34][S35][S93][S127][S219]

## 2. Expression includes seeking and receiving information, not only speaking

Article 19 protects not only imparting information but also seeking and receiving it.[S93][S127] General Comment No. 34 sharpens that by treating access to information held by public bodies as part of the article 19 structure, covering records regardless of form, source, or date of production, while requiring active arrangements that make access effective in practice.[S219]

This matters because power can silence without always banning words. It can hide the file, delay the record, classify too much, refuse accreditation, close the hearing, cut the feed, block the witness, or price the information path so that only the already powerful can know enough to object. The portable point is narrower than total openness: some secrecy can be justified. But when knowledge needed for challenge, self-protection, or public judgment is withheld mainly to preserve official convenience, reputational insulation, or narrative monopoly, righteousness is usually not protecting a legitimate secret. It is governing by controlled ignorance.[S32][S34][S35][S93][S219]

## 3. Restrictions must answer a real protected interest through narrow means

The ICCPR permits certain restrictions on expression, but only when they are provided by law and necessary for the specified protected interests.[S93][S127] General Comment No. 34 is morally useful because it insists that restrictions satisfy strict tests of necessity and proportionality and that the relation between norm and purpose must not be remote or speculative.[S219] OHCHR's current topic page is equally useful because it frames anti-incitement work through the Rabat Plan of Action: harmful expression can justify response, but rights-protective response must not become a general permission slip for censorship.[S220]

So the archive should now ask a sharper question whenever speech, publication, teaching, art, reporting, or circulation of information is burdened: **what concrete protected interest was at stake, and why could it not be protected by a narrower response?** Where the real aim is to reduce criticism, avoid panic about official failure, suppress unwelcome narratives, or make public challenge harder to organize, the restriction is usually not righteous order but silencing by power.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S93][S219][S220]

## 4. Journalists and media workers bear a public-accountability burden that must be protected

OHCHR's current expression guidance explicitly links freedom of expression to media freedom and the rights of journalists and media workers.[S220] UNESCO's current safety-of-journalists work sharpens the practical point: protecting journalists and combating impunity for attacks on them are central to press freedom and freedom of expression, because independent journalism reports on matters of public interest, denounces wrongdoing, and helps hold the powerful accountable.[S221]

That matters because officials often describe pressure on journalists as ordinary law enforcement, collateral risk, isolated hostility, or a regrettable cost of conflict and polarization. But where reporting is made physically unsafe, legally ruinous, or procedurally impossible, the burden is not borne only by the targeted reporter. Public answerability is being degraded upstream. The archive should therefore treat attacks on journalists, confiscation or destruction of their working capacity, and tolerated impunity for those attacks as standing-relevant defects, especially where they predictably chill broader truth-telling.[S34][S35][S41][S220][S221]

## 5. Silencing often works through attrition, intimidation, and managed uncertainty rather than outright ban

The archive already learned from delay, reprisal, protest suppression, and surveillance that power often prefers deniable burdens to explicit prohibition. Expression materials intensify that lesson. Because article 19 protects seeking, receiving, and imparting information, and because journalist protection is tied to accountability rather than prestige, the morally relevant defect is not limited to formal censorship. It also appears where licensing, visa or permit control, access denial, selective prosecution, equipment seizure, legal threat, or tolerated harassment make speaking and reporting predictably unsafe or futile.[S93][S127][S219][S220][S221]

So the archive should now resist a familiar laundering move: claiming that speech remains free because no universal ban was enacted. If only the compliant can publish safely, only the connected can obtain records, only flattering narratives avoid sanction, or only the silent can keep ordinary security, then power may be silencing without saying the word.[S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S219][S220][S221]

## 6. Working rule

When criticism, reporting, publication, access to information, or media work is burdened under pressure, ask six questions:
1. **What concrete protected interest justified the burden?** Was it a real rights-protective concern, or mainly embarrassment, narrative control, reputational insulation, or administrative convenience?[S93][S219][S220]
2. **What part of article 19 was being burdened?** Holding an opinion, speaking, publishing, seeking information, receiving information, or all of these together?[S93][S127][S219]
3. **What narrower route was tried first?** Correction, reply, redaction, delayed release, limited protective secrecy, targeted removal, or another rights-preserving alternative may matter before broad suppression does.[S34][S35][S93][S219]
4. **Who was exposed to special chilling risk?** Journalists, editors, witnesses, researchers, artists, minority speakers, and those already dependent on the burdening office often face the highest practical silencing cost.[S41][S220][S221]
5. **What happened to public-interest information?** Did records, hearings, data, or witness channels remain usable enough that public challenge could still proceed?[S34][S35][S93][S219]
6. **What review and repair stayed live afterward?** Challenge, appeal, correction, evidence preservation, and protection against retaliatory carryover matter because silencing is often laundered after the decisive moment passes.[S32][S34][S35][S219][S221]

## Compact maxim

> **Do not govern by silencing.** Rights-protective limits on expression can exist, but righteousness is heavily burdened when criticism, reporting, or access to public-interest information is suppressed mainly to shield power from truthful public challenge.[S93][S127][S219][S220][S221]
