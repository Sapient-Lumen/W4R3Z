# Delegation, Proxies, and Nondelegable Remainder

## Why this surface now

The archive had become increasingly precise about office, contestability, uncertainty, and repair, but it still lacked a compact rule for **delegated judgment**.

That omission matters because modern action is routinely mediated by:
- subordinates,
- committees,
- contractors,
- bureaucratic procedures,
- software,
- and increasingly automated decision-support systems.[S43][S44][S46]

Without a delegation rule, two opposite mistakes stay too easy:
- **proxy innocence** — pretending that once a task is delegated, the original office or institution no longer owns the standing burden; and
- **anti-delegation purity** — pretending that righteous action must always be personal, immediate, and undelegated even where scale, expertise, or institutional coordination make delegation necessary.

The archive now rejects both mistakes.

## Working-canon thesis

> **Delegation may distribute labor, discretion, and expertise, but it does not automatically transfer away righteous standing. What remains nondelegable is answerability for what claims were recognized, what safeguards governed the proxy, what review and appeal remained open, and who must answer and repair if delegated action wrongs.**[S17][S19][S34][S43][S44][S45][S46]

This is not a ban on delegation. It is a rule against treating delegation as moral disappearance.

## 1. Delegation is often necessary and still morally structured

Roles and institutions often cannot act without using deputies, administrative routines, or technical systems. Representation theory is useful here because it refuses to treat representation as simple substitution. Even when representatives exercise judgment on behalf of others, authorization and accountability remain part of the evaluative structure.[S45]

So the archive now takes delegation to be normal rather than exceptional. But the standing burden does not vanish merely because action is mediated through others.

## 2. Many hands is a hazard, not a solvent

Collective-responsibility and computing-responsibility work both show the same recurring problem: harms can emerge from many actors, layered roles, and temporally distant design choices. That complexity can make responsibility harder to trace, but it does not make it unreal.[S43][S44]

The archive therefore rejects a familiar laundering move:

> **"many hands" may complicate answerability; it does not erase it.**

When institutions claim that no one is really answerable because everyone only handled a piece, righteousness is presumptively defeated unless some agent or office has actually preserved responsibility-bearing oversight, contestability, and repair capacity.[S34][S43][S44]

## 3. Delegation cannot purify capture or hide judgment

An office or institution cannot become righteous by routing a standing-defective judgment through a cleaner-seeming intermediary.

Examples include:
- using a vendor, consultant, or committee to supply a politically desired answer while pretending neutrality;
- using procedure or software to disguise that certain testimony was never seriously heard;
- blaming "the system" for a burden that was designed, procured, approved, or left without appeal by identifiable officeholders.[S19][S34][S44][S46]

So the archive now adds a sharper anti-laundering rule:

> **No righteousness by proxy where the proxy exists mainly to blur ownership, mute contestation, or distribute blame until nobody answers.**

## 4. Nondelegable remainder

The archive does **not** say that every decision must be taken personally by the highest officeholder. It says that some burdens remain with the delegating actor or institution even after the task is handed off.

The clearest nondelegable remainder includes:

1. **claim-setting** — deciding which claims, parties, and vulnerabilities matter enough to count in the delegated process;[S17][S34]
2. **guardrail-setting** — fixing the limits, reasons, evidence rules, and escalation triggers under which the delegate may act;[S34][S46]
3. **contestability preservation** — ensuring record, explanation, review, override, or appeal rather than sealed substitution;[S34][S45][S46]
4. **repair ownership** — making sure someone remains institutionally on the hook for correction, compensation, or structural repair when delegated action harms;[S26][S28][S43][S44]
5. **standing cost** — accepting that a delegator may bear the standing damage when it knowingly benefits from a proxy structure that predictably launders domination, error, or exclusion.[S19][S30][S43]

This is why the archive calls the remainder *nondelegable*: the task can move, but the burden of answerability does not simply move with it.

## 5. Righteous delegation under uncertainty and scale

The delegation rule strengthens rather than weakens the archive's recent uncertainty discipline.

Under uncertainty, righteous delegation should usually do at least five things:
- preserve records of who set the system up and why;
- keep a reviewable path for affected parties;
- allow challenge to proxy outputs rather than treating them as self-vindicating;
- assign named human or institutional owners for suspension, override, and repair;
- and prefer narrower deployments where evidence is thin or burdens are hard to reverse.[S34][S40][S41][S44][S46]

This matters especially for AI and automated systems. NIST's AI RMF is useful not because it solves righteousness, but because it treats governance, documentation, lifecycle risk management, and multidisciplinary oversight as cross-cutting requirements rather than as optional extras.[S46]

## 6. More exact verdicts

This surface improves verdict language again.

More exact judgments now include:
- **substantively serious but overdelegated**;
- **proxy-laundered**;
- **many-hands evasive**;
- **review-capable delegation**;
- **delegated correctly, remainder still owned**.

These are often better than a flat yes-or-no.

## Office-facing note

For office, the rule is straightforward:

> **A righteous office may delegate tasks, but it may not delegate away public answerability.**

If the office cannot say who set the proxy's remit, who can override it, who hears those burdened by it, and who owes repair after proxy failure, then the delegation structure itself counts against righteousness rather than in its favor.[S19][S32][S34][S44][S46]

## What this does not settle

This surface does **not** settle:
- which office functions are never properly delegable at all;
- how far democratic authorization can legitimate bureaucratic or technical mediation;
- when a proxy's standing defects should defeat only the proxy output and when they should defeat the delegating office more broadly;
- or how thick human oversight must be for highly technical systems.

Those remain live.

## Compressed rule

> **Delegate labor if needed; do not delegate away answerability. Keep claim-setting, guardrails, contestability, and repair visibly owned, or the righteousness claim will be proxy-laundered.**
