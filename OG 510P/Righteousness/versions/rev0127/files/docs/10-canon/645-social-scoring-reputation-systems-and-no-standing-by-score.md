# Social scoring, reputation systems, and no-standing-by-score

## Core claim

> **Righteousness is burdened, and often defeated, when public or private actors evaluate or classify persons or groups over time through composite scores, reputation systems, or similar standing metrics built from social behaviour, inferred traits, cross-context data, or repeated interactions, such that the score itself does the real work of deciding trust, access, scrutiny, opportunity, or penalty across contexts rather than a narrow, purpose-bound, reviewable, and contestable judgment tied to the specific relation at issue.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S252][S254][S437][S438]

## 1. The missing moral question is when a score stops being a tool and becomes a standing verdict

The archive already rejected no-status-ranked-standing, no-rightlessness-by-model, no-rights-by-list, and no-suspicion-by-category. But it still lacked a direct rule for a specific administrative temptation: **compressing a person's practical standing into a running score that can travel across domains more easily than reasons can**.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49]

That gap matters because institutions often describe scores as modest technical aids. They say they are only organizing signals, predicting risk, ranking trust, targeting resources, or summarizing prior behaviour. But once a score begins to function as the portable answer to whether a person should be served, admitted, watched, deprioritized, punished, or distrusted, the institution is no longer only evaluating a bounded relation. It is quietly assigning civic or practical rank.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S252]

## 2. Specific-purpose evaluation and standing-by-score are not the same thing

The archive does **not** need to say that every score, rating, priority cue, or bounded risk indicator is automatically unrighteous. The AI Act is useful precisely because it draws a morally important line. It prohibits certain AI-based social-scoring practices when persons or groups are evaluated over time on the basis of social behaviour or known, inferred, or predicted personal or personality characteristics and the resulting score leads to detrimental treatment in unrelated social contexts or to treatment that is unjustified or disproportionate to the behaviour at issue; yet it also states that this prohibition does not affect lawful evaluation practices of natural persons carried out for a specific purpose under law.[S437][S438]

That distinction is load-bearing for righteousness. A narrow and reviewable assessment tied to one real question is one thing. A metric that follows the person from school to work, from platform to payment, from welfare to housing, from complaint history to generalized trustworthiness, is another. The second is not merely stronger evidence. It is a different governing posture.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S437][S438]

## 3. Composite scores tempt institutions to fuse contexts that should stay morally distinct

Righteousness is relation-first and context-sensitive. Different relations carry different burdens: teacher and student, lender and borrower, regulator and association, platform and user, police and resident, office and claimant. A composite score tempts institutions to treat those relations as if they could be collapsed into one portable answer about the person. That is morally dangerous because it lets a weaker relation inherit the residue of a stronger one and lets one domain silently punish or disqualify in another.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49]

This is why social scoring is not just a more sophisticated form of recordkeeping. It creates a drift from **judging an act, event, or bounded risk** toward **administering the person as a ranked object**. Once that happens, explanation, proportionality, and repair all become harder. The burdened person may not know which domain supplied the penalty, which inference mattered, how to separate one context from another, or how to earn release from a standing verdict that is always being recomputed.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S252][S254]

## 4. Current governance materials treat social scoring as a serious human-rights danger

The AI Act's Article 5 materials are morally revealing because they do not merely say that some scoring systems are risky in a general sense. They say certain social-scoring practices are prohibited, and the Commission's official summary explains why: they violate fundamental rights and EU values when the score leads to unrelated or unjustified detrimental treatment.[S437][S438]

OHCHR's 2021 privacy report sharpens the wider human-rights burden because it says AI systems that pose a serious risk to human rights require a moratorium until safeguards exist and that AI applications that cannot comply with international human-rights law should be banned.[S252] UNESCO's AI ethics recommendation then names the point even more directly: AI systems should not be used for social scoring.[S254]

The archive therefore now rejects another laundering move: because a score is dynamic, statistical, multi-factor, or supposedly personalized, it is treated as more morally mature than ordinary reason-giving judgment. But some forms of scoring are standing defects precisely because they convert many thin signals into a portable status that institutions then treat as permission to burden the person elsewhere.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S252][S254][S437][S438]

## 5. Working rule

When an actor claims righteousness while relying on a social score, reputation metric, behavioural rank, or comparable composite standing device, ask seven questions:
1. **What exactly was being scored?** Conduct, complaints, payment history, social ties, inferred traits, platform behaviour, compliance history, neighbourhood proxy, or mixed cross-context data?[S437][S438]
2. **What relation was the score supposedly serving?** Safety, fraud prevention, moderation, lending, tenancy, welfare administration, school discipline, border screening, or something much broader and vaguer such as trustworthiness or civic reliability?[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S437][S438]
3. **How portable was the metric?** Did it stay inside one purpose-bounded context, or did it travel across agencies, platforms, employers, landlords, schools, payment systems, or other unrelated domains?[S437][S438]
4. **What adverse treatment followed?** Delay, denial, de-prioritization, added scrutiny, higher price, exclusion, list-like monitoring, throttled opportunity, or another burden that the person could not meaningfully isolate to one context?[S252][S254][S437][S438]
5. **What explanation and contest path existed?** Notice, meaningful explanation, data correction, human review, override, appeal, expiration, or another route out of inherited bad standing?[S93][S252]
6. **What narrower route was available?** Case-specific review, ordinary evidence, role-specific criteria, temporary flags, protected investigation, or purpose-limited assessment rather than person-totalizing score governance?[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S437][S438]
7. **Was the score doing the real work?** Did institutions quietly replace reasons, hearing, and relation-specific judgment with a portable metric that treated the person's practical standing as already known?[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S252][S254][S437][S438]

## Compression sentence

> **Do not let a composite score stand in for a person's practical standing: where social scoring, reputation systems, or similar metrics do the real work of deciding trust, access, scrutiny, opportunity, or penalty across contexts, righteousness fails unless the evaluation remains narrow, purpose-bound, contestable, and clearly less totalizing than governance by score.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S252][S254][S437][S438]
