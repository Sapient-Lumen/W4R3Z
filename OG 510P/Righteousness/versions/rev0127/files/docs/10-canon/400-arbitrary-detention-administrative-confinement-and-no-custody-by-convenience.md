# Arbitrary Detention, Administrative Confinement, and No-Custody-by-Convenience

## Why this surface matters

The archive already rejects family fracture, disappearance architecture, punitive noncare, torture, and trapdoor procedure. What it still lacked was a compact rule for **what happens when power treats custody itself as an easy administrative answer**: pretrial detention as default, preventive confinement with vague factual basis, prolonged immigration or security detention without real review, psychiatric or quarantine confinement that drifts past necessity, or ordinary governance that reaches first for holding cells, camps, wards, and locked sites because release, supervision, support, or less restrictive alternatives are harder to organize.[S93][S128][S199][S200]

That missing rule matters because detention is not morally neutral once law can name some basis for it. ICCPR Article 9 is morally revealing because it prohibits **arbitrary** arrest or detention, not only detention that is literally unauthorized by domestic law; requires reasons for arrest, prompt presentation to a judge in criminal cases, court review of lawfulness, and compensation for unlawful detention; and adds that pretrial detention should not be the general rule.[S93][S127] OHCHR's current arbitrary-detention guidance sharpens the point by stating that arbitrariness is broader than illegality and includes inappropriateness, injustice, lack of predictability, and due-process failure, while also making clear that the mandate extends to deprivation of liberty without any kind of trial, including administrative detention.[S199] So the archive should now treat arbitrary detention as a distinct standing defect: **custody becomes the method by which uncertainty, risk, dissent, poverty, migration status, illness, or inconvenience are managed without carrying the burden of narrower justification**.[S93][S128][S199][S200]

## Working-canon thesis

> **Righteousness is burdened, and often defeated, when a person, office, or institution deprives someone of liberty without a clear, lawful, necessary, and reviewable basis, or keeps custody in place because detention is administratively easier than narrower alternatives. Criminal detention requires prompt reasons, prompt judicial control, and release as the ordinary baseline unless concrete reasons justify continued custody. Administrative, security, migration, quarantine, psychiatric, or other non-penal confinement does not escape the same moral burden: once detention is prolonged, weakly reasoned, discriminatory, unpredictable, or insulated from timely challenge, righteousness is usually not preserving order under burden but governing through convenience custody.**[S93][S127][S128][S199][S200]

## 1. Detention is not just another tool; it is one of power's heaviest burdens

Article 9 matters because it starts with liberty itself. Everyone has the right to liberty and security of person, and no one may be subjected to arbitrary arrest or detention.[S93][S127] That gives the archive a sharper starting point than a simple question about whether confinement served some useful end. The real question is whether the actor carried the burden required before taking hold of another person's body and time.

That matters because institutions often describe custody as a temporary holding measure while quietly letting detention do the practical work of policy. The person is jailed because release planning is hard. The migrant is confined because individualized supervision is administratively expensive. The psychiatric patient is held because community care is scarce. The protester is detained because processing later challenge is easier than tolerating immediate inconvenience. But where custody is solving evidentiary weakness, bureaucratic overload, political anxiety, or general social discomfort, righteousness is usually already badly burdened.[S34][S35][S41][S93][S199][S200]

## 2. “Arbitrary” is broader than “against local law”

OHCHR's current arbitrary-detention guidance states the key compression point directly: arbitrariness is not equivalent to mere unlawfulness. It also includes inappropriateness, injustice, lack of predictability, and due-process failure, and detention can be arbitrary where there is no legal basis, where protected rights are being penalized, where fair-trial defects are grave, where migrants or asylum seekers face prolonged administrative custody without review, or where detention is discriminatory.[S199]

That matters for the archive because public power often launders custody through formal paperwork. A warrant exists. A statute authorizes preventive detention. A form has been signed. A technical hearing occurred. But righteousness should now ask a harder question: **was this person held on terms that were intelligible, individualized, proportionate, and challengeable, or was legality being used as a shell around fear, convenience, or discrimination?** Where prediction replaces proof, vague danger replaces concrete ground, or review is too slow or weak to matter, custody is often not merely legal-hard. It is arbitrary in the morally deeper sense the archive should care about.[S32][S34][S35][S93][S199][S200]

## 3. Criminal detention is burdened by reasons, speed, and a release baseline

ICCPR Article 9 sharpens criminal-process burdens with unusual clarity. Anyone arrested must be informed at the time of arrest of the reasons for arrest and promptly informed of charges; anyone arrested or detained on a criminal charge must be brought promptly before a judge or other officer authorized to exercise judicial power; and trial within a reasonable time or release must remain available. The Covenant adds an especially important sentence for the archive: **it shall not be the general rule that persons awaiting trial shall be detained in custody**.[S93][S127]

The archive should generalize the moral shape without collapsing every case into the same domain. The portable lesson is that custody before adjudication is not morally self-justifying merely because accusation exists. If people are jailed first and reasons are supplied later, if judicial presentation is delayed until the detention has already done its coercive work, or if release is treated as exceptional grace rather than the ordinary baseline, then power is often governing through incapacitation instead of carrying the burdens of proof, speed, and individualized necessity.[S34][S35][S93][S127][S128][S200]

## 4. Administrative detention is still detention, not a loophole around due burden

OHCHR's current materials on arbitrary detention are especially useful because they make explicit that the mandate extends to deprivation of liberty before, during, or after trial, and also to deprivation of liberty in the absence of any kind of trial, including administrative detention.[S199] The Body of Principles gives the same portable lesson from another angle: any form of detention or imprisonment and all measures affecting the human rights of a detained person must be ordered by, or be subject to the effective control of, a judicial or other authority; the detained person must have an effective opportunity to be heard promptly; and a judicial or other authority must be empowered to review the continuance of detention.[S128]

This matters because institutions routinely rename custody in order to lower the moral temperature: holding room, processing center, protective custody, preventive placement, transit zone, medical observation, disciplinary segregation, national-security measure, quarantine facility, or welfare accommodation. Some of those forms may sometimes be justified. The archive's point is narrower: **non-penal labels do not erase liberty burdens**. Where confinement is prolonged, reasons are generic, review is formalistic, or release turns on conditions the detainee cannot realistically satisfy, righteousness is usually not preserving safety under burden but governing through captive convenience.[S34][S35][S41][S93][S128][S199][S200]

## 5. Records, notice, family and counsel access, and court challenge are part of the substance, not external extras

The Body of Principles is morally revealing because it treats procedure here as protection rather than ornament. Arrest and detention must be strictly lawful and under competent authority; people must be told the reasons; detention orders and reasons must be communicated promptly to the detained person and counsel; records of reasons, time, officials, and place of custody must be kept; family or other appropriate persons are to be notified promptly after arrest or transfer; complaints must be possible; and places of detention should be visited regularly by qualified persons distinct from the authority directly in charge.[S128]

This helps the archive avoid a familiar mistake. Counsel access, family notification, recorded reasons, visiting bodies, and speedy court challenge are often described as procedural extras attached to an already justified detention power. But in arbitrary-detention cases they are closer to part of the protective substance itself. If no one outside the detaining chain can test grounds promptly, find the person, communicate with them, or review continuation of custody, then detention is not merely hard power. It is power drifting toward self-certification, disappearance risk, coerced dependency, and convenience rule.[S34][S35][S93][S128][S199]

## 6. Working rule

When arrest, confinement, internment, involuntary placement, or prolonged holding is defended under pressure, ask six questions:
1. **What concrete basis justified custody?** Criminal charge, immediate safety risk, health necessity, immigration processing, mental-health emergency, battlefield capture, or something vaguer?[S93][S128][S199][S200]
2. **Why detention rather than a narrower alternative?** Summons, release with conditions, supervised release, community treatment, ordinary hearing, de-escalation, or other less restrictive route?[S34][S35][S41][S93][S200]
3. **How quickly did review arrive?** Reasons at arrest, prompt information about charges, prompt judicial control, and real ability to challenge continuation?[S93][S127][S128]
4. **What made the detention non-arbitrary in the deeper sense?** Not only a legal basis, but also predictability, necessity, proportionality, non-discrimination, and fair process?[S93][S199][S200]
5. **What custody architecture existed?** Records, official site, counsel access, family notification, visiting mechanisms, complaint channels, and a review body distinct from the immediate custodian?[S128][S199]
6. **What exit and repair followed?** Release, compensation where detention was unlawful, record correction, restored status, and institutional learning against recurrence?[S93][S128]

Where the honest answer is that custody was doing the practical work of convenience, intimidation, preemptive control, dissent management, or discrimination without timely and effective challenge, the archive should now treat the righteousness claim as presumptively defeated unless the case can be redescribed as a genuinely narrower, necessary, and reviewable confinement that did not make detention the default answer.[S34][S35][S41][S93][S128][S199][S200]

## Short formula

> **Do not make custody the easy answer.** Confinement may sometimes be necessary, but righteousness requires clear ground, prompt reasons, prompt review, real challenge, and release as the baseline where continued detention is not concretely justified. Administrative relabeling does not purify arbitrary detention; it often hides it.[S93][S127][S128][S199][S200]
