# Anti-Domination, Power Asymmetry, and Benevolent Mastery

## Why this surface matters

A portable theory of righteousness already cares about due, vulnerability, office, repair, and standing. But it still needs a compact way to say why some arrangements remain morally bad even when the stronger party behaves kindly much of the time.

A child, worker, patient, prisoner, welfare claimant, subordinate, or dependent partner may receive occasional benefits and still live too much at another's mercy. The archive therefore needs a rule against mistaking **benevolent mastery** for righteous order.[S08][S49][S70][S71]

## Starter thesis

> **Righteousness is not reducible to non-domination, but it is incompatible with leaving people subject to uncontrolled power while calling that arrangement benevolent. Where one party can unilaterally set terms, retaliate, withdraw protection, block exit, or demand gratitude from the dependent, a righteousness claim is presumptively weakened even if overt interference is infrequent or the superior is often kind.**[S34][S49][S70][S71]

## Why benevolence is not enough

Republican thought is useful here because it distinguishes actual interference from standing under another's arbitrary or uncontrolled power. One can be dominated even by a mild master if one's security depends mainly on that master's will.[S70][S71]

That matters for this archive because righteousness has always been relation-first and standing-sensitive. If the weaker party stands mainly by permission, indulgence, or personal favor, then the due is not yet secure. Kind use of power may reduce harm; it does not by itself make the relation righteous.[S08][S17][S34][S49]

## Three recurring sites of domination pressure

### 1. Intimate and dependency relations

Dependence is not itself domination. Children, illness, disability, care work, and unequal capacity all create real asymmetries that cannot be wished away. But righteousness in such settings requires more than benevolent intention from the stronger side. It should aim to reduce humiliating discretion, make expectations legible, protect voice, and avoid turning care into a demand for silence, gratitude, or moral surrender.[S08][S49][S71]

### 2. Organizational and workplace relations

A formally voluntary relation can still be domination-shaped when one side cannot safely refuse, exit, contest, or survive retaliation. The archive should therefore not let contract language, managerial competence, or occasional generosity do the whole normative work. When livelihoods, access, status, or speech depend on pleasing a superior, righteousness requires stronger counter-power, review, appeal, and anti-retaliation structure than benevolence alone.[S34][S70][S71]

### 3. Public office and administrative dependency

This pressure is especially sharp in office. A state agency, court, police force, school, welfare office, prison, hospital, immigration authority, or licensing body may act gently in tone and still rule unrighteously if people remain exposed to opaque discretion, clientelism, selective waiver, or fear of retaliation for complaint. Rule-of-law and republican materials are useful here because they ask not only whether power was used well this time, but whether those subject to power are protected against having to live at another's mercy at all.[S34][S35][S70][S71]

## What this rule does not say

The archive is **not** claiming that all unrighteousness is domination, that every asymmetry is abusive, or that righteousness can be reduced to a single republican metric. Promise-keeping, truthfulness, repair, mercy, sanction, sacred command, and office-duty remain irreducible parts of the theory.

The claim is narrower and more useful: where people are structurally dependent on another's uncontrolled discretion, a righteousness claim is burdened. Non-domination is therefore a sharpening test against paternalism, clientelism, managed dependency, and benevolent-master rhetoric.[S17][S34][S49][S70][S71]

## Working rule

When a righteousness claim is made under steep power asymmetry, ask five questions:
1. **Who could unilaterally change the terms?** Who could grant, withdraw, or condition basic security, access, or relief?[S70][S71]
2. **What protected exit, appeal, or refusal existed?** Could the weaker party say no, complain, contest, or leave without ruinous retaliation?[S34][S35][S71]
3. **Was dependence being converted into compliance?** Was gratitude, silence, loyalty, or moral deference being extracted as the price of protection or aid?[S08][S49][S71]
4. **What constrained the stronger party besides character?** Rules, review, representation, publicity, independent oversight, and anti-retaliation protections matter because they reduce mercy-dependence.[S34][S35][S70]
5. **Would the relation still look righteous if the stronger party became less benevolent tomorrow?** If the answer is no, the standing may have been too person-dependent to count as secure righteous order.[S70][S71]

## Compression sentence

> **Do not call power righteous merely because it was used kindly this week; ask whether the weaker party stood protected against having to please a master at all.**[S34][S49][S70][S71]
