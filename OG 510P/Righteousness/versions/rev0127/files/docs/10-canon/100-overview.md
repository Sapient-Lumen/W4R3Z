The archive already rejected no-association-by-sufferance, no-order-by-suppression, no-rightlessness-by-paper, and no-rights-by-list, but it still lacked a direct rule for when permits, licenses, prior authorization, or registration status become the practical source of whether people may assemble, organize, or otherwise exercise standing-bearing rights at all. ICCPR articles 21 and 22 are morally revealing because they recognize assembly and association as rights and allow restriction only under steep legality-and-necessity burdens.[S93] General comment No. 37, the 2012 and 2013 Special Rapporteur materials, and the 2015 Chile statement sharpen the operative distinction by insisting that peaceful assembly should not be subject to prior authorization, that notification may sometimes be used to facilitate rather than license the right, that associations should not be formed only after prior authorization, and that requiring authorization turns a right into a privilege.[S217][S421][S422][S423] The 2024 Joint Declaration adds the associational corollary that registered and unregistered associations may seek resources without prior authorization and should not be compelled to register in order to exist and operate freely.[S424] The archive therefore now adds a permits / licenses / no-rights-by-permission rule: where official approval, registration status, or discretionary licensing does the real work of deciding whether people may assemble, organize, or otherwise exercise standing-bearing freedoms rather than using a narrow, facilitative, reviewable, and non-discriminatory administrative tool, righteousness is heavily burdened and often defeated.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S217][S421][S422][S423][S424]

Another clarification now matters where institutions govern by lists. The archive already rejected no-suspicion-by-category, no-identity-by-extraction, no-conviction-by-opacity, and no-rightlessness-by-paper, but it still lacked a direct rule for when a watchlist, no-fly list, sanctions designation, or proscription register becomes the practical instrument through which movement, property, access, or scrutiny are burdened without ordinary hearing. OHCHR’s *Human Rights at International Borders* guide is morally revealing because it says terrorist watch lists may be constituted without due process and effective independent oversight, that unjustified inclusion can violate privacy and freedom of movement, and that transparency and appeal mechanisms are required.[S415][S93] The fair-trial guide and the proscription guide sharpen the same point by insisting that due process and effective challenge apply even in counter-terrorism and administrative settings, including proscription decisions and their effects.[S416][S417] The 2010 and 2012 Special Rapporteur statements add that due-process rights remain at stake in terrorist listing systems, that listed persons should have access to courts, and that list inclusion can seriously interfere with movement, property, and privacy while remaining difficult to judicially review or undo.[S418][S419] The archive therefore now adds a watchlists / blacklists / no-rights-by-list rule: where list inclusion itself does the real work of restricting movement, access, association, property, or scrutiny through opaque or weakly contestable designation rather than through clear criteria, timely notice, independent review, correction, delisting, and periodic expiration, righteousness is heavily burdened and often defeated.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S153][S252][S415][S416][S417][S418][S419]

Another clarification now matters before search, arrest, or data capture even begin. The archive already rejected status-ranked standing, no-order-by-suppression, no-security-by-intrusion, no-rightlessness-by-model, and no-identity-by-extraction, but it still lacked a direct rule for when police, border, or security scrutiny is initiated through profiling, stop-and-search by category, identity checks by appearance, or suspicion by perceived foreignness rather than by individualized grounds. ICERD article 5 is morally revealing because it guarantees equality before the law, including before organs administering justice; CERD’s General Recommendation No. 36 and OHCHR’s 2019 guide sharpen the operational rule by insisting that law-enforcement determinations be based on individual conduct and objective criteria rather than on race, colour, descent, national or ethnic origin, or comparable group markers; the 2025 Expert Mechanism says racial discrimination permeates all layers of the criminal justice system, including racial profiling and arbitrary deprivation of liberty; and the 2026 joint recommendation on xenophobia toward migrants and others perceived as such shows the same structure beyond race in a narrow sense.[S233][S411][S412][S413][S414] The archive therefore now adds a policing-stops / profiling / no-suspicion-by-category rule: where institutions make race, ethnicity, religion, nationality, migration status, neighbourhood proxy, or another group marker do the real work of deciding who gets stopped, checked, searched, questioned, or tracked rather than using individualized and conduct-linked grounds that can be publicly explained and contested, righteousness is heavily burdened and often defeated.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S153][S252][S396][S233][S411][S412][S413][S414]

Another clarification now matters where institutions claim to know people by extracting the body or genome itself. The archive already rejected arbitrary intrusion into privacy, no-rightlessness-by-model, no-security-by-intrusion, and no-second-sentence-by-record, but it still lacked a direct rule for when identity systems become standing defects because face, fingerprint, iris, voice, gait, or DNA are treated as ordinary keys to access, suspicion, or public-space trackability. Current OHCHR privacy work is morally revealing because it treats the collection and processing of data as a site of discrimination and unequal enjoyment of privacy; the 2022 privacy report warns that widespread monitoring of public spaces can create systems of pervasive surveillance and control; OHCHR protest guidance says facial recognition can chill assembly and should face a moratorium in peaceful-protest contexts until strong safeguards exist; the privacy mandate treats forensic DNA databases as a distinct area of concern; and UNESCO’s human-genome declaration insists that persons may not be reduced to their genetic characteristics and that identifiable genetic data must remain confidential under law-bound limits.[S404][S405][S406][S155][S408][S409] The archive therefore now adds a biometrics / DNA / no-identity-by-extraction rule: where biometric or genetic capture makes bodily or genomic extractability the ordinary condition of verification, access, suspicion management, or public-space legibility rather than using narrow, necessity-bound, contestable, retention-disciplined, and non-discriminatory forms of verification, righteousness is heavily burdened and often defeated unless a less extractive route is unavailable and strong deletion, challenge, and anti-discrimination safeguards are real.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S153][S252][S404][S405][S406][S155][S408][S409]

Another clarification now matters where institutions claim to preserve safety, evidence, or order by accessing the body itself. The archive already rejected humiliation, punitive noncare, punishment by degradation, and arbitrary intrusion into privacy, but it still lacked a direct rule for when search becomes governance through exposure. ICCPR article 17 supplies the outer boundary by forbidding arbitrary or unlawful interference with privacy.[S93] The Body of Principles says all persons under detention or imprisonment remain entitled to humane treatment and inherent dignity.[S128] The Bangkok Rules sharpen that intrusive search burdens are not morally generic: women prisoners’ dignity and respect must be protected during personal searches, alternative screening methods should replace strip and invasive body searches where possible, and searches of children in prison or visiting prisoners must preserve dignity.[S375] The OHCHR law-enforcement manual and OHCHR stopping-and-search guide add the operational burden: extensive body searches should be private, same-sex where required, professionally handled, non-coercive, governed by legality, necessity, and proportionality, and intrusive searches require clear justification, strict guidelines, and the least intrusive means possible.[S401][S402] The Nelson Mandela Rules compress the same lesson: intrusive searches, including strip and body-cavity searches, should be undertaken only if absolutely necessary and alternatives should be developed.[S403] The archive therefore now adds a searches / bodily-inspection / no-security-by-intrusion rule: where frisking, strip search, body-cavity search, scan-based screening, or repeated bodily inspection makes exposure, humiliation, or compelled bodily legibility do the real work of security, discipline, or intimidation rather than using lawful, necessity-bound, least-intrusive, privacy-preserving, and reviewable forms of search, righteousness is heavily burdened and often defeated unless the measure is specifically justified and not better replaced by narrower alternatives.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S93][S128][S375][S401][S402][S403]

Another clarification now matters where punishment, investigation, or migration control touches property itself. The archive already rejected no-punishment-by-poverty, no-second-sentence-by-record, no-rightlessness-by-paper, and no-office-by-sale, but it still lacked a direct rule for when taking becomes a standing defect rather than a narrowly justified remedy. OHCHR’s Recommended Principles on Human Rights and Asset Recovery are morally revealing because they affirm that asset recovery can be legitimate while insisting that investigations, prosecutions, restraint, and confiscation comply with fair-trial, property, privacy, life, anti-torture, and non-retroactivity guarantees, and because they extend those protections to witnesses and bona fide third parties.[S397] UNODC confiscation guidance sharpens the same point in more operational language: affected persons need a route to relief or hearing, innocent-owner and third-party protections matter, and systems in which police or agencies keep confiscated assets have generated controversy because they may encourage spurious or aggressive takings.[S398] OHCHR treaty texts add a simpler boundary rule: the UDHR forbids arbitrary deprivation of property, and the Migrant Workers Convention forbids both arbitrary deprivation of property and unlawful confiscation or destruction of identity and work documents.[S399][S400] The archive therefore now adds an asset-seizure / forfeiture / no-punishment-by-taking rule: where freezing, seizure, forfeiture, document-taking, or equivalent-value confiscation is doing punitive, coercive, or revenue work through weak proof, thin contestability, third-party spillover, or agency self-funding rather than through narrow, reviewable, and rights-disciplined recovery of genuinely illicit or danger-linked property, righteousness is heavily burdened and often defeated unless the taking is law-bound, proportionate, promptly challengeable, and not better addressed by narrower restraint, return, or compensation.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S397][S398][S399][S400]

Another clarification now matters after formal sentence completion. The archive already rejected no-punishment-by-poverty, no-freedom-by-tether, no-livelihood-by-domination, and no-status-ranked-standing, but it still lacked a direct rule for when criminal records and collateral consequences quietly continue punishment through work, housing, benefit, education, licensing, or civic exclusion. The 2025 OHCHR reintegration report is morally revealing because it names unrestricted access to criminal records as a barrier to reintegration; the accompanying OHCHR story says released persons remain entitled to housing, employment, health care, dignity, and freedom from stigma and names criminal records as an obstacle to return; current OHCHR materials also show that record-linked consequences can include disenfranchisement and barriers to employment, housing, education, and public benefits; current OHCHR experts on homelessness and poverty have even urged expungement of criminal records of sanctioned individuals; and the Beijing Rules sharpen the anti-stigma principle by requiring juvenile records to remain confidential and closed to third parties.[S380][S381][S392][S394][S395][S396] The archive therefore now adds a criminal-records / collateral-consequences / no-second-sentence-by-record rule: where record visibility, disclosure, or automatic collateral consequences continue punishment by blocking ordinary civic and social standing after sentence completion rather than through narrow, time-bounded, reviewable, and necessity-linked restrictions, righteousness is heavily burdened and often defeated unless sealing, expungement, or sharply limited access is made real.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S380][S381][S392][S393][S394][S395][S396]

Another clarification now matters at the point where punishment becomes debt. The archive already rejected no-liberty-by-wealth, no-freedom-by-tether, punitive noncare, and no-rights-by-austerity, but it still lacked a direct rule for when fines, fees, and non-payment enforcement quietly make poverty itself into a sentencing multiplier. The Guiding Principles on Extreme Poverty and Human Rights are morally revealing because they call for addressing the disproportionate effect of criminal sanctions on persons living in poverty, reviewing disproportionate fines, and considering abolition of prison sentences for non-payment by those unable to pay; the 2024 OHCHR homelessness-and-poverty study is revealing because it says fines often cascade into arrest or imprisonment and that inability to pay can itself expose people to imprisonment for minor conduct; current OHCHR and UNODC materials on women and non-custodial measures sharpen that women are often detained because of poverty and inability to pay fines or fees; and Philip Alston's OHCHR statement names the laundering pattern in which fines and fees become a revenue device that keeps poor people poor.[S382][S386][S387][S388][S389][S390][S391] The archive therefore now adds a monetary-sanctions / fines-fees / no-punishment-by-poverty rule: where punishment is made heavier through inability to pay, added fees, arrest exposure, or imprisonment rather than through means-sensitive and reviewable accountability, righteousness is heavily burdened and often defeated unless proportionality, indigency relief, and non-custodial alternatives are made real.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S382][S386][S387][S388][S389][S390][S391]

# Overview

Another clarification now matters for life outside prison walls. The archive already rejected arbitrary detention, wealth-gated bail, punishment by degradation, and rightlessness by model, but it still lacked a direct rule for when non-custodial measures become their own mode of custody. The Tokyo Rules are morally revealing because they treat alternatives to imprisonment as a field of minimum-intervention safeguards rather than a moral blank cheque; the 2025 OHCHR reintegration report is revealing because it says technical violations of probation and parole can trigger reimprisonment for minor or non-criminal acts, warns that uniform parole conditions can ignore actual circumstances, and says electronic monitoring can replicate detention through undue surveillance, rights restrictions, and net-widening; current OHCHR materials on release and women’s incarceration sharpen that reintegration requires basic rights and community-based alternatives rather than tethered exposure; and recent Working Group findings from Australia show how infrastructure gaps and culturally mismatched programme design can make parole and electronic monitoring selective, stigmatizing, and counter-reintegrative.[S374][S380][S381][S377][S377][S377][S377][S382][S384][S385] The archive therefore now adds a community-supervision / parole / no-freedom-by-tether rule: where probation, parole, electronic monitoring, or related non-custodial measures administer liberty mainly through surveillance, technically breachable conditions, or automatic return-to-custody rather than through the least restrictive reviewable conditions genuinely needed for reintegration and safety, righteousness is heavily burdened and often defeated unless strong necessity, material support, and individualized review can be shown.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S153][S154][S252][S374][S375][S380][S381][S377][S377][S377][S377][S382][S384][S385]

Another clarification now matters for the archive as a whole. It already rejected opacity, delay-attrition, paper-based exclusion, and rights-thinning austerity, but it still lacked a direct rule for when rights remain formally recognized while complaint, hearing, and repair are made practically unusable. Current OHCHR materials are morally revealing because they treat access to justice as foundational to rule-of-law order and even as a precondition to exercising all human rights; UDHR article 8 and ICCPR article 2(3) anchor effective remedy as a baseline demand; the Basic Principles and Guidelines insist that remedy be adequate, effective, and prompt; disability access-to-justice guidance shows that formal equality without usable process is not equal justice; business-and-human-rights remedy materials clarify that grievance and accountability must connect to effective remedy rather than complaint theater; and right-to-housing access-to-justice materials show that social rights must be claimable and remediable rather than merely aspirational.[S289][S95][S93][S292][S259][S294][S295][S296] The archive therefore now adds an access-to-justice and effective-remedy rule: rights are heavily burdened once they are recognized only in forms that cannot be timely claimed, heard, or repaired because complaint, review, or remedy routes are too delayed, inaccessible, retaliatory, powerless, or exhausting to use before decisive harm hardens.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S43][S44][S45][S46][S49][S66][S67][S93][S111][S112][S145][S146][S147][S153][S154][S155][S164][S167][S232][S233][S289][S95][S93][S292][S259][S294][S295][S296]

Another clarification now matters for public finance itself. The archive already rejected welfare by sanction, hunger by administration, thirst by administration, housing insecurity by clearance, and schooling by exclusion, but it still lacked a direct rule for when those same burdens are laundered upstream through budgets, debt service, fiscal consolidation, or public-finance design. Current OHCHR debt and austerity materials are morally revealing because they state that economic choices must comply with human-rights obligations even in crisis and that States must devote maximum available resources to progressive realization; current sovereign-debt materials say nearly 3.4 billion people live in countries spending more on interest than on health or education, warn that austerity conditionalities can disproportionately hurt people in vulnerable situations, and press for human-rights impact assessments that ringfence fiscal space for basic rights; the 2024 fiscal-legitimacy report frames resource collection and allocation as a principle-guided human-rights domain; and current financing-for-development materials tie debt, tax, and financing architecture to the practical ability to invest in rights.[S282][S283][S284][S285][S286][S287][S288] The archive therefore now adds a public-finance and debt-governance rule: budgets, debt workouts, tax systems, and economic reforms may involve real tradeoffs, but righteousness is heavily burdened once food, housing, schooling, care, water, or environmental safety are made effectively conditional on fiscal tidiness, creditor confidence, or politically convenient under-collection rather than protected as rights-bearing floors under equal concern.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S66][S67][S93][S163][S164][S167][S236][S237][S261][S269][S275][S282][S283][S284][S285][S286][S287][S288]

Another clarification now matters for penal custody itself. The archive already rejected torture, arbitrary detention, punitive noncare, family fracture, and disappearance architecture, but it still lacked a direct rule for when even formally lawful imprisonment is administered through overcrowding, degrading conditions, prolonged isolation, blocked family contact, untreated illness, unsafe infrastructure, or emergency abandonment. ICCPR article 10 is morally revealing because it requires humane treatment and respect for dignity and gives the penitentiary system a rehabilitative aim; the Basic Principles say prisoners retain rights except for limitations demonstrably necessitated by incarceration, call for access to health services without discrimination, and encourage abolition or restriction of solitary confinement as punishment; Mandela-facing materials say prison administrations manage deprivation of liberty but not further hardship and should keep prison life as close as possible to life outside, including regular family contact and comparable services; the 2024 prison-management report identifies enduring implementation gaps; OHCHR's detention reports page describes overcrowding as part of a global prison crisis; and current OHCHR climate materials warn that prisoners can be abandoned in disasters unless detention sites are built into evacuation and adaptation planning.[S93][S367][S368][S369][S370][S371] The archive therefore now adds a penal-custody / prison-conditions / no-punishment-by-degradation rule: where sentence administration works mainly through filth, crowding, heat, isolation, decay, severed contact, or other degrading conditions rather than through humane and reviewable deprivation of liberty, righteousness is heavily burdened and often defeated unless unusually strong necessity, independent review, and active protection of health, dignity, and reintegration can be shown.[S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S93][S128][S367][S368][S369][S370][S371][S372]

Another clarification now matters for liberty before conviction. The archive already rejected arbitrary detention, conviction by opacity, delay attrition, and punishment by degradation, but it still lacked a direct rule for when release pending trial is structured so that money, fixed housing, lawyer access, or impossible compliance conditions do the real work of deciding who stays free. Article 9 is morally revealing because it says pretrial detention should not be the general rule; General Comment No. 35 says detention pending trial must be based on an individualized determination of necessity and that alternatives must be examined; the Tokyo Rules say criminal-justice systems should build non-custodial measures at all stages under minimum-intervention discipline; the Bangkok Rules add gender-specific pretrial alternatives and due regard to family ties and victimization history; and recent OHCHR Working Group materials from Canada, Australia, and the Bahamas show how onerous bail conditions, poverty, homelessness, and inability to pay can turn nominal release into selective remand.[S93][S128][S373][S374][S375][S376][S377][S378][S379] The archive therefore now adds a pretrial-release / bail / no-liberty-by-wealth rule: where liberty before trial depends mainly on money, stable housing, document stability, or conditions poorer and more precarious people cannot realistically satisfy rather than on individualized necessity and materially reachable alternatives, righteousness is heavily burdened and often defeated unless the burden is tightly tailored, reviewable, and genuinely feasible to carry.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S128][S373][S374][S375][S376][S377][S378][S379]

## Starter thesis

Righteousness is best treated, for this archive, as a concept of **right standing in action**: a mode of agency, character, and institutional ordering that renders what is due, resists corrupt gain, and, once breach appears, becomes answerable to truthful and reparative response.

This starter thesis is intentionally narrower than “the good” and broader than “legal compliance.”

## Why this shape is promising

Across otherwise very different traditions, righteousness repeatedly appears where at least five features meet:

1. **Relation** — righteousness is about how one stands toward others, not only about interior purity.[S01][S08][S14]
2. **Claim / due** — righteousness concerns what is owed, fitting, fair, faithful, or proper in the case.[S01][S04][S06][S07][S10]
3. **Pressure** — righteousness is tested when profit, status, fear, ritualism, or convenience tempt departure from the due.[S04][S09][S10][S12]
4. **Public consequence** — unrighteousness is not only private defect; it distorts institutions, burdens the vulnerable, and corrupts judgment.[S10][S11][S12][S13][S15]
5. **Role-sensitivity** — public office, covenant, and dependency change what right action requires; purely private reasoning is often insufficient.[S04][S09][S16][S17][S18]

## Compression hazard

English “righteousness” is dangerous because it can slide into any of these:

- smug moral self-certification,
- merely religious respectability,
- generic virtue,
- strict legalism,
- or vague niceness.

The archive rejects all five reductions.

## Minimal working contrast

- **Goodness** asks what is valuable or worth pursuing overall.
- **Justice** asks what is due in distributions, claims, institutions, and treatment.
- **Righteousness** asks whether an agent or institution is *actually standing and acting rightly* with respect to those dues, under real temptation and with real accountability.[S02][S07][S08][S13]

That makes righteousness a useful bridge concept between moral theory and lived evaluation.

## Portable but not flattened

The archive now adds a second clarification:

> righteousness has a **portable core** and **tradition-thick forms**.

The portable core is the recurrent structure that survives comparison: answerability to valid claims under pressure, with fidelity to due order and breach-responsive repair. Thick forms identify what finally grounds that answerability — God, covenant, created order, political reciprocity, cultivated role ethics, or another larger moral frame.[S01][S04][S08][S09][S13][S21]

The archive now adds a further clarification: portability claims are not all of one kind. A translated account may be recognized by a tradition as righteousness proper, as a thin but genuine slice, as a nearby analogue, or as a failed rendering. Separately, it may or may not function as a publicly shareable module for common institutions.[S13][S18][S22][S23]

This lets the archive remain comparative without pretending that sacred vocabularies are ornamental or that portability requires secular flattening.

A further clarification now matters for actual judgment: righteousness is **not** satisfied by bare nonwrongdoing, but it also should not be inflated into universal sainthood or moral maximization. The current archive view is a threshold-band: righteous action stands above minimal blamelessness because it must actually render the due under pressure, yet it does not require every extraordinary sacrifice that would count as heroic or supererogatory.[S24][S25]

A further clarification now enters canon: repair is **breach-responsive** rather than always-on. Before breach, righteousness mainly renders the due and resists corruption. After breach, righteousness must also become truthful, reparative, and non-evasive. That includes acknowledgment, responsibility uptake, material or structural repair where due, and protection against recurrence; it does **not** include demanding that victims forgive as a condition of calling the response righteous.[S26][S27][S28]

A final clarification now enters canon as well: righteousness is **standing-sensitive**. Some defects — especially capture, hypocritical self-exemption, laundering through legality / ritual / ideology, and emergency rhetoric that treats necessity as purification — do not merely lower the score of a righteousness claim. They presumptively defeat it unless the defect itself is openly confronted.[S19][S29][S30][S31]

One more clarification is now required for practical judgment. A defeated righteousness claim need not be a permanent identity verdict. But neither can it be instantly repaired by confession, apology, or humble tone. The current archive therefore adds a requalification rule: where standing can be regained, it runs through avowal, interruption of the corrupted posture, reparative action where due, and time-bound re-earning of standing — not retroactive purification of the tainted act.[S26][S30][S32][S33]

Another clarification now matters for emergency and necessity. The archive already rejected emergency rhetoric when it functioned as a purifier, but it had not yet given itself a clean positive rule. Derogation materials are morally revealing here because they treat emergency as exceptional and temporary, require strict necessity, and orient extraordinary powers back toward normalcy rather than indefinite rule by crisis. Strasbourg and Venice materials add the same burden in another idiom: emergency is limited, supervised, reviewable, and not a second constitution for ordinary convenience.[S93][S127][S159][S160][S161][S162]

Another clarification now matters for avowal itself. The archive now distinguishes truthful self-implicating avowal from compelled confession and scripted recantation. Because righteousness already cares about answerability, institutions are often tempted to demand apology, admission, or ideological self-accusation as proof of seriousness, loyalty, rehabilitation, or public closure. But due-process and detention materials are morally revealing here: they treat compelled self-incrimination, confession extracted from custodial asymmetry, and statements produced by torture or coercive interrogation as serious defects, not clean paths to truth.[S91][S93][S127][S128][S129][S130][S131] So the archive now treats avowal as morally weighty only when it is free enough not to be authored by the very power it is supposed to answer.

Another clarification now matters for conscience and publicly demanded assent. The archive now distinguishes role-fitting oath and factual disclosure from compelled creed. Human-rights materials protect freedom of thought, conscience, religion, and opinion against coercion and even against compelled revelation of inward adherence; constitutional materials on compelled speech and test oaths warn that government may require support for constitutional duty while still lacking authority to prescribe orthodoxy or make office depend on ideological or religious profession.[S127][S132][S133][S134][S135][S136] So the archive now treats righteousness as burdened when office, schooling, work, safety, or ordinary civic belonging is conditioned on ideological profession, factional loyalty, religious test, or ritual assent beyond what truthful role performance actually requires.

Another clarification now matters for public power. Private righteousness can sometimes remain hidden or quiet. But offices and institutions cannot rely on inaccessible virtue, insider confidence, or sealed process alone. Where public power burdens, judges, excludes, or coerces, a righteousness claim normally requires contestable reasons, preserved record, challenge or review, and stronger substitute safeguards when secrecy blocks ordinary visibility.[S18][S32][S34][S35][S36]

Another clarification now matters for time. The archive has been treating righteousness as steadfast, but it can no longer leave that word vague. A single righteous act does not by itself prove settled righteous standing; a single grave failure does not by itself answer every later question about present standing or trajectory. The archive now distinguishes episode, standing, and trajectory so that one bright act cannot launder a bad pattern and one confessed failure cannot be treated as either instantly erased or permanently essence-fixing.[S03][S26][S30][S32][S33][S39]

Another clarification now matters for uncertainty. A righteousness claim should not be treated as sound merely because the actor cared about the good while facts were incomplete. Under material uncertainty, the archive now requires proportionate inquiry, fair uptake of testimony, explicit uncertainty, and action designed to remain reviewable, revisable, or repairable as evidence improves. This especially matters where harms may be grave, hard to reverse, or loaded onto the less powerful.[S34][S40][S41][S42]

Another clarification now matters for speech and disclosure. Righteousness is not satisfied by private sincerity, technically true wording, or benevolent tone when others are being steered by induced false belief, misleading implication, manipulative framing, or false reassurance. Because social and institutional life run heavily on testimony, the archive now prefers truthful candor, explicit uncertainty, or protected refusal over deceptive confidence; where secrecy is justified, it raises burdens of narrowing, oversight, later record, and repair rather than licensing falsehood.[S03][S34][S35][S72][S73][S74]

Another clarification now matters for time and procedure. The archive now treats delay, repeated procedural friction, and exhausting waiting not as morally empty background but as standing-relevant when they predictably turn valid claims into abandonment, capitulation, unmanaged exposure, or stale review. Administrative-law and human-rights materials are revealing here because they treat reasonable time, speediness, and compulsion of unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed action as part of what is due, not mere efficiency.[S34][S111][S112][S93][S114][S115]

Another clarification now matters for reasons and procedure. Righteousness is not satisfied merely because a clean label, facially neutral rule, or administratively valid pathway can be cited after the fact. The archive now treats pretext as a standing defect: where a stated rationale is contrived, selectively invoked, or not the real reason doing the work, formal compliance becomes form-laundering rather than justification. Proper-purpose use of process and genuinely explanatory reasons matter especially in office, where records, categories, and procedures can otherwise be used to hide retaliation, favoritism, targeting, or institutional self-protection behind respectable surfaces.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S100][S101][S102][S103]

Another clarification now matters for promise, oath, and assurance. Righteousness is burdened by self-assumed claims created through promise, vow, office undertaking, and induced reliance. But it is not promise-idolatry: a wrongful, corrupt, or unauthorized undertaking does not become righteous simply because it was promised, and when fidelity cannot rightly be rendered the burden shifts to timely avowal, release-seeking where possible, mitigation, and repair rather than quiet betrayal or literalistic performance.[S17][S26][S39][S48][S75][S76]

Another clarification now matters for gift and benefaction. The archive does not flatten gratitude into nothing; real beneficence can fittingly call for thanks, goodwill, or remembered help. But gifts, favors, hospitality, rescue, mentorship, and discretionary aid do not buy silence, deference, access, or preferential treatment. In public office especially, gift rules are morally revealing because they mark the point where benefaction threatens to privatize judgment, create donor dependence, or convert public trust into patronage.[S19][S32][S71][S77][S78][S79][S80]

Another clarification now matters for exploitation and consent. A transaction is not righteous merely because the weaker party said yes, signed, clicked through, or came out better than total abandonment. The archive now treats exploitation as a standing defect when the stronger party turns desperation, dependency, ignorance, or badly constrained options into extractive leverage; informed-consent materials are philosophically useful here because they show why real understanding, meaningful refusal, and anti-waiver guardrails matter when power is asymmetric.[S71][S81][S82][S83][S84]

Another clarification now matters for work and exit. The archive now distinguishes hard work from captive service: wages, prior agreement, migration, prison status, or nominal resignation rights do not by themselves make labour righteous when threat, debt, document seizure, withheld pay, confinement, or status dependence are doing the real work of keeping the person in place. Forced-labour materials are morally revealing because they define unfree service through involuntariness plus menace, list indicators such as document retention, withheld wages, and debt bondage, preserve only narrow civic and emergency exceptions, and reject using compulsory labour for political coercion, labour discipline, strike punishment, or discrimination.[S127][S137][S138][S139][S140][S141][S142]

Another clarification now matters for accusation and enforcement. A righteousness claim is burdened when it materially engineers the very breach later cited as evidence of guilt, danger, or disqualification. Undercover inquiry, integrity testing, or controlled offers can sometimes reveal a live wrongful disposition, but manufactured guilt, provoked breach, and baited failure do not yield clean standing merely because the target technically chose the act. Opportunity is not identical to incitement; where pressure, cultivated dependence, or strategic provocation did the real work, later condemnation becomes standing-defective.[S19][S29][S32][S34][S72][S81][S96][S97][S98][S99]

Another clarification now matters for mediated action. Righteousness may rely on delegates, contractors, committees, bureaucratic routines, or technical systems, but it cannot treat delegation as moral disappearance. The archive now treats claim-setting, guardrails, contestability, and repair ownership as at least partly nondelegable even when labor and discretion are distributed through proxies.[S17][S19][S34][S43][S44][S46]

Another clarification now matters for complaint and witness. A righteousness claim is burdened when complaint, testimony, disclosure, refusal of illegality, or cooperation with review is punished, chilled, or strategically burdened so that challenge becomes too costly to use. Anti-retaliation materials are morally revealing because they treat protected participation as part of maintaining access to remedial mechanisms, while anti-reprisal human-rights materials show that punished cooperation attacks the channels by which institutions learn, hear, and correct.[S34][S35][S36][S104][S105][S106][S107]

Another clarification now matters for record, proof, and future challenge. Righteousness is not vindicated merely because an institution can say today that no contrary evidence exists. The archive now treats cover-up, evidence suppression, and answerability sabotage as standing defects: where records are destroyed, key decisions are kept off the books, witnesses are pressured or corrupted, or evidence paths are intentionally damaged so that later review cannot fairly proceed, the wrong is not only falsehood but sabotage of the conditions of later truth.[S34][S35][S36][S121][S122][S123][S124][S125][S126]

Records-management, spoliation, obstruction, and investigative-integrity materials are morally revealing here because they treat preserved record and protected evidence not as administrative clutter but as part of protecting rights, enabling adjudication, and holding power answerable. Once challenge, litigation, investigation, or grave-harm inquiry is reasonably in view, deletion, selective nonmemorialization, witness interference, and evidence tampering are no longer neutral housekeeping choices.[S121][S122][S123][S124][S125][S126]

Another clarification now matters for coercive leverage through persons. The archive already rejects collective punishment, exploitation, and benevolent mastery, but it now adds a sharper rule: one person's custody, dependence, suffering, or protected presence may not be used mainly as leverage over another person's will. Hostage-taking and human-shields materials are morally revealing because they name a portable structure broader than war law alone: someone under one actor's control is turned into a bargaining chip or buffer so that a third party's fear, restraint, or sympathy does the real work.[S34][S88][S93][S116][S117][S118][S119][S120]

The archive does not thereby collapse every negotiation, release condition, or security precaution into the same category. The narrower point is that lawful authority over A does not license using A as collateral pressure on B. Where family detention, threatened continuation of confinement, denied access, exposed civilian presence, or other controlled vulnerability is being used mainly to compel, immunize, or extract concession from someone else, righteousness is usually not governing through answerability but through innocent leverage.[S19][S32][S34][S93][S116][S117][S118][S119][S120]

Another clarification now matters for notice and timing. A righteousness claim is burdened when serious accusation, discipline, exclusion, or penalty rests on hidden, retroactive, or unduly vague standards that denied ordinary agents a fair chance to know, contest, or conform beforehand. Rule-of-law and legality materials are morally revealing here because they connect prospectivity, promulgation, and fair warning not merely to technical legality but to governance that addresses people as planners rather than trapping them by ad hoc power.[S18][S32][S34][S35][S34][S109][S110][S93]

Another clarification now matters for blame, sanction, and inherited taint. The archive now rejects collective punishment, corruption of blood, and guilt by mere association: kinship, constituency, demographic overlap, or organizational proximity do not by themselves carry guilt, punishment, or civic disability. But righteousness also rejects fake moral atomism. Real group authorship, office responsibility, knowing participation, or retained tainted benefit can still generate burdens of answerability, remedy, or withdrawal. The question is not only who belonged, but who acted, authorized, knew, benefited, or still owed once the wrong was visible.[S19][S43][S88][S89][S90]


Another clarification now matters for rules and particulars. Righteousness normally prefers general, public, and reviewable rules because they check arbitrary power. But rules are general and cases are particular. So the archive now admits an equity rule: sometimes righteousness requires openly correcting a rule's underfit to the particulars, provided the correction is tied to the rule's point, limited to morally relevant differences, and constrained by like-cases discipline, record, and review rather than favoritist exception.[S01][S34][S50][S51]

Another clarification now matters for compromised roles. Righteousness is not always continued compliant performance from within a role or institution. When ordinary participation would materially launder abuse, lend false standing to a captured process, or block due answerability, righteousness may require interruption: truthful objection, recusal, protected disclosure, resignation, withdrawal of cooperation, or in burdened last-resort cases public disobedient witness. These acts do not certify themselves merely by being oppositional; they remain answerable to truth, proportion, and later review.[S16][S19][S30][S32][S34][S52][S53]

Another clarification now matters once wrong has been named and sanction is live. Righteousness does not prove itself by maximum exaction, humiliation, or severity theater. Nor may it rename connected leniency or unrepaired waiver as mercy. The archive now treats righteous sanction as censure, protection, and repair-compatible burden rather than sheer suffering, and righteous mercy as authority-side restraint that does not pressure victims, falsify the wrong, or reopen favoritist impunity.[S19][S26][S34][S54][S55][S56]

Another clarification now matters for humiliation and degradation themselves. Even when discipline, custody, exposure, or public naming is justified, righteousness is burdened — and often defeated — when it governs through abasement, humiliative spectacle, degrading treatment, or contempt-driven display. Dignity and recognition literatures matter here because humiliation can attack a person's standing rather than merely wound their feelings; human-rights materials matter because they pair anti-degrading-treatment rules with affirmative dignity burdens in custody. The archive therefore now adds a nonabasement rule: persons do not stop being claim-bearers when they are guilty, dependent, detained, defeated, or publicly discredited.[S19][S32][S34][S54][S91][S92][S93][S94]

Another clarification now matters for self-application. The archive has long treated hypocritical self-exemption as a defeater, but it now sharpens the point: righteousness is burdened when a norm is enforced mainly downward, outward, or against rivals while insiders, allies, officeholders, donors, or one's own side receive indulgence without a publicly relevant difference. Equal burden does not mean identical burden in every case; some roles rightly carry heavier duties and some vulnerabilities justify different protection. But side, rank, and convenience do not by themselves justify one law for others and another for self.[S17][S19][S30][S32][S34][S51][S95]

Another clarification now matters for access and compliance. The archive already rejects fake neutrality and over-the-head governance, but it now sharpens a separate defect: institutions can turn avoidable barriers into proof of noncooperation, incapacity, unfitness, or waived claim. Disability-justice and accommodation materials are useful here because they treat apparently neutral environments, rules, and communication channels as morally loaded when they block equal participation, and major disability-rights materials go further by treating denial of reasonable accommodation as discrimination itself. So the archive now adds an accommodation rule: identical treatment is not enough where participation, communication, hearing, work, or claim-making are inaccessible and the stronger party could reasonably modify the channel, provide an equally effective alternative, or route around the barrier instead.[S34][S51][S66][S67][S143][S67][S145][S146][S147]

Another clarification now matters for who decides. The archive already requires hearing, contestability, recusal, and anti-capture, but it now sharpens a distinct defect: a claim to righteousness is burdened when the accuser, beneficiary, implicated office, or materially interested superior also controls the verdict or the appeal. Fair-hearing and due-process materials are useful here because they do not rely only on proof of subjective bad faith; they insist on competent, independent, and impartial tribunals and on objective rules against too-high risks of bias, including situations where the same actor effectively appears as both accuser and adjudicator. So the archive now adds an anti-self-judging rule: righteous office should prefer recusal, structural separation, preserved record, and independent review over self-ratifying internal confidence.[S32][S34][S68][S93][S114][S150][S151][S152]

Another clarification now matters for visibility itself. The archive already rejects compelled creed, reprisal, humiliation, and cover-up, but it now sharpens a separate defect: a person or governed population can be wronged by being made more searchable, trackable, exposable, or permanently knowable than the task really requires. Privacy materials are morally revealing here because they treat arbitrary interference, overbroad surveillance, and data-intensive behavior tracking as threats not only to secrecy but also to dignity, autonomy, belief, association, and participation; data-protection materials add a practical compression rule by preferring anonymous or minimized data, purpose-fit collection, and time-bounded retention over indiscriminate legibility. So the archive now adds a proportional-legibility rule: righteous institutions need enough record to answer for what they do, but they should not solve every safety, review, or management problem by making persons maximally visible.[S34][S93][S114][S153][S154][S155][S156][S157][S158]

Another clarification now matters for office itself. The archive has been saying that office is entrusted power, but it now sharpens that idea: office powers are **held rather than owned**. The public-trust cue is useful here because existing ethics rules say public service is a public trust and prohibit using office, nonpublic information, or conflicting interests for private advantage; fiduciary-political theory is useful because it sharpens loyalty, care, accountability, and anti-appropriation while also warning against simplistic importation from private fiduciary law.[S18][S19][S32][S39][S57][S58]


Another clarification now matters for office-performance itself. Even a well-aimed public act should not count as righteous office if it was carried out by the wrong bearer, beyond the office's remit, or without the competence and authorization needed to impose the burden in that way. The archive now treats jurisdiction, competence, and authority bounds as part of righteousness rather than as mere technical afterthoughts. This keeps morally serious interruption distinct from office usurpation and blocks the fantasy that a good goal purifies unauthorized public power.[S18][S32][S34][S52][S59][S60][S61]

Another clarification now matters for who is made present in judgment. Public reasons and later review are not enough if those most burdened remain structurally absent from the decision that names and allocates their burden. Where public power materially burdens identifiable people or communities, righteousness now normally requires a domain-fit way of making the burdened present: direct hearing, meaningful participatory channel, accountable representation, or a justified emergency substitute that remains open to later challenge and repair. Decisions taken simply over their heads are now treated as presumptively standing-defective even when benevolent in aim.[S35][S40][S45][S63][S64]

Another clarification now matters when the burdened cannot directly appear at all. The archive now states that absent claimants are not absent claims: where children, persons needing support, diffuse publics, or future persons are materially burdened, righteousness requires support for direct agency where possible, accountable proxy voice where necessary, and stewardship rather than self-authorized ventriloquism where no present speaker can fully stand in for those affected.[S45][S63][S65][S66][S67]

Another clarification now matters for special ties. The archive now rejects both flat impartiality and unrestricted loyalty. Family, friendship, promise, dependency, and entrusted representation can thicken what is due; but they do not erase baseline due to outsiders, and in offices structured for adjudication, allocation, or discipline, private ties often trigger recusal, anti-preferential-treatment constraints, or anti-nepotism barriers rather than righteous preference.[S17][S32][S45][S68][S69]

Another clarification now matters for power asymmetry itself. The archive now refuses to equate righteous order with gentle administration from above. Where one party remains vulnerable to another's uncontrolled discretion, retaliation, or withdrawal of basic security, domination remains a standing problem even if direct interference is infrequent. So the archive now treats anti-domination as a sharpening rule: righteousness is not exhausted by non-domination, but benevolent mastery is not enough.[S34][S49][S70][S71]

Another clarification now matters for conflict itself. The archive no longer says only that valid claims can collide and leave a remainder. It now adds a starter set of **portable priority cues**: serious harm and urgent need normally press harder than gain or convenience; dependency and vulnerability intensify a claim; voluntarily induced reliance and office-generated expectation intensify it further; self-created conflicts are burdened rather than morally neutral; and hard-to-repair burdens need stronger reasons before they are shifted onto others.[S17][S38][S41][S47][S48][S49]

Another clarification now matters for the floor beneath conflict. The archive has been using need and subsistence as priority cues, but it now makes the point explicit: food, water, basic shelter or housing, and essential care are not merely ordinary preferences to be balanced against optics, convenience, or administrative face-saving. Covenant and OHCHR materials are morally revealing here because they tie dignity to adequate standard of living, freedom from hunger, accessibility and affordability of water, and the duty to prevent homelessness rather than merely manage it. Scarcity can require triage, but it does not make abandonment righteous. So the archive now adds a subsistence-floor rule: where persons or peoples are left without reasonable access to the essentials of a minimally decent life, the righteousness claim is heavily burdened and often defeated unless the claimant can show fair ranking, visible floor protection, and answerable remainder under real scarcity.[S34][S41][S47][S49][S163][S164][S165][S166][S167][S168][S169]


Another clarification now matters for the body itself. The archive already rejected punitive noncare, inaccessible compliance, civil death by guardianship, and status-ranked standing, but it had not yet said plainly that bodily and reproductive life cannot be governed through permission, punishment, or substitute judgment without triggering a standing defect. Current OHCHR sexual-and-reproductive-health materials are morally revealing because they frame these rights as freedoms to make decisions and choices regarding one's body free of violence, coercion, and discrimination plus entitlements to accessible, non-discriminatory, good-quality care; the Working Group says autonomous decisions about one's body and reproductive functions sit at the core of equality, privacy, and bodily integrity and that the decision whether to continue or terminate a pregnancy is fundamentally and primarily the woman's decision; the Special Rapporteur on health warns that restrictions on abortion, contraception, family planning, and information can violate health, dignity, and bodily integrity; CEDAW General Recommendation No. 24 treats refusal to legally provide certain reproductive-health services and prejudicial third-party authorization requirements as discriminatory; and CRPD General Comment No. 3 warns that restricted legal capacity can facilitate forced sterilization, abortion, and contraception for women and girls with disabilities.[S306][S307][S308][S309][S310][S311] So the archive now adds a no-body-by-compulsion rule: where pregnancy, contraception, abortion, sterilization, fertility, or intimate medical choice are governed mainly through criminal threat, parental or spousal permission, coerced intervention, or substitute judgment because the institution distrusts the burdened person's own standing, the righteousness claim is heavily burdened and often defeated unless unusually strong necessity, genuine support for agency, and live remedy can be shown.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S63][S64][S66][S67][S71][S82][S83][S84][S93][S145][S146][S147][S153][S154][S164][S232][S233][S256][S257][S306][S307][S308][S309][S310][S311]

Another clarification now matters for home itself. The archive already rejected subsistence abandonment, displacement, and humiliation, but it had not yet said plainly that adequate housing is not exhausted by a cot, a permit, or temporary permission to remain until the next sweep. Housing materials are morally revealing because they define adequate housing as living somewhere in security, peace, and dignity; stress security of tenure, affordability, habitability, services, accessibility, and location; and reject both forced eviction and the criminalization or clearance of homelessness as substitutes for meeting the claim. So the archive now adds a housing rule: where people are made homeless, eviction-vulnerable, or clearance-exposed mainly to simplify control, urban image, creditor leverage, or administrative tidiness, the righteousness claim is heavily burdened and often defeated unless serious alternative-testing, due process, preservation of dignity and belongings, and a credible path to secure housing rather than repeated emergency shelter can be shown.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S93][S164][S167][S172][S236][S237]

Another clarification now matters for the service infrastructure beneath survival. The archive already rejected subsistence abandonment, housing precarity, punitive noncare, and welfare by sanction, but it still lacked a direct rule for when thirst, unsafe sanitation, or disease exposure are governed through water systems themselves. Current OHCHR materials are morally revealing because they treat water and sanitation as basic human rights indispensable to dignity, require availability, accessibility, affordability, safety, acceptability, and priority to those most in need, insist that no individual or group should be denied safe drinking water because they cannot afford to pay, and increasingly frame sanitation as private, dignified, hygiene-bearing, and inclusive of wastewater treatment itself.[S261][S166][S263][S264][S266][S267] The archive therefore now adds a water-and-sanitation rule: where safe water, sanitation, hygiene, or wastewater protection are governed mainly through shutoff, unaffordability, contamination, inaccessible design, discriminatory siting, or humiliating dependence because carrying the exposed is costlier than protecting them, the righteousness claim is heavily burdened and often defeated unless genuine hardship protection, equal concern, public reason, and live remedy can be shown.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S66][S67][S93][S166][S261][S166][S263][S264][S265][S266][S267][S268]

Another clarification now matters for food itself. The archive already rejected subsistence abandonment, collective deprivation, punitive noncare, welfare by sanction, and thirst-by-administration, but it still lacked a direct rule for when hunger, inadequate nutrition, or meal insecurity are governed through food systems themselves. Current OHCHR food materials are morally revealing because they define the right through availability, accessibility, adequacy, and sustainability; the Special Rapporteur's current framing adds regular and unrestricted access to adequate food for a dignified life and emphasizes minimum-core duties to alleviate hunger; the 2025 finance report warns that debt crisis, inflation, and high food prices can push governments into feeding-people-versus-servicing-debt choices; and the 2025 corporate-power report says food-system power can be concentrated around profit rather than the public good.[S269][S165][S271][S272][S273] The archive therefore now adds a food-and-nutrition rule: where adequate food is governed mainly through unaffordability, rationing design, opaque eligibility, punitive administration, concentrated market power, or discretionary feeding mercy such that some people predictably live through hunger or malnutrition because carrying them was treated as optional, the righteousness claim is heavily burdened and often defeated unless equal concern, hardship protection, fair priority, and live remedy can be shown.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S66][S67][S93][S163][S164][S167][S269][S165][S271][S272][S273][S274]

Another clarification now matters for movement and removal. The archive already rejected collective punishment, subsistence abandonment, and over-the-head rule, but it now sharpens a distinct defect: solving conflict, danger, or administrative inconvenience by uprooting people. Displacement and collective-expulsion materials are morally revealing because they protect against arbitrary displacement, forbid using movement as collective punishment or demographic engineering, insist on individualized examination where expulsion is in view, and keep removal tied to safety, legal remedy, family-preserving conditions, and return or restitution rather than to warehousing or disappearance. So the archive now adds an anti-displacement rule: where persons, families, or populations are removed, expelled, or blocked from return mainly to simplify control, alter composition, retaliate, or export risk, the righteousness claim is heavily burdened and often defeated unless necessity, explored alternatives, dignified conditions, and a real path out of displacement can be shown.[S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S88][S89][S170][S171][S172][S173]

Another clarification now matters for return across borders. The archive already rejected displacement, disappearance architecture, and conviction-by-opacity, but it now sharpens a distinct defect: solving migration, extradition, or border pressure by handing a person over into persecution, torture, or comparable irreparable harm, or by denying the individualized protection assessment needed to know whether such danger is present. Refugee, anti-torture, and OHCHR return/border materials are morally revealing because they prohibit expulsion or return into threatened life, freedom, or torture danger; insist that borders are not zones of exception; and treat individualized protection assessment as part of what humane return requires. So the archive now adds a non-refoulement rule: where a person is removed, rejected at the frontier, pushed back, or routed through a third-country arrangement mainly by exporting risk rather than carrying it, the righteousness claim is heavily burdened and often defeated unless real safety, usable procedure, and non-opaque review can be shown.[S34][S35][S41][S93][S128][S171][S206][S207][S129][S208][S210][S211]
Another clarification now matters for political membership itself. The archive already rejected rightlessness-by-paper, displacement, and risk export, but it now sharpens a distinct defect: solving danger, disfavored identity, or demographic anxiety by making a person legally unreal to the state that should answer for them. Nationality and statelessness materials are morally revealing because they treat nationality as a fundamental human right, insist that states prevent statelessness at birth and on loss or deprivation of nationality, and show how discrimination against minorities and women can turn membership rules into engines of exclusion. So the archive now adds an anti-statelessness rule: where nationality is denied, stripped, or withheld mainly so that a person becomes removable, immobilized, family-fractured, or civically unreal, the righteousness claim is heavily burdened and often defeated unless non-discrimination, review, live safeguards against statelessness, and a real path to legal belonging can be shown.[S34][S35][S41][S66][S93][S170][S171][S185][S186][S208][S212][S213][S214][S215][S216]


Another clarification now matters for durable organized life. The archive already rejected order-through-suppression and silencing by power, but it now sharpens a distinct defect: making collective organization itself depend on sufferance, capture, or retaliation. Article 22 and OHCHR materials are morally revealing because they treat association, including trade unions, as a right to interact and organize in order to pursue and defend common interests rather than as a ruler-granted privilege; ILO association and collective-bargaining conventions sharpen the same point by rejecting prior authorization, protecting organizational independence, forbidding anti-union discrimination and employer interference, and promoting genuine collective bargaining. So the archive now adds an anti-sufferance rule for association: where people may organize only through obedient bodies, selective permission, or punishing personal risk, the righteousness claim is heavily burdened and often defeated unless narrow necessity, real independence, anti-retaliation protection, and practical room for collective self-organization can be shown.[S34][S35][S41][S93][S104][S105][S106][S107][S218][S222][S223]


## Current sharpened answer

The archive now leans toward a non-flat architecture:

> righteousness is **relation-first, person-carried, and institution-shaped**.[S01][S08][S15][S16]

That is, relations and claim-structures explain what is due; persons bear the immediate burden of answerability; and institutions make righteous or unrighteous response more or less realistic at scale. Public office is the clearest place where all three layers become visible at once.

Another clarification now matters for pressure through populations. The archive has long rejected collective punishment in passing, but it now gives the rule its own sharper surface. Collective-penalties materials are morally revealing because they forbid punishing persons for offences they did not personally commit, while starvation, relief, and siege materials show how whole populations can be made to carry hunger, blocked aid, immobility, or infrastructural collapse so that pressure accumulates on others.[S88][S89][S88][S175][S176][S177][S178]

Another clarification now matters for family relation under power. The archive now treats family separation, blocked contact, concealed whereabouts, and delayed reunification as standing-relevant burdens rather than as morally empty byproducts of detention, migration control, child-protection procedure, or emergency administration. Child-rights, detention-principle, humanitarian-tracing, and ICCPR materials are morally revealing here because they converge on a narrower rule: separation may sometimes be justified for protection or other concrete necessity, but not mainly to punish, deter, simplify control, or make institutions easier to run; once the State causes or prolongs separation, it acquires live burdens of notice, contact, and humane reunion rather than discretionary grace.[S34][S35][S66][S128][S180][S93]

Another clarification now matters for care itself under power. The archive now treats denied treatment, delayed referral, blocked medication, attacks on health infrastructure, and coercive capture of clinicians as standing-relevant burdens rather than as morally empty side effects of detention, security management, scarcity, or war. Right-to-health, medical-ethics, WHO, and Geneva materials are morally revealing here because they converge on a narrower rule: care may be triaged or limited for genuine medical and public-health reasons, but not mainly to punish, deter, sort by status, or make untreated suffering do the work of control; and those engaged in medical duties may not be punished for treating the person in need.[S34][S35][S41][S128][S164][S168][S181][S182][S183][S184]

Another clarification now matters for civil recognition under ordinary administration. The archive now treats birth registration, proof of legal identity, and access to correctable documentation as standing-relevant burdens rather than as morally empty paperwork. ICCPR, CRC, OHCHR, legal-identity, and ID-system materials are morally revealing here because they converge on a narrower rule: institutions may verify identity, residence, family link, or eligibility where those questions matter, but they may not make legal existence, ordinary services, or family continuity depend on papers that are discriminatorily unavailable, impossible to correct, confiscable, or digitally exclusionary. So the archive now adds an anti-rightlessness-by-paper rule: once documentary burden is doing the real work of exclusion, dependency, or invisibility before institutions, the righteousness claim is heavily burdened and often defeated unless universalizing access, realistic fallback, correction, and privacy discipline can be shown.[S34][S35][S66][S93][S153][S154][S157][S185][S186][S187][S188][S189][S190]

Another clarification now matters for custody before it becomes disappearance or overt cruelty. The archive now treats arbitrary detention, prolonged administrative confinement, and pretrial custody-by-default as standing-relevant burdens rather than as morally empty consequences of accusation, migration control, psychiatric crisis, quarantine, or security management. Article 9 and detention-principle materials are morally revealing here because they prohibit arbitrary arrest or detention, require reasons, prompt judicial control, and court challenge, and insist that detention not become an administratively easy substitute for narrower alternatives; OHCHR's current arbitrary-detention guidance sharpens the same point by treating arbitrariness as broader than bare illegality and by extending the burden to administrative detention as well.[S34][S35][S41][S93][S127][S128][S199][S200]

Another clarification now matters for force at the point where coercion becomes death. The archive now treats arbitrary killing, lethal-force-by-convenience, and death in custody or enforcement operations as standing-relevant burdens rather than as morally empty endpoints of danger, flight, public-order stress, border control, or security management. Right-to-life, force-and-firearms, and unlawful-death-investigation materials are morally revealing here because they converge on a stricter rule: life may not be taken merely because killing is faster, cleaner, or more controllable for the stronger party; non-violent or narrower means must remain first where realistically available, firearms are tightly restricted in law-enforcement settings, and every potentially unlawful death triggers duties of investigation and truthful accounting rather than euphemism or self-exoneration.[S34][S35][S41][S93][S201][S202][S203][S204][S205]

Another clarification now matters when accusation hardens into judgment. The archive already rejected trapdoor norms, compelled confession, interested adjudication, and delay as separate defects, but it now names the further defect they can jointly produce: conviction-by-opacity. Fair-trial materials are morally revealing here because they treat equality before courts and tribunals, competent and impartial decision, adequate notice, time and facilities for defence, counsel, witness challenge, interpretation, and review as part of what a valid serious judgment requires rather than as procedural ornament.[S93][S128][S151][S206][S207] So the archive now treats serious adjudication as standing-defective when the burdened person cannot meaningfully know, answer, test, or appeal the case against them, even if the process keeps the outer grammar of legality.

Another clarification now matters for custody plus concealment. The archive now treats secret detention, concealed transfer, false release story, unrecorded custody, and blocked locating channels as standing-relevant burdens rather than as morally empty byproducts of investigation, security, intelligence, or emergency procedure. Disappearance materials are morally revealing here because they converge on a sharper rule: a person may sometimes be detained or some information may sometimes be temporarily withheld, but power may not make the person legally unfindable by refusing to acknowledge custody, hiding fate or whereabouts, blocking family or court access, or falsifying the records through which law keeps hold of the detained.[S34][S35][S93][S128][S180][S191][S192][S193][S194]

Another clarification now matters for pain and fear under power. The archive now treats torture, cruel treatment, and degrading treatment as standing-relevant burdens rather than as morally empty side effects of interrogation, discipline, extraction, transport, detention, riot control, or emergency management. CAT and ICCPR materials are morally revealing here because they converge on a narrower rule: severe pain or suffering may not be intentionally used to obtain information or confession, punish, intimidate, coerce, or discriminate; Article 7 remains non-derogable even in public emergency; and cruel or degrading treatment remains prohibited even where the torture threshold is disputed. Mandela, OPCAT, and Istanbul-Protocol materials add the practical point that righteous order is not answered by a bare ban on paper: it also requires inspection, complaint, documentation, and impartial investigation strong enough that power cannot make suffering its instrument and then hide behind damaged records or institutional secrecy.[S93][S94][S129][S195][S196][S197][S198]

So the archive now adds an anti-collective-punishment rule: broad restrictions may sometimes be genuinely protective, temporary, and tightly justified, but righteousness is burdened once civilians are being made to absorb serious hardship mainly to punish, intimidate, or coerce persons within the group. Siege, closure, blockade, or emergency language does not cleanse that defect; the harder question is whether deprivation itself has become the method.[S34][S41][S88][S89][S88][S175][S176][S177][S178]

Another clarification now matters for public dissent in visible form. The archive already rejected arbitrary detention, lethal-force-by-convenience, reprisal, and conviction-by-opacity, but it now sharpens a distinct defect: solving criticism, oppositional visibility, or civic friction by suppressing peaceful assembly itself. Article 21 materials are morally revealing because they treat assembly as a recognized right rather than a discretionary favor, protect spontaneous and online participation, presume assemblies peaceful, and insist that restrictions be lawful, necessary, and proportionate rather than blanket or convenience-driven; force-and-firearms materials add that non-violent means should come first, that unlawful but non-violent assemblies should be handled with minimal force if force is unavoidable, and that firearms remain tightly confined to the strict life-protective conditions already governing lethal force generally. So the archive now adds an assembly rule: where offices treat protest, vigil, strike, march, sit-in, or other peaceful gathering mainly as a visibility problem to be erased rather than a claim-bearing practice to be facilitated and protected, the righteousness claim is heavily burdened and often defeated unless narrow necessity, differentiated protection, and answerable restraint can be shown.[S34][S35][S41][S93][S202][S217][S218]

Another clarification now matters for public criticism and public knowledge. The archive already rejected deception, cover-up, and protest suppression, but it had not yet given itself a clean positive rule for speech and press. Article 19 materials are morally revealing here because they protect opinion from interference, protect seeking, receiving, and imparting information through any media, and treat access to public information, media freedom, and journalist safety as part of the conditions of accountability rather than as decorative liberties.[S93][S127][S219][S220][S221] So the archive now treats righteousness as burdened when power silences criticism, reporting, or access to public-interest information mainly to avoid scrutiny, monopolize narrative, or make answerability practically unusable.

Another clarification now matters for public rule itself. The archive already rejected silencing, assembly suppression, and association-through-sufferance, but it had not yet given itself a direct rule for participation in public affairs. Article 25 materials are morally revealing here because they protect participation in public affairs, voting, candidacy, and equal access to public service, treat effective opportunity rather than paper permission as the relevant burden, and require genuine periodic elections under universal and equal suffrage, secret ballot, independent supervision, and reviewable integrity. OHCHR participation materials add the practical point that discrimination, inaccessible registration, intimidation, and the weakening of expression, association, assembly, movement, information, or consultation rights can hollow participation out without formally abolishing it. So the archive now treats righteousness as burdened when rulers preserve authority by engineering who can vote, stand, serve, or influence public affairs in practice; managed inclusion is not the same as public rule.[S18][S19][S34][S35][S41][S93][S218][S219][S224][S225][S226]

Another clarification now matters for conviction and worship themselves. The archive already rejects compelled creed, no-silencing-by-power, and no-rule-by-exclusion, but it now sharpens a broader defect: power can govern by deciding whose religion or belief may be held, changed, manifested, or publicly lived without precarity. Article 18 materials are useful here because they distinguish the unlimitable inward forum from the limitable manifestation of religion or belief, protect theistic, non-theistic, and atheistic commitments, and prohibit coercion that impairs freedom to have or adopt a religion or belief of one's choice; the declaration and current OHCHR materials add that discrimination on religion-or-belief grounds and undue interference with worship or observance are not side-issues but direct standing defects.[S127][S132][S227][S228][S229] So the archive now adds a no-orthodoxy-by-power rule: public order may sometimes justify narrow limits on manifestation, but righteousness is heavily burdened once safety, schooling, office, custody, or ordinary civic standing are distributed through forced conformity, anti-conversion pressure, selective permission, or managed precarity for disfavored believers and nonbelievers.[S18][S19][S34][S35][S41][S127][S132][S227][S228][S229]


Another clarification now matters for ordinary equal standing across domains. The archive already rejected double standards, inaccessible compliance, rightlessness-by-paper, and no-rule-by-exclusion, but it now sharpens the more general defect those surfaces kept depending on: power can sort whose life counts as ordinary by status or by neutral-seeming proxies that keep doing status work. Equality materials are morally revealing because current OHCHR guidance treats equality and non-discrimination as bedrock principles requiring comprehensive anti-discrimination law, ICCPR Article 26 protects equal and effective protection of the law for all persons, CRPD article 5 adds reasonable accommodation and permits specific measures for de facto equality, and ICERD clarifies that discrimination can be defined by purpose or effect while allowing advancement-oriented special measures.[S67][S93][S232][S233] So the archive now adds a no-status-ranked-standing rule: righteousness is heavily burdened when safety, burden, opportunity, remedy, movement, work, care, education, or civic belonging are distributed by protected status or by facially neutral design whose practical effect is subordination, exclusion, or thinner benefit of the law.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S66][S67][S93][S145][S146][S147][S232][S233]

Another clarification now matters for ordinary movement and ordinary reentry. The archive already rejected displacement, no-risk-export, and exile-by-paper, but it now sharpens the opposite defect: power can govern not by uprooting people but by trapping them. Article 12 materials are morally revealing because they protect movement within the territory, choice of residence, leaving any country, and return to one’s own country, while General Comment No. 27 insists that restrictions be interpreted narrowly, not nullify liberty, not impair the essence of the right, and not turn documents, permissions, or no-return measures into hidden instruments of immobilization.[S93][S127][S230] OHCHR’s current internally displaced persons guidance adds that even after displacement, people retain movement and residence rights and are owed assistance toward voluntary, safe, and dignified return, resettlement, or local integration.[S231] So the archive now adds a movement-and-return rule: where offices solve danger, dissent, scarcity, or administrative inconvenience mainly by closure, encampment, exit denial, checkpointed immobility, or arbitrary no-return, the righteousness claim is heavily burdened and often defeated unless narrow necessity, temporariness, review, and a real path back to ordinary mobility can be shown.[S18][S19][S34][S35][S41][S93][S127][S170][S173][S230][S231]


Another clarification now matters for childhood and dependency. Another clarification now matters for algorithmic governance itself. The archive already rejected opacity, self-review, privacy overreach, status-ranked standing, rightlessness-by-paper, destitution by sanction, schooling by exclusion, and livelihood by domination, but it still lacked a direct rule for when those burdens are routed through scoring, profiling, recommender systems, biometric gates, and automated decision tools themselves. Another clarification now matters for personhood in law itself. Another clarification now matters for environmental health itself. Another clarification now matters for land and territory themselves. Another clarification now matters for shared life itself. Another clarification now matters for office itself at the point where service and protection become saleable. Another clarification now matters for private power and outsourced governance. The archive already rejected delegation as a moral solvent, public-trust privatization, no-office-by-sale, no-rights-by-austerity, and no-rights-without-redress, but it still lacked a direct rule for when states or lead enterprises govern through contractors, concessionaires, platforms, school chains, landlords, utilities, or supply chains while claiming the decisive burden was only a market outcome or vendor failure. A further rule now matters for intimate and household life. Another clarification now matters where institutions or platforms gain their practical advantage by quietly changing the amount a person must pay. Current official materials are morally revealing because the FTC's surveillance-pricing work says personal and behavioural data such as location, browser history, shopping patterns, and mouse movements can be used to tailor prices or promotions to individual consumers,[S464][S465] EU consumer materials now require traders to inform consumers when a price is personalised on the basis of automated decision-making,[S466] and OECD materials say consumers hardly ever noticed personalised price disclosures and that complementary protections are often necessary.[S467] The archive therefore now adds a surveillance-pricing / personalised-pricing / no-price-by-profile rule: where hidden profile inference does the real work of setting what this person must pay for materially the same good, service, or opportunity rather than leaving price on public, contestable, and general enough terms, righteousness is heavily burdened and often defeated.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S464][S465][S466][S467]

Another clarification now matters where speech is not silenced mainly by formal prohibition or one explicit takedown, but by the quieter governance of visibility itself. The archive already rejected no-silencing-by-power, no-rightlessness-by-model, and no-agency-by-stealth, but it still lacked a direct rule for when major platforms and comparable intermediaries make public voice depend on opaque moderation, secret demotion, or profiling-based recommendation. Current Commission materials are morally revealing because the DSA transparency materials require statements of reasons for removals and restrictions and make them publicly scrutinisable through the DSA Transparency Database,[S492] the Commission's current user-rights factpage says affected users must receive understandable moderation explanations, may use internal complaint systems, may turn to independent dispute-settlement bodies, and must be told the main parameters of recommendation systems while very large platforms must offer at least one non-profiling option,[S493] the Commission's February 2026 DSA update says users have already secured reversals in almost 50 million moderation decisions affecting content or accounts and notes that 99% of first-half 2025 moderation decisions were taken under platforms' own terms and conditions,[S494] and Commission dispute-settlement materials preserve a low-cost contest route in addition to courts.[S495] So the archive now adds a platform-moderation / recommender-systems / no-speech-by-secret-ranking rule: where visibility, reach, or account standing in major digital speech environments are substantially governed through opaque moderation, hidden ranking control, profiling-based recommendation, or weak redress rather than clear rules, stated reasons, user choice, and contestable review, the righteousness claim is heavily burdened and often defeated.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S127][S219][S492][S493][S494][S495]

Another clarification now matters where institutions claim they are merely modernizing by moving access onto portals, apps, and digital-identity flows. The archive already rejected no-rightlessness-by-paper and no-rightlessness-by-disconnection, but it had not yet said plainly that a right can remain valid while becoming practically unreachable once the ordinary route is a portal the burdened person cannot reliably traverse. Current official materials now make the defect visible enough to name: DOJ guidance says inaccessible online forms can block access to critical government services including voting registration, public education, benefits, and hearings; the Commission's web-accessibility materials require accessible public websites and apps with feedback and alternatives; Interoperable Europe principles say inclusion and accessibility often require multi-channel delivery, including paper or face-to-face coexistence; and the UK's current digital-inclusion and digital-identity materials say services need offline options and must not leave behind people who struggle with technology or lack passport-style ID.[S501][S502][S503][S504][S505] So the archive now adds a portal rule: where the real burden shifts from the underlying claim to a person's ability to clear an app, portal, or proofing gate without reachable human, proxy, or offline support, righteousness is heavily burdened and often defeated unless the digital route is genuinely accessible, failure-tolerant, and not the only practical path.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S501][S502][S503][S504][S505]

Another clarification now matters where child protection and lawful age control begin to normalize identity-demanding gates. The archive already rejected no-thought-by-extraction, no-face-library-by-hoovering, no-rights-by-portal, and no-agency-by-stealth, but it still lacked a direct rule for when protecting minors is made to depend on full-ID disclosure, retained biometric or profiling traces, cross-service linkage, or opaque age error. Current official materials are useful here because the Commission's age-verification work aims to let users prove they are over 18 without sharing any other personal information,[S506] the protection-of-minors guidelines explicitly pair child safety with privacy and security,[S507] Ofcom now presses major platforms to enforce minimum ages with highly effective age checks,[S508] and ICO materials insist that age-assurance methods be risk-based, fair, transparent, data-minimizing, secure, and challengeable.[S509][S510] The archive therefore now adds a no-child-safety-by-compulsory-identification rule: where a stronger identity demand or unreviewable age classification does the real work of access rather than bounded proof of only the age fact the setting truly needs, righteousness is heavily burdened and often defeated.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S506][S507][S508][S509][S510]

Another clarification now matters where ordinary movement is turned into a marketable reservoir. The archive already rejected disproportionate legibility, no-face-library-by-hoovering, and no-status-by-body-signal, but it still lacked a direct rule for when apps, ad-tech systems, and brokers sell or segment precise location traces so that other actors can buy who visited clinics, churches, shelters, protests, schools, union offices, military sites, or other sensitive places. Current official materials now make that defect plain enough to name: the FTC's Mobilewalla, Gravy/Venntel, and X-Mode actions say sensitive location sales can reveal healthcare visits, places of worship, home location, political or union activity, and other highly personal routines,[S511][S512][S513] while California's Attorney General now warns that location data can track people's precise movements without them realizing it and can expose sensitive visits and everyday habits.[S514] The archive therefore now adds a no-movement-by-sale rule: where commercially transferable movement traces or geofenced audience lists do the real work of exposure, targeting, profiling, or exclusion rather than leaving movement legible only under bounded and contestable grounds, righteousness is heavily burdened and often defeated.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S511][S512][S513][S514]
