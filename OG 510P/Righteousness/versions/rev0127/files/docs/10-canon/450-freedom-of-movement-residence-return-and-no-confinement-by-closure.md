# Freedom of Movement, Residence, Return, and No-Confinement-by-Closure

## Why this surface matters

The archive already rejects displacement, no-risk-export, exile-by-paper, arbitrary detention, protest suppression, and rightlessness-by-paper. What it still lacked was a compact rule for **what happens when power governs by trapping rather than by uprooting**: checkpoint grids that make ordinary life impossible, encampment or district-confinement that turns whole populations into administratively parked bodies, passport denial or confiscation used to control dissent or dependency, internal-permit systems that decide where one may live, no-return orders that quietly become de facto banishment, or border and emergency closures that operate less like targeted protection than like population immobilization.[S34][S35][S41][S93][S127][S170][S173][S208][S230][S231]

That missing rule matters because Article 12 does not treat movement, residence, departure, and return as discretionary conveniences. It protects liberty of movement and freedom to choose residence for those lawfully within the territory, protects everyone’s freedom to leave any country, and bars arbitrary deprivation of the right to enter one’s own country.[S93][S127] General Comment No. 27 sharpens the moral architecture: liberty of movement is an indispensable condition for the free development of the person; restrictions may not nullify the principle of liberty; the right includes moving from place to place and establishing oneself in a place of one’s choice; forced internal displacement is forbidden under the residence guarantee; the right to leave includes access to necessary travel documents; and the right to enter one’s own country is broader than formal nationality and is only rarely restrictable on reasonable grounds.[S230] OHCHR’s current internally displaced persons page compresses the same moral structure after displacement has already happened by affirming that displaced persons retain freedom of movement and residence and have a right to assistance in voluntary, safe, and dignified return, resettlement, or local integration, alongside restitution or compensation where return cannot fully restore what was lost.[S231] So the archive should now treat confinement-by-closure as a distinct standing defect: **public safety, health, border administration, and emergency management can justify some movement burdens, but they may not become standing excuses for governing through immobilization, paperless trapping, or indefinite no-return**.[S93][S127][S230][S231]

## Working-canon thesis

> **Righteousness is burdened, and often defeated, when a person, office, or institution governs by trapping people in place, withholding practical exit, preventing ordinary movement or residence choice beyond narrow necessity, or arbitrarily denying re-entry to one’s own country. Movement may sometimes be limited by law for genuinely protective reasons tied to public safety, health, rights of others, or comparably serious ends, but the burden stays steep: restrictions may not reverse the relation between norm and exception, may not impair the essence of the right, may not operate through discrimination or hidden dependency, and may not convert displacement, encampment, travel documents, checkpoints, curfews, or return permission into instruments of domination.**[S93][S127][S170][S173][S230][S231]

## 1. Movement is not a ruler-managed privilege

Article 12 and General Comment No. 27 are morally revealing because they begin from an ordinary-life premise rather than a border-policing premise. Movement inside a territory, choosing where to reside, leaving a country, and returning to one’s own country are not treated as occasional indulgences for the compliant. GC27 says the right to move freely covers the whole territory of the State, includes moving from one place to another and establishing oneself in a place of one’s choice, and must not be made dependent on some specially approved purpose for moving or staying.[S230] It also insists that restrictions under article 12 may not nullify liberty of movement, must remain necessary and consistent with the Covenant’s other rights, and must not reverse the relation between norm and exception.[S230]

That matters because offices often try to make motion itself into a favor they can selectively release: you may pass if your papers are current, if your relative consents, if your employer signs, if your district is not under closure, if your encampment permit remains active, if your protest activity is low enough, if your religion is quiet enough, if your school or workplace status is useful enough, or if return serves the authority’s demographic or political interests. But once the ability to leave, return, relocate, visit family, seek care, reach school, work, protest, worship, or ordinary social life depends mainly on administrative indulgence rather than narrowly justified protection, righteousness is already drifting toward control through closure.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S93][S127][S230]

## 2. Confinement can be administered without calling itself detention

The archive already learned from arbitrary detention and displacement that power often prefers soft or distributed coercion to explicit imprisonment. Movement materials sharpen that lesson. One need not be behind bars to be unfree in the morally relevant sense. Permit grids, internal checkpoints, district closures, travel-document withholding, carrier sanctions that trap people upstream, camp confinement, residence bans, and endless processing delay can together produce a condition in which life is geographically narrowed by official design.[S93][S127][S209][S210][S230]

GC27 is useful precisely because it does not allow the right to movement to be hollowed out by bureaucratic form. It criticizes requirements for permission to change residence, delays in processing such requests, and a wide range of practices that make leaving harder, including lack of access to authorities, excessive fees, supportive-statement requirements, unreasonable delays, route-description requirements, harassment of applicants, and refusal to issue passports for impermissible reasons.[S230] The archive should therefore resist a familiar laundering move: claiming no one is detained because the burden appears as forms, checkpoints, pass systems, sponsor control, or logistical impossibility. When a person or population is practically trapped, closure may be doing detention-like work without detention’s admitted name.[S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S93][S128][S185][S186][S230]

## 3. Return is not merely a discretionary readmission question

General Comment No. 27 matters even more sharply for return because it says the right to enter one’s own country recognizes a special relationship between a person and that country, implies the right to remain there, includes return after departure, and reaches beyond formal nationality to at least some persons with special ties or claims that make them more than mere aliens.[S230] It adds that there are few, if any, circumstances in which deprivation of the right to enter one’s own country could be reasonable, and warns that nationality stripping or expulsion to a third country may not be used to arbitrarily prevent return.[S230]

That matters because offices often present no-return as a side effect of status administration rather than as a grave substantive burden. Passports lapse. Nationality reviews stall. Border blacklists persist. Residence restoration never arrives. Emergency closures become semi-permanent. Exclusion orders outlive the condition that allegedly justified them. But where return is functionally withheld from those with enduring claims of belonging, the office may not be merely regulating entry. It may be governing by controlled un-belonging, using distance itself to weaken claim, memory, property, family continuity, and political voice.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S93][S127][S208][S212][S213][S230]

## 4. Dignified post-displacement return cannot be replaced by indefinite warehousing

The archive’s anti-displacement surface already rejected uprooting as a convenience tool. The present surface adds the opposite side of that burden: once people have been displaced, office does not regain righteousness simply by keeping them somewhere safer than the original site while leaving return or durable settlement practically unreal. OHCHR’s current internally displaced persons guidance matters because it preserves displaced persons’ freedom of movement and residence after displacement and then adds a further duty of assistance toward voluntary, safe, and dignified return, resettlement, or local integration, including help with recovery of lost property and possessions and compensation or just reparation when restitution is not possible.[S231]

So the archive should now reject the thought that camp life, managed warehousing, prolonged closed-zone residence, or endless “temporary” shelter automatically satisfies what is due after forced movement. Righteous response to displacement must ask whether the person can actually move, choose residence, reunite with family, rebuild work and school pathways, recover property or compensation, and return or resettle without humiliation, coercion, or renewed danger. A system that stabilizes bodies while freezing lives can still fail righteousness even when it avoids the worst immediate violence.[S34][S35][S41][S47][S49][S170][S172][S173][S231]

## 5. Restrictions may be real, but the burden of justification stays steep

Nothing in this surface implies absolute open movement at all times. Article 12 itself allows some restrictions for national security, public order, public health or morals, or the rights and freedoms of others, and GC27 recognizes that lawful limitation can sometimes be justified.[S93][S127][S230] The archive’s point is narrower: movement restrictions demand unusually concrete justification because closure is such an easy way for office to simplify its problems. Public-health quarantine, targeted evacuation, narrowly tailored access control around active violence, or time-limited scene containment can all be morally intelligible. But broad closure, no-exit regimes, internal banishment, indefinite encampment, or no-return administration demand stronger answers than “safety required it.”[S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S93][S127][S170][S230]

The archive should therefore ask not only whether a movement burden was formally lawful, but whether it remained precise, temporary, reviewable, non-discriminatory, consistent with other rights, and less restrictive than available alternatives. If fear, demographic ambition, punishment, narrative control, or bureaucratic convenience did the real work, then a superficially legal restriction may still be a case of no-confinement-by-closure rather than righteous protection.[S18][S19][S32][S34][S35][S41][S93][S127][S230]

## 6. Working rule

When movement, residence, departure, or return is burdened under pressure, ask six questions:
1. **What concrete protected interest required the burden?** Was it real public safety, health, rights-protection, or something looser such as protest aversion, demographic preference, labor control, reputational comfort, or administrative ease?[S93][S127][S230]
2. **What form of movement was burdened?** Internal travel, choice of residence, exit, re-entry, post-displacement return, local integration, family visiting, access to work, care, school, worship, or political participation can each be impaired differently.[S93][S127][S230][S231]
3. **What narrower route was seriously explored first?** Targeted screening, time-limited routing, escort, individualized orders, localized safety design, or document correction may matter before broad closure, encampment, passport denial, or blanket no-return does.[S34][S35][S93][S127][S230]
4. **Who was most predictably trapped by the measure?** Poorer people, women dependent on family consent, migrants, minorities, displaced persons, dissidents, the undocumented, children, the elderly, or persons needing frequent care often bear closure more heavily than the formally average person.[S41][S66][S93][S185][S186][S230][S231]
5. **What made return or durable settlement real rather than rhetorical?** Safety, voluntariness, property recovery, compensation, papers, family unity, workability, and review matter because “temporary” exclusion often becomes long-term un-belonging.[S170][S173][S208][S212][S230][S231]
6. **What review and repair stayed live afterward?** Challenge, exemption, timely reconsideration, document correction, re-entry path, restitution, and compensation matter because closure often outlives the event that first justified it.[S32][S34][S35][S93][S173][S230][S231]

## Compact maxim

> **Do not govern by trapping.** Protective movement limits can exist, but righteousness is heavily burdened when power solves danger, dissent, scarcity, or administrative inconvenience by immobilizing people, withholding ordinary exit, or making return depend on indefinite indulgence.[S93][S127][S170][S173][S230][S231]
