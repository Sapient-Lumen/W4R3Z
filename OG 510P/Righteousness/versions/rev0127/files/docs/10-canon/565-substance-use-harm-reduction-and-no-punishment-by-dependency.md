# Substance Use, Harm Reduction, and No-Punishment-by-Dependency

## Why this surface matters

The archive already rejects punitive noncare, arbitrary detention, no-body-by-compulsion, no-rights-by-austerity, no-homelessness-by-clearance, no-care-by-coercion, and no-home-as-exception. What it still lacked was a compact rule for **what happens when substance use, dependency, withdrawal risk, or overdose vulnerability is governed mainly through punishment, coerced abstinence, or blocked life-saving support while institutions call the arrangement treatment, public order, or responsible control**.[S93][S181][S236][S251][S256][S342][S348][S354][S355][S356][S357][S358][S359]

That gap matters because drug policy is one of the clearest sites where institutions reclassify a rights-bearing person as a compliance problem. The person is said to need discipline, detox, a criminal record, a compulsory programme, a shelter exclusion, a child-protection penalty, or a police-managed encounter before they can count as someone who may safely receive care. But if overdose risk, withdrawal, infection exposure, housing precarity, stigma, or fear of arrest are doing the real work, righteousness is not preserved by calling the arrangement prevention or recovery.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S181][S236][S251][S342][S354][S355][S356][S357][S358][S359]

## Working-canon thesis

> **Righteousness is burdened, and often defeated, when substance use, dependency, withdrawal risk, or overdose vulnerability is governed mainly through criminalization, compulsory treatment, forced abstinence, denial of harm reduction, or other punitive control that makes survival, care, or ordinary standing depend on fear, compliance, or criminal exposure. Not every regulation of intoxicants is unrighteous. But once punishment, stigma, or coerced treatment are doing the practical work because voluntary care, housing, income support, and harm-reduction access were not made real, righteousness usually fails unless the restriction is narrowly tailored, non-discriminatory, reviewable, and leaves life-saving care continuously available.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S181][S236][S251][S342][S354][S355][S356][S357][S358][S359]

## 1. Drug policy is a human-rights field, not a punishment exception

Current OHCHR materials matter because they refuse to treat drug control as a zone where ordinary rights fall away. OHCHR's drug-policy page says States remain bound to respect, protect, and fulfil human rights while responding to drugs, says criminalization of consumption and possession for personal use has led to negative consequences for health, security, and rights, and adds that such criminalization drives people away from vital health interventions and into prison exposure.[S354] The 2023 OHCHR report page matters for the same reason: it presents drug policy as a human-rights problem with identifiable positive developments toward more rights-centred approaches rather than as a self-justifying enforcement field.[S355]

That is decisive for this archive. It means the relevant question is not whether the actor could invoke drugs, danger, or disorder. The question is whether the person remained a bearer of standing through the response, or whether drug-policy language became a permission slip for thinner liberty, weaker care claims, and easier abandonment.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S181][S354][S355]

## 2. Criminalization and compulsory treatment are not morally neutral techniques

OHCHR's 2023 High Commissioner speech is morally revealing because it says punishing people who use drugs with jail and criminal records adds to their suffering, deepens stigma and exclusion, drives them underground beyond reach of help, and that compulsory or coercive treatment creates serious challenges to human dignity.[S356] The Special Rapporteur's 2024 report page sharpens the same point by treating harm reduction as part of the right-to-health analysis precisely because punitive laws and policies compound stigma and disproportionately burden people who are homeless, poor, detained, disabled, racialized, or otherwise already easier to override.[S355]

This helps the archive name another laundering move. A court, police force, clinic, welfare office, shelter, or family system may say that punishment or mandatory treatment was regrettable but necessary to make the person stop. Yet once arrest, forced abstinence, compelled programme attendance, or custody threat is doing the practical work of managing visible disorder, discouraging use, or satisfying public anger because voluntary care and survival support were not built, the institution is no longer merely protecting health. It is governing dependency through punishment.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S181][S236][S251][S342][S355][S356][S359]

## 3. Harm reduction and voluntary care are part of the baseline

The current WHO materials matter because they identify concrete life-preserving measures rather than leaving care at the level of sentiment. WHO's 2025 opioid-overdose update says people with opioid dependence and those at risk of overdose need access to prevention, harm reduction, treatment, and care that are affordable, ethical, and evidence-based; it describes opioid agonist treatment and naloxone access as central recommendations.[S357] WHO's current page on people who inject drugs says harm reduction — including needle and syringe programmes, opioid agonist maintenance treatment, and community distribution of naloxone — is evidence-based, essential to HIV and hepatitis prevention, and blocked by criminalization, stigma, and restrictive laws.[S358] The 2026 UNAIDS guidance note adds that decriminalization combined with investment in harm reduction can expand access to care and that punitive laws continue to drive people away from life-saving services.[S359]

That matters because the archive needed a sharper baseline than “offer some treatment.” Once institutions know that overdose reversal, safer-use supplies, voluntary medication-assisted treatment, and low-threshold care preserve life and reduce wider harm, refusal to make those things genuinely available is not just a neutral policy preference. It is a standing choice about whose lives remain survivable before institutions.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S181][S251][S357][S358][S359]

## 4. Structural supports matter because punishment often fills a welfare vacuum

OHCHR's current materials and 2025 story are useful because they frame the better direction not simply as softer policing but as a shift toward prevention, treatment, and support rooted in dignity.[S354][S356] WHO's drug-use-disorder activity page adds that drug dependence should be understood as a health condition shaped by psychosocial, environmental, and biological determinants and best managed within a public-health system rather than as a self-acquired bad habit.[S357] The 2024 Special Rapporteur report page and the 2026 UNAIDS guidance note also matter because they emphasize compounded discrimination and the importance of housing, employment, education, community participation, and legal reform around people who use drugs.[S355][S359]

This is morally clarifying for the archive because it blocks a recurring innocence story: that punitive policy is unavoidable because people are still using drugs. Often what is unavoidable is that the stronger party failed to build the housing, income security, peer support, voluntary treatment, overdose response, and low-threshold services that would have made crisis and dependency less domination-shaped in the first place.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S181][S236][S251][S342][S355][S356][S357][S358][S359]

## 5. This is not a ban on all bounded regulation

The archive needed a narrow rule here. It does **not** say that every drug regulation, every age limit, every intoxicated-driving rule, every sales restriction, or every time-limited emergency intervention is automatically unrighteous. It says the justificatory burden becomes steep once substance-use governance is doing its practical work by making care, housing, parenting, mobility, livelihood, or freedom from violence depend on criminal exposure, forced abstinence, or denial of life-saving supports.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S181][S354][S355][S356][S357][S358][S359]

So the question is not whether an institution could point to public health, child protection, or public order in the abstract. The question is whether the response was truly narrow, evidence-responsive, non-discriminatory, reviewable, and continuously compatible with harm reduction and voluntary care — or whether regulation became the grammar by which a class of people could be arrested, separated, withdrawn, denied shelter, denied medicine, or left overdose-exposed because their dependency had made them politically easier to punish.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S181][S236][S251][S354][S355][S356][S357][S358][S359]

## 6. Working rule

When an actor claims righteousness while substance use, dependency, overdose risk, or drug policy is at issue, ask seven questions:
1. **What standing-bearing thing was actually at stake?** Immediate preservation of life, infection prevention, treatment, shelter, family continuity, public safety, income, or ordinary community presence?[S181][S236][S251][S354][S355][S357][S358][S359]
2. **What mechanism did the real burden work?** Arrest, criminal record, police stop, compulsory treatment, forced detoxification, shelter exclusion, child-custody threat, syringe confiscation, naloxone denial, medication cutoff, or another punitive lever?[S354][S355][S356][S357][S358][S359]
3. **Who carried the burden in practice?** Poorer people, homeless people, women and girls, racialized groups, Indigenous peoples, migrants, detained persons, disabled persons, queer people, or others already easier to stigmatize and override?[S355][S358][S359]
4. **What made the arrangement genuinely life-preserving rather than merely abstinence-demanding?** Voluntary treatment, overdose prevention, needle and syringe access, opioid agonist treatment, infection prevention, peer support, housing, income support, or realistic low-threshold entry to care?[S357][S358][S359]
5. **What supports were missing?** Stable housing, benefits, trauma-informed care, mental-health support, family support, community leadership, or service continuity that would have changed the case?[S236][S251][S342][S355][S356][S357][S358][S359]
6. **What review and remedy remained live?** External review, complaint, legal challenge, record correction, non-retaliatory health access, release from compelled treatment, or redesign of the enforcement pathway before harm hardened?[S93][S181][S259][S354][S355][S359]
7. **Was punishment doing the moral laundering?** If the stronger party can say “it was treatment,” “they had to hit rock bottom,” “the law required arrest,” “we could not enable them,” or “public order came first” while survival and ordinary standing were being made to depend on fear, abstinence, or criminal exposure, the righteousness claim is presumptively corrupted.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S181][S236][S251][S342][S354][S355][S356][S357][S358][S359]

## Compression sentence

> **Do not let dependency become a permission structure for punishment: where institutions answer substance use mainly through arrest, forced abstinence, or blocked harm reduction because voluntary care and survival supports were not made real, righteousness fails unless the response is truly narrow, reviewable, and continuously life-preserving.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S181][S236][S251][S342][S354][S355][S356][S357][S358][S359]
