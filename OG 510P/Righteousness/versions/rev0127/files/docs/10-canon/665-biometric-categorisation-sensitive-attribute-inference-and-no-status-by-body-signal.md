# Biometric categorisation, sensitive-attribute inference, and no-status-by-body-signal

## Core claim

> **Righteousness is burdened, and often defeated, when public or private actors use biometric data to deduce, infer, or sort persons by sensitive or protected characteristics, so that embodied traces do the real work of assigning civic, social, or administrative status rather than serving a narrowly bounded, strictly necessary, proportionate, contestable, and non-discriminatory purpose.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S438][S449][S452][S453]

## 1. The missing moral question is when bodies become status classifiers

The archive already rejected no-identity-by-extraction, no-suspicion-by-category, no-standing-by-score, and no-inward-state-by-surface. But it still lacked a direct rule for a different temptation: **treating biometric traces as if they authorized institutions to decide what kind of person someone is in politically or socially loaded terms**.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49]

That gap matters because biometric systems do more than identify or authenticate. They can also claim to deduce race, ethnicity, religion, political orientation, trade-union relation, sex, disability, health condition, or comparable sensitive status from face, voice, gait, iris, or other embodied data. Once those inferences start deciding scrutiny, suspicion, targeting, access, segmentation, or differential treatment, institutions are no longer only checking who someone is. They are governing by what someone's body supposedly reveals about membership, belief, or protected standing.[S438][S449][S452][S453]

## 2. This is distinct from both identification and emotion inference

The archive needs this as its own canon surface because the wrong is not exhausted by either identification or inward-state inference. Identification asks whether a person is this individual rather than another. Emotion recognition asks what the person supposedly feels or intends. **Biometric categorisation asks what protected or sensitive class the person supposedly belongs to, and then lets that class do institutional work**.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49]

That difference matters morally. A regime may avoid naming race, religion, disability, sex, sexuality, or political affiliation in a form field and still let biometric categorisation quietly recreate those categories through face, voice, or other bodily traces. The archive should therefore not let the more technical or indirect route obscure the fact that embodied data has become a pathway for status assignment.[S438][S449][S452]

## 3. Current governance materials already treat sensitive biometric categorisation as a red-line area

This is no longer only a philosophical worry about classification. The AI Act Service Desk's current Article 5 materials state that prohibited AI practices include biometric categorisation systems that categorise individually natural persons based on biometric data to deduce or infer race, political opinions, trade union membership, religious or philosophical beliefs, sex life, or sexual orientation.[S438] The Commission's current AI Act materials list biometric categorisation to deduce certain protected characteristics among the prohibited practices whose bans have been effective since February 2025.[S452]

That matters because the line being drawn is not merely about weak accuracy or bad calibration. It marks out a class of uses where turning biometric traces into sensitive social classification is itself treated as fundamentally dangerous to rights and standing. The archive should learn from that: some uses are burdened not only because they may be wrong, but because they make embodied inference into an administrative route for classifying persons in ways that predictably invite discrimination, stigma, or exclusion.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S438][S452]

## 4. Necessity and proportionality still do real work even where every use is not flatly prohibited

The archive does **not** need to say that every use touching biometric categorisation is automatically unrighteous. The same legal materials are morally useful because they preserve distinctions: Article 5 contains carve-outs around certain lawfully acquired biometric datasets, and Council of Europe facial-recognition guidance insists that biometric uses must be shown to be strictly necessary and proportionate in context.[S438][S449] Recent Parliamentary Assembly materials likewise say the Framework Convention on AI limits biometric categorisation to strictly necessary and proportionate cases.[S453]

That is an important discipline rather than an escape hatch. It means the real question is whether a bounded and reviewable use is being kept inside a narrow rights-respecting lane, or whether the existence of biometric data is being used to reopen old temptations toward ethnic sorting, political suspicion, religious marking, sexuality inference, disability exposure, or health-coded differentiation. The archive should therefore treat no-status-by-body-signal as a standing discipline with a high burden even where the law stops short of a total ban in every setting.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S438][S449][S453]

## 5. Righteousness should not let embodied traces become civic or moral rank

The deeper moral point is portable. A righteous office may sometimes need to verify identity, preserve safety, or tailor a service. But it should resist a different administrative appetite: the desire to read a person's body as a shortcut to protected status and then let that inferred status decide how the person is watched, trusted, ranked, admitted, questioned, or excluded.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S438][S449][S452]

So the archive should reject another laundering move: because the system never asked the question directly, never named the category in a visible form, or only produced a probabilistic cluster, institutions assume the resulting status assignment is morally cleaner. But a hidden route into protected or sensitive classification can burden standing just as much as an overt one, and sometimes more because it weakens notice, contest, and accountability at the same time.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S438][S449][S452][S453]

## 6. Working rule

When an actor claims righteousness while relying on biometric categorisation or sensitive-attribute inference, ask seven questions:
1. **What status was being inferred?** Race, ethnicity, religion, political opinion, trade-union relation, sex, sexuality, disability, health condition, nationality proxy, or some other sensitive or protected characteristic?[S438][S449][S452][S453]
2. **What biometric trace was doing the work?** Face, voice, gait, iris, fingerprint, vein pattern, or another bodily marker presented as evidence of protected status?[S438][S449][S452]
3. **What relation was the system supposedly serving?** Border control, policing, migration, employment, education, insurance, welfare integrity, advertising, eligibility, safety, or anti-fraud?[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S438][S449][S453]
4. **What made the classification contestable?** Notice, explanation, refusal without penalty where possible, meaningful appeal, correction, deletion, and a path to challenge both the category and its downstream effects?[S438][S449][S453]
5. **What narrower route was available?** Direct self-identification where appropriate, conduct-linked evidence, universal design, ordinary anti-discrimination safeguards, or a service rule that did not require sorting persons by sensitive embodied status?[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S449][S453]
6. **Who bore the risk of error or stigma?** Did already burdened groups predictably absorb the cost of hidden racialisation, politicisation, religious marking, disability exposure, sexuality inference, or health-coded suspicion?[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S449][S453]
7. **Was sensitive status doing the real work?** Did the system quietly replace bounded reasons tied to the specific relation with an embodied shortcut to who the person supposedly is in socially consequential terms?[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S438][S449][S452][S453]

## Compression sentence

> **Do not let biometric traces become an ordinary administrative route to sensitive or protected status: where face, voice, gait, iris, or comparable embodied data do the real work of assigning politically or socially consequential class-membership, righteousness fails unless the use remains narrowly bounded, strictly necessary, proportionate, non-discriminatory, and meaningfully contestable.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S438][S449][S452][S453]
