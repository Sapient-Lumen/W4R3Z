# Predictive policing, future-dangerousness, and no-suspicion-by-forecast

## Core claim

> **Righteousness is burdened, and often defeated, when police, security, border, school, platform, or comparable authorities treat a modelled forecast of future offending, disorder, or danger as administratively usable title to scrutiny, deployment, stop, surveillance, or restraint, so that prediction itself does the real work of suspicion rather than a bounded, individualized, reviewable, and conduct-linked judgment.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S411][S412][S446][S447][S450][S451]

## 1. The missing moral question is when forecast becomes title to suspicion

The archive already rejected no-rightlessness-by-model, no-suspicion-by-category, no-rights-by-list, and no-standing-by-score. But it still lacked a direct rule for a different temptation: **treating prediction of future danger as if it were already enough to justify present burden**.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49]

That gap matters because predictive policing is not just ordinary profiling with newer machinery. A system may claim to identify who is likely to offend, where disorder will occur, or which network, block, or traveler deserves extra scrutiny. Once those forecasts start deciding patrol patterns, stops, questioning, watchfulness, or preemptive intervention, institutions are no longer only observing conduct. They are governing through anticipated wrongdoing.[S41][S93][S446][S447][S450][S451]

## 2. The current regulatory line already treats some forecast-based policing as unacceptable

This is no longer only a philosophical complaint about overconfident policing. The European Commission's current AI Act materials explicitly list **individual predictive policing based solely on profiling people** among prohibited AI practices.[S446] The AI Act Service Desk repeats the same point and places predictive policing based solely on profiling inside the Article 5 prohibitions.[S447]

That distinction is morally important. Official materials are not merely saying police should calibrate such systems more carefully. They are saying there is a class of forecast-based policing where the very governing posture is unacceptable: people become burden-worthy because a model projects risk rather than because officials can show a narrower, individualized, and reviewable basis tied to real conduct.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S446][S447]

## 3. Predictive policing turns historical attention into future burden

OHCHR's 2024 racism-and-AI materials sharpen why this deserves its own canon surface. They describe predictive policing tools as making assessments about who will commit future crimes, and where future crime may occur, based on location and personal data.[S450] They then identify the feedback loop: because law-enforcement attention has historically concentrated in certain neighborhoods, those communities become overrepresented in police records, which in turn drives new algorithmic predictions and still more deployment.[S450]

That is morally revealing because forecast systems often inherit prior policing patterns and then present them back as neutral future knowledge. What appears to be anticipation may just be amplified memory plus administrative deference. Righteousness should therefore treat no-suspicion-by-forecast as more than an accuracy complaint. It is a standing complaint about how institutions convert historical surveillance and unequal attention into self-refreshing grounds for new suspicion.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S411][S412][S450][S451]

## 4. The human-rights problem is not only error but opacity, proxy bias, and responsibility laundering

Council of Europe materials on AI in policing and criminal justice widen the warning. The Parliamentary Assembly notes that predictive policing is among the live police uses of AI, and it identifies core human-rights risks including opacity, indirect discriminatory correlation, overreliance by officials, and weakened responsibility, oversight, and remedy.[S451]

That matters because a forecast can burden people even when no one claims certainty. Patrol allocation, stop patterns, intelligence focus, border friction, youth monitoring, school security, or housing enforcement may all tighten around the people and places made newly legible as risk. Once officials defer to the model, the institution can start acting as though it never chose the burden at all. Righteousness should reject that laundering move. Forecast does not dissolve authorship.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S451]

## 5. Righteousness may attend to concrete risk without turning prophecy into grounds

The deeper moral point is portable. Offices sometimes must act under uncertainty and sometimes must manage real danger before the worst outcome occurs. The archive does **not** need to say that every attempt to anticipate harm is unrighteous. But there is a large difference between acting on immediate, particularized, and reviewable grounds and acting because a model says a person, group, or place resembles prior danger.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93]

A righteous office may sometimes increase presence around a credible threat, respond to specific intelligence, or take temporary precautions around an articulable and proximate risk. What it may not do lightly is let actuarial resemblance, historically saturated data, neighborhood proxy, or closed-model forecasting become the ordinary title to suspicion. Once forecast does the real work, the burden shifts onto those already most watched.[S41][S93][S411][S412][S446][S447][S450][S451]

## 6. Working rule

When an actor claims righteousness while relying on predictive policing or comparable future-risk forecasting, ask seven questions:
1. **What future claim was being made?** A forecast about offending, disorder, victimization, dangerousness, hotspot probability, network risk, or some related future event?[S41][S446][S447][S450][S451]
2. **What current burden followed from that forecast?** Patrol concentration, stop and search, questioning, watchlisting, detention pressure, resource withdrawal, school discipline, platform scrutiny, or some other practical burden?[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S451]
3. **What inputs were doing the work?** Historical arrest records, complaint data, location proxies, association patterns, prior police attention, or other data likely to reproduce selective scrutiny?[S411][S412][S450][S451]
4. **What individualized ground existed apart from the model?** What present conduct, concrete intelligence, imminent threat, or case-specific reason could still be stated without hiding behind prediction?[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93]
5. **What made the forecast contestable?** Notice, disclosure, explanation, auditable criteria, human override, independent review, and a live path to separate historical saturation from present grounds?[S447][S451]
6. **Who bore the recursion cost?** Which neighborhoods, racialized groups, migrants, students, or already watched populations became easier to burden precisely because they were previously more observed?[S411][S412][S450]
7. **Was forecast doing the real work?** Did the institution quietly replace conduct-linked judgment with suspicion-by-forecast while preserving only the rhetoric of individualized decision?[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S446][S447][S450][S451]

## Compression sentence

> **Do not let a projected future offense, hotspot, or dangerousness score become the ordinary title to present suspicion: where predictive forecasting does the real work of patrol, stop, surveillance, or restraint rather than a bounded, individualized, reviewable, and conduct-linked judgment, righteousness fails unless the case can be redescribed without reliance on suspicion-by-forecast.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S411][S412][S446][S447][S450][S451]
