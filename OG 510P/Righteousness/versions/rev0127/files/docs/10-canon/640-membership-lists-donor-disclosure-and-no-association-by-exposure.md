# Membership lists, donor disclosure, and no-association-by-exposure

## Core claim

> **Righteousness is burdened, and often defeated, when states, regulators, funders, compliance systems, or courts make the mandatory revelation of members, donors, beneficiaries, staff, controllers, or internal association records do the real work of thinning, sorting, or disabling association through exposure, fear, stigma, or targeted vulnerability rather than through a narrow, reviewable, privacy-disciplined, and genuinely necessary accountability function.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S430][S435][S436]

## 1. The missing moral question is when association is not banned but exposed

The archive already rejected no-association-by-sufferance, no-rights-by-permission, no-association-by-poison-label, and no-association-by-financial-chokepoint. But it still lacked a direct rule for a quieter move: **leaving association formally lawful while making participation legible enough to chill, deter, or target it**.[S93][S430][S435][S436]

That gap matters because institutions often describe membership rolls, donor lists, beneficiary identities, controller registers, staff disclosures, or internal-record demands as if they were morally minor matters of transparency. The current official materials show why that description is often false. Exposure can do the work of suppression without a formal ban, especially where reprisals, harassment, blacklisting, discrimination, surveillance, or public stigmatization are foreseeable consequences of being named.[S93][S430][S435][S436]

## 2. Privacy and association are not separate here; they protect each other

ICCPR article 17 protects against arbitrary or unlawful interference with privacy, while article 22 protects freedom of association.[S93] The 2012 Special Rapporteur report sharpens the connection directly: authorities must respect the right of associations to privacy, and even where independent bodies may examine records for transparency and accountability, that examination must not be arbitrary and must respect privacy and non-discrimination because otherwise it puts the independence of associations and the safety of their members at risk.[S430]

That matters for righteousness because some associational goods depend on selective opacity. Solidarity, dissent, minority self-organization, victim support, labor organizing, legal defense, and watchdog work are often standing-bearing precisely because they let vulnerable or unpopular people act together without first exposing every tie and every supporter to the stronger party.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S430]

## 3. The moral difference between bounded accountability and exposure-governance is load-bearing

The archive therefore now distinguishes **narrow, protected, purpose-limited accountability review** from **exposure-governance**. The former may sometimes be justified: public funds can require audits; targeted investigations can require specific records; serious wrongdoing can justify bounded disclosure to a competent authority. But the latter is different. There the institution makes revelation itself do the main work of discipline by teaching people that to associate is to become searchable, monitorable, or punishable.[S93][S430][S435][S436]

This distinction is morally load-bearing because similar administrative verbs can hide opposite structures. “Report,” “register,” “declare,” “notify,” or “identify” can describe a narrow safeguard. But the same vocabulary can also hide a standing defect in which donors withdraw, members do not join, beneficiaries stop seeking help, staff become targets, or dissenting groups become easy to map. Once that happens, exposure is no longer a side effect. It has become the governing instrument.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S430][S435][S436]

## 4. Recent communications show how disclosure duties can become severe restrictions

The 2023 Bosnia and Herzegovina communication is morally revealing because it warns that burdensome reporting and disclosure requirements tied to foreign funding can deter individuals from joining or leading organizations, may expose individuals to serious risks of reprisals, and should protect the rights of donors, beneficiaries, and staff.[S435] The 2021 Thailand communication is useful from another angle because it treats fundraising restrictions as interferences with article 22 and warns that compelled access to NGO communications can violate article 17 privacy and expose victims and beneficiaries to reprisals.[S436]

These materials matter because they show that the relevant harm is not only public shaming. Exposure can also arrive through regulator-side record access, beneficiary identifiability, controller lists, donor disclosure without threshold discipline, or broad access to communications and internal files. The archive therefore needs a direct rule not only against banning association, but against making people pay for association with unsafe legibility.[S93][S430][S435][S436]

## 5. Working rule

When an actor claims righteousness while demanding member, donor, beneficiary, staff, controller, or internal-record disclosure, ask seven questions:
1. **Whose identities or records were demanded?** Members, donors, beneficiaries, staff, controllers, informal participants, communication logs, or other internal files?[S430][S435][S436]
2. **What concrete legitimate aim required this?** Specific fraud investigation, public-fund audit, conflict-of-interest control, or something much broader and vaguer such as transparency, order, morality, or general suspicion?[S93][S435][S436]
3. **How wide was the exposure?** Confidential submission to a competent body, broad inter-agency access, public posting, searchable registry, or open-ended disclosure without threshold discipline?[S430][S435][S436]
4. **What narrower route was available?** Aggregate reporting, threshold-triggered disclosure, anonymization, protected audit, targeted subpoena, or ex post review instead of routine exposure?[S430][S435][S436]
5. **What reprisals or chilling effects were foreseeable?** Harassment, blacklisting, surveillance, donor withdrawal, refusal of services, employment loss, intimidation, or reduced help-seeking by beneficiaries?[S430][S435][S436]
6. **What privacy and data-use safeguards existed?** Purpose limitation, retention limits, leak prevention, independent oversight, notice, challenge rights, and remedies against misuse?[S93][S430][S435][S436]
7. **Was exposure doing the real work?** Did the institution rely on legibility itself to thin dissent, weaken solidarity, or sort acceptable from disfavored association without openly banning it?[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S430][S435][S436]

## Compression sentence

> **Do not let transparency language launder exposure-governance: where compulsory revelation of members, donors, beneficiaries, staff, controllers, or internal association records does the real work of chilling, sorting, or disabling lawful association, righteousness fails unless the disclosure is narrowly justified, privacy-disciplined, non-arbitrary, and clearly less restrictive than governing by exposure.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S430][S435][S436]
