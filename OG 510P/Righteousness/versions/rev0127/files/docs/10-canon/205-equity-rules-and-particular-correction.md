# Equity, Rules, and Particular Correction

## Why this surface now

The archive now has contestability, priority cues, uncertainty discipline, and anti-capture rules. But it still lacked a compact account of a common hard case:

> **what makes a departure from strict rule-application righteous rather than merely arbitrary, sentimental, or favoritist?**

Without such a surface, the archive risks two opposite failures:
- **rigid formalism** — pretending that righteous judgment is exhausted by literal rule-application even when the rule underfits the morally relevant particulars;
- **favoritist exception** — renaming private sympathy, insider access, or ad hoc discretion as humane equity.

Both are too easy.

## Working-canon thesis

> **Righteousness normally prefers publicly known, general, and reviewable rules. But because rules are general and cases are particular, righteous judgment sometimes requires equitable correction when strict application would miss the rule's own point or place burden in the wrong place. Equity is not a private exemption for favorites; it is particular correction disciplined by the rule's purpose, morally relevant differences, comparable treatment of like cases, publicly articulable reasons, and preserved record or review.**[S01][S34][S50][S51]

This is not a permission slip for free-form discretion. It is a discipline for when fidelity to the due requires more than literal sameness.

## 1. Why rules matter

The archive does **not** turn against rules. Public norms matter because they reduce arbitrariness, make expectations legible, stabilize cooperation, and limit the power of officials to improvise burdens case by case.[S34]

So righteous judgment normally prefers:
- general rules over extemporary decrees;
- publicly knowable standards over secret carve-outs;
- and reviewable application over pure personal say-so.[S34]

This is especially important in office, where power is already asymmetrically held.

## 2. Why rules underfit some cases

But rules are general while cases are particular. Aristotle's ethics already treats practical judgment as operating in a field where many generalizations hold only for the most part, and the rule-of-law literature preserves the same point in its discussion of *epieikeia* / equity.[S01][S34][S50]

So the archive now rejects **rule-idolatry**: the thought that once a rule exists, righteousness is exhausted by applying it without remainder.

Sometimes literal sameness is itself a way of missing what is due. Equality is not always identical treatment, because cases can differ in morally relevant ways.[S51]

## 3. What makes equity righteous rather than arbitrary

The archive now treats equitable correction as righteous only when at least four constraints are visible.

### A. The difference must be morally relevant

The particular feature doing the work must bear on the claim itself — dependency, induced reliance, emergency tempo, hard-to-repair harm, incapacity, prior wrong, or another relevant difference — rather than mere sympathy, status, social familiarity, or elite access.[S34][S51]

### B. The reason must be publicly articulable

The actor should be able to say why the case is different in terms that could in principle be reviewed by others, not only felt privately or intuited by insiders.[S34]

### C. Like cases must remain alike

Equity does **not** license bespoke moral gifts. If the same morally relevant difference recurs, the office or institution should either treat comparable cases comparably or revise the rule, standard, or guidance so the correction is no longer a hidden favor.[S34][S51]

### D. Record and review should be preserved

Especially in office, equitable correction should leave enough explanation, record, or appeal path that it can later be distinguished from patronage, bias, or covert capture.[S34]

## 4. Office-facing consequence

Public office needs rules, but righteous office cannot hide behind rigid rule literalism when the rule underfits the claim structure of the case. Sometimes a waiver, exception, accommodation, narrower remedy, or individualized judgment is more righteous than strict sameness.

But the archive now adds the counter-pressure just as strongly: office may not turn equity into patronage. Quiet carve-outs for insiders, bespoke mercy for the connected, or opaque hardship judgments without reasons or record are not righteous particularization; they are standing-damaging favoritism.[S19][S34][S51]

So the office-facing rule is:

> **prefer stable public rules; correct their underfit openly; never call covert favoritism or unreviewable discretion “equity.”**

## 5. What this changes in verdicts

This surface improves verdict language again.

Better verdicts now include:
- **rule-faithful but equitably corrected**;
- **formally equal but claim-blind**;
- **merciful-looking but favoritist**;
- **particularity-sensitive and reviewable**;
- **authorized discretion used righteously rather than privately**.

This helps the archive resist both bureaucracy worship and exception theater.

## Compressed rule

> **Righteousness normally works through public rules. When rules underfit the particulars, correct them openly, for reasons that would still make sense across like cases, and never rename favoritism as equity.**
