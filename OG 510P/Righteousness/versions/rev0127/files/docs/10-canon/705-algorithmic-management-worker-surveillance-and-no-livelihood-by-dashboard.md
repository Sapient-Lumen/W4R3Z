# Algorithmic management, worker surveillance, and no-livelihood-by-dashboard

> **Righteousness is burdened, and often defeated, when public or private actors use tracked data, automated monitoring, opaque metrics, or algorithmically supported assignment and evaluation systems so that dashboards, scores, and workflow automations do the real work of setting pace, pay, opportunity, discipline, or retention rather than leaving work governed by bounded monitoring, intelligible standards, human answerability, and contestable judgment.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S468][S469][S470][S471][S472]

## 1. The missing moral question is not only whether a model made a decision, but whether livelihood has been made to live under the dashboard

The archive already rejected no-rightlessness-by-model, no-livelihood-by-domination, privacy-surveillance excess, and no-agency-by-stealth. But it still lacked a direct rule for a different and increasingly common defect: **workers are not merely evaluated once by a model; they are made to work inside a continuously tracked managerial environment in which scheduling, task allocation, pace, scoring, incentive, warning, suspension, and dismissal are increasingly shaped by automated monitoring and automated decision support.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S468][S469][S470][S471][S472]

That gap matters because many actors defend these systems by pointing to efficiency, optimisation, objectivity, or ordinary software. The dashboard only coordinates shifts. The scanner only records output. The app only routes tasks. The performance score only summarizes work already done. The warning was only system-generated. The manager could always override. But righteousness has to ask a narrower question: **what actually did the practical work of deciding how this person had to labor, what risks they had to absorb, what pace they had to sustain, what pay or opportunities they could reach, and what discipline or precarity they had to live under?**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S468][S469][S470][S471][S472]

So the archive needs a canon surface that is not reducible either to ordinary workplace hierarchy or to one-off automated decisions. No-livelihood-by-dashboard asks when a livelihood relation becomes standing-defective because tracked managerial automation, opaque metrics, or pervasive digital surveillance do the real work of governing the worker while the stronger party later points to technology as if it were only neutral infrastructure.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S468][S469][S470][S471][S472]

## 2. Current official materials now name this family clearly enough to justify canon admission

The Commission's current future-of-work materials are morally revealing because they define algorithmic management as the use of automated monitoring systems and automated decision-making systems powered by algorithms that replace functions managers usually perform, such as allocating tasks, pricing assignments, determining working schedules, giving instructions, evaluating work, providing incentives, or applying adverse treatment.[S469] The same materials say that, if unregulated, these technologies can produce excessive surveillance, opacity about decision-making, discrimination risks, data breaches, psychosocial risks, and harms to decent working conditions, health and safety, equal treatment, and personal-data protection.[S469]

ILO materials sharpen the point from a labor-process angle. The ILO says algorithmic management uses tracked data and other information to organize, assign, monitor, supervise, and evaluate work, and that under algorithmic management workers interact with a system rather than a human manager, reducing contact with managers and co-workers.[S470] The Joint Research Centre widens the same concern by saying digital tools enable increasingly pervasive monitoring and surveillance of work, that algorithmic management creates information asymmetries that weaken bargaining power and worker involvement, and that fuller forms of digital monitoring and platformisation are associated with generally worse working conditions and strong potential for intrusive worker surveillance.[S471]

Consilium and Parliament materials show that this is now an admitted governance seam rather than merely an academic worry. Current EU materials say the Platform Work Directive establishes the first EU rules on algorithm systems in the workplace, requires information about automated monitoring and decision-making systems, restricts processing of several highly sensitive categories of data, and guarantees qualified human oversight for significant decisions.[S468] Parliament's current summary adds that platform workers cannot be fired or dismissed on the basis of a decision taken by an algorithm or automated decision-making system alone.[S472] Together these materials show that worker governance through dashboards, scores, and monitoring systems is now being treated as a live dignity, data-protection, and power-allocation problem.[S468][S469][S470][S471][S472]

## 3. The righteousness issue is not simply technology at work; it is livelihood governed through opaque and surveillant managerial automation

Righteousness should not treat every scheduling tool, payroll system, routing aid, safety sensor, or productivity dashboard as automatically unrighteous. Some monitoring and some automation can be bounded, reviewable, and justified: genuine safety systems, wage accounting, accessibility supports, anti-fraud checks, maintenance planning, capacity coordination, or worker-chosen tools that do not quietly become the title by which management loads risk, pace, or precarity onto labor.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S468][S469][S470][S471][S472]

The narrower defect appears where the worker's practical standing at work depends on continuously captured traces, opaque thresholds, unilateral metric design, automated task assignment, pace escalation, hidden ranking, system-generated penalties, or nominally reviewable decisions that are in fact ratified by humans only after the dashboard has already framed what counts as normal, urgent, deviant, or disposable.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S468][S469][S470][S471][S472]

That matters across more than platform work. Warehouses, call centers, hospitals, logistics chains, retail, delivery, offices, schools, and public administrations can all be organized so that work quality, break time, route choice, shift access, job retention, or disciplinary exposure are substantially set by tracked managerial automation. In those cases the wrong is not just that software exists. It is that a livelihood relation becomes structured by no-livelihood-by-dashboard: the worker must continually prove themselves legible to a monitored system whose standards, inferences, and escalation pathways they cannot fully see, challenge, or collectively shape.[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S469][S470][S471][S472]

## 4. Questions righteous judgment should now ask

When an actor claims righteousness while relying on algorithmic management, worker monitoring, or automated workflow control, ask seven questions:

1. **What managerial function was delegated?** Scheduling, task allocation, performance evaluation, pace-setting, ranking, pricing, discipline, suspension, dismissal recommendation, or incentive assignment?[S469][S470][S471][S472]
2. **What data and traces did the system need in order to work?** Output counts, location, keystrokes, screen time, communications, biometrics, emotional-state inference, private conversations, union-activity proxies, or belief- and health-adjacent data?[S468][S470][S471][S472]
3. **What practical burden did the system place on the worker?** Speed-up, stress, opacity, surveillance, isolation, diminished autonomy, weaker bargaining power, reduced contact with human managers, or harder challenge and repair?[S469][S470][S471]
4. **Was human oversight real or ceremonial?** Could a competent person actually inspect, explain, override, and repair significant decisions, or was the human only ratifying dashboard outputs after the fact?[S468][S472]
5. **What standards were intelligible and contestable?** Could the worker know what counted as good performance, what inputs mattered, what triggered adverse treatment, and how errors could be corrected?[S468][S469][S471]
6. **What narrower route could have served the legitimate aim?** Could the actor have used bounded sampling, worker participation, clearer standards, less intrusive monitoring, or genuinely advisory tools rather than continuous extraction and unilateral metric rule?[S469][S470][S471]
7. **Was the dashboard doing the real work?** If the opaque metrics, constant monitoring, or automated escalation had been removed, would the same pace, pay, discipline, or retention pattern still have been justified on openly defensible human terms?[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S468][S469][S470][S471][S472]

## 5. Canonical compression

> **Do not let efficiency rhetoric, neutral-software language, or nominal human review masquerade as righteous labor governance: where tracked managerial automation, opaque metrics, or pervasive worker surveillance do the real work of setting pace, pay, opportunity, discipline, or retention, righteousness fails unless monitoring is bounded, standards are intelligible, significant decisions remain genuinely human-reviewable, and the worker's standing is contestable without having to live by dashboard alone.**[S34][S35][S41][S45][S47][S49][S93][S468][S469][S470][S471][S472]
