# Thresholds, Minima, and Excess

## The unresolved pressure

The archive now has a portable core, a levels-and-carriers view, and typed portability claims. But it still needs a cleaner answer to a simpler question:

> **how much must be true before conduct, office, or institutional order counts as righteous at all?**

Without a threshold rule, the archive drifts in two opposite directions:
- downward into mere non-wrongdoing, legality, or procedural tidiness;
- upward into sainthood, heroic sacrifice, or maximized beneficence.

Both drifts are distortive.

## Current working answer

The archive now adopts a **threshold-band** view:

> **righteousness is stricter than blameless minimal compliance, but it does not require heroic or saintly excess.**

That means three things.

### 1. Below the band: mere nonwrongdoing is not yet righteousness

An act may avoid obvious violation and still fall short of righteousness if it withholds what is due, hides behind formal permission, or abandons vulnerable parties while keeping clean hands in appearance only.[S01][S09][S11][S12][S19]

Righteousness therefore cannot be reduced to:
- not being the worst actor in the room,
- obeying the rulebook in bad faith,
- or avoiding blame while declining real duty.

### 2. Inside the band: righteousness requires due-fidelity under pressure

Conduct counts as righteous, on the current archive view, when it reliably does three things together:

1. **renders the central due** rather than evading it;
2. **resists the main corrupting pressure** in the case;
3. **remains open to truth, review, and repair** if breach or uncertainty appears.

This is more than minimal permission, because it requires active fidelity rather than mere abstention. But it is less than maximal moral aspiration, because it does not require agents always to perform the single best or most sacrificial act available.[S24][S25]

### 3. Above the band: supererogatory excess is admirable but not required

Moral theory has long marked a category of acts that are praiseworthy yet not strictly owed: saintly rescues, costly generosity, and forms of going beyond duty.[S24]

The archive now treats these as important **neighbors** of righteousness, not as its universal measure. Heroism may witness righteousness vividly, but righteousness should not be defined so maximally that ordinary due-fidelity always looks deficient by comparison.[S24][S25]

## Why this matters for this archive

This threshold-band view clarifies four recurring confusions.

### A. Righteousness is not bare permissibility

Something can be permissible, excused, or procedurally acceptable without being righteous. The archive's core concept remains thicker: due-rendering, incorruptibility, and repair-accountability.

### B. Righteousness is not moral maximization

The archive does not require every agent to optimize all good outcomes or make every large sacrifice morality could conceivably ask for. Demandingness debates exist precisely because many theories become implausible when all moral value is turned into requirement.[S25]

### C. Righteousness is not identical with sainthood

Saints and heroes may exceed righteousness's threshold, but their extraordinary merit should not become the archive's default floor. Otherwise the concept collapses into aspiration talk and loses comparative operational use.[S24]

### D. Office can raise the threshold

The band is not flat across contexts. Entrusted office, asymmetrical power, fiduciary burden, and dependence can raise what counts as due. What is above-and-beyond for a private person may be ordinary righteousness for a judge, guardian, minister, trustee, or emergency official.[S17][S18][S19]

## Disqualifiers

The archive now treats some failures as strong defeaters even where other good features are present.

A claim of righteousness is heavily burdened, and usually defeated, when the conduct or institution depends on:
- **capture** — private gain, faction, or vanity redefining the due;[S04][S19]
- **laundering** — legality, ritual, or procedure used to hide abandonment or bad faith;[S09][S11][S12]
- **repair refusal** — blocking review, restitution, truth-telling, or correction after breach.

These disqualifiers matter because righteousness is not a net-score concept. Some defects do not merely subtract points; they undercut the standing of the claim itself. See `docs/10-canon/165-defeaters-laundering-and-standing.md` for the archive's sharper rule on defeaters, laundering, and dirty-hands necessity.

## Working rule

The archive now adopts the following working-canon rule:

> do **not** infer righteousness merely from the absence of overt wrongdoing; do **not** deny righteousness merely because an agent failed to be heroic.

Instead ask:
- what was centrally due here;
- what pressure pushed against it;
- whether the due was actually rendered;
- and whether truth and repair remained open.

## Portable use

This threshold-band view is intentionally portable because traditions can disagree about how wide the band is, how demanding righteousness finally becomes, and whether saintly excess is optional, exemplary, or obligatory in some callings.[S21][S24]

But the distinction itself is useful across traditions:
- a **floor** below which one is plainly failing,
- a **band** where one is actually standing rightly,
- and a **ceiling** of aspiration or extraordinary merit.

That structure helps the archive avoid both flattening and inflation.

## What remains open

The archive still does not know how much the threshold varies by tradition, vocation, emergency, or covenantal frame.

So this document is a sharpening move, not closure. It partially answers the old question about minimum conditions, but leaves live the harder question of how thresholds shift across roles and traditions.
