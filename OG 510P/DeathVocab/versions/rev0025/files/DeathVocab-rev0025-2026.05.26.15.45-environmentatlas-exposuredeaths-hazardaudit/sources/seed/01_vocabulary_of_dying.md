# The Vocabulary of Dying — A Seed Document for Corpus Construction

This document is **not** the corpus. It is the briefing for what the corpus could become. It exists to seed considerations that the builder is unlikely to reach on their own. Treat every section as a prompt for further research, not a closed list.

---

## 1. The premise

Hospice and palliative-care workers — nurses, doctors, doulas, chaplains, social workers, funeral directors, coroners, home-health aides, family carers — collectively hold a body of knowledge about how humans die that is:

- mostly oral
- mostly never written down
- distributed across people who are aging, leaving the profession, or burning out
- culturally fragmented (each tradition holds part of it)
- medically partial in the published literature (textbooks describe symptoms but not what dying *looks like, sounds like, smells like, feels like* from the bedside)
- *the most universally relevant body of knowledge that exists* — 100% of readers will need it

The corpus is the **textured, observational, phenomenological, practical, and linguistic** record of how dying actually goes. Not the medical model. Not the spiritual model. The bedside record.

The target audience is everyone who will encounter death (everyone) and especially: people sitting with a dying person for the first time, new hospice workers, medical students, death doulas in training, bereaved family members who want to understand what they saw, and anyone facing their own death.

---

## 2. Why capitalists will not build this

- No subscription model (people use it for weeks, not years)
- No upsell (you can't sell premium grief)
- Cultural taboo limits virality
- The labor required is qualitative, slow, and ethically constrained
- The closest existing products (Cake, Empathy, Lantern) are constrained by their need to monetize end-of-life logistics, not the phenomenology of dying

## 3. The major axes of the corpus

The corpus should be organized along **at least these orthogonal axes**, and any record should be locatable in all of them:

### 3.1 Phenomenology axis — what is observed/experienced
What the body does. What the mind appears to do. What is sensed by people present.

### 3.2 Timeline axis — when in dying
Months/weeks → weeks/days → days/hours → hours/minutes → moment of death → first hours after → first days → first weeks → first year.

### 3.3 Trajectory axis — *how* someone is dying
- Cancer (relatively predictable decline)
- Organ failure (stepwise decline with crises)
- Frailty/dementia (long flat decline)
- Sudden (cardiac, trauma, stroke)
- Catastrophic (mass casualty)
- Self-determined (VSED, MAID where legal)
- Suicide (a distinct trajectory with distinct aftermath)
- Overdose
- Maternal
- Perinatal/stillbirth
- Pediatric
- Death of the very old (>90)
- Death in custody
- Death of the unhoused
- Death in war
- Death by famine
- Death by drowning
- Death by hypothermia (the "paradoxical undressing")
- Death by hyperthermia
- Death from neurodegenerative disease (ALS, Huntington's, advanced Parkinson's)
- Death from chronic respiratory disease (drowning in air)

### 3.4 Setting axis — where
Home / hospital / ICU / ED / hospice facility / nursing home / prison / street / battlefield / sea / wilderness / disaster site.

### 3.5 Perspective axis — whose record
The dying person / the family / the hospice worker / the doula / the chaplain / the funeral director / the coroner / the bystander / the responding professional / the survivor (in attempted suicide / sudden cardiac arrest cases).

### 3.6 Cultural/tradition axis
Each tradition handles dying differently in practice, language, ritual, and meaning. The corpus should preserve specificity, not flatten.

## 4. Substantive content categories

These are categories of *thing* the corpus should document. Not exhaustive.

### 4.1 The signs of approaching death

**Months to weeks:**
- Withdrawal from social contact
- Reduced interest in news, plans, future
- Increasing sleep
- Loss of appetite (specifically: loss of interest in meat first, then carbs, then liquids)
- Weight loss
- "Putting affairs in order" behavior
- Sometimes a final productive burst

**Weeks to days:**
- Mottling of extremities (knees, then feet, then hands)
- Decreased urine output
- Decreased blood pressure
- Cooler extremities
- Increasing somnolence
- Restlessness or agitation
- "Visioning" — talking with the unseen / dead relatives
- Reaching, picking, plucking at the air or bedding
- The "death stare" — eyes open but fixed
- Decreased response to stimuli

**Days to hours:**
- Cheyne-Stokes breathing (cycle of deep breaths and apnea)
- The "death rattle" — terminal secretions in the throat
- Mandibular breathing (jaw moving with each breath)
- Apneic pauses lengthening
- Skin color changes — pale, waxy, sometimes a final flush
- "Terminal lucidity" — a sudden brief return of clarity
- The "rally" — a final period of awareness, often hours before death
- Cessation of urine output entirely
- Inability to swallow
- Glassy eyes
- Tear secretion sometimes increases
- Specific smell (ketones, sometimes a sweet smell)

**Moment of death:**
- Last breath patterns (often two or three "agonal" breaths)
- Pupil dilation
- Skin pallor advancing
- The "Lazarus signs" — postmortem muscle twitches
- Bowel/bladder release
- The way the mouth falls open
- The "death sigh"

**First hours after:**
- Pallor mortis (immediate)
- Algor mortis (cooling, ~1.5°F/hour)
- Rigor mortis onset (2–6 hours)
- Livor mortis (pooling of blood, visible 30 minutes to 2 hours)
- Changes in eye appearance (corneal clouding within hours)
- Skin slippage in some circumstances
- Smell changes within first day

### 4.2 What dying people say

Not as theater — as observation. Document patterns:

- "I want to go home" (said by people who *are* home)
- "Mom" / "Mama" / "Mommy" (across languages and ages)
- References to travel, trains, buses, packing, going somewhere
- Greetings to people not in the room (often dead relatives)
- Apologies and reconciliations
- Specific questions ("Where are my shoes?" / "Am I dying?")
- Requests (water, music, a specific person)
- Final coherent statements (often briefer than expected)
- Last meals requested (often childhood foods)
- The role of pets being brought in
- Silence — and how often dying people just stop talking days before
- What they DON'T say (the things they take with them)

### 4.3 What dying people experience (when we can know)

- Reports from near-death survivors (cardiac arrest, etc.)
- Reports from people who recovered after being declared moribund
- Christopher Kerr's research on end-of-life dreams and visions
- The phenomenology of terminal lucidity (when patients describe it after recovering)
- Reports of pain (often less than expected at the end)
- Reports of fear (variable; often less in the final stages than mid-illness)
- Awareness of dying — when they know
- The interior experience of dementia at end of life (hardest to access; some recoveries from severe states give partial reports)

### 4.4 What hospice workers do

The actual practice. Not theory.

- Repositioning schedules (every 2 hours, even for very small movements)
- Mouth care (swabs, ice chips, lip balm — and the dispute over hydration)
- Skin care to prevent breakdown
- Catheter management
- Bowel care (constipation from opioids is constant)
- Medication titration:
  - Morphine (and its derivatives) for dyspnea and pain
  - Lorazepam for anxiety and terminal agitation
  - Haloperidol for delirium
  - Scopolamine or glycopyrrolate for secretions
  - Subcutaneous infusions when oral isn't possible
  - The "comfort kit" / "anticipatory meds"
- Oxygen — and the active debate about whether it helps at end of life
- Positioning for comfort (not for pressure prevention) in final hours
- How to speak in the room (assuming hearing is preserved)
- How to involve children
- How to handle the body after death
- The dance with families — pacing information, holding space, witnessing fights
- The phone calls (waking estranged relatives at 3am)
- The pronouncement of death
- The release of the body to the funeral home
- The follow-up calls / bereavement support

### 4.5 The "kit"

The actual physical and verbal toolkit. Many hospice workers carry a personal mental kit, often unwritten:

- Music they put on when nothing else is helping
- The mints they offer family members
- The phrases they use to invite people to say goodbye
- The way they explain Cheyne-Stokes to a panicked spouse
- How they handle a dying person whose family won't accept it
- How they handle a family member screaming at God
- The objects they keep in their bag (lip balm, swabs, a small fan, tissues, lotion, a small radio, lavender oil — practitioners vary)
- The questions they ask families ("What did your mother love?")

Specific traditions — many under-documented:

- Catholic last rites (specific prayers, anointing, who performs them)
- Jewish chevra kadisha practice (washing the body, watching with it)
- Muslim ghusl, kafan, janazah
- Buddhist tukdam (advanced practitioners' meditation in death)
- Tibetan bardo practice
- Hindu practices at the Ganges and home equivalents
- Yoruba traditions
- Akan (Ghanaian) funeral practices
- Indigenous traditions across the Americas (when traditions can be shared)
- Pacific Islander vigil practices
- Roma death customs
- Quaker practice (silent witness)
- Mormon ordinances
- Mexican and Central American Día de los Muertos practice (mourning beyond the death)
- Vietnamese ancestor practices
- Korean death rituals
- Japanese itai-kaiyaku (washing) and modern hybrid practice
- Bali cremation practice (Ngaben)
- Sky burial (Tibet, Mongolia, Parsi)
- Sea burial
- Green burial movement
- Home funeral movement
- Death doula approaches (themselves cross-cultural and varied)

### 4.7 The afterward — the first days

What practitioners know about the first 24 hours, week, month:

- The "second goodbye" when the funeral home takes the body
- The first night sleeping alone after a spouse dies
- The mail and bills problem
- The disposal of belongings — when, what, by whom
- The decision about cremation vs. burial under duress
- The funeral planning conflicts
- The "where do I sit" problem at the service
- The eulogy nobody wants to write
- The phantom calls (reaching for the phone to call the dead)
- The first time someone asks "how's your mom?" not knowing
- Sleep disturbance patterns
- Appetite patterns
- The grief "waves" model — peaks and troughs
- The "first" everything (first birthday, holiday, anniversary)
- The 6-month mark when condolences stop and grief intensifies
- The 1-year mark
- "Anniversary reactions" forever

### 4.8 Pediatric dying — its own subcorpus

- Children's understanding of death by developmental stage (under 5, 5-9, 9-12, adolescent)
- What dying children say
- How children dying ask about death (often direct)
- The "last things" parents preserve
- How siblings experience and grieve
- The school's role
- Perinatal loss as a specific grief
- Stillbirth — the photographs (Now I Lay Me Down to Sleep), the footprints, the holding of the body
- SIDS and the absence of cause
- Childhood cancer wards as their own world
- The protocols at children's hospices (e.g., Helen House)
- Bereaved parents' organizations (Compassionate Friends)
- Sibling support (Sibshops)
- The unique grief of losing a child

### 4.9 The aftermath of suicide

- The "police call" experience
- Coroner involvement
- The note (or absence of one)
- "Postvention" — the work after

- The specific guilt
- The "why" that never resolves

- Means-restriction conversations afterward
- The cleaning question (specialized services exist; many families don't know)

### 4.10 Death in custody / death in institutions

- What ICU death looks like (often more traumatic, less acknowledged)
- The "compassionate extubation" process
- Death in jail / prison (often unwitnessed, often opaque to families)
- Death in immigration detention
- Death in psychiatric facilities

### 4.11 The death of caregivers

## 6. Schema for the corpus

A record in the corpus might have:

- **ID**: unique identifier
- **Source**: who recorded this (with credential / role)
- **Source type**: observation / interview / literature / archive / etc.
- **Setting**: home / hospital / ICU / etc.
- **Trajectory**: cancer / sudden / etc.
- **Phase**: months / weeks / days / hours / moment / after
- **Cultural frame**: tradition(s) involved
- **Demographics** (when relevant and ethical): age range, broad geography — never identifying
- **The observation/quote/practice**: the actual content
- **Interpretation** (clearly marked separately): what the contributor thinks it means
- **Cross-references**: similar records, contrasting records
- **Provenance & consent**: how this was obtained, what permissions exist
- **Stability**: is this a one-off observation or a recurring pattern?
- **Tags**: rich free-tagging for emergent categories

The corpus should reward both browsing and search. A bereaved person searching "my dad reached for the air" should find: the recurring pattern in days-before-death, what hospice workers call it, examples from many traditions, what dying people have said about it when they recovered, and what this isn't (not always agitation; often appears purposive).

## 7. Edge cases and especially difficult areas

These are areas the builder should plan for explicitly. They tend to get skipped.

- **The deaths that aren't pretty.** Drug deaths, alcoholic deaths, deaths of the unhoused, deaths in domestic violence, deaths in custody. The corpus must not become an aesthetic of "good death."
- **The deaths that are political.** Death of activists, dissidents, journalists, in protest. Deaths in war. Deaths from policy choices (heat deaths, austerity deaths).
- **The deaths that institutions hide.** Nursing home deaths during COVID. Deaths in immigration detention. Deaths of children in state custody.
- **The deaths nobody witnessed.** The death of the unhoused person found days later. The death by suicide nobody saw coming.
- **Mass death.** Battlefield. Disaster. Pandemic. The corpus cannot be built only on the "one-on-one" model.
- **Medically assisted dying.** Oregon, Canadian MAID, Swiss assisted suicide, Belgian and Dutch protocols. A growing, distinct phenomenology.
- **The dying of those who do not want to die.** Most accounts come from those who reached acceptance. The corpus needs the angry, the bargaining, the unreconciled.
- **The dying of those who do not believe.** The corpus skews religious because religious traditions document this most. The atheist deathbed is under-documented.
- **The very old.** Death after 100 has its own character — often very gentle, often after long decline. Underdocumented because it's seen as unremarkable.
- **Maternal death.** Both rare in the developed world and devastating; profound in low-resource settings.
- **The death of the person dying alone.** Most accounts assume witnesses. Many deaths have none.
- **The death where the body isn't recovered.** Disappearance, drowning, fire, war. Survivors have a distinct experience.
- **Animal companions of the dying.** The dog who won't leave the bedside. The cat that crawls under. Underdocumented; widely observed.
- **The death of the caregiver before the cared-for.** Statistically common; emotionally devastating.
- **Posthumous medical events.** Organ donation timing. Autopsy. Embalming choices. Body donation. These are part of the corpus.
- **The funeral that doesn't happen.** Bodies unclaimed. Cremation by the county. The corpus should reach the dignity-of-the-unclaimed question.

## 8. Ethical principles

The corpus must rest on:

- **No identification without consent.** Even after death, families consent.
- **Cultural humility.** Some practices should be documented as existing without their interior contents being shared. The corpus respects the difference between "exists" and "is yours to see."
- **No voyeurism.** The reader should leave a record more capable of presence, not more excited about death.
- **No flattening.** Specificity wins over generalization. "Many people say…" is weaker than three named-or-anonymized records with full context.
- **Living contributors get edit rights.** Hospice workers who contribute should be able to amend, withdraw, or contextualize.
- **The corpus must not be weaponized.** It cannot become a tool for, e.g., predicting time of death to terminate insurance.
- **Indigenous knowledge requires sovereignty.** Indigenous traditions are not the corpus's to take; they participate on their terms or not at all.
- **Children's words.** Children's dying words and family stories require the highest care. Most should probably remain unpublished.
- **Suicide and means.** The corpus should follow safe-messaging guidelines (no specific methods, no romanticization).

- "Going downhill"
- "Won't see morning"
- "Hours, maybe days"
- "Not appropriate for further intervention"

The corpus should document the language with the same care as the phenomenology.

## 10. What "done well" looks like

After 10,000 turns, the corpus should be something like:

- A bereaved adult child can read, in the first week, a description of what they witnessed in their parent's last hours, and feel less alone and more accurately informed.
- A new hospice nurse can use it as a working reference for what to expect across trajectories.
- A medical student can find phenomenological detail their textbooks don't carry.
- A scholar can find primary sources, traditions, and patterns.
- A novelist can write dying scenes that don't ring false to people who have witnessed death.
- A doula can train new doulas with it.
- A person facing their own death can read first-person accounts from those who have — and find honesty about fear, regret, acceptance, anger, grace.
- A person from a specific tradition can find their tradition treated with care, in its own voice.
- Every entry has provenance. Every entry has a name (of the contributor, the source, the tradition). Nothing is generic.

The corpus is **observational, not prescriptive**. It does not tell the reader how to die or how to grieve. It tells them what has been seen, said, done, felt, by witnesses and the witnessed.

## 11. Specific things ChatGPT (or any builder) might miss

- **Smell.** The smell of dying — ketones, urine, sometimes a sweet smell — is rarely written about, universally known to hospice workers.
- **Sound.** The death rattle, the agonal breathing, the silence. The sounds of the family. The hum of the oxygen concentrator. The clock.
- **The cat / dog / pet.** Underdocumented; widely observed.
- **The estranged child returning.** A near-universal pattern. Often the most volatile family member at the bedside.
- **Money fights at the bedside.** Real. Documented poorly because it's distasteful.
- **The body's specific weight after death.** Hospice nurses and morticians know. Almost no one else does.
- **The "rally."** Hours-before-death return of clarity. Devastating to families who think recovery is happening.
- **"Choosing the moment."** The pattern of people dying when family steps out. Widely observed; physiologically real (sympathetic withdrawal); routinely missed by families.
- **Terminal lucidity in dementia.** Patients with severe dementia sometimes briefly return shortly before death.
- **The first night alone.** A genre unto itself.
- **The clothes question.** What did they wear; what to dress the body in; what to do with the clothes after.
- **The wedding ring decision.** Bury with / cremate with / keep.
- **The body in the home.** In the US most families don't know they're allowed to keep the body at home for hours.
- **The phone tree.** Who calls whom in what order. Sociologically rich.
- **The "they were a fighter" obituary trope.** And its absence in some traditions.
- **Anticipatory grief.** Grieving while the person is still alive.
- **Disenfranchised grief.** Grief for relationships society doesn't recognize (ex-spouses, affair partners, estranged family, queer relationships before recognition, pet loss).
- **The "second loss"** — the death of memory of the dead, the loss of objects, the dissolution of shared friend groups.
- **Continuing bonds.** The contemporary alternative to "moving on." How survivors maintain relationship with the dead.
- **Children's drawings of death.** A corpus in itself.
- **The dying of those who outlive everyone.** The 102-year-old whose entire generation is gone.
- **The role of the radio / TV / music in the dying room.** Specific to each death.

## 12. Adjacent corpora that compound

The vocabulary of dying touches:

- The vocabulary of caregiving (months and years before the end)
- The vocabulary of grief (the years after)
- The vocabulary of illness narratives
- The vocabulary of medical decision-making
- The vocabulary of disability and chronic illness (a related but distinct corpus)
- The vocabulary of birth (the bookend; many practitioners work both ends)
- The vocabulary of body work (the hands-on knowledge of nurses, aides, doulas)
- The vocabulary of waiting (vigil, ICU, hospice — long-form waiting has its own knowledge)

Any of these could be a subsequent corpus. The dying corpus done well makes them easier.

## 13. A note on tone

The corpus should never be reverent in a way that excludes. It should not be morbid in a way that performs. The right tone is the tone of an experienced hospice nurse talking to a colleague: precise, kind, unsentimental, specific, and unafraid.

The corpus is for the living, because all of us are dying.
