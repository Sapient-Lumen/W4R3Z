# What this could become — rev0002 direction memo

Status: directional operating thesis, not evidence. This memo records what the project seems to want to become after the seed brief and prior-art archive patterns have been absorbed.

## The central shape

DeathVocab should become a **bedside atlas of witnessed moments**.

A glossary is necessary, but it is not sufficient. The real thing to preserve is the room: what was seen, heard, smelled, said, withheld, misread, repeated, ritualized, argued over, cleaned, carried, documented, or remembered. A term is a handle. A witnessed moment is the thing.

The seed brief asks for a textured, observational, phenomenological, practical, and linguistic record rather than a medical or spiritual model. Rev0002 sharpens that into an architecture: every future entry should keep the observed scene intact before it is mapped to terms, traditions, practices, or explanations.

## The three-sided public object

The eventual project likely needs three public faces that share one underlying corpus.

1. **For the newly bereaved or bedside witness:** a plain-language search surface. The user does not know the technical term. They search from shock: “reaching for the air,” “rattling sound,” “wanted to go home,” “suddenly better,” “sweet smell,” “mouth open,” “funeral home took the body.” The surface returns careful examples, nearby terms, cautions, and uncertainty.

2. **For practitioners and students:** an apprenticeship atlas. This is where one can learn what experienced hospice nurses, aides, doulas, chaplains, funeral directors, coroners, first responders, and family carers notice. It should preserve the small practical moves: mouth care, how people explain breathing changes, how families react, when a room changes, what not to say.

3. **For scholars, tradition holders, and future stewards:** a provenance corpus. Every record should have source, role, axis locations, consent/publication state, stability, interpretation separation, contradiction links, cultural permission, and rollback paths.

## The atomic unit: witnessed moment

A witnessed moment is not merely a symptom, quotation, or anecdote. It is a bounded record with enough context to remain humane and useful.

A good witnessed moment captures:

- who is witnessing, and from what role;
- where and when in the dying/afterward arc;
- what trajectory or institutional setting is involved;
- what sensory channel is active: sound, smell, touch, sight, silence, language, timing;
- the raw observation or utterance;
- what the witness thought it meant, clearly separated from what was observed;
- what other records resemble or contradict it;
- what cannot be concluded from it;
- what permission exists for using it.

This lets a future glossary entry remain alive. “Death rattle” is not just a definition. It becomes a cluster of sounds, family reactions, bedside explanations, clinical cautions, cultural framings, and aftermath memories.

## The danger of becoming too pretty

The project will fail if it becomes only a literature of peaceful hospice deaths. It must preserve ordinary, ugly, institutional, hidden, political, unwitnessed, pediatric, perinatal, suicide, overdose, custody, unhoused, disaster, and mass-death material without turning those deaths into spectacle.

The corpus should make room for the deaths that do not console the reader. It should document anger, fear, estrangement, bad rooms, paperwork, money fights, institutional opacity, bodies not recovered, funerals that do not happen, and grief that is socially illegible.

## The danger of becoming too helpful

A public reader may arrive frightened and tempted to use the archive as advice. The project must therefore be useful without becoming a clinical decision engine. It can say what has been observed. It can say what practitioners often call something. It can say when urgent professional help may be appropriate in general safety language. It must not become a medication, prognosis, eligibility, timing, or body-handling instruction tool.

The ideal tone is precise, kind, unsentimental, and bounded: the tone of an experienced hospice nurse who knows what she knows and knows what she should not claim.

## The deeper public service

The project is not just “information about death.” It is a way to reduce the loneliness of witnessing.

Many people carry a last image or sound for years without language for it. A well-built DeathVocab entry lets someone say: I saw this; others have seen something like it; here are the words people use; here is what is known; here is what is not known; here are ways different people have held it; here is why my memory is not monstrous.

That is the public service: not reassurance, but orientation.

## The 10,000-revision horizon

Over 10,000+ revisions this could grow from scaffold to living atlas:

- first: governance, consent, source protocol, and pilot records;
- then: public-source records and glossary/search crosswalks;
- then: practitioner testimony and role-specific kits;
- then: tradition-governed rooms and institutionally hidden deaths;
- then: multilingual lay search, teaching surfaces, scholarly tooling, corrections, withdrawals, and long-term stewardship.

The corpus should be allowed to stay unfinished. DeathVocab’s promise is not final authority. Its promise is accumulated witness under ethical constraint.
