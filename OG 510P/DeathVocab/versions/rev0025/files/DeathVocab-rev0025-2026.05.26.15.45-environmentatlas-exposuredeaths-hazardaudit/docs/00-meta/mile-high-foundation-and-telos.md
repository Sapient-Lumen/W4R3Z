# Mile-high foundation and telos

Rev0013 is a deliberate pause in domain expansion.

The cube has already opened threshold phenomena, practice, utterance, institutional decision language, aftermath, tradition, trajectory, setting, and perspective. That breadth is useful, but breadth can create a false feeling of maturity. The archive is still pre-public, pre-private-testimony, pre-review, and pre-field-test. It is mostly a machine for holding future seriousness.

## The deepest telos

DeathVocab should make dying more legible without making it smaller.

That means the archive should not domesticate death into a checklist, a stage theory, a symptom engine, a spiritual proof machine, a grief-product funnel, or a gallery of extraordinary last words. It should preserve the room: the sound, smell, body, clock, pet, mouth, machine, law, family fight, prayer, silence, paperwork, worker’s hands, unanswered question, and memory afterward.

The project is trying to create a public good for a universal human need that normal markets do not serve well: the need to understand what happened around dying and death without being sold reassurance, ideology, logistics software, or performance spirituality.

## The thing being earned

The archive is not entitled to exist at full power just because the subject matters.

It must earn:

- trust from bereaved people;
- trust from dying people;
- trust from practitioners whose knowledge is often private, oral, and carried in the body;
- trust from communities and traditions whose practices are not automatically public;
- trust from scholars and clinicians who need provenance and review;
- trust from future operators who must know what is claim, hope, scaffold, debt, and refusal.

The archive earns that trust by being more careful than it is ambitious in public, while remaining more imaginative than it is bureaucratic in private scaffolding.

## The dream after 10,000 revisions

After 10,000 revisions, the archive should feel less like a database and more like a governed memory commons.

A bereaved adult child can search from the crude phrase they have — “my mother’s mouth fell open,” “he sounded like he was drowning,” “she kept saying she needed to go home,” “the funeral home took him and I lost him again” — and find a page that meets the scene without pretending to know more than it does.

A new hospice worker can find what experienced workers notice but textbooks often do not: the room’s clock, the oxygen concentrator hum, the way families hear breathing, the phrases that calm or inflame, the bodily details nobody warned them about, the deaths that stay with them.

A person facing death can find accounts that do not force acceptance, transcendence, bravery, or peace. Fear, anger, bargaining, boredom, humor, exhaustion, relief, regret, and refusal all have to fit.

A person from a specific tradition can find that the archive knows the difference between naming a practice, describing a public-facing frame, and claiming authority to teach it.

A scholar can trace claims, sources, contradictions, permissions, and withdrawals. A novelist can stop writing dying scenes that ring false. A clinician can see what patients and families actually heard, not only what the chart says.

## Why “vocabulary” is still the right name

The project is not merely a glossary, but vocabulary is the hinge.

People arrive with words. They arrive with wrong words, euphemisms, local words, family phrases, clinical leaks, tradition terms, whispered guesses, and search-engine panic. The archive’s job is to crosswalk those words into scenes and sources.

“Death rattle” is not the record. A room where a spouse hears wet breathing and thinks the person is drowning is the record. But the term matters because it is one of the bridges between panic and orientation.

## What must remain alive

The archive must keep space for:

- contradiction;
- refusal;
- silence;
- local variation;
- unpretty deaths;
- institutional opacity;
- private knowledge;
- mistakes in earlier revisions;
- grief that does not improve;
- dying that is angry or unreconciled;
- records that cannot be public;
- future axes we have not yet imagined.

If the cube becomes too tidy, it is probably lying.

## The rule of the office

Be strict about harm. Be loose about form.

Consent, sovereignty, source, safety, non-voyeurism, and non-advice are hard constraints. Ontology, interface, sequencing, prose style, and the current axes are working hypotheses.
