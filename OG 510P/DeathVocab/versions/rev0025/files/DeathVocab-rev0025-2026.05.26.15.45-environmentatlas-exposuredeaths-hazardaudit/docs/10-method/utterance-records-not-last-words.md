
# Utterance records are not last words

The seed asks for what dying people say, but the archive must not confuse that with a collection of dramatic final sentences.

The primary unit remains the witnessed moment. A phrase is not enough. A record needs the room: who heard it, whether it was exact or remembered, whether it was translated, whether the speaker was responsive or drifting, whether the witness interpreted it as literal, symbolic, delirious, spiritual, ordinary, or unknown, and how the room responded.

This revision therefore adds utterance records in quarantine. The records are allowed to preserve phrases such as wanting to go home, travel language, fear, direct questions, goodbye words, permission language, and no last words. But every one of those phrases has a boundary:

- not a prophecy;
- not exact timing;
- not proof of a spiritual claim;
- not proof of delirium in every case;
- not a universal last-word pattern;
- not a script that families owe the dying;
- not a closure story forced onto unfinished relationships.

The archive should become very good at storing words without worshiping them. Silence, ordinary talk, jokes, repeated small phrases, and failed or absent goodbyes are as central as quotable last sentences.
