# Phased adoption roadmap

The archive's current rollout logic is **sequence matters**.

## Phase 0 — Readiness

Before broad deployment:

- establish privacy, procurement, and rights review;
- decide which uses are low-, medium-, and high-stakes;
- train leaders and early teacher cohorts;
- choose a governed institutional workspace;
- define the first proof metrics;
- and decide how accessibility, accommodation, and multilingual support requests route without forcing public disclosure.

## Phase 1 — Teacher productivity and teacher minimum

Start where upside is visible and risks are manageable:

- lesson adaptation,
- feedback drafting,
- translation/accessibility support,
- administrative compression,
- and teacher AI fluency.

This phase builds staff competence before normalizing student dependence.

## Phase 2 — Student AI literacy and structured study use

Introduce student-facing AI in clearly bounded ways:

- a minimal literacy spine across grades or sectors,
- study companions,
- practice generation,
- scaffolding,
- guided writing support,
- and tutoring support.

But pair this with explicit rules for disclosure, appropriate delegation, and course-level norms. Institutions should also name one or two mode-tagged subject routines per family so AI literacy becomes visible in ordinary classes rather than only in policy, publish one short shared legend for mode/disclosure expectations, and state which support questions route privately through accessibility/accommodation channels. The archive's current preferred sequence is: **without AI**, then **with guided educational AI**, then **with general-purpose AI**. Outside ordinary school or university enrollment, public systems should also stand up low-barrier adult entry points through libraries, community colleges/VET, and workforce navigation rather than assuming that only current students need structured AI learning. See [`../10-core/minimal-ai-literacy-spine.md`](../10-core/minimal-ai-literacy-spine.md), [`subject-embedded-exemplar-kernel.md`](subject-embedded-exemplar-kernel.md), [`course-level-ai-use-grammar.md`](course-level-ai-use-grammar.md), [`../20-governance/accommodation-aware-disclosure-and-accessibility.md`](../20-governance/accommodation-aware-disclosure-and-accessibility.md), and [`lifelong-public-ai-learning-stack.md`](lifelong-public-ai-learning-stack.md).

## Phase 3 — Assessment redesign

This phase is often skipped and should not be. Once students have ordinary access to capable AI, institutions need stronger proof surfaces:

- more live tasks,
- more oral explanation,
- more checkpoints,
- more revision visibility,
- and fewer unauthenticated polished artifacts as stand-alone evidence.

The archive's current improvement is to define default **bundle** patterns so this phase is actionable rather than purely rhetorical, and to link those bundles to mode-tagged subject routines. See [`../40-assessment/proof-of-learning-bundles.md`](../40-assessment/proof-of-learning-bundles.md) and [`subject-embedded-exemplar-kernel.md`](subject-embedded-exemplar-kernel.md).

## Phase 4 — Evidence-backed specialized tools

Only after governance, literacy, and assessment have moved should institutions scale more ambitious tools, especially those making personalization or tutoring claims. Public R&D and emerging trials suggest this is where long-run gains may come from, but the evidence bar must remain high. See `B12`, `B13`.

## Phase 5 — Continuous evaluation and retirement

Institutional AI adoption should include explicit stop conditions:

- retire tools that do not produce value,
- demote tools that create dependency without understanding,
- and re-run equity and accessibility checks as use patterns change.

## Compact warning

The failure mode is easy to name:

> institutions adopt AI for convenience before they build literacy, guardrails, and new proof-of-learning surfaces.

That sequence makes cheating panic, brittle policy, and bad pedagogy more likely.


## Cross-cutting public route

Institutions should not wait until perfect school deployment to think about adults and workers. In parallel with the formal phases above, jurisdictions should identify at least one low-cost civic first-contact point, one bridge provider for deeper learning, and one workforce-navigation route so AI education remains a lifelong public pathway rather than a perk of current enrollment. They should also adopt a small shared handoff-and-recognition rule so learners do not restart intake at every node: low-friction entry, warm transfer, learner-controlled portability, recognition before duplication, a tiny machine-readable claim layer, a clear boundary between portable claims and protected local support records, published sector defaults for when outside AI-learning completions should count only for continuity, for waiver of duplicate basics, for review/challenge, or for formal credit, dated standing-equivalency lists for recurring outside cases, and a tiny publication profile with review and expiry windows matched to the family of outside learning being recognized.
