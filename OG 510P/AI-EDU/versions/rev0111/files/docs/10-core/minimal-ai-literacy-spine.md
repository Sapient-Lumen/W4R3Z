# Minimal AI literacy spine

This document closes the archive's first broad AI-literacy gap.

The archive's current position is that **AI literacy is not “prompt cleverness.”** It is a staged capacity to:

- understand what AI systems are and are not doing,
- use them with bounded educational purpose,
- inspect and challenge their outputs,
- disclose and govern their use,
- and eventually help shape tools and norms rather than merely consume them.

The spine below is intentionally minimal. It is designed to be embedded across subjects and institutions, not treated as a giant new standalone discipline.

## Core rule

Students should normally encounter important capabilities in three modes:

1. **without AI** so they build baseline competence and productive struggle,
2. **with guided or educational AI** so support is aligned to learning goals,
3. **with general-purpose AI** so they learn realistic judgment under ordinary conditions.

OECD 2026 is the strongest current support for this ordering. See `B11`.

## Four recurring strands

Every age band should revisit four strands at increasing depth.

### S1. Understand the system

What AI is, what it is trained on, where it tends to fail, and why it is not an authority.

### S2. Use with purpose

When AI use helps learning, when it weakens learning, and how to choose bounded forms of assistance.

### S3. Check, disclose, and take responsibility

How to verify outputs, cite or disclose help, protect authorship, and remain accountable for final work.

### S4. Rights, impacts, and design

How AI affects privacy, bias, work, accessibility, language, culture, and civic life—and, later, how systems can be shaped or built more responsibly.

## Age-banded progression

### Band A — Early primary

Target posture: **recognize, ask, compare**.

Learners should be able to:

- recognize that some digital systems generate text, images, or suggestions;
- understand that these systems can be wrong or make things up;
- keep private information private and ask a trusted adult before use;
- compare an AI answer with something they already know, read, or observe;
- and explain that the point of school is still to learn, not only to get answers.

Institutional implication: keep use highly guided, brief, and teacher-mediated.

### Band B — Upper primary

Target posture: **use safely for practice, not substitution**.

Learners should be able to:

- describe AI as a pattern-based tool rather than a person or truth source;
- use AI for explanation, vocabulary help, questioning, or practice;
- notice obvious errors, bias, or mismatches with instructions;
- state simple classroom rules for allowed and disallowed use;
- and disclose when AI helped with a task.

Institutional implication: begin explicit norms for verification and bounded use.

### Band C — Lower secondary

Target posture: **interrogate and verify**.

Learners should be able to:

- choose between unassisted work, guided AI support, and general-purpose tools for different tasks;
- ask better questions and refine prompts without treating prompting as the goal;
- cross-check claims with sources, evidence, or worked examples;
- identify common failure modes such as hallucination, shallow explanation, or biased framing;
- and explain why some assessments or situations require less or no AI help.

Institutional implication: move from rule-following to judgment, with explicit discussion of authorship and academic integrity.

### Band D — Upper secondary

Target posture: **delegate selectively, preserve authorship**.

Learners should be able to:

- use AI for brainstorming, tutoring, critique, coding help, translation, or planning while preserving responsibility for final reasoning;
- decide when AI use would undercut the learning goal even if it improves the immediate product;
- manage privacy, intellectual-property, and data-sharing risks at a basic level;
- disclose AI assistance in ways that are accurate and not theatrical;
- and critique how AI systems shape work, information environments, and opportunity.

Institutional implication: AI literacy becomes partly disciplinary. Science, writing, coding, and vocational programs should each specify good and bad delegation patterns.

### Band E — Postsecondary, vocational, and adult learning

Target posture: **disciplinary judgment and local governance**.

Learners should be able to:

- evaluate tools against domain-specific standards of accuracy, evidence, safety, and professional responsibility;
- distinguish acceptable assistance from misconduct in their field;
- design repeatable workflows that use AI without hollowing out expertise;
- understand sector-specific accessibility, privacy, and rights obligations;
- and participate in improving local norms, procurement feedback, or tool design.

Institutional implication: AI literacy is no longer only about student behavior. It becomes part of professional formation, lifelong learning, and workforce transition. See `B19`, `B21`.

## Teacher sidecar

UNESCO's teacher framework is the cleanest current scaffold for staff progression. The archive's minimal interpretation is:

- **Acquire:** teachers can use AI safely, explain local rules, and recognize obvious misuse;
- **Deepen:** teachers can redesign lessons and assessment around the presence of AI;
- **Create:** teachers can help select tools, shape local policy, mentor peers, and co-design better workflows.

See `B03`.

## What this spine is not

It is **not**:

- a stand-alone prompt-engineering course,
- a mandate that every child use general-purpose AI early,
- a license to replace subject knowledge with tool familiarity,
- or a claim that technical model-building should dominate general education.

## Current archive bet

The archive's current best guess is that a sane system will embed this spine across subjects, advisories, library/media work, and key transition years rather than bolt on a large separate AI class for everyone.

That bet now rests on five connected surfaces: the spine, the exemplar kernel, a light course-level use/disclosure grammar, a small profile for inherited override rows where construct-sensitive supports recur, and a tiny starter row set for the most reusable cases. The next design problem is no longer whether starter defaults are possible, but which of them are stable enough to promote, split, or retire as sectors learn from implementation.
