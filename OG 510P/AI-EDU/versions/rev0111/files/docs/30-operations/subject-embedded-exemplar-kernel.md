# Subject-embedded exemplar kernel

This document closes the archive's first exemplar gap.

The archive now has a minimal AI literacy spine, but a spine alone does not tell teachers what to do on Tuesday. The point of this kernel is to name a **small** set of subject-embedded routines that schools, universities, and adult-learning providers can actually carry.

The archive's current position is that institutions do **not** need dozens of AI lessons. They need a few recurring routines that:

- protect the main learning goal of the subject,
- make the role of AI explicit,
- produce evidence of student judgment rather than only polished output,
- and can be reused across year levels with increasing sophistication.

## Two operating rules

### 1. Mode-tag the task

Every exemplar should make the task mode visible using the shared syntax in [`course-level-ai-use-grammar.md`](course-level-ai-use-grammar.md). The archive's default instructional sequence remains: **NO-AI**, then **GUIDED-AI**, then **OPEN-AI** when the learner and context are ready. See `B11` and [`../10-core/minimal-ai-literacy-spine.md`](../10-core/minimal-ai-literacy-spine.md).

### 2. Keep one signature routine per subject family

A good institution does not ask every teacher to invent AI literacy from scratch. It names one or two repeatable routines per subject family and lets local teachers adapt them.

## What counts as a good exemplar

A good exemplar:

- teaches a real subject objective rather than generic tool use;
- makes a specific AI failure mode legible;
- preserves a human proof surface;
- and can scale from one band to the next by changing difficulty, not by changing philosophy.

## The kernel

## E1. Writing and humanities — feedback is not authorship

**Typical bands:** upper primary through higher education.

**Mode sequence:** `NO-AI` draft -> `GUIDED-AI` critique -> `NO-AI` revision or short defense.

**Learning target:** distinguish between receiving feedback and outsourcing thinking.

**Routine:**

- learner writes a paragraph, claim set, or short argument unassisted;
- AI is then used only to generate feedback, questions, counterarguments, or revision suggestions;
- learner decides which suggestions to accept, reject, or rewrite;
- learner submits a revised product plus a short explanation of what changed and why.

**Why this earns its place:** UNESCO's competency work and school-level implementation examples show that AI can be used as a tutor or feedback partner inside ordinary subjects, including English, without making the system the author. See `B04`, `B22`, `B23`.

**Proof surface:** original draft, revision memo, and a short live or timed transfer paragraph.

## E2. History and social studies — source, claim, interest

**Typical bands:** upper primary through postsecondary.

**Mode sequence:** `GUIDED-AI` or `OPEN-AI`, followed by `NO-AI` or source-bound verification.

**Learning target:** evaluate credibility, bias, and purpose when AI summarizes, frames, or invents claims.

**Routine:**

- learner asks AI for an explanation, summary, or comparison about a historical event or civic issue;
- learner then checks the answer against a teacher-provided source set;
- learner marks what was supported, distorted, omitted, or oversimplified;
- learner explains which interests or assumptions may have shaped the response.

**Why this earns its place:** MAIL and the OECD/EC AI literacy work both treat credibility, critical evaluation, and responsible participation as central competences rather than side issues. See `B18`, `B22`.

**Proof surface:** annotated source check, short explanation of errors or bias, and a no-AI exit response.

## E3. Mathematics — explain, verify, transfer

**Typical bands:** upper primary through early postsecondary.

**Mode sequence:** `NO-AI` attempt -> `GUIDED-AI` explanation -> `NO-AI` transfer.

**Learning target:** use AI to inspect method without letting it replace mathematical reconstruction.

**Routine:**

- learner first attempts a problem or set of problems alone;
- AI is then used to explain a method, compare solution paths, or diagnose likely errors;
- learner must solve a related problem with altered numbers, assumptions, or representations without AI;
- learner explains which part of the method transfers and which does not.

**Why this earns its place:** the AILit work explicitly notes that mathematics, especially statistics and probability, is a natural site for understanding how AI models work, while OECD 2026 warns against confusing answer production with learning. See `B11`, `B22`.

**Proof surface:** first attempt, checked explanation, and no-AI transfer problem.

## E4. Science — design, critique, revise

**Typical bands:** lower secondary through higher education.

**Mode sequence:** `NO-AI` plan -> `GUIDED-AI` critique -> `NO-AI` revision.

**Learning target:** use AI to strengthen experimental reasoning, not to replace it.

**Routine:**

- learner proposes a hypothesis, procedure, model, or explanation;
- AI critiques the design, highlights confounds, or suggests missing variables;
- learner accepts, rejects, or modifies those critiques with reasons;
- learner then carries out or simulates the task and reports uncertainty.

**Why this earns its place:** science is a strong home for structured AI critique because the discipline already values method, evidence, and uncertainty over polished prose. The archive treats this as a natural place to operationalize human judgment under AI conditions. See `B11`, `B12`, `B22`.

**Proof surface:** original plan, critique triage, revised plan, and follow-up explanation of uncertainty.

## E5. Coding and data work — generate, inspect, modify, defend

**Typical bands:** lower secondary through adult learning.

**Mode sequence:** `OPEN-AI` allowed, but never as stand-alone proof.

**Learning target:** distinguish code generation from system understanding.

**Routine:**

- learner uses AI to draft code, queries, or analysis steps;
- learner must explain the architecture or logic in ordinary language;
- learner completes a live modification challenge or debugging task under changed conditions;
- learner discloses where AI was used and what had to be corrected.

**Why this earns its place:** both the literacy frameworks and the archive's proof-of-learning thesis imply that high-capability AI makes generated technical artifacts cheap, so understanding has to be shown through inspection, modification, and defense. See `B04`, `B11`, `B18`.

**Proof surface:** artifact, walkthrough, modification task, and disclosure note.

## E6. Vocational, professional, and adult learning — workflow with accountable handoff

**Typical bands:** upper secondary VET, higher education, workforce transition, adult learning.

**Mode sequence:** `OPEN-AI` with explicit boundary conditions.

**Learning target:** build workflows that use AI without surrendering professional responsibility.

**Routine:**

- learner uses AI for drafting, summarizing, scheduling, customer communication, troubleshooting, or planning;
- learner identifies what parts of the workflow remain human-only because of safety, compliance, care, or reputational stakes;
- learner produces a brief handoff note describing where AI entered the process, what was checked, and what remains uncertain;
- learner completes a live scenario where conditions shift and AI suggestions must be revised.

**Why this earns its place:** OECD's work on neurodivergent learners in VET and UNESCO's rights-based framing both point toward real-world usefulness, accessibility, and bounded responsibility rather than school-only norms. See `B19`, `B20`.

**Proof surface:** workflow artifact, accountable handoff note, and live adaptation.

## Implementation note

Institutions should treat these exemplars as a **kernel**, not a compliance checklist. The goal is not to force every teacher into the same sequence. The goal is to give each subject family one recognizable way to instantiate:

- the literacy spine,
- the proof-of-learning bundle logic,
- and the rule that AI can assist cognition without dissolving authorship or judgment.

## Current archive bet

The archive's current best guess is that a small number of recurring, mode-tagged subject routines will do more to operationalize AI literacy than broad competency rhetoric alone.

That is now canon, and it is now paired with a small cross-course grammar, a tiny profile for inherited override rows where construct-sensitive support rules recur, and a small starter set for the most reusable repeated cases. The next problem is which of those starter rows are stable enough to promote, split, or retire as sectors learn from real deployment.
