# Design principles

The archive's current canonical principles are below.

## 1. Optimize for learning, not output production

If a tool mainly improves surface-level task completion while weakening understanding, transfer, recall, or metacognition, it does not deserve educational centrality. OECD's 2026 Digital Education Outlook is especially clear that successful task performance with GenAI does not automatically lead to learning and can even reduce exam performance when productive struggle disappears. See `B11`.

## 2. Keep teachers in the accountability loop

Teachers remain responsible for pedagogical intent, contextual judgment, safeguarding, escalation, and final evaluation. UNESCO's teacher competency framework and U.S. DOE guidance both support a teacher-centred model rather than a teacher-optional one. See `B03`, `B07`, `B08`.

## 3. Treat AI literacy as a core competency with progression

Students need to understand what AI is, what it is good at, where it fails, and when its use is or is not appropriate. Teachers need equivalent but deeper competence. UNESCO's competency frameworks make this explicit for both groups, and OECD's PISA 2029 MAIL work shows that media-and-AI literacy is moving toward formal assessment territory. The archive now interprets this through a minimal age-banded spine, a three-mode learning sequence, a compact subject-embedded exemplar kernel, and a light course-level grammar for mode, disclosure, and proof. See `B03`, `B04`, `B11`, `B18`, `B21`, `B22`, `B24`, [`minimal-ai-literacy-spine.md`](minimal-ai-literacy-spine.md), and [`../30-operations/course-level-ai-use-grammar.md`](../30-operations/course-level-ai-use-grammar.md).

## 4. Distinguish low-stakes assistance from high-stakes judgment

Lesson planning assistance, practice-question generation, and tutoring prompts are not the same as admissions, placement, grading, discipline, disability supports, or other high-stakes determinations. The evidence and governance bar should rise with stakes. See `B07`, `B08`, `B09`.

## 5. Prefer purpose-built and pedagogically legible tools

General-purpose systems can help, but the archive currently gives priority to tools that expose pedagogical intent, align with subject-specific learning goals, and can be inspected by educators. OECD and IES both push in this direction. See `B11`, `B12`.

## 6. Demand evidence before scale

Teacher time savings matter, but they are not enough. Institutions should prefer tools supported by trials, strong implementation evidence, or at minimum a clear theory of change plus bounded pilots. Tutor CoPilot is important here because it provides live-tutoring evidence rather than only aspirational marketing. See `B13`.

## 7. Redesign assessment around proof of learning

The more capable AI becomes, the less credible unauthenticated take-home artifacts become as stand-alone evidence. Schools and universities should move toward process evidence, oral defense, live transfer tasks, checkpoints, revision histories, teacher sampling, and lighter disclosure coupled to stronger proof surfaces. See `B06`, `B11`, `B17`, `B26`.

## 8. Preserve privacy, rights, and opt-out paths

Educational AI operates in high-duty-of-care environments. Privacy, civil rights, disability rights, transparency, and meaningful opt-out are not optional extras. UNESCO's right-to-education framing sharpens this further by treating access, adaptation, and the best interests of the child as system requirements rather than afterthoughts. The archive now adds a concrete corollary: pedagogically relevant AI use may require course-level disclosure, but disability- or access-related support should usually route through protected channels rather than public coursework metadata. See `B07`, `B09`, `B20`, `B24`, `B28`.

## 9. Use AI to widen access, not stratify opportunity

The target condition is not that affluent students get premium AI tutors while others get generic chatbots. The target condition is universal baseline access to safe, high-quality, teacher-visible support, with accessibility and inclusion designed in from the start. The archive also treats neurodivergent and vocational learners as first-order design cases rather than afterthoughts, and now treats accessibility-by-design plus procurement review as part of the educational intervention rather than an after-market patch. See `B01`, `B09`, `B10`, `B19`, `B27`.

## 10. Separate canon from vendor churn

Provider features matter as market signals, but they should not become the archive's normative centre. Provider evidence is illustrative of where the ecosystem is moving; public standards and learning evidence remain governing. See `B14`, `B15`, `B16`.


## 11. Build lifelong public routes, not only school AI policy

Education with AI is not only a K-12 or campus problem. Adults need trustworthy first-contact points, bridge institutions, navigable training routes, and support during workforce transition. The archive therefore treats community colleges, VET providers, adult education, workforce systems, libraries, and learning-city/community spaces as part of the learning infrastructure AI will reshape. It now adds two corollaries: those nodes should share a small handoff standard built around warm transfer, learner-controlled portability, and recognition before duplication; and any recurring recognition rules should be published in dated, machine-readable form with family-specific freshness windows rather than hidden PDF tables or timeless provider trust. See `B29`, `B30`, `B31`, `B32`, `B33`, `B34`, `B35`, `B37`, `B38`, `B39`, `B41`, `B48`, `B51`, `B52`, [`../30-operations/lifelong-public-ai-learning-stack.md`](../30-operations/lifelong-public-ai-learning-stack.md), [`../30-operations/minimum-public-entitlement-and-handoff-standard.md`](../30-operations/minimum-public-entitlement-and-handoff-standard.md), and [`../30-operations/standing-list-publication-profile-and-recency-windows.md`](../30-operations/standing-list-publication-profile-and-recency-windows.md).
