# Presumptive access-support categories and override triggers

This document closes the archive's earlier ambiguity about **which AI-enabled supports can be presumed allowed across most courses** and which still require local override because they may change the construct being assessed.

The archive's current bet is simple:

> protect access by default, but force local override when a support starts doing the very work the course is meant to measure.

The point is to stop institutions from oscillating between two bad extremes:

- **single-regime confusion** — every support is treated like authorship and forced into artifact-level disclosure;
- **pure local drift** — every teacher improvises the boundary differently, so legitimate support use is chilled;
- **hidden construct substitution** — supports that remove friction are mixed together with supports that perform the target skill;
- **access without infrastructure** — schools allow support in theory but buy inaccessible tools, weak routing, and no human fallback.

UNESCO's rights framing, OECD's work on neurodivergent learners, the European Commission's ethical-use guidance, DOJ/OCR accessibility and civil-rights rules, CAST's UDL/accessibility work, and UNESCO's multilingualism roadmap all push in the same direction: accessibility, adaptability, multilingual access, and privacy-protective support routing should be treated as first-order design requirements, but not every transformation of a learner artifact counts as mere access. See `B19`, `B20`, `B24`, `B27`, `B28`, `B84`, `B85`, `B86`, `B87`.

## The governing distinction

A support stays on the **protected access rail** when its main function is to help the learner perceive, navigate, communicate with, or participate in the learning environment **without replacing the core performance claim**.

A support moves onto the **ordinary pedagogical AI rail** when it begins to supply, reshape, or optimize the very language, reasoning, procedure, or performance that the task is meant to evidence.

That distinction is not perfect, but it is far better than asking whether a tool is “AI” in the abstract.

## The five access-support classes

The archive now names five compact classes.

### `A1` — presentation and perception supports

Examples:

- text-to-speech;
- speech-to-text captioning for instruction;
- alternative format conversion;
- font, spacing, contrast, and reading-interface controls;
- reading-level controls or plain-language rewrites for instructions and administrative text;
- translation of navigation, directions, and support information.

**Default rule:** presumptively allowed across most courses and kept on the protected access rail.

Reason: these supports mostly change how material is received, not what the learner ultimately knows. OECD's 2026 VET work explicitly names captioning, speech-to-text, text-to-speech, reading-level controls, plain-language rewrites, and instant translation as accessibility features that can make materials easier to access for multilingual and neurodivergent learners. See `B19`.

### `A2` — communication and expression supports

Examples:

- dictation and voice input;
- augmentative and alternative communication support;
- spelling/mechanics stabilization where mechanics are not the target construct;
- translation for teacher-learner communication, planning, or support requests;
- interface simplification for form completion or workflow participation.

**Default rule:** presumptively allowed across most courses **so long as idea ownership and final judgment remain with the learner**.

Reason: these supports mainly help learners get into the task and express what they know in an accessible medium. CAST's accessibility guidance highlights voice dictation and real-time speech-to-text as concrete ways AI can improve communication and educational access for learners with disabilities. See `B84`.

### `A3` — executive-function and navigation supports

Examples:

- step-by-step task breakdown;
- visual schedules;
- reminders;
- simplified rubrics or checklists;
- planning prompts;
- guided questioning that helps a learner get started without supplying the answer.

**Default rule:** presumptively allowed across most courses and kept on the protected access rail.

Reason: these supports help learners organize and sustain participation rather than silently complete the work. OECD's 2026 guidance specifically points to AI planners, visual schedules, smart reminders, rubric simplification, and on-demand exemplars as supports for executive-function barriers, especially in ADHD-related contexts. See `B19`.

### `A4` — transformative drafting and shaping supports

Examples:

- paraphrasing or sentence-level rewriting;
- tone smoothing or fluency polishing;
- translation of learner-authored prose into the submission language;
- summarization that decides salience for the learner;
- code completion or symbolic manipulation that materially shortens the target reasoning path.

**Default rule:** **not** presumptively allowed across all courses. Local override is required whenever the support could substitute for the construct being assessed.

Reason: these supports may still be appropriate, but they are no longer merely helping the learner access the task. They are often changing the artifact in ways that can collapse evidence about writing, language production, procedural fluency, or independent judgment.

### `A5` — surrogate production supports

Examples:

- answer generation;
- full paragraph or solution drafting;
- automatic completion of core reasoning steps;
- simulated discussion participation standing in for the learner;
- end-to-end completion of a task whose point is direct human performance.

**Default rule:** not part of the protected access rail.

These uses belong under the ordinary course AI grammar (`NO-AI`, `GUIDED-AI`, `OPEN-AI`) and the proof-of-learning layer, except in rare case-specific accommodation settings where the institution is deliberately changing what counts as performance and documenting that fact through the support process rather than hiding it.

## Override triggers

The archive now names four triggers that move a case out of the presumptive default.

### `T1` — direct construct substitution

Use local override when the support performs the very skill the task is meant to measure.

Examples:

- translation in a target-language reading or writing assessment;
- rewriting support in a course measuring sentence control, style, or argumentation;
- solver assistance in a task meant to show procedural mathematics.

### `T2` — foundational first-attempt evidence

Use local override when the institution needs initial unaided evidence before later supported performance.

Examples:

- phonics or decoding checks;
- first-pass arithmetic or symbolic manipulation;
- early-stage writing fluency checks;
- first safety drill or first live skill demonstration.

The archive still prefers a sequence of **unaided first evidence, then supported growth, then realistic AI-mediated performance** rather than permanent prohibition.

### `T3` — live accountability or safety relevance

Use local override when a task carries direct clinical, legal, industrial, safeguarding, or other safety-relevant accountability and the institution needs to know what the learner can personally do under the relevant conditions.

This does not justify blanket bans across whole programmes. It just means the relevant live or supervised performance surface must stay legible.

### `T4` — interactional authenticity

Use local override when spontaneous oral interaction, negotiation, listening, or interpersonal responsiveness is itself part of the construct.

Examples:

- spoken-language exchange;
- counseling or advising simulations;
- oral defense;
- customer/client interaction in vocational settings.

## Default institutional map

The archive's current recommendation is:

- **pre-clear `A1` to `A3` institution-wide** across most ordinary courses;
- require **program- or subject-level local tables** for `A4` and `A5` uses;
- require **narrow local override notes** for any `A1` to `A3` case that hits `T1` to `T4`.

That is a better equilibrium than making every teacher reinvent the entire rule set. The archive now pairs this support map with a tiny publication profile for inherited subject- and age-sensitive override rows so repeated exceptions become public and reviewable rather than private syllabus drift. See [`subject-and-age-override-table-profile.md`](subject-and-age-override-table-profile.md).

## What courses should publish

Every institution should publish one short support legend alongside the course-level AI grammar:

- `A1-A3` are presumed protected supports unless a listed override trigger applies;
- `A4-A5` are construct-sensitive and follow local course or programme rules;
- instructors may not demand artifact-level disclosure that effectively reveals disability or support status when the support remains on the protected rail;
- when a support is restricted because of construct fit, the institution should name an alternative proof surface rather than simply saying “no.”

## Construct-sensitive domains that almost always need local tables

The archive's current list is short:

- target-language reading, listening, speaking, and writing;
- foundational decoding and early literacy;
- writing courses where wording, syntax, or rhetorical control is itself the target;
- mathematics or technical courses where procedure and symbolic manipulation are the target;
- coding tasks where syntax, tracing, debugging, or architectural explanation is the target;
- live professional, clinical, trade, or safeguarding performances.

These are not reasons to collapse back into blanket anti-AI policy. They are reasons to publish a narrow local override table using the profile in [`subject-and-age-override-table-profile.md`](subject-and-age-override-table-profile.md).

## Procurement and rights floor

Protected access routing still requires governed infrastructure.

Institutions should not approve an AI support tool for routine educational use unless it:

- meets the institution's accessibility floor for web and app surfaces;
- preserves effective communication and reasonable-modification duties;
- fits civil-rights, privacy, and data-minimization obligations;
- offers a human fallback or non-AI route when the tool fails;
- and does not force unnecessary artifact-level confession of support use.

DOJ's 2024 Title II rule and current compliance guidance make accessibility requirements concrete for public web and app surfaces, while WCAG 2.2 remains the best shared accessibility target for current development and procurement. OCR's AI discrimination guidance matters here too: protected support use cannot be governed as though civil-rights law disappeared because a vendor added AI. See `B27`, `B28`, `B85`.

## Current archive bet

The archive's current best guess is that a **small presumptive support map plus narrow override triggers** will outperform both extremes:

- one disclosure regime for everything;
- and fully local teacher-by-teacher improvisation.

This claim is now canon, but not finished. The archive now has a separate starter-row surface for the most reusable construct-sensitive cases.
