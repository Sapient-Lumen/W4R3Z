# Lifelong public AI learning stack

This document closes one of the archive's clearest remaining scope gaps.

The archive has become strong on course design, teacher judgment, disclosure, proof of learning, and
accessibility inside formal institutions. But **education with AI** cannot stop at the classroom or
campus edge. Workers re-entering training, older adults, parents, immigrant learners, unemployed
adults, and people navigating career transitions also need trustworthy routes into AI learning.

The archive's current rule is:

> treat AI education as a **lifelong public learning stack**, not only as a set of course policies.

That means the system should not depend only on whoever happens to be enrolled in a school or
employed by a generous firm. OECD's 2025 adult-learning review warns that participation is uneven
and that much adult training remains short, compliance-driven, and too weak for rapidly changing
labour markets. UNESCO UIL's 2025 adult-educator course shows that AI capability is now needed
across formal, non-formal, and informal adult-learning spaces. California's community college system
is already moving toward role-appropriate AI literacy across an open-access postsecondary sector,
while U.S. workforce guidance now explicitly prioritizes AI literacy, AI-powered tools, and more
navigable public training pathways. Libraries and learning-city systems matter here because they
already function as low-barrier civic learning infrastructure rather than only as content
warehouses. See `B29`, `B30`, `B31`, `B32`, `B33`, `B34`.

## The four public nodes

The archive's current best model is a **four-node public stack**.

## N1. Formal institutions

Schools, colleges, and universities carry the deepest curricular work:

- subject teaching,
- proof of learning,
- teacher development,
- credentials,
- and high-duty-of-care governance.

They remain essential, but they are not enough for the whole population.

## N2. Community colleges, VET, and adult-education providers

These institutions are the archive's preferred **bridge node**.

They are often more open-access than selective universities, closer to workforce transition than
schools, and better positioned to combine:

- foundational AI literacy,
- short applied programmes,
- stackable credentials,
- prior-learning recognition,
- adult-basic-education or ESL support,
- and teacher/trainer professional development.

The archive now treats this bridge function as structurally important rather than incidental.

## N3. Public workforce systems

Workforce agencies should not become the sole pedagogy layer, but they are critical for:

- intake and navigation,
- skill and credential mapping,
- employer linkage,
- benefits-cliff awareness,
- rapid reskilling,
- and connecting adults to funded pathways.

Their job is not to certify deep understanding on their own. Their job is to make pathways
**legible, navigable, and economically relevant**.

## N4. Libraries, learning cities, and community learning spaces

These are the archive's preferred **low-barrier first-contact points**.

They are especially important for people who are:

- not currently enrolled,
- unsure whether they even need formal study,
- older or low-confidence users,
- parents or caregivers,
- immigrants and multilingual learners,
- or residents who trust civic institutions more than vendors or employers.

Libraries and city-level learning networks can host introductions, workshops, device/connectivity
access, multilingual help, information literacy, and referral into deeper programmes.

## What each node should and should not own

The archive now recommends a clear division of labor.

### Libraries and community spaces should own

- first exposure;
- no-cost orientation;
- basic AI and media-literacy workshops;
- access to devices, connectivity, and human help;
- and referral to deeper routes.

They should **not** be expected to carry high-stakes credentialing or the whole burden of workforce
placement.

### Community colleges, VET, and adult-education providers should own

- deeper instruction;
- applied practice;
- stackable certificates or credit-bearing progression;
- trainer development;
- and bridge pathways from basic skill to employability or further study.

They are the most plausible place for the archive's literacy-spine logic to persist beyond K-12.

### Workforce agencies should own

- pathway navigation;
- labor-market and funding alignment;
- referral and intake simplification;
- and support for transitions into jobs or further training.

They should **not** become opaque AI gatekeepers or replace human advisors with chatbot triage.

### Formal schools, colleges, and universities should own

- durable curriculum;
- stronger proof surfaces;
- professional judgment;
- and high-stakes decisions that need educational accountability.

## Minimal public entitlement

The archive's current minimum entitlement is modest but real.

Every resident should be able to reach, at low or no cost:

1. a **trusted first-contact point** for AI and media literacy;
2. a **human navigator** who can help choose between self-study, short courses, credential routes,
   or employment pathways;
3. an **accessible and multilingual access path**, including disability support and translation
   where appropriate;
4. a **short foundational AI-learning offer** that covers safe use, critical evaluation, disclosure,
   rights, and ordinary work/study applications;
5. a **bridge into deeper programmes** such as community-college courses, VET, apprenticeships, or
   workforce-funded training;
6. and a **rights and redress path** when AI systems affect access, eligibility, or opportunity
   unfairly.

This is intentionally lighter than promising every person a full public AI degree. The goal is to
make the first serious steps universal and the next steps navigable.

## Handoff rules

A public stack fails when each node behaves like a silo. The archive therefore recommends four
handoff rules.

### 1. No dead-end literacy

A short AI workshop should point somewhere next. Public orientation without onward routes is
awareness theatre.

### 2. No credential maze

Adults should not have to reverse-engineer which provider, funder, or local office owns the next
step.

### 3. No private-only escalation

Institutions may partner with vendors and employers, but the next serious route should not depend
entirely on private goodwill, private pricing, or proprietary credentials.

### 4. No surveillance-first routing

Navigation, support, and eligibility systems should not quietly turn into behavioral tracking or
opaque scoring systems.

## What this document is trying to prevent

- **school-only thinking** — assuming education with AI is solved once syllabus language exists;
- **employer-only skilling** — leaving workers to learn AI only inside firms, if their firms choose
  to help;
- **dead-end public workshops** — basic exposure with no bridge to deeper study or credentials;
- **chatbot front desks** — replacing human navigation for adults facing complex transitions;
- **older-adult exclusion** — designing only for already-confident digital users;
- **fragmented localism** — many small pilots with no handoff logic across institutions.

## Current archive bet

The archive's current best guess is that a **small public lifelong-learning stack** will outperform
both campus-only policy design and employer-only AI skilling.

That does **not** mean one national platform or one giant bureaucracy. It means a clearer public
division of labor:

- civic first-contact points,
- bridge institutions for deeper learning,
- workforce systems for navigation and transition,
- and formal providers for curriculum, credentials, and stronger proof of learning.

## Current infrastructure refinement

The archive now treats the public stack as needing not just a handoff packet and recognition rules,
but also a **small publication layer** for recurring recognition decisions. When public nodes
repeatedly honour the same outside AI-learning completion, they should publish that rule with dates,
identifiers, and family-specific review windows rather than rely on local memory or static PDF
tables. See
[`standing-list-publication-profile-and-recency-windows.md`](standing-list-publication-profile-and-recency-windows.md).
