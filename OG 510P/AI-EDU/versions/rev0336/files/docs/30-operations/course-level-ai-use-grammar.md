# Course-level AI use and disclosure grammar

## Current overlay

Course-level AI-use language should now start from the construct map. Use
[`../40-assessment/construct-map-and-ai-use-disclosure-matrix.md`](../40-assessment/construct-map-and-ai-use-disclosure-matrix.md)
before writing allow/ban language, then attach the relevant cognitive-effort posture, disclosure
rule, independent proof, accessibility route, and appeal/correction path.

This document closes the archive's first syntax gap.

The archive now has a literacy spine and a subject-embedded exemplar kernel, but institutions still
need a **small common grammar** that teachers, students, and families can actually remember.

The archive's current bet is:

> standardize a little, disclose lightly, and demand human proof where stakes are real.

The point is not to create more paperwork. The point is to reduce confusion, lower false-accusation
anxiety, and make allowed use legible without turning school into a surveillance system. RAND's 2025
and 2026 findings, the OECD AILit consultation, the European Commission's 2026 ethical-use guidance,
and OECD's 2026 VET guidance all point in the same direction: schools need shared language, explicit
rules, and practical disclosure paths. See `B06`, `B17`, `B19`, `B21`, `B24`.

## The minimal grammar

Every assessed task should name four things:

1. **mode** — whether the task is `NO-AI`, `GUIDED-AI`, or `OPEN-AI`;
2. **allowed help** — what AI may and may not do on this task;
3. **disclosure level** — how much the learner must say about AI use;
4. **proof surface** — what human evidence still carries the claim of learning.

That is enough for most courses.

This grammar describes **tasks**, not the institution's whole service architecture. A task may be
`OPEN-AI` while the institution still insists that recurring official student support live in a
governed wrapper or a purpose-built tool, and while teacher-side uses such as grading assistance or
official communication remain subject to separate sign-off rules. Those procurement/deployment rules
now live in
[`../20-governance/general-purpose-vs-purpose-built-deployment-ladder.md`](../20-governance/general-purpose-vs-purpose-built-deployment-ladder.md),
[`../20-governance/student-facing-function-deployment-defaults-and-handoff-triggers.md`](../20-governance/student-facing-function-deployment-defaults-and-handoff-triggers.md),
and
[`../20-governance/teacher-facing-function-delegation-defaults-and-sign-off-triggers.md`](../20-governance/teacher-facing-function-delegation-defaults-and-sign-off-triggers.md).

## 1. Mode tags

### `NO-AI`

The learner performs the core cognitive work without AI.

Use for:

- first attempts on foundational skills,
- timed or live demonstrations,
- transfer checks,
- and any task where the institution needs direct evidence of unaided performance.

`NO-AI` should be used deliberately, not nostalgically.

### `GUIDED-AI`

The learner may use bounded educational or teacher-shaped AI support.

Use for:

- explanation,
- retrieval practice,
- feedback,
- questioning,
- vocabulary support,
- translation/access support where appropriate,
- and error diagnosis.

The key constraint is that AI helps the learner do the work rather than silently doing the work for
them. For recurring official study companions, feedback assistants, or tutoring surfaces, the
archive now makes that narrower interaction claim explicit through a separate learning-first
contract: diagnosis, hinting, bounded scaffold, named answer-release triggers, and
return-to-learning after release. See
[`../20-governance/learning-first-interaction-defaults-and-answer-release-triggers.md`](../20-governance/learning-first-interaction-defaults-and-answer-release-triggers.md).

### `OPEN-AI`

The learner may use general-purpose AI, but must still verify, disclose, and defend the resulting
work in proportion to stakes.

Use for:

- realistic workflow tasks,
- research planning,
- coding and debugging,
- drafting and revision in advanced settings,
- and professional or vocational tasks where AI use is part of the environment being prepared for.

The archive's default learning sequence remains: `NO-AI` -> `GUIDED-AI` -> `OPEN-AI` when the
learner and context are ready. See `B11`, `B21`.

## 2. Allowed-help rule

Do **not** stop at the mode tag. Every major task should also say, in ordinary language, what AI may
do.

Good examples:

- “AI may explain the concept and quiz you, but may not write the paragraph.”
- “AI may suggest debugging steps, but you must explain the architecture and complete the live
  modification yourself.”
- “AI may help generate counterarguments, but final source selection and interpretation must be your
  own.”

The archive prefers plain-language boundaries over giant prohibited-use lists.

## 3. Disclosure levels

The archive's current recommendation is a four-step ladder. The aim is to keep low-stakes work light
while preserving real accountability for major outcomes.

### `D0` — no artifact-level disclosure required

Use for routine practice, especially when AI is either prohibited (`NO-AI`) or obviously built into
a teacher-led activity.

This avoids pointless bureaucracy on low-stakes work.

### `D1` — simple checkbox or one-line note

Use for ordinary assessed work when bounded AI help is allowed.

Examples:

- “I used AI for feedback only.”
- “I used AI to explain terms, not to write my response.”

### `D2` — short structured note

Use for major take-home tasks, projects, and substantial `OPEN-AI` work.

At minimum, the learner should name:

- the tool or tool type,
- what it was used for,
- and one thing they verified, changed, or rejected.

### `D3` — accountable handoff note

Use for gateway, credential-bearing, professional, clinical, or safety-relevant work where AI use is
part of the workflow but cannot carry final responsibility.

The learner should name:

- where AI entered the workflow,
- what remained human-only,
- what was checked,
- and what uncertainty remains.

This level is closest to professional documentation, not school confession.

## 4. Proof surfaces still matter more than disclosure

Disclosure alone does not prove learning.

The archive's current rule is:

- use **lighter disclosure** for ordinary work,
- use **stronger proof surfaces** for major outcomes,
- and do **not** ask students to narrate every keystroke when a short transfer task, oral defense,
  walkthrough, or in-class checkpoint would tell the institution more.

This aligns with UNESCO's current assessment direction toward higher-order thinking, process
evidence, and dialogue, and with calls for clear disclosure protocols plus human oversight in higher
education. See `B25`, `B26`.

## Defaults by task type

### Routine practice

Default: `GUIDED-AI / D0 or D1 / short no-AI checkpoint`

The aim is learning support, not documentation.

### Ordinary graded assignment

Default: `NO-AI` or `GUIDED-AI / D1 / small proof surface`

The aim is to keep expectations clear without inflating labor.

### Major take-home product

Default: `GUIDED-AI` or `OPEN-AI / D2 / bundle element`

The aim is to preserve evidentiary value once polished products become cheap.

### Gateway, capstone, credential, or workplace-simulated task

Default: `NO-AI` or tightly bounded `OPEN-AI / D3 / live defense or transfer`

The aim is accountable performance, not mere artifact production.

## Anti-surveillance constraints

The archive's current grammar is explicitly **not** a detector-first regime.

Institutions should not, by default:

- require full chat-log submission for ordinary coursework,
- require continuous screen recording or keystroke capture,
- demand impossible claims of total non-use outside controlled settings,
- or use AI detectors as routine screening or as sole evidence in integrity cases.

A better posture is clearer task design, lighter disclosure, selective human follow-up, and a
published observability rule that prefers aggregate signals or selected excerpts over routine
full-trace capture. RAND documents student worry about false accusations and inconsistent
teacher-by-teacher rules; UNESCO's recent assessment writing argues that detector-led boundary
maintenance is unreliable and losing ground. See `B06`, `B17`, `B26`.

## Accessibility and accommodation rule

Do not collapse all AI-mediated support into the same bucket.

The archive now makes this explicit through a **two-channel rule**:

- use the ordinary course grammar when AI use changes the pedagogical claim of the work;
- use protected accessibility/accommodation routing when the main function is access, communication,
  or approved support and public disclosure would reveal more than the course needs to know.

This document keeps the public syntax small. The detailed boundary now lives in
[`../20-governance/accommodation-aware-disclosure-and-accessibility.md`](../20-governance/accommodation-aware-disclosure-and-accessibility.md),
[`../20-governance/presumptive-access-support-categories-and-override-triggers.md`](../20-governance/presumptive-access-support-categories-and-override-triggers.md),
and
[`../20-governance/subject-and-age-override-table-profile.md`](../20-governance/subject-and-age-override-table-profile.md).
The practical rule for courses is simple: `A1-A3` supports are presumed protected unless a listed
override trigger or published override row applies, while `A4-A5` uses are construct-sensitive and
need ordinary course or programme rules. Never let disclosure requirements punish the learners who
most need support. See `B19`, `B20`, `B24`, `B27`, `B28`, `B84`, `B86`, `B88`, `B90`, `B91`.

## Minimal institutional packet

A school, district, university, or provider should publish one short shared legend:

- what `NO-AI`, `GUIDED-AI`, and `OPEN-AI` mean;
- what `D0` through `D3` mean;
- one sentence on accessibility/accommodations;
- one link to the institution's inherited override-table rows for construct-sensitive supports;
- one link to the institution's default table for recurring student-facing AI services and their
  handoff triggers;
- one internal planning rule for which tasks sit in `P0-P3` proof-intensity bands and when `PX`
  escalation is allowed, usually by inheriting or lightly editing a published subject-family
  default;
- one link to the institution's observability/retention rule for recurring educational-AI uses;
- and one sentence stating that detectors are not the institution's primary integrity strategy.

Then every major course artifact can simply name the local setting, for example:

- `Mode: GUIDED-AI`
- `Allowed: feedback and questioning only`
- `Disclosure: D1`
- `Proof: revised draft + no-AI exit paragraph`

That is enough to create coherence without smothering teacher judgment. See also
[`../40-assessment/bundle-intensity-bands-and-sampling-ceilings.md`](../40-assessment/bundle-intensity-bands-and-sampling-ceilings.md)
for the planning-side rule that keeps proof surfaces proportional rather than ambient, and
[`../20-governance/minimum-observability-and-retention-without-surveillance.md`](../20-governance/minimum-observability-and-retention-without-surveillance.md)
for the cross-cutting rule that keeps visibility cooler than blanket trace capture.

## Current archive bet

The archive's current best guess is that a small shared course grammar will outperform both
extremes:

- totally local teacher-by-teacher AI rules,
- and heavy provenance or detector regimes that create fear without restoring educational meaning.

That claim is now canon, but still live. The archive now has a starter row set for the most reusable
construct-sensitive cases. The next problem is narrower: which of those starter rows should remain
provisional, and what evidence should promote, split, or retire them as the construct varies by
discipline, age band, or stakes. See
[`../20-governance/reusable-starter-override-rows.md`](../20-governance/reusable-starter-override-rows.md)
and `OQ-0005`.
