# AI exceptional-levy trigger routing

This note answers a narrower question than "should AI be taxed?": **when is a special AI levy morally justified rather than ordinary taxation doing the work?** The archive's answer is trigger-first and controller-first. Ordinary taxes remain the baseline; a special AI levy needs a real exceptional trigger such as place-bound burden, scarce-capacity appropriation, concentrated bottleneck rent, automation-linked windfall, or disproportionate draw on publicly financed inputs. Without one of those triggers, do not manufacture an AI tax merely because the activity is novel or politically salient.[S10][S11][S13][S14][S15][S16][S17][S22][S67]

## Core rule

Route proposed AI-specific taxes through this order:

1. **start with ordinary taxation of profit, labor, consumption, land, energy, and external harms**,  
2. **name the exceptional trigger: local burden, scarce-capacity draw, concentrated rent, automation-linked windfall, or public-input reciprocity**,  
3. **attach the levy to the strongest observable controller-side aggregate rather than to token mysticism or fake model personhood**,  
4. **keep special levies inside ordinary justice tests on incidence, downside, floor protection, and jurisdiction**,  
5. **route proceeds visibly to repair, transition, equal access, or shared public capacity rather than incumbent subsidy loops**,  
6. and **publish review triggers, conversion paths, and exit conditions so temporary exceptionalism does not harden into permanent theater**.[S10][S11][S13][S15][S16][S17][S22][S67][S68]

A proposal therefore fails if it cannot say why AI needs a special levy *beyond* what ordinary profit tax, minimum tax, corrective pricing, competition policy, labor policy, or safety governance already do.

## Default routing by trigger or context

| Trigger or context | Best first tax answer | Why this answer usually wins | Main warning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary profitable AI deployment with no special burden, no visible bottleneck rent, and no automation shock | use ordinary corporate, destination-consumption, labor, and capital-income taxation | novelty alone does not create a new moral tax base | do not invent a universal AI surcharge just because model use is visible or politically charged.[S1][S9][S10][S11] |
| Place-bound burden from data centres, training clusters, or heavy inference infrastructure | facility-side energy, water, congestion, land-value, carbon, and local equalisation charges | the strongest claim often begins where the physical burden lands and can be metered honestly | do not call a locality burden "purely digital" simply because revenue is booked elsewhere.[S5][S6][S7][S15][S28][S70] |
| Scarce frontier-training capacity on stressed grids or strategic accelerator bottlenecks | threshold-based registry fee, scarce-capacity charge, bond, or reserve on large controller-side training events | exceptional use of scarce industrial capacity can justify a specific levy before ordinary profits appear, especially where external burden and concentration are both real | do not pretend a compute threshold is a measure of consciousness, total value, or moral status.[S10][S15][S16][S17] |
| AI-linked margin expansion, payroll compression, or bargaining-power displacement | automation dividend or windfall contribution keyed to controller-side rent or gain indicators, with worker-side transition support | when controllers capture large gains while workers and localities absorb adjustment costs, part of that upside should be socialised | do not tax gross activity as a fake windfall when no rent or margin expansion is actually present.[S11][S13][S14][S22][S67] |
| Disproportionate draw on publicly financed research, data, compute, or other shared inputs | controller-side public-input reciprocity contribution using auditable aggregates such as revenue tier, training/deployment scale, or beneficiary class rather than per-token billing | if value extraction from publicly built inputs is real, the contribution should be aggregated at a workable controller-side level rather than relying on impossible micro-accounting | do not confuse reciprocity with state ownership of every downstream output or with universal taxes on all open knowledge use.[S10][S11][S22] |
| Small-scale, open, nonprofit, educational, or public-interest AI use without concentrated rent or system burden | ordinary baseline taxation only, with special levies usually absent or lighter | the archive taxes bottlenecks, harms, and extraordinary gains before it taxes socially useful experimentation or shared capability access | do not tax open and public-interest uses like oligopoly utilities merely because the same technical substrate is involved.[S10][S11][S22] |
| Domain with unresolved catastrophic or non-compensable risk | governance first: approval, caps, tiering, bonding, or refusal, with taxes only as sidecars | where ex post repair is morally too late, the main question is permission and control, not revenue | do not sell a frontier levy as proof that unsafe scaling has been made acceptable.[S15][S17][S53][S54] |

## Routing rules

### 1. Ordinary taxes remain the baseline

Ordinary taxes on profit, wages, payroll, consumption, property, energy, and pollution already apply. The moral work of a special AI levy is narrower: it must answer for a specific exceptional burden or rent that ordinary taxation leaves materially undertaxed.[S1][S10][S11][S15]

### 2. Name the exceptional trigger explicitly

A special levy is strongest when it is attached to one of five triggers the archive already treats as morally serious: **place-bound external burden**, **scarce-capacity appropriation**, **concentrated bottleneck rent**, **automation-linked windfall**, or **public-input reciprocity**.[S10][S11][S13][S15][S22][S67]

If none of these can be named, the proposal is probably aesthetic AI exceptionalism rather than morally grounded taxation.

### 3. Use auditable controller-side aggregates, not impossible micro-accounting

Prefer bases that can be measured honestly: facility electricity, accelerator-hours, deployment tier, revenue bands, beneficiary class, or other controller-side aggregates. Usually reject per-token, per-parameter, or benchmark-score taxation as moral proxies, and reject public-data reciprocity schemes that require impossible attribution of each output back to atomized training fragments.[S10][S15][S16][S17]

### 4. Separate public-input reciprocity from ownership fantasies

A public-input contribution is not a declaration that the state owns every model output built partly on public knowledge, nor that all training data can be traced and billed like a toll road. The cleaner moral claim is reciprocity: where controllers derive disproportionate gain from publicly financed research or shared knowledge infrastructure, some controller-side contribution to public capacity or commons maintenance may be justified. That contribution should still be bounded, reviewable, and compatible with open knowledge circulation.[S10][S11][S22]

### 5. Pair AI exceptional levies with human-side protection and local repair

Even a well-triggered AI levy fails if the burden leaks downward while the benefits vanish upward. Automation-dividend finance should reach displaced workers, care-heavy households, and weak localities; facility-side charges should help repair local infrastructure and service strain; scarce-capacity levies should not quietly recycle into incumbent subsidy loops.[S15][S22][S67][S68][S70]

### 6. Keep open and public-interest uses from being taxed like oligopolies

The archive taxes bottlenecks and concentrated rents before it taxes ordinary experimentation. Special levies therefore need thresholds, exemptions, or lighter treatment that distinguish concentrated commercial control from genuinely small, open, nonprofit, or public-serving use without opening a giant avoidance hole.[S10][S11][S22]

### 7. Every exceptional levy needs a review and conversion path

Because AI-specific triggers are noisy and fast-moving, a levy should publish the evidence that justifies it, the thresholds that activate it, the indicators that may convert it into an ordinary charge or retire it, and the cadence for review. Otherwise temporary exceptionalism drifts into permanent category error.[S15][S16][S17][S59][S60][S61][S62][S63]

## Five anti-patterns

1. **AI branding without trigger** — creating a bespoke AI tax because AI is salient, not because a distinct burden or rent has been shown.  
2. **Gross-throughput windfall theater** — taxing visible activity as if it were rent even when margins are thin or downside is still genuine.  
3. **Token fetishism** — pretending token counts, parameter counts, or benchmark scores reveal the real moral base.  
4. **Commons micro-toll fantasy** — proposing impossible per-fragment billing for public data or knowledge use when workable controller-side reciprocity contributions would be more honest.  
5. **Exceptionalism without exit** — allowing an emergency or experimental AI levy to persist without trigger review, conversion, or sunset.[S10][S15][S16][S17][S22][S59][S60][S61]

## Order of preference

When a government wants to single out AI for a special tax or contribution, the archive usually prefers the design that is:

1. clearest about the exceptional trigger rather than the sector label,
2. easiest to meter honestly at the controller or facility side,
3. strongest on final-incidence protection and weakest on downward leakage,
4. most explicit about proceeds routing toward repair, transition, or public capacity,
5. least hostile to open, nonprofit, and public-interest capability development,
6. and easiest to review, convert, or retire once the trigger weakens or ordinary taxation catches up.

This is why controller-side facility charges, scarce-capacity levies, windfall contributions tied to real gain, and bounded public-input reciprocity usually beat universal token taxes, vague machine surcharges, or theatrical taxes on AI as such.
