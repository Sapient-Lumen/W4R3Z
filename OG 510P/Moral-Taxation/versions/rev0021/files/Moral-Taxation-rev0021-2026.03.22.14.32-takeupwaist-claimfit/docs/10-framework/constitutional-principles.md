# Constitutional principles

The archive's current constitution is built from twenty-three principles.

## 1. Subsistence first

No just tax regime should force people below a civic minimum simply because they are present, poor, or unlucky.

## 2. Tax subjects are not the same as tax bases

A rights-bearing person, a firm, a parcel of land, a carbon ton, and a GPU-hour are different kinds of things. Moral confusion starts when they are treated as interchangeable.

## 3. Rents before effort

The default order is to tax unearned or weakly earned advantage before ordinary work: land rents, natural-resource rents, monopoly rents, inherited fortune, pollution, congestion, and cross-border profit shifting before routine wages.

## 4. Broad base, visible rebate

Where broad-base taxation is desirable, regressivity should usually be corrected through transfers, rebates, or negative taxes rather than opaque exemption jungles.[S3][S4]

## 5. Proceeds are part of the moral design

A tax is not justified only by its base and rate. Its proceeds should, where appropriate, protect the floor, repair harms, socialise common rents, or build shared civic capacity rather than vanishing into opaque sinkholes or captured subsidy loops.[S1][S4][S7]

## 6. Control and benefit anchor liability

For current AI systems, liability should track who controls deployment, who captures windfalls, and who can internalize harms.

## 7. Personhood changes category

If an artificial system ever becomes a rights-bearing agent, the problem stops being how to tax a tool and becomes how to tax a person without domination or ownership.

## 8. Scale and jurisdiction matter

Some taxes are municipal by nature, some national, some irreducibly international. Land and congestion are local-heavy; multinational profit shifting, cross-border consumption, and compute bottlenecks require transnational coordination. Taxing rights should usually track the strongest anchor for the base: situs for immovable assets, locality for place-bound harms, destination or market access for final consumption and some borderless profit, and co-ordinated club rules where no single jurisdiction can hold the base alone.[S3][S9]

## 9. Administration, measurement, and timing are moral substance

Opacity, arbitrariness, fake precision, stale valuation, impossible compliance, and badly matched payment timing are not side issues. A tax that cannot be understood, appealed, measured honestly, updated as conditions change, verified, or paid without unjust liquidity pressure is morally defective even when its goals are attractive.[S16][S23][S25][S27]

## 10. Prospectivity and reliance matter

A tax should normally operate prospectively, with fair notice and visible transition rules. Narrow retroactivity can sometimes be justified to close abuse windows, match already-announced cutoffs, or answer genuine emergencies, but indefinite grandfathering of incumbent advantage is also a moral failure. The archive therefore protects legitimate reliance without letting certainty harden into permanent rentier privilege.[S23][S27][S36][S37][S38]

## 11. Assessment units should not entrench domination

For human taxation, the default direct subject should usually be the individual person rather than a compulsory joint household. Dependency, caregiving, and child support should still matter, but the archive prefers to recognize them through transfers, services, credits, or carefully designed means tests rather than by presuming full income pooling or spousal ownership. Anti-avoidance aggregation for family wealth is compatible with this; collapsing persons into a single taxable household by default is not.[S31][S32][S33]

## 12. Net fiscal stacks matter

No tax should be judged in isolation from tax expenditures, abatements, accelerated depreciation, preferential public-input pricing, or direct subsidies that target the same base. Hidden negative taxes can nullify or reverse the moral meaning of headline rates. Floor-protecting credits and rebates are different: they are part of the protected baseline, not loopholes.[S29][S30][S31]

## 13. Crisis taxes should sunset

Emergency taxation may be justified in war, collapse, or climate shock, but it should be time-bounded, trigger-bound, and publicly reviewable.

## 14. Enforcement should be proportionate and privacy-bounded

A morally serious tax state should collect what is due through the least intrusive path that can still preserve compliance and prevent asset flight. Collection should be staged, hardship-aware, reviewable, and bounded by purpose. Predictive tools may assist triage, but unconstrained data access, black-box enforcement, or coercive recovery that strips the protected floor is a constitutional defect, not a mere administrative blemish.[S16][S17][S27][S39][S40][S41]

## 15. Real control outruns paper fragmentation

A morally serious tax system should follow beneficial ownership, related-party control, and real economic coordination rather than letting shells, trusts, nominee structures, and AI-stack subsidiaries multiply one taxpayer into many paper fragments. Aggregation should be used narrowly to defeat avoidance, not so broadly that it erases genuinely independent actors or collapses ordinary persons back into compulsory household taxation.[S9][S20][S31][S34][S35]

## 16. Direct tax subjection needs standing, remedy, and representation

A morally serious tax system should hesitate before making any being or entity a **direct civic taxpayer** unless that subject can be informed, heard, represented, and given access to review. Children and some incapacitated adults remain direct subjects because they are rights-bearing persons with guardianship or fiduciary representation inside a public legal order. Current LLMs do not yet satisfy that bundle, so direct machine taxation remains a category mistake; liability should continue to run through controllers, owners, deployers, or other accountable beneficiaries instead.[S19][S20][S42][S43][S44]

## 17. Formal neutrality is not enough

A morally serious tax system should reject both direct discrimination and apparently neutral rules that work as status proxies. Taxes and transfers should be checked for disparate effects across protected grounds such as sex, disability, nationality, ethnicity, social origin, residence, and family status, with special caution where automation, documentation burdens, or geography silently exclude already marginalised people. Differential treatment is more defensible when it tracks need, caregiving, repair, or harm; it is less defensible when it simply encodes hierarchy or administrative convenience. If future artificial persons ever become rights-bearing subjects, they should not face a machine-species surcharge merely for substrate.[S32][S33][S44][S45][S46]

## 18. Failure ordering and limited liability must not dump losses downward

A morally serious tax system should ask what happens when a taxpayer fails. Ordinary tax claims should not casually jump ahead of unpaid wages, estate-preserving administration, or serious public-cleanup and victim-repair claims merely because the state is also a claimant. Withheld or trust taxes can justify narrower priority because the debtor was holding others' money, but high-risk sectors should usually be handled ex ante through bonds, reserves, insurance, and anti-undercapitalization rules rather than by letting limited liability dump cleanup and wage losses on the public.[S47][S48][S49]

## 19. Gains and losses should not live in different moral universes

A morally serious tax system should hesitate before taxing upside immediately while recognizing downside only weakly, only after long delay, or only for actors rich enough to wait. Real losses, negative years, and failed but genuine investments should usually receive carry-forwards with meaningful value preservation where appropriate, carry-backs or refundability where liquidity and neutrality matter, and similar treatment for loss-making firms when the policy is meant to subsidize risky socially valuable activity. Anti-abuse rules should target artificial losses, duplicate deductions, and loss trafficking rather than denying recognition to genuine downside.[S50][S51][S52]

## 20. Catastrophic or irreversible risk is not just another tax base

A morally serious tax system should not assume that every danger can be converted into a legitimate price. Where harms are catastrophic, irreversible, or not realistically remediable after the fact, taxation should usually play only a secondary role to ex ante governance: licensing, safety cases, caps, bonding, decommissioning powers, or disapproval. Revenue needs do not justify turning a levy into a moral permit for risks that should first be constrained or refused.[S15][S17][S53][S54]

## 21. Nominal values are not moral values

A morally serious tax system should hesitate before taxing inflation, bracket drift, or nominal appreciation as if they were real gains or real ability-to-pay. Where inflation would otherwise do hidden distributive work, the archive prefers transparent indexation, honest approximation, or an openly defended real policy choice over stealth tightening through frozen nominal thresholds, erosion of loss relief, or decaying bond amounts.[S50][S55][S56][S57][S58]

## 22. Review architecture is part of justice

A morally serious tax system should not act as though enactment settles the matter forever. Where uncertainty, novelty, emergency, concentrated power, or rapid technical change is material, the rule should name what evidence would count as success or failure, who will review it, what cadence and triggers force reconsideration, and how the instrument can tighten, loosen, convert, or expire. Otherwise policy inertia and capture silently replace justification.[S15][S54][S59][S60][S61][S62][S63]

## 23. Claim friction and take-up are part of justice

A morally serious tax system should not design floor protection, ordinary rebates, routine refunds, or harm-repair payments as if paper eligibility were enough. Where the state already has enough information to know that support or return-of-overcollection is due, it should prefer automatic payment, prefilled claiming, rolling confirmation, or similarly low-friction delivery with human and offline fallback. High-friction application processes are more tolerable for exceptional privileges than for protected minima. A tax state that automates collection but leaves relief trapped behind forms, digital hurdles, or low take-up has made a constitutional error, not a minor service mistake.[S64][S65][S66]

## Archive judgment

These principles imply a simple constitutional answer: **ideal taxation is neither labor-first nor machine-first; it is rent-first, harm-first, floor-protecting, assessment-unit-aware, anti-fragmentation-aware, incidence-aware, proceeds-conscious, timing-aware, downside-symmetry-aware, precaution-aware, real-value-aware, measurement-honest, status-proxy-aware, anti-subordination-aware, prospective-by-default, net-stack-aware, jurisdiction-fit, enforcement-aware, privacy-bounded, standing-aware, failure-ordering-aware, limited-liability-bounded, and personhood-sensitive**.
