# 106 — Corporate rent taxation, monopoly, and global minimums

## One-line thesis

Corporate taxation should focus less on punishing ordinary investment and more on capturing residual profit, monopoly rent, and profit shifting that exploit global mismatch.

## Why this matters

In open economies, badly designed corporate taxes can discourage real investment or simply leak across borders. Yet leaving large digital and multinational firms lightly taxed invites a race to the bottom and rewards legal arbitrage over productive value creation.[S9]

## Design rule

Target multinational and platform profit where rents and market power are strongest, and coordinate internationally where unilateralism is too brittle.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Residual profit focus

Not all corporate return is alike. Normal return tied to real investment is different from excess return tied to market power, IP enclosure, or network lock-in.

### 2. Minimum-tax floors

A global or club-based minimum tax reduces pressure for destructive undercutting.[S9]

### 3. Destination and user-base sensitivity

Digital and AI-heavy firms often earn value from markets and users far from nominal booking jurisdictions.

### 4. Pair tax with competition policy

Tax alone cannot cure bottleneck domination; competition, interoperability, and transparency still matter.


## Guardrails

- Do not let tax design substitute for antitrust where monopoly is the main problem.
- Keep small firms and genuinely local cooperatives out of compliance overkill.
- Avoid using the corporate code as a patchwork subsidy dispenser.


## Failure modes

- **Race to the bottom**: governments compete to host booked profits, not real activity.
- **Paper-profit sovereignty**: intellectual property shells replace real tax presence.
- **Two-track capitalism**: ordinary firms pay while global platforms arbitrage rules.


## Practical tests

1. Would the tax still bite if profits are shifted to low-tax affiliates?
2. Is residual or monopoly profit being targeted more than routine return?
3. Is the design coordinated enough to survive digital mobility?
