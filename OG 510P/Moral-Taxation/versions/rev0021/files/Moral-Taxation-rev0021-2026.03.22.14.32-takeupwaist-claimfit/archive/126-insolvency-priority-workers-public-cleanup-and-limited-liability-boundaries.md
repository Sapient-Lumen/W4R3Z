# 126 — Insolvency priority, worker claims, public cleanup, and limited-liability boundaries

## One-line thesis

Tax justice does not end when liability is assessed. When a taxpayer fails, ordinary tax claims should not casually outrank unpaid wages, serious victim or cleanup claims, or estate-preserving administration merely because the state is also a claimant.

## Why this matters

A tax that looks just on the way in can become unjust on the way out. If the archive never asks what happens at insolvency, limited-liability firms can privatise upside, leave workers or localities holding the damage, and let the tax state recover first from a too-small estate that should have gone to more urgent claims. This matters especially for extractive sites, hazardous industry, and frontier AI facilities with concentrated downside and thinly capitalised shell structures.

## Design rule

Keep the failure-order predictable, narrow, and ex ante where possible:

1. ordinary assessed tax debts should usually not enjoy broad superpriority over unpaid wages, estate administration, or serious public-cleanup and victim-repair claims,
2. withheld or trust-like taxes can justify narrower priority because the debtor was holding other people's money rather than its own,
3. high-risk activities should rely more on ex ante bonds, reserves, insurance, escrow, or controller-side capital requirements than on ex post insolvency grabs,
4. repeated undercapitalisation, asset stripping, or affiliate dumping should reopen the controller question instead of being hidden behind limited-liability formalism.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Ordinary tax claims

Treat routine income-tax, corporate-tax, and similar assessed debts as claims that must live inside a predictable insolvency ranking rather than as automatic reasons for the state to jump every other claimant.

### 2. Withheld or trust taxes

Where an employer withheld payroll taxes or a merchant collected VAT/GST from customers, the moral case for priority is stronger because the debtor was acting partly as a collector of money already sourced from others.[S47]

### 3. Worker and subsistence claims

Unpaid wages, accrued leave, pension contributions, and similar livelihood claims deserve stronger protection than ordinary residual tax collection because they sit closer to the archive's protected floor than the treasury's general queue position.[S48][S49]

### 4. Cleanup and abandonment costs

Where a facility can leave toxic waste, water stress, unsafe decommissioning, or major public recovery costs, do not rely mainly on ex post tax priority. Use bonds, insurance, reserve accounts, reclamation obligations, or controller guarantees before the failure arrives.

### 5. Limited-liability boundaries

Limited liability is a useful production tool, not a moral permission slip to socialise extreme downside. Where controllers deliberately undercapitalise a high-risk vehicle, strip assets, or split ownership and risk across affiliates, the archive favors reserve rules, bonding, anti-fragmentation grouping, or controller liability over ritual respect for the shell.

## Failure modes

- **Treasury leapfrogging**: ordinary tax debts outrank unpaid wages or serious public-repair claims by default.
- **Public-loss dumping**: cleanup and decommissioning costs are left to localities because no bond or reserve existed.
- **Thin-capitalization theater**: profits are extracted upstream while the operating shell is left too weak to pay either taxes or harms.
- **Trust-tax confusion**: withheld taxes are treated as identical to all other tax claims, obscuring the stronger case for targeted priority in those narrower cases.

## Practical tests

1. If this taxpayer failed tomorrow, who would rank ahead of whom?
2. Is the proposal relying on broad tax priority where a bond, reserve, insurance requirement, or controller guarantee should have been imposed earlier?
3. Are unpaid workers, tort victims, or public-cleanup claimants being pushed behind ordinary fiscal collection?
4. Is limited liability being respected after genuine entrepreneurial risk, or after deliberate undercapitalisation and loss dumping?

## Archive judgment

The archive's default is therefore:

- **narrow tax priority where the debtor was holding money for others,**
- **stronger protection for wages, administration, and serious repair / cleanup claims,**
- **ex ante bonding or reserve design for high-risk sectors,**
- **and a willingness to revisit controller liability when shells are used to privatise upside and socialize downside.**

That is especially important for AI facilities and model companies whose failures could leave unpaid workers, abandoned contracts, electricity arrears, site cleanup, safety incidents, or public rescue costs behind a thin operating shell.
