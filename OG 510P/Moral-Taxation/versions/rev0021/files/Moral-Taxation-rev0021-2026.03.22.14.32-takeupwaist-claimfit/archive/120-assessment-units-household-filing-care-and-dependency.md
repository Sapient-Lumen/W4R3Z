# 120 — Assessment units, household filing, care, and dependency

## One-line thesis

For human taxation, the archive defaults to **individual liability for earnings and personhood claims**, with dependent-aware transfers and care recognition layered on top; compulsory jointness is a tool of last resort because it can hide intra-household inequality, create second-earner penalties, and intensify dependency.

## Why this matters

A tax system can look progressive at the household level while still being unjust inside the household. When liability, allowances, or means tests presume that partners fully pool income and power, the system may understate domination, mismeasure ability to pay, and discourage the person whose earnings are treated as "secondary" from taking paid work or increasing hours. OECD work on tax policy and gender treats these implicit biases as a real design problem and identifies second-earner taxation, credits, allowances, and social contributions as live policy priorities. IMF research likewise treats household taxation as an important part of labor-tax design and gender equality.[S32][S33]

## Archive rule

Use the **individual person** as the default direct tax subject for human earnings, legal liability, and civic standing. Then layer on:

- **dependent recognition** for children and others who genuinely rely on support,
- **care recognition** through refundable credits, transfers, services, social insurance, or caregiver allowances,
- and **anti-avoidance aggregation** where wealthy households or family vehicles split assets or income across nominal persons only to escape a justified burden.

The archive therefore separates three things that are often blurred together:

1. the **person** who bears rights and direct liability,
2. the **household or care network** that matters for support and need,
3. and the **beneficial ownership group** that may matter for anti-fragmentation rules.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Individualize earnings taxation by default

Wages, salaries, self-employment income, and other ordinary earnings should usually be taxed on an individual basis rather than through mandatory joint household filing. This better tracks personhood, avoids treating one adult as a derivative of another, and reduces second-earner penalties.[S32][S33]

### 2. Recognize dependents without presuming spousal ownership

Children, disability, elder care, and other dependency facts should matter. But the archive prefers to recognize them through direct payments, refundable credits, public provision, or care-side benefits rather than by making one spouse's tax position the property of the other.

### 3. Keep means tests from becoming domination machines

Targeted benefits sometimes need household information. When they do, the archive prefers:

- short and legible definitions of household membership,
- direct payment to the entitled person where possible,
- appeal paths for separation, abuse, estrangement, or unstable housing,
- and designs that do not punish informal care or force surveillance-heavy proof.

### 4. Use anti-splitting rules for wealth without collapsing persons into households

For wealth, inheritance, trusts, family offices, and closely held firms, anti-avoidance rules may need aggregation across related parties or beneficial owners. That does **not** require treating all adults in a household as a single moral person. The archive permits aggregation to stop gaming while still keeping direct rights, floors, and ordinary earnings liability person-based.

### 5. Prefer care support to marriage engineering

The archive does not treat marriage bonuses or marriage penalties as a primary justice instrument. Where care burdens are real, support should attach to the care relation, the dependent load, or the constrained earner rather than to formal marital status alone.

### 6. Apply the same caution to AI-adjacent household claims

A household that uses AI tools remains a household of persons, not a merged human-machine tax subject. The archive therefore resists designs that treat owned assistants, bots, or domestic agents as independent household taxpayers before rights-bearing personhood is established.

## Guardrails

- No mandatory joint filing as a default for ordinary human earnings without a strong and explicit justification.
- No household means test that assumes full income pooling where abuse, estrangement, or unequal control are common and unaddressed.
- No care recognition that works only for married couples while excluding equivalent care burdens outside that form.
- No anti-avoidance aggregation rule so broad that it erases individual standing.

## Failure modes

- **second-earner trap**: the next dollar earned by the lower earner faces a much higher effective burden because the couple is treated as one unit
- **household shadowing**: an adult disappears from fiscal view because another adult is presumed to speak and pay for them
- **care blindness**: the system sees earnings but not dependency load or unpaid care time
- **anti-avoidance overreach**: related-party rules become an excuse to collapse genuine persons into a single taxable blob

## Practical tests

1. Is the direct income-tax liability attached to a person, or is it forced through a joint unit by default?
2. Are children, disability, and unpaid care recognized through transfers, services, or credits without creating second-earner traps?
3. If a means test uses household income, how does the design handle abuse, separation, unstable housing, or non-pooling?
4. Are anti-fragmentation rules aimed at beneficial ownership and avoidance rather than at erasing ordinary personal standing?
5. Would the same rule still look just if one partner had much less bargaining power, much less cash access, or intermittent paid work?
