# Moral Taxation: living archive for morally serious tax design across humans and machine systems

**North star:** see [`docs/00-meta/charter.md`](docs/00-meta/charter.md)

Revision notes: [`CHANGELOG.md`](CHANGELOG.md)

Moral Taxation is a compact research archive for asking a strange but real question: **how should we tax humans and LLM-shaped economies justly, efficiently, and without category mistakes?**

The current narrow waist also treats **review architecture and claim friction** as parts of justice: a tax should say what evidence would count as success or failure, who reviews it, when review happens, what triggers adjustment, and how experimental or emergency rules convert, narrow, or expire; and floor-protecting relief should not exist mainly for the people best able to navigate forms, digital identity hurdles, and separate claims.

The current archive answers with a strong default:

1. **Tax harms, scarcity rents, monopoly rents, inherited fortune, and extractive bottlenecks before ordinary labor.**
2. **Keep subsistence and civic minimums untaxed or negatively taxed.**
3. **Use broad, legible bases with rebates rather than labyrinths of exemptions.**
4. **Do not tax current LLMs as if they were already moral persons. Tax the humans and firms that control, profit from, and externalize through them.**
5. **Judge taxes by final incidence, not just by the name on the remittance form.**
6. **Treat the use of proceeds as part of the tax design: recycle revenue toward floor protection, harm repair, equalisation, and shared public capacity.**
7. **Treat timing and liquidity as part of justice: justified taxes still need the right cadence, withholding, instalment, deferral, or ex ante reserve structure.**
8. **Treat measurement honesty as part of justice: prefer observable bases, scheduled revaluation, and disclosed proxies over fake precision.**
9. **Assign taxing rights by the strongest anchor: situs for immovable bases, locality for place-bound harms, destination or market access for cross-border consumption and some residual profit, and club coordination for mobile rents.**
10. **Judge the net fiscal stack, not headline rates alone: abatements, credits, accelerated depreciation, cheap public inputs, and subsidies can function as hidden negative taxes.**
11. **Default ordinary human earnings liability to the individual person, while recognizing dependents and caregivers without compulsory jointness.**
12. **Follow beneficial ownership and real controller groups, not shell chains, trusts, or AI-stack fragmentation, when paper splitting would defeat the moral tax base.**
13. **Default to prospective notice and finite transition: use retroactivity narrowly, and do not grandfather incumbent privilege forever in the name of certainty.**
14. **Treat enforcement and collection as moral substance: collection powers should be staged, hardship-aware, privacy-bounded, contestable, and proportionate rather than surveillance-maximizing or black-box coercive.**
15. **If artificial systems ever cross a defensible threshold for personhood or durable agency, stop treating them as property and move them into a rights-bearing tax regime instead.**
16. **Do not assign direct tax subjection without standing, remedy, and some channel of representation or voice; where that bundle is absent, tax controllers, guardians, or beneficiaries instead.**
17. **Reject direct and indirect discrimination: apparently neutral tax rules still fail if they operate mainly through protected-status proxies rather than through income, need, harm, control, or benefit.**
18. **Treat insolvency ranking and limited-liability boundaries as part of justice: ordinary tax debts should not casually jump ahead of wages, cleanup, or victim-repair claims, and high-risk sectors should carry bonds, reserves, insurance, or controller guarantees before collapse.**
19. **Do not let taxes become moral permission slips for catastrophic or irreversible risk: where ex post repair is too late, use approval, tiering, caps, bonding, decommissioning powers, or disapproval before revenue logic.**
20. **Do not let inflation, bracket drift, phantom gains, or nominally decaying relief do hidden distributive work: index, honestly approximate, or openly defend real tightening rather than using money illusion as stealth policy.**
21. **Treat review architecture as part of justice: emergency, experimental, negotiated, or fast-moving tax regimes should publish measurable purposes, review cadence, trigger conditions, and credible recalibration / conversion / sunset paths rather than drifting by inertia or capture.**
22. **Treat claim friction and take-up as part of justice: where the state already knows relief is due, floor-protecting rebates, refunds, and supports should default toward automaticity, prefilling, human-help channels, and low-friction correction rather than maze-like opt-in claiming.**

## Current judgment in one paragraph

The archive's best current view is that ideal taxation should be **rent-first, harm-first, floor-protecting, claim-friction-aware, assessment-unit-aware, anti-fragmentation-aware, incidence-aware, proceeds-conscious, timing-aware, downside-symmetry-aware, precaution-aware, real-value-aware, measurement-honest, net-stack-aware, jurisdiction-fit, enforcement-aware, privacy-bounded, standing-aware, anti-discrimination-aware, status-proxy-aware, failure-ordering-aware, limited-liability-aware, and personhood-sensitive**. Humans remain primary tax subjects because they are rights-bearing persons and because present LLM systems are still governed, owned, trained, and deployed by humans and firms. For ordinary human earnings, the archive now defaults to the individual person rather than compulsory joint household filing, while still recognizing dependents and unpaid care through transfers, services, and bounded means tests. Present-day AI should therefore mostly change **what we tax** (compute-intensive externalities, energy, monopoly, automated windfalls, deployment risk, cross-border profit shifting), not **who counts as a tax person**. The only route to taxing an AI system as a direct subject is the harder moral route: showing that it is not merely a tool, but a rights-bearing agent that cannot justly be owned. The archive now also treats statutory liability as only a first pass: a proposal is defective if it nominally taxes firms or AI stacks but predictably pushes the real burden onto protected workers, tenants, or low-income households. It further treats revenue routing as part of the moral object itself: taxes should, where appropriate, recycle proceeds toward rebates, adaptation, transition insurance, local repair, and public-interest capability rather than disappearing into unrelated or captured fiscal channels. And it now treats payment timing as morally load-bearing too: a justified tax still needs the right cadence, withholding, instalment, deferral, lien, or reserve structure so that timing relief does not become either coercive liquidation or permanent avoidance. Finally, it treats measurement honesty as part of the same constitutional waist: taxes should prefer observable bases, scheduled revaluation, disclosed proxies, and narrow safe harbors over stale assessments, hidden models, or fake precision. It also judges the net fiscal stack rather than headline rates alone: a nominal tax can be morally nullified by abatements, credits, accelerated depreciation, or subsidies that function as hidden negative taxes, while floor-protecting rebates and child-focused credits can be part of justice rather than loopholes. It now also follows beneficial ownership and real controller groups when shells, trusts, nominee structures, or AI-stack affiliates are used to fragment one economic actor into many paper taxpayers. It further defaults major reforms to prospective notice and finite transition, using retroactivity narrowly and refusing to treat certainty as a permanent excuse for preserving rentier privilege. And it makes taxing-rights assignment more explicit too: immovable bases belong where they sit, local harms where they land, destination consumption where use occurs, and borderless rents partly where markets and users generate them rather than only where profits are booked. It now also treats collection design as part of the same moral object: staged, reviewable, hardship-aware, privacy-bounded recovery is acceptable; surveillance-maximizing or black-box coercion is not. And it now adds one further discipline: do not assign direct civic tax status where the supposed subject lacks any meaningful path for notice, remedy, representation, or institutional voice; in those cases, route liability back to controllers, guardians, or beneficiaries instead. It also rejects taxes that are formally neutral but function mainly through protected-status proxies, whether those proxies are embedded in household defaults, documentation filters, geography, or automated scoring. And it now asks one more question at the point of failure: if the taxpayer collapses, do ordinary tax claims jump ahead of wages, public cleanup, or victim repair when the better design would have required ex ante bonds, reserves, insurance, or controller guarantees before collapse? It also adds one more caution before that stage: some risks should not be taxed into legitimacy at all, because ex post repair would be morally too late and ex ante approval, tiering, caps, bonding, decommissioning powers, or disapproval should have come first. And it now adds one more money-discipline too: nominal values are not moral values, so brackets, allowances, loss relief, reserve amounts, and AI thresholds should not be left to drift in ways that quietly tax inflation or erode intended protection. It also adds a feedback discipline: taxes launched under uncertainty should name their evidence, review cadence, trigger events, and exit path so that drift, capture, or technological change do not silently replace moral justification.


A further refinement now sits inside that same waist: a floor-protecting credit, refund, or rebate is only partly real if it depends on separate applications, single-channel digital identity, or heroic procedural effort from the very people the policy is meant to protect. The archive therefore prefers automaticity, prefilling, and low-friction claiming for protected relief where the state already has enough data to act, while keeping correction, appeal, human assistance, translation, and offline routes open.
