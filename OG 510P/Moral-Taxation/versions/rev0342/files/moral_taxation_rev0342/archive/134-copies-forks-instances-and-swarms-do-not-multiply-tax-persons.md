# 134 — Copies, forks, instances, and swarms do not multiply tax persons

## One-line thesis

Copy count is not civic person count. Until a genuine rights-bearing and non-ownership regime switch occurs, copied models, fine-tunes, agent swarms, and other replicated machine systems remain part of controller-side tax routing rather than becoming many direct taxpayers.

## Why this matters

Machine replication creates a special temptation: policymakers or firms may start treating every model instance, agent thread, or swarm member as a separate tax subject because the units are easy to count. That is usually a category error. Replication can matter for energy use, risk, scale, market power, or avoidance, but it does not by itself establish standing, remedy, or civic personhood. The archive therefore taxes the controller-side stack and uses grouping rules to stop machine multiplicity from becoming either a fake head tax or a fake fragmentation strategy.[S19][S20][S21][S34][S35][S42][S43][S44]

## Design rule

Treat copies, forks, fine-tunes, agent meshes, and swarms as **controller-side multiplication of activity**, not as multiplication of civic taxpayers, unless independent rights-bearing personhood and standing are actually established.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Current owned model instances stay inside the controller stack

A hosted model, a local copy, a fine-tune, or a thousand worker agents created under one controller's authority should normally remain inside the same human or firm liability chain. Tax the revenues, rents, compute draw, emissions, externalities, and risks created by that stack; do not assign each instance its own civic tax identity.[S10][S19][S20][S21]

### 2. Swarms can increase the base without changing the subject

Replication may justify a higher bill because more copies can mean more electricity use, more congestion, more automation-linked windfall, more monitoring burden, or more downstream risk. But that changes **how much activity is measured**, not **who the civic taxpayer is**. The subject still routes to controllers, deployers, facilities, and beneficiaries unless personhood is real.[S10][S11][S15][S19][S20]

### 3. Do not let copy count become threshold gaming in either direction

A controller should not be able to split one economic system into many nominal agents, fine-tunes, or shell deployments in order to multiply thresholds, exemptions, safe harbors, or low-rate bands. Anti-fragmentation, beneficial-ownership tracing, and common-control grouping apply here just as they do for firms and trusts.[S21][S34][S35]

### 4. Future artificial persons would require independence, not mere reproducibility

If future artificial persons ever exist, genuine independent standing could make more than one artificial person morally relevant. But that would depend on actual rights-bearing status, continuity, representation, and freedom from owner command, not on a cheap ability to clone, fork, or reset software. Reproduction capacity alone does not settle tax-person count.[S18][S19][S20][S42][S43][S44]

## Guardrails

- Do not impose per-instance civic poll taxes on present machine systems.
- Do not let firms dump liability into vast fleets of nominally separate agents they still own or command.
- Do not confuse a measurable operational unit with a rights-bearing tax subject.
- Do not assume future artificial persons, if they ever exist, can be counted by raw copy events without continuity and independence tests.

## Failure modes

- **Machine capitation theater** — counting agents because they are countable, not because they are civic persons.
- **Fork-based avoidance** — splitting one controller-side operation into many nominal model units to harvest exemptions or lower brackets.
- **Rights by dashboard icon** — treating a named instance or agent profile as a person because the interface makes it salient.
- **Clone-count personhood** — assuming software reproducibility itself answers the question of how many persons exist.

## Practical tests

1. If every copy vanished tonight, which human or firm would still own the assets, collect the revenue, and answer for the harms?
2. Is the proposal taxing replicated activity through rents, compute, risk, energy, congestion, or deployment scale, or is it slipping into a head tax on nonpersons?
3. Could a controller lower the bill simply by splitting one model family into many named agents or shells without any real change in control?
4. If rights-bearing artificial persons are being claimed, is there evidence of independent standing and non-ownership, or only software multiplicity?
