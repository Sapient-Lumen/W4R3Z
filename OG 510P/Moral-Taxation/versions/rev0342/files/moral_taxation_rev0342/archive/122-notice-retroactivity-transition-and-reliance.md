# 122 — Notice, retroactivity, transition, and reliance

## One-line thesis

A morally serious tax system should be **prospective by default**: it gives fair notice, uses visible transition rules, respects legitimate reliance where people are locked into care, housing, saving, or production decisions, and uses retroactivity only narrowly enough to stop gaming, handle genuine emergencies, or align already-announced rules with their stated effective date.

## Why this matters

A tax can have the right base, the right payer, and even the right proceeds, yet still become morally defective if it arrives as a surprise charge on choices that are already closed. Households make long-lived plans around housing, care, disability, schooling, retirement, and migration. Firms sign contracts, choose locations, and build compliance systems. Frontier AI actors commit to facilities, power contracts, model-training cycles, and deployment schedules that often extend over years. OECD work treats tax certainty as a fundamental goal tied to stability, predictability, and investment, and its recent simplicity work argues that unnecessary complexity weakens certainty and growth.[S36][S38] IMF legal-design materials also distinguish retroactive tax laws as those that affect already-closed transactions, and note that legislatures usually prefer largely prospective tax operation.[S37]

At the same time, **reliance is not an all-purpose veto**. If every incumbent privilege is grandfathered forever, unjust tax advantages harden into permanent property. The archive therefore treats notice and reliance as a discipline against arbitrary surprise, not as a blank cheque for rentier lock-in.

## Design rule

**Prospective by default; narrow retroactivity; finite transition; no permanent grandfathering of privilege.**

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from prospectivity

The baseline is that new liability should apply to future periods, future flows, or future realizations after a published effective date. A tax that materially changes the treatment of a base should normally give people enough notice to understand the change, rearrange conduct where that is the point, and contest obvious drafting errors before liability crystallizes.[S36][S38]

### 2. Distinguish legitimate reliance from protected rent

The archive is more sympathetic where people are locked in by:

- primary-residence decisions,
- disability or elder-care commitments,
- child-rearing or schooling plans,
- ordinary retirement saving,
- or long-lived small-firm capital already placed in service.

It is less sympathetic where the main thing being protected is:

- speculative land appreciation,
- monopoly or bottleneck rents,
- fossil or extractive privilege,
- negotiated tax holidays,
- or incumbent AI advantages built on subsidy and market power.

### 3. Use transition tools that fit the base

Good transition tools include:

- announced effective dates,
- phase-ins,
- stepped rate ramps,
- deferral or instalments for illiquid justified bases,
- elective realization windows,
- limited carryforwards,
- and temporary safe harbours while measurement systems mature.

The archive prefers these to blanket grandfathering because they preserve direction while reducing arbitrary shock.

### 4. Keep retroactivity narrow and named

Retroactivity may be justified more often when:

- a public announcement has already created an avoidance window that can be gamed immediately,
- the rule mainly closes an abusive shelter or drafting defect,
- an emergency measure is tied to a clearly declared event,
- or the law is aligning legal effect with an already-published cutoff date rather than rewriting ancient expectations.[S37]

Even then, the state should say **why** prospectivity alone would fail and how far back the rule reaches.

### 5. Grandfather people, not permanent privilege

Where reliance protection is warranted, prefer:

- time-bounded grandfathering,
- person-focused relief for vulnerable or illiquid households,
- or staged transition into the new regime.

Do **not** freeze unjust sectoral favors, monopoly privileges, or dynastic shelters indefinitely under the label of certainty.

### 6. Apply the rule to AI stacks explicitly

For AI facilities, labs, and deployment chains, the archive prefers:

- pre-announced charging bases for major compute, grid, water, or risk-linked levies,
- explicit effective dates for new reporting, bonding, or reserve rules,
- and short anti-avoidance cutoffs where model-training or siting decisions can be rushed to beat an announced rule.

A surprise retroactive levy on already-completed training runs is morally worse than a clearly announced future charge tied to facility use, energy burden, or deployment risk.

## Guardrails

- No secret effective dates.
- No treating ordinary households as if they could unwind already-closed life decisions overnight.
- No permanent grandfathering of incumbent rents in the name of certainty.
- No invoking “anti-abuse” as a generic excuse for arbitrary backdating.

## Failure modes

- **Midnight surprise**: liability appears after conduct is already closed.
- **Fake prospectivity**: formal future dates are announced only after the key commitment is economically irreversible.
- **Grandfathered oligarchy**: a reform is announced but powerful incumbents are exempted indefinitely.
- **Backdated emergency drift**: crisis language is used to justify a vague or overlong retroactive reach.

## Practical tests

1. Would a reasonable non-insider have had meaningful notice and adjustment time?
2. Is any retroactive reach narrow enough to be explained in one paragraph?
3. Does the transition protect vulnerable reliance or merely lock in incumbent privilege?
4. For AI and large capital projects, was the reporting / charging base announced before the main irreversible commitment?
5. Is there a visible end-state, or has transition become permanent special treatment?
