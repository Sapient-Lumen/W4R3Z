# When many actors each look partly controller-like, primary controller status follows durable governance, continuation power, and residual upside

When several actors each touch the same AI stack, do **not** assign primary controller status to the easiest visible node. The archive's stable tie-break is to ask which actor can durably set the deployment's purpose, persistent operating envelope, continuation, and residual appropriation of gains. That actor is the primary controller unless the evidence instead shows genuinely split governance, in which case duties should be divided by governed layer rather than sprayed across every visible participant.[S10][S16][S17][S20][S21][S34][S35][S83]

## Why this note exists

The archive already says that ordinary users, thin resellers, delegated operators, outside overseers, passive infrastructure, channels, tools, and ordinary training-input suppliers do **not** by themselves become the controller, while durable customization or maintained release governance sometimes **can**. What remained too implicit was the next step: when several actors each satisfy part of that story, who outranks whom? This note supplies the compact tie-break.[S10][S16][S17][S20][S21][S34][S35][S83]

## Tie-break order

When more than one actor looks controller-like, rank them by the first stable difference that matters:

1. **deployment purpose and objective-setting** — who decides what the standing system is for,
2. **persistent policy and permissions** — who can set or revise the live model, retrieval scope, tool permissions, safety posture, or approval gates,
3. **continuation, update, and rollback power** — who can keep the system live, halt it, ship updates, or veto major changes,
4. **capacity and budget allocation** — who can decide whether scarce compute, staff, or market access is devoted to this deployment,
5. **residual upside and downside** — who captures the concentrated gains and must absorb the strategic losses when things go badly.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S34][S35][S83]

The actor that dominates the highest-ranked live lever is the primary controller. Lower-ranked actors can still be co-controllers, remitters, ordinary taxpayers, or liability anchors for the layers they actually govern.

## Hard defaults

1. **Visibility is not the tie-break.** The biller, host, registry, reviewer, or action rail may be easiest to see without being the actor that governs the deployment.[S20][S21][S83]
2. **Residual upside should not float free from governance.** If one actor reliably captures the concentrated strategic rents while another merely performs or meters the work, start by asking whether governance has been papered away from profit on purpose.[S21][S34][S35][S83]
3. **Use co-controller treatment narrowly.** Do not inflate every meaningful supplier into a co-controller merely because the stack is interdependent; assign shared duties only where the actor materially governs a persistent layer.[S16][S17][S20][S21]
4. **Follow the stop / redesign power when the paper story is muddy.** The actor that can halt, redesign, or continue the system without asking permission elsewhere usually outranks the actor that can only observe, price, or complain.[S16][S17][S20][S21]

## Failure modes

- **visibility bias** — primary liability is pinned to the actor with the clearest logs, invoices, or public name rather than to the actor with the governing levers.
- **governance laundering** — objective-setting, permissions, or continuation power is spread across affiliates, vendors, or fronts so that no one appears to control the whole.
- **co-controller inflation** — every important supplier is labeled a controller, making the rule so broad that no one is clearly responsible.
- **rent / control split fiction** — one actor harvests the durable upside while another is cast as the formal decision-maker mainly to absorb public duties.[S20][S21][S34][S35][S83]

## Practical tests

1. Who can decide the standing purpose of the deployment?
2. Who can change persistent model policy, retrieval scope, tools, or safety defaults?
3. Who can stop, continue, or materially update the system without asking permission elsewhere?
4. Who allocates the scarce capacity or budget that keeps this deployment alive?
5. Who captures the residual upside after ordinary fees and wages are paid?

If the same actor wins most of those questions, that actor is the primary controller. If different actors win different layers, keep one primary controller for the highest-ranking strategic layer and assign narrow co-controller duties only where real persistent governance is shared.
