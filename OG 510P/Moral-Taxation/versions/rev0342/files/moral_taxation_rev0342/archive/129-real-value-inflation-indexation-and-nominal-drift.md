# 129 — Real value, inflation, indexation, and nominal drift

## One-line thesis

A morally serious tax system should tax **real ability-to-pay, real rents, and real harms**, not inflation artifacts. If a regime quietly raises burdens through frozen brackets, taxes nominal gains as if they were real gains, or lets inflation erode loss relief, depreciation, credits, or bonds, it is using money illusion as hidden tax policy.

## Why this matters

OECD's 2023 *Taxing Wages* special feature says indexation can automatically or discretionarily adjust thresholds, brackets, and benefits, and warns that inflation can erode the real value of tax thresholds and benefits, creating fiscal drag with important transparency and distributional consequences.[S55] IMF's 2023 paper on tax distortions from inflation says taxing inflationary gains can have a tremendous effect on effective tax rates even at relatively low inflation, that partial adjustment across only some income types creates further distortions, and that inflation can erode the value of depreciation allowances in ways that affect investment.[S56] IMF's cross-country indexation dataset also shows that indexation practices vary widely and are less common on taxes than on pensions or social assistance, which means inflation surprises can quietly reallocate burdens unless the design is explicit.[S57]

## Design rule

Where inflation or nominal drift would otherwise do hidden distributive work, express the tax in **real terms or with transparent reset rules**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Do not let inflation do stealth redistribution

If the state wants a **real** tax increase, it should usually announce a real tax increase. Freezing brackets, allowances, credits, filing thresholds, or benefit phase-outs in nominal terms may be administratively simple, but it can also become an opaque way to tighten burdens on workers and caregivers whose real incomes are flat or falling.[S55][S57]

### 2. Distinguish real gains from phantom gains

For assets, inventories, long holding periods, and capital recovery rules, ask whether the proposal is taxing appreciation that is mostly inflation rather than real enrichment. Full inflation adjustment is not always administratively worth it, but the archive prefers one of three honest approaches:

- actual indexation where feasible,
- blunt but explicit approximations where full indexation is not available,
- or an openly acknowledged decision not to index, justified as a real policy choice rather than left to occur by stealth.[S56]

### 3. Preserve the real value of downside recognition

Loss carry-forwards, minimum exemptions, depreciation schedules, safe harbors, and refundable thresholds should not be allowed to wither silently in inflationary periods. Otherwise the archive's downside-symmetry rule is defeated by nominal erosion even when the statute still appears symmetric on paper.[S50][S56]

### 4. Index thresholds, bonds, and safe harbors in AI and infrastructure policy

This matters outside ordinary wage taxation too. Compute thresholds, registry fees, bond amounts, reserve minima, small-firm thresholds, and local utility-burden charges can all drift into overreach or triviality if fixed in nominal terms while prices, wages, or facility costs move quickly. For AI and data-centre governance, the archive prefers public reset rules keyed to transparent indicators rather than ad hoc nominal freezes.

### 5. Prefer transparent reset formulas over silent discretion where possible

Automatic indexation is not always morally superior to discretionary changes, but opaque discretion is worse than either. A good design states:

- what indicator is used,
- what cadence is used,
- which elements are indexed and which are not,
- what floors or caps apply,
- and when legislators are deliberately choosing a real tightening or loosening instead of letting inflation make the choice for them.[S55][S57][S58]

## Guardrails

- Do not pretend every tax instrument must be perfectly inflation-indexed.
- Do not let indexation mechanically preserve bad thresholds forever when real redesign is needed.
- Do not use selective indexation to favor capital over labor, incumbents over entrants, or profitable firms over loss-making ones without saying so.
- Do not let nominal bond amounts or cleanup reserves decay until the public again becomes the insurer of last resort.

## Failure modes

- **Bracket-creep extraction**: nominal freezes quietly raise burdens on households whose real position has not improved.
- **Phantom-gain taxation**: inflationary appreciation is taxed as if it were real enrichment.
- **Inflation-eaten relief**: loss carry-forwards, depreciation, exemptions, or credits lose real value while the nominal statute stays unchanged.
- **Nominal-safe-harbor rot**: small-firm regimes, AI thresholds, and reserve requirements drift into either overreach or irrelevance because no reset rule exists.
- **Partial-adjustment bias**: some tax bases are indexed and others are not, creating hidden tilts rather than neutral reform.[S56]

## Practical tests

1. If prices rose but real wages, real profits, and real asset values did not, would this proposal still increase liability?
2. Are brackets, allowances, credits, depreciation rules, loss relief, filing thresholds, and bond amounts indexed, partially approximated, or being left to drift silently?
3. Is any non-indexation being used as an openly chosen real tightening, or merely as stealth policy through nominal freeze?
4. Would the proposal still make moral sense after three years of moderate inflation without legislative repair?

## Archive judgment

The archive therefore adds a simple warning: **nominal values are not moral values**. Where inflation would otherwise do hidden distributive work, a just tax system should either index, approximate honestly, or defend a deliberate real policy choice in public rather than letting money illusion make the choice in the dark.
