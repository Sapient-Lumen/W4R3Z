# Mission-specific commissioning, procurement, and commanded vendor hosting can make the buyer or principal a real controller

When a buyer, principal, ministry, hospital, bank, platform, or other commissioning actor does more than merely use a vendor-hosted model — setting the standing mission, service levels, approval rules, escalation path, data / retrieval scope, or stop / continue authority for a dedicated deployment — do **not** treat the vendor host as the whole controller story. The archive's stable rule is that mission-specific commissioning can make the buyer or principal a real controller or co-controller for that governed layer even when infrastructure, weights, or day-to-day operations sit with a separate supplier.[S10][S16][S17][S20][S21][S27][S34][S35][S41][S83]

## Why this note exists

The archive already says ordinary prompting, thin resale, passive hosting, white-label packaging, outside oversight, and ordinary third-party tooling do **not** by themselves create controller status, while durable downstream customization and public-body deployment sometimes **can**. What remained too implicit was the commissioned middle case: a customer or principal that writes the real mission, risk tolerances, approvals, and continuation terms for a standing deployment while a vendor merely runs the machinery.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S27][S34][S35][S41][S83]

## Hard defaults

1. **Mission-setting beats vendor frontage.** If the buyer or principal decides what the standing system is for, who it may act on, and what counts as acceptable performance or escalation, that actor is not a mere user anymore.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S83]
2. **Dedicated command matters more than generic hosting.** A vendor can host, maintain, or meter the stack without being the only controller if a separate principal sets service levels, approval gates, workflow rules, or continuation decisions for a dedicated deployment.[S20][S21][S27][S34][S35][S83]
3. **Procurement language is not morally magic.** Calling the arrangement a contract, managed service, or procurement vehicle does not erase the buyer's controller duties where the buyer governs mission, permissions, or incident posture.[S20][S21][S27][S41][S83]
4. **Keep layers distinct.** The principal may be the controller for mission and governed use while the vendor remains a co-controller or upstream controller for weights, update channels, or shared infrastructure it still governs.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S34][S35][S83]

## Practical tests

1. Who wrote the standing mission, use case, or target population for this deployment?
2. Who can set or revise service levels, approval rules, retrieval scope, or tool permissions?
3. Who can pause, continue, or materially redesign the deployment without asking the other side first?
4. Who owns the escalation path, incident acceptance threshold, or human override rule?
5. Who captures the residual strategic upside from this dedicated deployment after ordinary vendor fees are paid?

If the buyer or principal wins those questions, treat it as a real controller or co-controller for that governed layer even if a vendor still hosts or maintains the system.

## Failure modes

- **vendor-front illusion** — the operator with the clearest invoice or dashboard is mistaken for the whole controller story.
- **procurement laundering** — real mission control is hidden inside contract language that portrays the buyer as a passive customer.
- **command without burden** — the principal writes the objectives and risk posture while trying to leave public duties with the service provider alone.
- **shared-stack confusion** — dedicated commissioning over one deployment is ignored because the same vendor also serves many ordinary customers.[S20][S21][S27][S34][S35][S41][S83]
