# Step-in managers, receivers, and incident responders can become temporary controllers when they take live operational command

When a receiver, emergency administrator, incident manager, trustee, public conservator, or other step-in operator moves from outside oversight into inside-the-loop power over continuation, rollback, permissions, or emergency deployment choices, do **not** keep treating that actor as a mere overseer or agent. The archive's stable rule is that live operational command can create temporary controller or co-controller duties for as long as the actor actually governs the stack, even if longer-run beneficiary, successor, or owner-side duties still sit elsewhere.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S27][S41][S44][S83]

## Why this note exists

The archive already says that outside overseers, insurers, channels, tools, and delegated operators do **not** by themselves become the controller. What remained too implicit was the converse case. A receiver, incident manager, or emergency step-in team can cross the line once it stops merely reviewing or advising and starts deciding whether the live system continues, rolls back, resumes, or changes operating permissions.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S27][S41][S44][S83]

## Hard defaults

1. **Step-in power beats job title.** A receiver, trustee, regulator-appointed administrator, or incident contractor that can actually continue, pause, or reconfigure the live deployment is no longer outside the loop for that period.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S41][S83]
2. **Temporary control is still real control.** Controller-side duties can attach for the period of live command even when the arrangement is emergency, provisional, or aimed at winding the system down rather than expanding it.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S27][S41][S83]
3. **Step-in control does not erase predecessor or successor duties.** Emergency command can add temporary controller duties without wiping the continuing responsibilities of owners, grouped beneficiaries, or materially continuous successor chains.[S20][S21][S27][S34][S35][S41][S83]
4. **Handoffs should settle visibly.** When step-in control ends, the archive prefers an explicit handoff of reserves, reporting lanes, and unresolved duties rather than another round of paper ambiguity.[S21][S27][S41][S83]

## Practical tests

1. Who can decide right now whether the deployment stays live, pauses, rolls back, or decommissions?
2. Who can change emergency permissions, safeguards, routing, or incident posture without asking elsewhere?
3. Who signs off on resumed operation, restricted operation, or shutdown?
4. Is the actor merely advising, auditing, or funding the response, or actually issuing live operational instructions?
5. Are predecessor and successor duties being carried forward clearly, or being left behind while the step-in operator is treated as a convenient fiction?

If the same actor wins the live-command questions, treat that actor as a temporary controller or co-controller for the governed layer and period.

## Failure modes

- **receiver invisibility** — a step-in operator effectively runs the system while the archive keeps pretending control never moved.
- **emergency-shell laundering** — a principal installs a temporary manager to absorb public duties while the real beneficiary still directs the outcome offstage.
- **outside-review fiction** — an actor with rollback, restart, or permission power is mislabeled as a mere auditor, insurer, or contractor.
- **orphaned handoff** — temporary control ends and reserves, incident duties, or reporting lanes are left floating between predecessor and successor stories.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S27][S34][S35][S41][S83]
