# 118 — Nexus, situs, apportionment, and benefit jurisdiction

## One-line thesis

A tax is not fully justified until the archive can say **which jurisdiction has the strongest moral claim to levy it**: immovable bases by situs, local harms where they land, final consumption by destination, and cross-border rent partly by market access rather than by physical-presence fictions alone.

## Why this matters

Modern tax injustice often appears as a location error. Localities bear grid strain, water stress, congestion, or housing pressure from data centres and extractive sites while the cash is booked elsewhere. Market jurisdictions host customers and users that generate profit without always having old-style physical presence. And weak coordination can create two opposite failures at once: double taxation where claims overlap, and no-man's-land non-taxation where every jurisdiction steps back.[S3][S9][S15][S28]

## Archive rule

Assign taxing rights by the strongest morally relevant anchor for the base in question:

1. **immovable situs** for land, buildings, subsoil assets, and location rents,
2. **burdened locality** for congestion, cooling-water stress, local grid strain, and similar place-bound externalities,
3. **destination / market jurisdiction** for final consumption and part of cross-border residual profit where customers or users are located,
4. **controller residence / organisation** for personal income, many firm-level obligations, and ordinary administration,
5. and **club or treaty coordination** where unilateral claims are too weak or too distortionary to hold the base in place.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Tax immovable things where they are

Land value, location rent, extractive subsoil value, and many local facility burdens belong first to the jurisdiction where the site sits. These bases are visible, hard to move, and tightly connected to local public order and infrastructure.[S5][S23][S25]

### 2. Tax final consumption at destination

For broad consumption taxes, the archive continues to prefer destination logic: tax where final use occurs, not where the seller happens to book revenue. This is especially important for cross-border services, digital supplies, and platform-mediated sales.[S3][S28]

### 3. Give market jurisdictions a claim on borderless profit

Where very large multinational or highly digital firms earn substantial profits from customers or users in a market without meaningful physical presence, the archive treats exclusive origin or residence claims as morally incomplete. Market jurisdictions deserve a coordinated claim over part of those profits.[S9][S28]

### 4. Keep local harm charges local, even when profits are global

If a frontier lab or hyperscale data centre imposes local electricity, water, congestion, or housing burdens, the siting jurisdiction should not have to wait for distant profit allocations. Local impact fees, utility surcharges, land-value capture, and adaptation finance belong near the harmed place.[S5][S15][S23]

### 5. Use coordinated floors for mobile rents

For highly mobile profit streams, unilateral taxation is often too brittle. Minimum-tax floors, information exchange, and treaty or club coordination are justified when they prevent a race to the bottom without denying local or market claims altogether.[S1][S9][S28]

### 6. Separate destination logic from personhood confusion

Taxing a market-facing AI business in the market jurisdiction does not make the model a tax person. It still means taxing firms, deployers, or beneficiaries according to where profits are earned and harms are imposed, not according to where the software seems most vivid.[S19][S20]

### 7. Prefer apportionment when precision theater takes over

When exact source tracing becomes legal theatre, rougher but disclosed apportionment by destination sales, user base, payroll, assets, or clearly stated hybrid formulas can be more honest than pretending that every intangible profit has a single natural home.[S2][S9][S28]

## Guardrails

- no treating server location alone as the sole home of cross-border digital profit
- no leaving local siting burdens uncompensated because profits are booked elsewhere
- no pretending destination logic turns tools into persons
- no overlapping claims without credit, coordination, or priority rules
- no exclusive residence/origin claims for borderless rents when markets and users clearly co-create value

## Failure modes

- **booking-haven triumph** — profits flee to legal shells while real markets and harms remain untaxed
- **siting abandonment** — data-centre or extractive host communities carry the burden without fiscal repair
- **double-claim chaos** — several jurisdictions assert incompatible full claims over the same base
- **physical-presence nostalgia** — old nexus rules miss digitally mediated profit
- **jurisdiction laundering** — firms restructure flows to exploit gaps among local, national, and treaty levels

## Practical tests

1. Which place bears the most immovable facts relevant to this base: land, extraction, congestion, or grid burden?
2. Where is the final customer or user?
3. Would the base disappear from view if physical presence were required?
4. Does the proposal give harmed localities a direct fiscal claim rather than only symbolic recognition?
5. If several jurisdictions have partial claims, what coordination or credit rule prevents overlap or no-man's-land escape?
6. Would the archive still endorse the assignment if booking entities and server locations were rearranged tomorrow?
