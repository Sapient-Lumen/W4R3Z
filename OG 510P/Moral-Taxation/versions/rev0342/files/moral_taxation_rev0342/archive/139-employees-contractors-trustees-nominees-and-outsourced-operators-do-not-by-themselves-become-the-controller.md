# 139 — Employees, contractors, trustees, nominees, and outsourced operators do not by themselves become the controller

## One-line thesis

Running, servicing, labeling, supervising, or even formally fronting an LLM deployment is **not by itself** the same as owning or governing it. Employees, contractors, trustees, nominees, managed-service operators, and other delegated agents may be remitters, representatives, or visible operators without thereby becoming the morally primary AI controller.

## Why this matters

Once the archive distinguishes ordinary users, passive infrastructure, open publication, and real downstream customization, another avoidance path appears: controller liability can be pushed onto the nearest human operator, outsourced vendor, nominee company, or fiduciary shell simply because that actor is legible, on the contract, or touching the keyboard. That would dump special AI-side burdens onto weaker or more replaceable actors while letting the principal that set the objectives, budget, deployment scope, and risk posture disappear behind delegation language.[S20][S21][S27][S34][S35][S44][S83]

## Design rule

Follow **delegated authority** rather than surface operation.

Ask separately:

- who set the standing objectives, budget, and deployment scope,
- who could materially change the model, safety posture, or approval design,
- who captures the concentrated gains or avoidance value,
- who could stop or redesign the system without breaching someone else’s instructions,
- and whether the visible operator is acting with independent discretion or mainly as an agent, custodian, or narrow service provider.[S20][S21][S27][S34][S35][S44][S83]

Where those levers remain with a principal, owner group, or governing institution, special controller-side AI liability should stay there even if delegated humans or vendors perform day-to-day operation.

## Boundary ladder

### 1. Employee or ordinary staff operator

An employee who prompts, monitors, labels, red-teams, or maintains a system inside an employer’s authority structure does **not** become the primary AI controller merely by operating it. Their own tax position remains ordinary labor or professional income unless they also hold real governance power over the deployment.[S20][S21][S27][S83]

### 2. Independent contractor or outsourced service provider on narrow instructions

A contractor or managed-service vendor may be a useful remittance point or compliance interface, but not the morally primary controller when it mainly executes a client’s standing instructions and cannot materially change capacity, safety posture, approval gates, or deployment scope.[S21][S27][S34][S35][S83]

### 3. Trustee, nominee, or fiduciary shell without real discretion

A trustee, nominee, or fiduciary shell that formally fronts the activity should not absorb controller-side liability if real beneficial control, gain capture, and strategic choice sit elsewhere. Anti-fragmentation and beneficial-ownership analysis should pierce this theater.[S20][S21][S34][S35][S44]

### 4. Delegated operator with real discretionary governance

The answer changes once the delegated actor can materially choose system objectives, persistent instructions, model changes, retrieval or tool policy, approval thresholds, reserve levels, or incident posture without waiting for principal approval. At that point the actor is no longer merely an agent; it becomes a real co-controller or controller for that layer.[S15][S16][S17][S20][S21][S27][S44]

### 5. Principal using delegation to launder control

Where a principal keeps the real strategic levers but tries to place the legal or fiscal burden on a weaker operator, contractor, or nominee, the archive should collapse the arrangement back to the principal or grouped controller. Delegation does not morally erase control.[S20][S21][S34][S35][S83]

## Hard defaults

1. **Do not confuse visible operation with governing discretion.** Keyboard proximity, formal job title, or service-contract frontage is not enough to relocate controller-side AI liability.[S20][S21][S27][S83]
2. **Do not let principals externalize AI-side burdens onto weaker agents.** Employees, dependent contractors, and nominee entities are common places to hide responsibility because they are easier to count and harder to resist.[S21][S27][S34][S35][S83]
3. **Do not erase delegated actors where they really govern.** A vendor or internal team that acquires durable power over objectives, model behavior, or risk posture can become a real co-controller for that layer.[S15][S16][S17][S20][S21]
4. **Keep remittance, representation, and controller status analytically separate.** The same actor can file, hold an account, or receive notices without being the morally primary home of the tax burden.[S21][S42][S44][S83]

## Failure modes

- **operator scapegoating** — the nearest worker or vendor is blamed because the real controller is organizationally distant.
- **fiduciary laundering** — a trustee, nominee, or shell fronts the activity while strategic control and gain capture remain elsewhere.
- **outsourcing theater** — a principal claims the vendor is now the controller even though the vendor mainly executes standing instructions.
- **agency overreach** — every delegated task is misread as independent governance, collapsing the archive into keyboard-proximity taxation.[S20][S21][S27][S34][S35][S44][S83]

## Practical tests

1. Who set the standing objectives and budget for the deployment?
2. Who could materially slow, suspend, redesign, or decommission it without asking permission?
3. Who could change the model, safety posture, retrieval or tool policy, or approval thresholds?
4. Who captures the concentrated rent, avoidance value, or strategic gain?
5. Is the visible operator acting as an independent governor or mainly under delegated instructions?

If the strategic choices and gains remain elsewhere, the visible operator is probably a worker, vendor, fiduciary, or remittance interface rather than the morally primary AI controller.
