# 143 — Third-party tools, APIs, and execution rails do not by themselves make the provider the controller

## One-line thesis

A search API, browser tool, payment action rail, messaging API, map service, robotic actuator layer, or similar third-party capability can sit **inside an agent's action path** without thereby becoming the morally primary AI controller. Supplying a callable capability is not the same thing as choosing the model, goals, rollout scope, or ordinary risk posture of the deployment.[S15][S16][S17][S20][S21][S27][S41][S68][S83]

## Why this matters

As AI systems become more agentic, outside tool and API providers become newly visible. They may meter calls, enforce quotas, log actions, or refuse categories of use. That creates a temptation to say that whichever outside service executed the action — the search provider, payment rail, communications API, robotics platform, or browsing tool — has become the real controller and should carry the deployment's special AI-side tax burdens. That would confuse **capability supply** with **deployment governance** and would let real controller groups wash liability through whichever toolchain looks most operationally close to the final act.[S15][S16][S17][S20][S21][S34][S35][S41][S68][S83]

## Design rule

Ask separately:

- who chose the standing goal, deployment scope, and economic purpose,
- who can change persistent instructions, retrieval policy, approval gates, or safety posture,
- who decides whether the agent may keep acting in the ordinary course,
- whether the outside provider mainly offers a callable capability or action rail on general terms,
- and whether the provider has crossed from supplying a tool into materially governing how the live deployment may ordinarily behave.[S15][S16][S17][S20][S21][S27][S41][S68][S83]

Where strategic discretion and concentrated gain remain with the lab, deployer, platform, or grouped beneficiary, special controller-side AI liability should remain there even if a third-party tool executes one step in the chain.

## Boundary ladder

### 1. Ordinary tool or API provider on general terms

A provider that offers search, messaging, storage, maps, payments, browsing, or other callable functions on standardized terms does **not** thereby become the primary AI controller. Metering calls or executing actions is not the same as choosing the model's standing objectives, risk posture, or rollout design.[S20][S21][S27][S68][S83]

### 2. Execution rail with compliance or abuse screening

A provider may filter abuse, block unlawful calls, impose rate limits, or require baseline policy compliance without becoming the deployment's morally primary controller. Those outside guardrails can justify ordinary fee income, remittance duties, or incident reporting without relocating the main controller burden by themselves.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S27][S41][S68][S83]

### 3. Embedded tool vendor with durable behavioral governance

The answer changes when the provider can materially choose which standing tools may run, set persistent action policy, define the ordinary operating envelope, approve high-risk continuance, or otherwise govern the system from inside the loop rather than merely serving calls from outside. At that point the actor may become a real co-controller for that governed layer.[S15][S16][S17][S20][S21][S41][S68]

### 4. Captive or affiliated tool stack

Do not let a controller group hide behind an affiliated browser tool, search API, payments API, robotic control layer, or execution shell that remains under the same beneficial ownership or strategic command. Where the supposed outside tool provider is functionally internal, follow the group rather than the paper service boundary.[S20][S21][S34][S35][S83]

### 5. High-hazard execution substrate

Even where the provider is not the primary controller, hazardous tools and execution rails can still justify ordinary regulation, reserve requirements, safety interfaces, auditable logs, or sector-specific charging tied to the provider's own activity. The key point is that those duties should be attached **as provider duties**, not by pretending every capability supplier automatically owns the deployment's full controller-side moral burden.[S15][S16][S17][S21][S27][S41]

## Hard defaults

1. **Do not mistake action execution for deployment governance.** A tool can perform a step without choosing why the system exists, how widely it runs, or who captures the concentrated gains.[S20][S21][S83]
2. **Do not let controller groups wash liability through toolchains.** The real lab, deployer, or beneficiary does not disappear because it routed actions through search, payments, browsing, communications, or robotics APIs.[S20][S21][S34][S35][S83]
3. **Do use tool providers as narrowly assigned remittance, logging, or safety anchors where that is the cleanest fit.** Useful collection or reporting interfaces do not by themselves become the morally primary taxpayer.[S21][S27][S41][S68][S83]
4. **Do escalate when the provider gains durable inside-the-loop governance.** Persistent action policy, approval power, standing permission structure, or continuation control can create real co-controller duties for that layer.[S15][S16][S17][S20][S21]

## Failure modes

- **action-rail scapegoating** — the provider that executed the visible step is treated as the controller because it is easiest to see or charge.
- **toolchain laundering** — a controller group routes behavior through outsourced capabilities mainly to blur where strategic power really sits.
- **guardrail confusion** — baseline abuse filters or rate limits are mistaken for ordinary deployment governance.
- **inside-loop blindness** — a provider that actually sets durable action policy hides behind the language of neutral tooling.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S34][S35][S41][S68][S83]

## Practical tests

1. Who chose the standing purpose, deployment scope, and ordinary risk posture of the system?
2. Who can change persistent instructions, approval gates, or the default permission structure?
3. Is the outside provider mainly serving calls on general terms, or governing live operation from inside the loop?
4. Does the actor capture mainly usage fees, or the concentrated rents from the deployment itself?
5. Would the same AI deployment still be privately directed if one tool or API provider were swapped for another?

If the answers still point to the lab, deployer, platform, or grouped beneficiary, keep controller-side AI taxation there. Tool and API providers can be taxed ordinarily and used as safety, logging, or remittance anchors without thereby becoming the primary moral home of the deployment.
