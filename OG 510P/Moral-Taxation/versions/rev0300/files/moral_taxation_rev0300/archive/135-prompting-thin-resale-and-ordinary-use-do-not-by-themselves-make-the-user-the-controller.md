# 135 — Prompting, thin resale, and ordinary use do not by themselves make the user the controller

## One-line thesis

In an LLM stack, **visibility is not control**. Ordinary prompting, API use, ordinary enterprise use, or thin resale does not by itself make a person or firm the proper target of controller-side AI taxation. Those special liabilities should follow the actors who can materially choose model capacity, deployment scale, risk posture, beneficiary extraction, or avoidance structure.

## Why this matters

Modern AI systems create a recurring tax-design temptation: the most visible actor is often the prompt author, API customer, or white-label intermediary, while the real control points sit upstream in labs, clouds, deployers, grouped affiliates, and major commercial beneficiaries. If taxation follows visibility instead of control, the archive drifts toward end-user surcharges, reseller theater, and blame-shifting onto actors who cannot meaningfully change the system-level burden or risk.[S10][S15][S16][S17][S20][S21][S83]

## Design rule

Treat **controller-side AI liability** as a question of real operational and economic control:

- who can choose or substantially modify the model or deployment stack,
- who can expand or contract compute scale,
- who can set or relax safety, logging, or incident-response posture,
- who captures the concentrated rent or major productivity gain,
- and who can restructure the chain through shells, resellers, or affiliate routing.[S10][S15][S16][S17][S20][S21][S34][S35][S83]

Where those levers are absent, keep the downstream actor inside ordinary person or business taxation rather than treating them as the upstream controller merely because they touched the interface.

## Boundary ladder

### 1. Prompt author or ordinary end user

An individual or ordinary firm using a hosted model through a standard interface is **not** the upstream controller by default. Their tax position should usually remain ordinary income, payroll, consumption, or business-profit taxation rather than a special model-stack levy.[S3][S20][S21][S83]

### 2. Thin reseller, broker, or pass-through integrator

A white-label seller or broker may be a useful remittance point for some narrow charges, but that does not automatically make it the morally primary controller. If it cannot materially choose the model, capacity, risk posture, or group structure, special controller-side liability should normally stay higher or deeper in the stack.[S21][S27][S34][S35][S83]

### 3. Deployer or integrator with material system discretion

Once a downstream actor can materially fine-tune, orchestrate agents, set automated decision rules, choose deployment scale, or shift risk onto workers, users, or the public, it stops being a mere user and becomes a real controller for that layer. Deployment-risk charges, bonding, reserve duties, and some AI-linked levies may then attach at that point as well as upstream.[S15][S16][S17][S20]

### 4. Self-hosting, substantial fine-tuning, or dedicated model operations

A firm that self-hosts weights, runs dedicated inference infrastructure, performs substantial fine-tuning, or controls a persistent model fleet usually has enough operational control to count as a controller for its own stack, even if some upstream lab or cloud still shares responsibility.[S10][S15][S20][S21]

### 5. Major beneficiary without technical control

A dominant firm can still be a proper tax target when it captures extraordinary gains from AI adoption even if it is not the model operator. Automation-dividend, windfall, or beneficiary-side contributions can attach to concentrated gains without pretending the ordinary end user is the controller.[S11][S13][S14][S21][S22]

## Hard defaults

1. **Do not tax prompts as persons or prompt volume as civic person-count.** Use remains evidence of activity, not of direct machine subjecthood or of user-side controller status.[S19][S20][S83]
2. **Do not treat ordinary users as the cleanup fund for upstream concentration.** Controller-side rent, infrastructure, and risk charges should sit with actors who can actually change those conditions.[S10][S15][S21]
3. **Do not let firms launder control through contract language.** A provider that keeps real power over model choice, pricing, throttling, data access, or incident handling should not escape controller treatment by naming everyone else the “responsible user.”[S20][S34][S35]
4. **Do not let thin intermediaries erase major beneficiaries.** A large firm gaining disproportionate automation rents can owe beneficiary-side contributions even when the technical stack is outsourced.[S11][S21][S22]

## Failure modes

- **end-user surcharge theater** — special AI taxes fall on visible users or ordinary customers because they are easy to count.
- **prompt blame shifting** — firms describe model failures or system burdens as if they were created mainly by user wording rather than by model design, deployment, or governance choices.
- **reseller laundering** — shell resellers are inserted so liability appears to sit with a thin pass-through instead of the real controller group.[S34][S35]
- **beneficiary invisibility** — major margin gains are ignored because the profitable firm is “only a customer.”

## Practical tests

1. Who can materially choose the model, weights, fine-tune, or core deployment architecture?
2. Who can scale compute, energy use, or agentic reach up and down?
3. Who can change safety posture, logging, override powers, or incident response?
4. Who captures the concentrated rents or major productivity windfall?
5. Could the actor meaningfully reduce the burden or risk without waiting for someone else’s permission?

If the answer is mostly “no,” the actor is probably not the right home for controller-side AI taxation.
