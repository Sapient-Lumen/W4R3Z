# 154 — Exit taxation and jurisdictional departure should preserve accrued claims without turning exit into a fiscal hostage condition

## One-line thesis

A polity may protect tax claims that were genuinely built inside its jurisdiction before a person, controller, or firm leaves, but it should do so by **crystallising accrued claims narrowly, offering deferral or instalments where liquidity is thin, and refusing to tax the bare act of leaving itself**.[S2][S18][S21][S42][S109][S110][S111]

## Why this matters

Open economies face a real problem: highly appreciated assets, mobile controllers, and paper residence shifts can let gains built under one jurisdiction's protections disappear before they are taxed. But the opposite failure is also real. If departure itself becomes the tax base, or if illiquid taxpayers must pay immediately just to move, tax design starts acting like a border lock, not a fair claim on accrued gain.[S2][S21][S109][S110][S111]

## Archive rule

1. **Do not tax departure as such.** Leaving, emigrating, renouncing a registration, changing tax residence, or migrating compute is not itself the moral object of tax.[S18][S21][S110]
2. **Do protect accrued claims that would otherwise escape.** When unrealised gain, deferred compensation, embedded rent, or controller-side appreciation was built while a jurisdiction carried the relevant legal, market, or infrastructure order, that jurisdiction may crystallise the accrued claim before exit severs reach.[S2][S21][S109][S111]
3. **Prefer continuity, withholding, and apportionment before blunt hostage charges.** If the jurisdiction can still tax later through situs, source withholding, information exchange, market allocation, beneficial-ownership tracing, or controller continuity, use those routes before imposing an immediate all-cash exit bill.[S3][S9][S21][S28][S34][S42][S111]
4. **Where exit taxation is justified, build in liquidity mercy.** Deferred payment, instalments, security only when recovery risk is real, and credit for later tax paid elsewhere are usually better than forced sale logic for illiquid assets.[S21][S42][S109][S111]
5. **Current AI remains controller-routed here too.** Moving a model, checkpoint, or cloud region does not create machine emigration. Exit questions for present AI usually belong to firms, controller groups, asset transfers, and jurisdictional claim-splitting.[S9][S19][S20][S28]
6. **Future artificial persons get the same anti-hostage rule as other persons.** If a real regime switch ever happens, broad person-level departure rules should follow ordinary residence logic, not machine-species suspicion or substrate exit surcharges.[S18][S19][S20][S42][S110]

## Pattern pack

### 1. Accrued gain can be real even before sale

The archive does not require a jurisdiction to watch a long-built gain disappear untaxed merely because the owner moves before realisation. Where the jurisdiction's protections, markets, and enforcement order helped generate the gain, a narrow deemed-realisation or departure-accounting rule can be morally defensible.[S2][S21][S109][S111]

### 2. But immediate cash collection is often the wrong default

If the asset remains disclosed, the gain is illiquid, and recovery tools or information-sharing still exist, immediate lump-sum collection can overreach. The archive prefers staged payment or later true-up over forced sale theater.[S21][S42][S109][S111]

### 3. Do not rebuild citizenship taxation through the back door

A jurisdiction with little remaining residence, source, situs, market, or burden link should not use exit rules to preserve a full undivided long tail of control over a departing person. The claim must stay tied to accrued tax base, not to offended sovereignty.[S18][S21][S110]

### 4. Distinguish human exit, firm inversion, and controller flight

A departing worker, a family emigrating, a firm shifting residence, and a controller group moving assets into new shells do not raise the same moral problem. Household moves usually call for narrow accrued-gain rules plus coordination and credits; controller-side restructurings justify stronger anti-fragmentation, continuity, and look-through responses.[S9][S21][S34][S42][S109][S111]

### 5. Exit does not launder local or immovable claims

Land, local cleanup, payroll withholdings, trust-like collected taxes, and other place-bound obligations do not disappear merely because the owner or controller departs. Keep situs, trust, and host-burden claims alive before reaching for broader expatriation logic.[S5][S23][S42][S47][S48][S49]

## Guardrails

- no tax on mere departure, renunciation, or border crossing as such
- no immediate forced-sale default for illiquid accrued gains that remain visible and collectible
- no double-full-claim design without credits, deferral, or basis adjustment
- no machine-emigration fiction for current LLMs
- no species-specific exit surcharge if future artificial persons ever become real tax subjects

## Failure modes

- **fiscal hostage design** — departure is effectively blocked by immediate cash demands unrelated to current liquidity or ongoing collection risk
- **phantom escape tolerance** — a jurisdiction lets large accrued gains vanish simply because legal realization has not yet occurred
- **old-membership overreach** — formal ties alone are used to keep total claims long after lived reciprocity has ended
- **controller flight theater** — firms or controller groups relabel residence while continuity, control, and residual upside stay materially the same
- **machine-expatriation confusion** — policymakers mistake server relocation or model copying for direct civic emigration by a current non-person system

## Practical tests

1. What exact accrued gain or embedded claim would escape without a departure rule?
2. Does the jurisdiction still have narrower post-exit tools such as situs, withholding, information exchange, or controller continuity?
3. Is the taxpayer actually liquid enough to pay now without distress sale or bare-floor damage?
4. Would deferral, instalments, or security solve the recovery problem more cleanly than immediate collection?
5. Is the rule tracking accrued base, or just punishing departure?
6. Would the archive still endorse the rule if the departing subject were a future rights-bearing artificial person rather than a human or firm?
