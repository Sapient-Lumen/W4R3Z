# 136 — Passive infrastructure, finance, and contract capacity do not by themselves make the provider the controller

## One-line thesis

Owning the racks, meter, loan, building, or contract-capacity layer is **not by itself** the same as choosing the model, deployment scale, or safety posture. Clouds, utilities, landlords, lenders, and contract hosts can be proper rent targets, remittance anchors, or host-burden payers without automatically becoming the morally primary AI controller.

## Why this matters

AI tax design can drift upward just as easily as it can drift downward. After correcting the mistake of treating ordinary users or thin resellers as controllers, the next mistake is to treat every visible upstream supplier as the controller merely because it owns a data centre, extends credit, holds the meter, or rents the site. That confuses **infrastructure exposure** with **strategic control** and can let the real deployment chooser disappear behind standardized service contracts or financing paper.[S10][S15][S16][S17][S20][S21][S34][S35][S83]

## Design rule

Separate three questions:

- **who should owe ordinary tax on rents, interest, utility sales, or site value,**
- **who is the best collection or remittance anchor for a particular charge,**
- and **who is the morally primary controller for special AI-side liability.**

Those answers can overlap, but they do not have to. The primary controller is the actor that can materially allocate scarce capacity, choose or veto deployment scale, shape safety and incident posture, or redirect the gain-producing use of the stack.[S10][S15][S16][S17][S20][S21][S83]

## Boundary ladder

### 1. Passive lender or ordinary landlord

A lender earning ordinary interest, or a landlord leasing space on standardized terms, does **not** become the primary AI controller merely by financing or hosting the activity. Tax ordinary finance income, site rent, property, and local burdens as such; reserve controller-side AI liability for the actor that actually chooses the model activity and its risk posture.[S5][S15][S20][S21][S23][S25][S83]

### 2. Utility, cloud, or capacity seller on standard terms

A utility or cloud seller can be an excellent remittance point or burden meter, yet still fail to be the morally primary controller when it offers broadly standardized service and does not materially pick the customer’s model, deployment logic, or safety posture. Meter visibility is not the same as strategic governance.[S10][S15][S21][S27][S83]

### 3. Contract host or manufacturer without strategic discretion

A firm that provides racks, assembly, managed hosting, or contracted technical execution on a buyer’s instructions may be part of the taxable chain without being the main controller. If it cannot materially decide capacity allocation, exclusivity, deployment scope, or incident handling, special controller-side liability should usually stay with the actor giving the economically decisive instructions.[S20][S21][S27][S34][S35]

### 4. Capacity allocator with gatekeeping power

The answer changes once the upstream provider can ration scarce accelerators, impose exclusivity, prioritize some deployments over others, veto safety spend, set default logging or incident rules, or otherwise decide whether high-impact activity can proceed. At that point the provider is no longer merely passive infrastructure; it becomes a real co-controller or controller for that layer.[S10][S15][S16][S17][S20]

### 5. Vertically integrated infrastructure-plus-deployment groups

Where the same grouped actor owns or effectively commands the facility, capacity, financing, deployment, and beneficiary chain, the archive should not split one real controller into many moral fragments. Anti-fragmentation and beneficial-ownership rules should collapse the theater and route the liability through the integrated controller group.[S10][S21][S34][S35]

## Hard defaults

1. **Do not confuse a rent target or remittance anchor with the morally primary controller.** A cloud invoice, utility meter, lease, or loan covenant can locate collection without settling controller status.[S15][S21][S27][S83]
2. **Do not let passive-supplier theater hide the real chooser.** The actor deciding the model, deployment scope, or safety posture should not escape by claiming the cloud, landlord, or lender is now the “real operator.”[S20][S34][S35]
3. **Do not erase upstream infrastructure rents or host burdens either.** A provider can owe ordinary tax, site-rent capture, utility-burden charges, or local obligations even when it is not the primary AI controller.[S5][S15][S21][S23][S25]
4. **Escalate when finance or infrastructure becomes governance.** Preferred-capacity rights, exclusivity terms, veto covenants, captive hosting, or incident-governance control can turn a nominal supplier into a real co-controller.[S10][S16][S17][S20][S34][S35]

## Failure modes

- **meter worship** — the actor with the most legible meter or bill is treated as the whole controller story.
- **landlord laundering** — a controller hides behind a site owner or facilities shell and claims the real stack is “just rented space.”
- **financing theater** — strategic control is exercised through covenants, exclusivity, or preferred-capacity rights while tax analysis still describes the financer as passive.
- **contract-capacity scapegoating** — a host or contractor with narrow technical duties is blamed for governance choices it could not materially change.[S20][S21][S34][S35][S83]

## Practical tests

1. Who can materially allocate scarce compute, queue priority, energy draw, or deployment access?
2. Who can veto, require, or relax safety, logging, reserve, or incident-response spending?
3. Who can decide whether the high-impact model activity proceeds at all?
4. Who captures the concentrated gains from that decision?
5. If this actor walked away, would the strategic choices move with it or remain elsewhere?

If the strategic choices remain elsewhere, the provider is probably a remittance point, rent target, or host-burden payer rather than the morally primary AI controller.
