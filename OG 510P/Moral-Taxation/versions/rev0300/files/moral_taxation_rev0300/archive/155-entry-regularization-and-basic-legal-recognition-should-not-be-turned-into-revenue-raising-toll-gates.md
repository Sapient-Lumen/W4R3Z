# 155 — Entry, regularization, and basic legal recognition should not be turned into revenue-raising toll gates

## One-line thesis

A polity may recover narrow administrative costs in limited cases, but it should not fund itself by pricing access to asylum, basic regularization, first legal identity, or initial person-before-law recognition. Those gateways belong to floor protection, due process, and equal standing, not to revenue-raising tax design.[S18][S42][S46][S112][S113][S114]

## Why this matters

The archive already rejects turning **exit** into a fiscal hostage condition. The matching entry-side rule was missing. States sometimes face real intake and administrative costs when people seek asylum, regularize status, replace lost papers, or move from legal invisibility into recognized standing. But a system that finances itself by charging for the gateway itself risks taxing desperation, statelessness, displacement, or bare legal personhood rather than ordinary taxable activity.[S42][S46][S112][S113][S114]

The same logic matters prospectively for any future artificial persons. If a real regime switch ever happens, the state or former controller should not be allowed to say: "you may be a person before the law, but only after you buy your own recognition." Basic legal recognition is part of the switch, not a revenue lever attached to it.[S19][S20][S42][S43][S46][S113]

## Archive rule

1. **Do not treat entry to safety or law as the tax base.** Seeking asylum, basic regularization, first identity documentation, or first recognition as a legal person is not itself the moral object of taxation.[S42][S46][S112][S113][S114]
2. **Allow only bounded cost recovery for genuinely optional or extraordinary services.** Ordinary replacement documents, premium expedition, or elective extras may carry moderate charges, but the baseline gateway should remain reachable without revenue-maximizing fees.[S42][S112][S114]
3. **Waive or absorb charges when the gateway is tied to the protected floor.** Displacement, statelessness, destitution, disability, family separation, or other floor-level vulnerability should push financing toward general revenue, public administration, or humanitarian support rather than applicant-paid tolling.[S18][S42][S46][S112][S114]
4. **Regularization usually improves later tax capacity.** Bringing people into visible residence, work, filing, schooling, and service systems often strengthens later ordinary taxation more cleanly than pricing the gate up front.[S21][S31][S42][S64][S114]
5. **Current AI gets no direct subject-side analogue because current AI is not a tax person.** For present systems, registration and licensing questions remain controller-side and facility-side questions.[S19][S20][S21]
6. **Future artificial persons should not buy their own legal recognition.** If a real rights-bearing switch ever occurs, initial person-regime recognition, baseline registration, and access to standing should arrive as part of the legal transition, not as a synthetic poll tax or owner-imposed conversion debt.[S19][S20][S42][S43][S46][S113]

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish a document fee from a toll gate

The archive does not insist that every paper, duplicate, or expedited administrative service must always be free. It insists on a different distinction: a **modest, waivable service charge** for a bounded administrative act is not the same thing as a **revenue-raising price on admission to safety, status, or legal visibility**.[S42][S112][S114]

### 2. The intake gateway is part of equal standing

Where the legal system is deciding whether someone may live safely, work lawfully, hold papers, or count as a person before the law, the gateway is part of equal protection and remedy architecture. The archive therefore treats it more like courts, identity, and basic standing infrastructure than like an ordinary taxable market transaction.[S42][S43][S46][S113]

### 3. Refugee and regularization cases are the clearest human example

The Refugee Convention already rejects higher fiscal charges on refugees than on nationals in similar situations, forbids penalties for certain unlawful entry cases, and requires necessary facilities while status is regularized or admission elsewhere is obtained. The archive extends the moral shape of that rule: where the gateway is fundamentally about safety and lawful standing, toll-road design is presumptively wrong.[S112][S114]

### 4. Recognition should not become synthetic debt peonage for future artificial persons

A future regime switch would already be morally dangerous if former owners could keep domination through upkeep billing. It would be worse if recognition itself arrived only after the subject incurred a mandatory debt for legal conversion, registration, or standing. The archive therefore blocks both moves: no upkeep meter for bare existence, and no recognition toll for entry into the person regime.[S19][S20][S42][S43][S46][S113]

## Guardrails

- no revenue target attached to asylum, basic regularization, or first legal recognition as such
- no refusal of standing, papers, or lawful visibility solely because the applicant cannot prepay the gate
- no owner-imposed conversion debt if future artificial personhood ever becomes real
- no confusion between optional premium service fees and baseline access to law
- no use of paperwork precarity as a reason to deny the floor while still demanding compliance

## Failure modes

- **entry-toll-road design** — safety, regularization, or first recognition is priced as a fiscal gate rather than handled as public administration
- **cost-recovery theater** — the state labels a fee administrative while quietly using it to ration or monetize access
- **paperwork invisibility trap** — undocumented or unstable-status people are denied lawful entry into systems that would later make ordinary tax and service coordination possible
- **recognition debt peonage** — a future artificial person must buy legal standing from the state or its former owner before rights become real
- **premium-lane bleed** — optional expedition or convenience fees quietly become the de facto path to timely baseline access

## Practical tests

1. Is the charge attached to an optional service, or to the baseline gateway into safety, regularization, identity, or legal recognition?
2. Could an indigent applicant still reach lawful standing, floor protection, and review without prepaying the charge?
3. Is the fee truly moderate and cost-linked, or is it being used to ration demand or raise revenue?
4. Would the archive still defend the rule if the applicant were stateless, displaced, disabled, or otherwise below the protected floor?
5. If the subject were a future rights-bearing artificial person, would the rule look like a recognition toll or conversion debt?
