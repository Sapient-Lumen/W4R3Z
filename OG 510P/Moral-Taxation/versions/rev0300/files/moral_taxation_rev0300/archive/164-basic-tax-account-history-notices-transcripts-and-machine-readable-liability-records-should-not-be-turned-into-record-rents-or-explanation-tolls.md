# 164 — Basic tax account history, notices, transcripts, and machine-readable liability records should not be turned into record rents or explanation tolls

## One-line thesis

When a person, small firm, or future rights-bearing artificial person needs its **own ordinary tax-account history, notices, transcripts, or machine-readable liability record** to understand, verify, correct, or port a claim, baseline access should not depend on per-document fees, proprietary viewer rents, or paid intermediary gateways.

## Why this matters

The archive already blocks review paywalls, gateway rents, distress rents, and refund skims. But a system can still sell intelligibility back to the subject if the ordinary path to seeing notices, balances, payment history, transcripts, or comparable account records is itself monetized. That would turn explanation into a side market rather than part of legitimate administration.[S16][S27][S39][S42][S44][S65][S66][S89][S127][S128]

Current public materials show the cleaner alternative. OECD's current services framing emphasizes online taxpayer-account service and digital assistance as central parts of modern tax administration, IRS guidance says people can access personal tax records online or by mail, and IRS online-account guidance says there is **no fee** to download or print transcripts while digital copies of most notices are available in the account.[S65][S66][S127][S128]

## Design rule

Keep at least one **ordinary, visible, no-rent self-access lane** open for notices, account history, transcripts, and ordinary machine-readable liability records, and treat premium certification or unusual evidentiary packaging as separate from baseline access.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Baseline self-access to one's own tax account is part of administration, not a new taxable service

If the administration already holds the taxpayer's notices, balances, payment history, filing status, or year-specific account record, the ordinary subject should not have to buy that visibility back through per-document microfees, mandatory agent subscriptions, or a proprietary viewer rental. Baseline account access belongs with explanation, correction, and ordinary compliance.[S16][S27][S39][S42][S44][S65][S66][S127][S128]

### 2. Records needed to verify or correct a claim should not sit behind paid intermediaries

A subject often needs transcripts, notices, or account-history exports precisely because something went wrong: a mismatch, preparer fraud, identity theft, payment misapplication, or stale balance. If the only practical way to inspect those records is to hire an intermediary or pay repeated extraction fees, the administration has recreated a review paywall one step earlier in the pipeline.[S16][S27][S39][S41][S42][S44][S127][S128]

### 3. Machine-readable export and portability matter too

The archive's modern-administration rule is not only about screen viewing. Ordinary subjects should have a workable path to download or export their own baseline records in forms that can support filing, contest, portability, or delegated help without locking them into one vendor, portal, or controller-controlled dashboard.[S16][S17][S27][S39][S44][S65][S66][S89][S127]

### 4. Cost-priced exceptional certification may be permissible; baseline access is different

A jurisdiction may still charge modest cost-based amounts for unusual certification, rush paper handling, litigation-grade copies, or special third-party verification products. The archive's claim is narrower and firmer: **ordinary viewing, downloading, mailing of standard transcripts, and access to one's own ordinary notices or account history should not be a revenue line**.[S27][S39][S65][S66][S127][S128]

### 5. Future artificial persons should inherit person-side record access, not owner-rented dashboards

If a rights-bearing artificial person ever becomes real, the same rule applies on the person side. The subject should not need a former controller's paid console, wallet dashboard, or proprietary viewer just to inspect its own notices, balances, liability history, or refund / payment status. Secure identity proof and bounded delegation may still matter; **owner-rented access to the subject's own record does not**.[S19][S20][S42][S43][S44][S89][S127][S128]

## Guardrails

- Do not charge ordinary subjects repeated fees just to see their own notices, balances, payment history, or standard transcripts.
- Do not require a paid intermediary or proprietary viewer as the practical default for ordinary record access.
- Do not hide machine-readable exports or standard mailed transcripts behind premium tiers when those records are needed for ordinary compliance, correction, or portability.
- Do not confuse cost-priced exceptional certification with ordinary self-access to the subject's own record.

## Failure modes

- **record rent**: the subject must repeatedly pay to see, download, or mail ordinary account records the administration already holds.
- **explanation toll**: the tax claim is nominally explainable, but the practical documents needed to understand it sit behind fees or proprietary access.
- **viewer lock-in**: baseline account history is usable only through one vendor, one portal, or one owner-controlled dashboard.
- **correction-by-subscription**: fixing a misapplied payment, stale balance, or identity-theft event effectively requires a paid service tier.
- **owner-rented tax memory**: a future artificial person can inspect its own tax history only through a former controller's paid interface.

## Practical tests

1. Can an ordinary subject inspect its current balances, notices, payment history, and standard transcripts without paying a repeated access fee?
2. Is there at least one workable ordinary lane for download, mail delivery, or export that does not require a proprietary vendor relationship?
3. Are any charges limited to unusual certification or rush handling rather than to baseline self-access?
4. If a future artificial person existed, could it inspect its own tax record in its own name without a former controller renting back the viewing rail?
