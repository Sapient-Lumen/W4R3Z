# 128 — Precaution, catastrophic risk, and non-compensable harm

## One-line thesis

A morally serious tax system should distinguish **ordinary priceable harms** from **catastrophic, irreversible, or otherwise non-compensable risks**. Some activities should face taxes or fees; others should face ex ante conditions, hard caps, decommissioning duties, or disapproval because taxation is the wrong first tool.

## Why this matters

Taxation can internalize many social costs, but it can also become a category mistake. OECD's 2025 licensing-and-permitting guidance says ex ante controls are appropriate where activities cannot be regulated effectively after the fact and where potential harm is significant and irreversible; it explicitly warns that licensing should be used for risk management rather than merely to raise revenue.[S53] NIST's AI RMF playbook likewise says organizations should define risk tolerances, allow approval or disapproval of AI deployment, and facilitate early decommissioning of systems that surpass an organization's ability to reasonably mitigate risks.[S54] That matters for frontier AI, hazardous extraction, toxic facilities, and any domain where a tax paid after deployment would arrive too late to repair the main wrong.

## Design rule

Use taxation for **ordinary measurable externalities, rents, and reciprocal public claims**. Use **ex ante governance** when the main problem is catastrophic, irreversible, or insufficiently remediable risk.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Keep pricing and permission conceptually separate

A Pigouvian-style charge can be a good tool when:

- the harm is measurable enough to price,
- affected parties can realistically be compensated or protected,
- ex post correction is not hopelessly late,
- and the tax does not function as a disguised sale of permission for conduct that should instead be gated.

The archive therefore distinguishes **pricing ordinary burdens** from **selling the right to create non-compensable risk**.

### 2. Use ex ante gates where ex post repair is morally too late

Where potential harms are significant and irreversible, the archive prefers combinations of:

- licensing or permitting,
- safety cases and deployment approval,
- bounded tiers or hard caps,
- incident reserves, bonds, and insurance,
- decommissioning obligations,
- and, where necessary, disapproval or cessation rather than continued taxed operation.[S53][S54]

### 3. Do not let revenue need turn risk control into fee collection

OECD warns that licensing and permitting should not be used mainly to raise revenue when prior approval is not actually needed for risk mitigation.[S53] The archive applies the mirror image too: if ex ante approval *is* morally needed, the state should not pretend a tax alone has done the job just because it raised money.

### 4. Controller-first AI taxes still matter, but as a sidecar to governance

For present AI systems, the archive still favors controller-side taxes on:

- electricity demand,
- carbon and cooling-water burdens,
- local grid congestion,
- monopoly and bottleneck rents,
- and automation-linked windfalls.

But where frontier deployment creates tail risks that could outrun post hoc compensation or ordinary supervision, taxation becomes a sidecar rather than a substitute for approval, tiering, red-teaming, incident response, and decommissioning authority.[S15][S17][S54]

### 5. Local concentration strengthens the case for ex ante controls

The IEA says AI-focused data centres are highly geographically concentrated and can account for substantial shares of electricity consumption in local markets.[S15] When harms are locally concentrated in grids, water systems, emergency planning, or cleanup exposure, jurisdictions should not rely on post hoc tax collection alone if the core risk needs to be constrained before operations scale.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat every uncertain activity as a candidate for prohibition.
- Do not confuse ordinary externalities with existential or irreversible harms.
- Do not let “precaution” become a pretext for arbitrary power or revenue extraction.
- Do not let a tax receipt be used rhetorically as proof that an activity has been made safe.

## Failure modes

- **Tax-as-permit theater**: a fee is treated as moral authorization for a risk that should have faced approval or disapproval.
- **Revenue-first regulation**: public authorities keep a risky activity alive mainly because it pays fees.
- **Tail-risk blindness**: expected-value pricing is used where catastrophe, irreversibility, or concentrated local burden make ex post repair implausible.
- **Controller laundering**: labs or deployers point to taxes paid as if that discharged duties of safety, testing, monitoring, or shutdown.

## Practical tests

1. If the worst plausible downside occurs, can the main wrong actually be repaired after the fact?
2. Is the proposed tax pricing an ordinary burden, or quietly selling permission for a catastrophic or irreversible one?
3. Would ex ante approval, tiering, bonding, or decommissioning do more moral work than another levy?
4. Are authorities using the instrument primarily to manage risk, or mainly to raise revenue from a risky activity?

## Archive judgment

The archive therefore adds a simple caution: **not every harmful activity should be taxed into legitimacy**. Some should be taxed *and* regulated; some should be regulated first and only secondarily charged; and some should not proceed at all unless and until risks can be reduced into a range where compensation, bonding, or ordinary taxation make moral sense.
