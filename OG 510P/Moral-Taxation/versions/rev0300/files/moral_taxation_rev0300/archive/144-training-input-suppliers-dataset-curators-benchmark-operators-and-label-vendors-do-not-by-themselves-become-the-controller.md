# 144 — Training-input suppliers, dataset curators, benchmark operators, and label vendors do not by themselves become the controller

## One-line thesis

Licensing training corpora, curating datasets, supplying labels, or running benchmarks can shape what an AI system learns or how it is scored without thereby making the supplier the morally primary controller. Input supply and evaluation are not the same thing as choosing the standing deployment goal, release scope, or ordinary risk posture of the live system.[S10][S16][S17][S20][S21][S22][S27][S83]

## Why this matters

Once the archive distinguishes users, operators, channels, tools, and open-weight release, another temptation appears: to say that whichever outside party supplied the training set, labels, benchmark, or licensing corpus has become the true controller and should bear the deployment's special AI-side tax burdens. That would confuse **input dependence** with **deployment governance** and would let real controller groups wash liability into the training and evaluation supply chain simply because those actors are upstream, contractual, or newly visible.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S27][S34][S35][S83]

The opposite mistake is also real. Training-input suppliers can still owe ordinary taxes on royalties, service income, data rents, or concentrated licensing gains, and some cases can justify bounded reciprocity or reporting duties. The point is only that those duties should attach **as supplier or licensor duties**, not by pretending every corpus vendor or benchmark operator automatically owns the deployment's full controller-side moral burden.[S10][S21][S22][S27][S83]

## Design rule

Ask separately:

- who chose the standing training objective, release strategy, and economic purpose,
- who can change persistent corpus policy, update policy, benchmark gates, or release conditions,
- whether the outside actor mainly licenses, curates, labels, or scores inputs on general terms,
- who captures the concentrated deployment rent rather than ordinary supplier revenue,
- and whether the supplier has crossed from selling inputs into materially governing ordinary training, updating, release, or live deployment choices.[S10][S16][S17][S20][S21][S22][S27][S34][S35][S83]

Where strategic discretion and concentrated gain remain with the lab, deployer, platform, or grouped beneficiary, special controller-side AI liability should remain there even if outside data or benchmark suppliers materially matter to performance.

## Boundary ladder

### 1. Ordinary training-data licensor or label vendor

A licensor or labeling vendor that supplies data, annotations, or access rights on negotiated or standardized terms does **not** thereby become the primary AI controller. Selling or preparing inputs is not the same as choosing the model's standing objective, deployment scope, or ordinary risk posture.[S20][S21][S27][S83]

### 2. Dataset curator or benchmark operator on general terms

A curator or benchmark operator that assembles evaluation suites, publishes scores, or sells access to a dataset is still usually operating as an input or measurement layer rather than as the live controller. Score visibility, licensing leverage, or curation effort can justify ordinary supplier taxation and narrow reporting duties without relocating the deployment's main controller burden by itself.[S10][S16][S17][S21][S22][S27][S83]

### 3. Embedded input gate with durable governance power

The answer changes when the supplier can materially choose persistent training policy, ongoing corpus inclusion, benchmark pass conditions that decide ordinary release, update approval, or other standing governance over whether the model may keep changing or shipping in the ordinary course. At that point the actor may become a real co-controller for that governed layer.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S22][S83]

### 4. Captive or affiliated data-and-eval pipeline

Do not let a controller group hide behind an affiliated corpus broker, labeling vendor, benchmark shell, or licensing conduit that remains under the same beneficial ownership or strategic command. Where the supposed outside supplier is functionally internal, follow the group rather than the paper input boundary.[S20][S21][S34][S35][S83]

### 5. Public-input reciprocity without ownership inflation

Dependence on public research, open scientific infrastructure, or shared knowledge resources can justify bounded public-input reciprocity from controllers that capture disproportionate gains. That does **not** mean every training fragment creates a private or public controller claim over the live deployment, nor that atomized input contributors become the deployment's taxpayer by backdoor logic.[S10][S22]

## Hard defaults

1. **Do not mistake upstream importance for controller status.** A training or evaluation input can matter enormously without deciding who governs the deployment.[S20][S21][S83]
2. **Do not let controller groups wash liability into the data or benchmark chain.** Input dependence does not erase the lab, deployer, or beneficiary that chose the system and profits from it.[S20][S21][S34][S35][S83]
3. **Do use input suppliers as narrow licensing, reporting, or reciprocity interfaces where that is the cleanest fit.** Useful records do not by themselves become the morally primary taxpayer.[S21][S22][S27][S83]
4. **Do escalate when the supplier gains durable governance over updates, release gates, or ordinary model policy.** Persistent control over whether the system may train, update, pass, or ship can create real co-controller duties for that layer.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S83]

## Failure modes

- **corpus scapegoating** — the visible data or benchmark supplier is treated as the controller because it is easiest to see, blame, or charge.
- **input-chain laundering** — a controller group routes behavior through affiliated licensors, label vendors, or benchmark shells mainly to blur who really governs the system.
- **score-gate confusion** — evaluation, ranking, or licensing leverage is mistaken for full ordinary deployment control.
- **reciprocity overreach** — bounded public-input claims are inflated into total ownership or total controller liability over every downstream model use.[S10][S16][S17][S20][S21][S22][S34][S35][S83]

## Practical tests

1. Who chose the standing training objective, release strategy, and ordinary deployment purpose?
2. Who can change persistent corpus policy, update approval, or benchmark gates without asking permission elsewhere?
3. Is the outside actor mainly selling inputs or scores, or governing whether the model may ordinarily train, ship, or continue?
4. Does the actor capture mainly royalties and service revenue, or the concentrated rents from the deployment itself?
5. Would the same AI deployment still be privately directed if one licensor, label vendor, dataset curator, or benchmark provider were swapped for another?

If the answers still point to the lab, deployer, platform, or grouped beneficiary, keep controller-side AI taxation there. Training-input suppliers can be taxed ordinarily and used as narrow reporting or reciprocity interfaces without becoming the primary moral home of the deployment.
