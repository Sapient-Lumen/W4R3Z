# 121 — Beneficial ownership, related-party groups, and anti-fragmentation

## One-line thesis

A morally serious tax system should follow **real control, benefit, and ownership** across shells, trusts, subsidiaries, nominee structures, and related parties rather than letting one economic actor split itself into many paper taxpayers.

## Why this matters

The rest of the archive can be defeated if a real controller can multiply or hide itself through entity design. Human wealth can be routed through trusts, family vehicles, foundations, and holding companies. Corporate profit can be sliced across affiliates. AI stacks can be split among captive landlords, data-centre SPVs, cloud subsidiaries, labs, resellers, and deployment shells. When that happens, the moral target is not the paper boundary but the underlying control-and-benefit structure.[S9][S20][S34][S35]

## Design rule

**Aggregate by real controller when fragmentation would defeat the tax base; disaggregate only where separate standing, risk, and benefit are genuinely real.**

## Pattern pack

### 1. Natural-person beneficial ownership first

Where firms, trusts, partnerships, or foundations are involved, tax design should still ask which natural person or genuinely independent collective ultimately owns, controls, benefits, or directs the arrangement.[S34][S35]

### 2. Related-party grouping as a backstop

If multiple entities share common control, circular contracting, captive financing, common beneficial owners, or strategic transfer pricing, the archive prefers group-level anti-avoidance rules, consolidation, or formula backstops over naive entity-by-entity formalism.

### 3. Household anti-avoidance is not the same as household personhood

The archive can aggregate family wealth or related-party transfers for anti-avoidance while still keeping ordinary human earnings liability person-based. Aggregation is a shield against evasion, not a license to erase persons into a single household blob.[S31][S32][S33]

### 4. AI stacks need control maps, not logo maps

For AI especially, administrations should map the real stack:

- site owner or captive landlord,
- utility-contract holder,
- cloud or accelerator provider,
- model developer,
- deployer or integrator,
- and final economic beneficiary.

Do not let a group escape by scattering one chain of control across affiliates that invoice one another.

### 5. Use the lightest rule that still defeats paper splitting

The archive does **not** require universal consolidation of everything. It prefers the smallest grouping rule that prevents obvious fragmentation of the chosen base:

- beneficial-ownership registries and disclosure,
- related-party definitions,
- transfer-pricing backstops,
- controlled-foreign-company rules,
- mandatory disclosure for opaque arrangements,
- and anti-splitting thresholds for small-firm regimes.

### 6. Keep aggregation contestable

Because over-aggregation can also be abusive, taxpayers need notice of the grouping rule invoked, the facts relied on, and a real appeal path.

## Guardrails

- No tax design should assume the invoice issuer is the only relevant actor.
- No trust, shell, nominee, or AI subsidiary should be treated as morally decisive merely because it sits between the state and the real controller.
- Do not collapse genuinely independent small actors into one group without evidence of common control or coordinated avoidance.

## Failure modes

- **Shell multiplication**: one controller splits a tax base into many thin entities.
- **Trust opacity**: dynastic control hides behind nominal trustees or foundations.
- **Stack laundering**: AI groups place facilities, labs, and deployers in separate entities so that no one appears to control the whole system.
- **Threshold camping by subdivision**: a large business masquerades as many small businesses to stay inside favorable regimes.

## Practical tests

1. If the paper entities vanished, would the same people still control the same base and benefits?
2. Would the proposal be easy to defeat by adding subsidiaries, trusts, nominees, or related-party contracts?
3. Is the grouping rule narrow enough to catch evasion without erasing genuinely independent actors?
4. For AI stacks, has the administration named the real controllers of facilities, models, deployment, and profit capture?
