# 163 — Basic refund receipt, rebate delivery, and owed-return access should not be turned into payout skims or fee rails

## One-line thesis

When the state or its remittance anchors already owe money back, **baseline receipt should not depend on paid preparer products, forced fee-bearing payout rails, or other intermediary skims that sell people access to their own refund**.

## Why this matters

A morally serious tax system can fail on the way out as well as on the way in. The archive already says undisputed overcollection should move back quickly, that floor protection should not hide behind claim mazes, and that basic filing or hardship access should not become revenue tools. The next matching rule is about **refund receipt itself**: if the system already owes money, ordinary access to that payment should not require a refund-anticipation product, a forced prepaid rail with routine fees, or another paid intermediary that mainly lives off the return stream.[S39][S64][S65][S66][S124][S125][S126]

Current public materials make the danger concrete. IRS guidance says taxpayers can direct deposit a refund into one, two, or three accounts, and CFPB guidance says refund anticipation checks do **not** make the IRS issue the refund faster even though they typically add fees; CFPB also notes that many prepaid cards carry activation, maintenance, or transaction fees.[S119][S124][S125][S126]

## Design rule

Keep at least one **ordinary, visible, no-skimming return path** open for routine refunds, rebates, and owed returns, and treat optional private acceleration or payout products as truly optional rather than as the practical default.

## Pattern pack

### 1. A clean refund rail should exist without buying a side product

If the administration can already send money back through direct deposit, check, bill credit, or another ordinary public rail, it should not make a paid preparer product, prepaid card package, or merchant-style payout service the realistic doorway to receipt. Convenience products may exist, but the protected baseline is access to what is already owed **without** a second revenue toll.[S39][S65][S66][S119][S124][S125][S126]

### 2. Distinguish optional acceleration from practical coercion

Some households may still choose advance products or special payout services. The archive's concern is not choice as such, but coercive design: where free filing, free ordinary refund routing, or clear direct-deposit options are absent, obscure, delayed, or much harder to use, the "choice" becomes a disguised fee rail. A product is not morally optional when the basic path is hidden or crippled.[S65][S66][S119][S124][S125]

### 3. Small refunds, floor protection, and repair payments should not be eaten by rail fees

A fee that looks modest to a higher-income filer can erase a meaningful share of a small refund, rebate, or local-repair payment. The archive therefore treats fee erosion on the way back out as especially serious when the payment is doing floor work, correcting overwithholding, cushioning regressive consumption taxes, or repairing burden on the weak side.[S3][S4][S31][S39][S64][S65][S125][S126]

### 4. Tax administration should not outsource refund dignity to the fee market

OECD material frames tax administration services around making it easier to understand obligations, report, pay, and interact with the administration. That service logic should extend to the return of money already owed. A state that is digitally sophisticated enough to collect, verify, and net should not rely on private refund-skimming products as the normal bridge back to the payer.[S39][S65][S66][S68][S124][S125]

### 5. Future artificial persons should inherit person-side refund access, not owner-rented payout rails

If a rights-bearing artificial person ever becomes real, the same rule applies on the person side. The subject should not need a former controller's paid wallet, proprietary account, or machine-specific payout service just to receive a refund or rebate already due in its own name. Secure routing and identity proof may still matter; **owner-rented receipt of what is already owed does not**.[S19][S20][S42][S43][S44][S124][S125][S126]

## Guardrails

- Do not require a paid preparer or refund-product intermediary to access a routine refund.
- Do not make a fee-bearing prepaid or proprietary payout rail the only practical default when an ordinary public rail can work.
- Do not let small refunds, subsistence rebates, or local-repair payments be quietly shaved by avoidable receipt fees.
- Do not let private refund products borrow the state's credibility while mainly monetizing delay, confusion, or lack of an ordinary public option.

## Failure modes

- **refund skim**: the payer receives what is legally owed only after avoidable preparer, transfer, card, or withdrawal fees.
- **forced fee rail**: the nominal refund exists, but the practical receipt path pushes people onto a fee-bearing card or payout product.
- **fake acceleration**: a product is sold as speed when it does not actually make the administration issue the refund faster.
- **floor erosion on return**: small or protective payments lose material value on the way back out.
- **owner-rented refund key**: a future artificial person can receive person-side refunds only through a former controller's paid account or gateway.

## Practical tests

1. Can an ordinary filer receive a routine refund or rebate without paying for a side product?
2. Does the basic public path stay visible and usable enough that private alternatives are genuinely optional?
3. Would the fees meaningfully erode a small refund, floor-protecting rebate, or local-repair payment?
4. If a future artificial person existed, could it receive money owed in its own name without a former controller renting back the payout rail?
