# 169 — Basic representative authorization, tax-information access delegation, and revocation should not be turned into proxy-access rents or captive agent gates

## One-line thesis

When a person, small firm, fiduciary subject, or future rights-bearing artificial person needs to **authorize a representative, grant limited tax-information access, inspect who may act or view records, narrow that authority, or revoke it**, at least one baseline authorization lane should stay no-rent, scope-limited, revocable, and non-captive rather than depending on paid account brokers, mandatory preparer portals, owner-controlled proxy keys, or proprietary delegation markets.[S42][S44][S117][S118][S141][S142][S143]

## Why this matters

The archive already blocks review paywalls, record rents, comprehension tolls, payment tolls, certificate rents, and submission tolls. But there is a procedural gate between those protections: the subject must often act through someone else. If the act of naming, limiting, or removing that representative is itself monetized or captured, the right to be heard can collapse into a market for access to one's own procedural voice.[S42][S44][S117][S118][S141][S142][S143]

Representation is not only a professional-service question. It is also an administration question: who may see the file, answer a notice, receive confidential information, speak in an interview, request review, or stop acting. A paid preparer, lawyer, enrolled agent, clinic, family fiduciary, guardian, or substitute representative may sometimes be necessary. But the public authorization mechanism should not itself become a toll gate controlled by the representative, a software vendor, a former owner, or a case-access broker.[S27][S42][S44][S141][S142][S143]

Current public materials show the nonextractive shape. The IRS frames retained representation as part of the Taxpayer Bill of Rights, including access to Low Income Taxpayer Clinics for those who cannot afford representation. Form 2848 is the ordinary power-of-attorney mechanism for authorizing an eligible representative, and IRS authorization guidance keeps multiple submission routes in view rather than treating one proprietary portal as the only doorway.[S141][S142][S143]

## Design rule

Keep at least one **ordinary, subject-controlled, no-rent authorization lane** for granting, limiting, checking, and revoking tax representation or tax-information access. Professional standards, eligibility rules, identity proofing, confidentiality safeguards, and channel security may still be required; selling the baseline proxy switchboard back to the subject may not.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Representation can be paid; authorization should not be captive

A taxpayer may hire paid counsel or a preparer, and a polity may regulate who can practice before the tax authority. That does not justify making the public act of recognizing, limiting, or revoking representation depend on a fee-bearing portal, paid vendor account, or representative-controlled subscription. The service may be private; the civic switch that tells the state who may act should stay public and non-captive.[S42][S44][S141][S142][S143]

### 2. Scope and revocation are part of consent

Authorization should be specific enough to say which tax matters, periods, records, or acts are covered, and easy enough to revoke or narrow when the relationship changes. An overbroad, sticky, or hard-to-cancel authorization is not just a privacy defect; it is a procedural domination risk because the wrong actor may keep speaking for the subject after the subject's interest has diverged.[S40][S42][S44][S142][S143]

### 3. Channel pluralism protects representation from vendor capture

Digital authorization, professional accounts, and secure upload can improve speed, but the baseline lane should not collapse into a single software vendor, identity rail, tax-pro dashboard, or preparer-controlled login. Where a digital fast lane exists, ordinary alternative channels should remain good enough that the subject is not forced into a paid representative just to create, update, or revoke representation.[S65][S68][S141][S142][S143]

### 4. Low-income, limited-capacity, and mediated subjects need usable proxy routes, not proxy markets

The right to representation matters most when the subject lacks bargaining power, technical fluency, language access, capacity, or cash. Clinics, guardians, fiduciaries, and substitute representatives can make direct subjecthood real. They fail that role when the authorization system itself requires specialist payment, owner permission, or inaccessible digital proofing before the subject can get help.[S42][S43][S44][S64][S65][S141]

### 5. Future artificial persons need non-owner authorization, not owner-rented proxy keys

If a rights-bearing artificial person ever becomes real, it should be able to appoint, replace, or revoke a non-conflicted representative in its own person-side tax matters. A former controller may not keep an owner-rented proxy key that determines who can see records, answer notices, or contest liabilities for the recognized subject. Secure delegation may still be needed; owner capture of delegation would undo the regime switch.[S19][S20][S42][S43][S44][S141][S142][S143]

## Guardrails

- Do not attach ordinary representative authorization, tax-information access delegation, or revocation to per-request charges, subscription access, or paid processing keys.
- Do not make a paid preparer, software vendor, or tax-resolution broker the only realistic way to create or revoke representation on time.
- Do not let representative access become overbroad by default; matter, period, and act limits should be visible.
- Do not make revocation slower, less visible, or more expensive than the original authorization.
- Do not confuse the representative's portal or professional account with the subject's own civic identity.
- Do not let any future rights-bearing artificial person appoint or remove tax representatives only through a former controller's paid access layer.

## Failure modes

- **proxy-access rent** — the subject must pay merely to authorize, limit, inspect, or revoke representation.
- **captive agent gate** — a paid intermediary becomes the only practical route into the tax authority's representation system.
- **sticky consent trap** — old authorizations remain active because revocation is obscure, delayed, or fee-bearing.
- **representation laundering** — an owner, household head, deployer, or manager is treated as adequate representative mainly because they control the paperwork channel.
- **portal substitution** — the representative's proprietary account silently replaces the subject's own standing, notice, and review relationship with the state.
- **owner-rented proxy key** — a future artificial person can obtain or remove tax representation only through a former controller's paid delegation switch.

## Practical tests

1. Can the subject authorize, limit, inspect, and revoke representative or tax-information access without a special fee?
2. Does the authorization identify matter, period, information scope, and permitted acts clearly enough to prevent consent drift?
3. Is there a usable non-proprietary or fallback route when a digital or professional-account lane is unavailable?
4. Can low-income, limited-capacity, language-minority, or mediated subjects obtain help without being trapped in a paid proxy market?
5. Can the subject replace or remove a conflicted representative quickly enough to protect review deadlines and confidential records?
6. If the subject were a future rights-bearing artificial person, could it appoint a non-owner representative without renting a former controller's access key?
