# Failure-waterfall and ex ante security ladder

## Question in one sentence

Given the archive's closed rule that ordinary assessed taxes should usually live inside a predictable insolvency order while wages, serious repair, and trust-like collected amounts are not morally interchangeable with routine treasury claims, **what is the smallest workable ladder for deciding when to rely on ordinary ranking, when to grant narrower priority or segregation, when to require ex ante bonds / reserves / insurance, and when to move upward to controller guarantees or regrouping because the operating shell is too thin to bear the downside honestly?**[S15][S16][S17][S19][S20][S34][S35][S47][S48][S49]

## Closed rules invoked

This memo does **not** reopen the archive's constitutional waist. It relies on:

The constitutional question is already settled: **ordinary assessed taxes are not general kings in insolvency; withheld or trust-like amounts are a narrower special case; wages, estate-preserving administration, and serious cleanup or victim-repair claims deserve stronger protection than routine treasury leapfrogging; and where predictable collapse would strand public harms behind an empty shell, the earlier moral answer is usually ex ante security or controller-side responsibility rather than a heroic fight over a bankrupt estate.**[S15][S16][S17][S34][S35][S47][S48][S49]

## Why calibration is still needed

The closed rule fixes direction, but a practical regime still has to decide **how early to move from ex post ranking to ex ante prefunding**. That remains live because failure design can still go wrong in five different ways:

1. **superpriority drift** where routine treasury claims quietly outrank wages, administration, or repair because the state treats all public claims as morally identical,[S47][S48][S49]
2. **trust-tax inflation** where the stronger case for withheld payroll taxes or collected VAT/GST gets stretched into a generic claim that every tax debt deserves priority,[S47][S49]
3. **prefunding delay** where hazardous, infrastructure-heavy, or high-downside sectors are allowed to operate first and only asked for cleanup money after failure,[S15][S16][S17][S47]
4. **thin-shell outsourcing** where the real controller extracts upside upstream while the operating subsidiary, SPV, or contractor tier is left unable to pay workers, tax, or repair costs,[S34][S35][S47][S48]
5. and **AI-stack evasion** where compute, deployment, energy, and facilities sit in separate entities so that no one node appears risky enough to bond even though the controller chain as a whole is running the burden.[S15][S16][S17][S19][S20][S34][S35]

## Small option set

### Option A — broad treasury superpriority

Let routine tax debts outrank most other claims because public finance matters. This is simple for the state and predictably harsh on workers, localities, and victims. It also encourages governments to underuse ex ante bonds or reserves because they expect insolvency priority to clean up later.[S47][S48][S49]

### Option B — ordinary ranking with only narrow trust-tax priority

Keep routine taxes inside the general queue and reserve stronger treatment for withheld or custodial amounts. This is a large improvement over Option A, but by itself it still fails in sectors where the estate will predictably be too small once serious harms arrive.[S47][S48][S49]

### Option C — mixed waterfall with ex ante security default

Keep routine taxes ordinary, protect the narrow trust-tax lane, preserve stronger shields for wages / administration / serious repair, and require ex ante bonds, reserves, insurance, escrow, or decommissioning funds wherever foreseeable downside can outrun the operating estate. Escalate to controller guarantees or regrouping when thin shells or affiliate extraction make the prefunding lane unreliable.[S15][S16][S17][S34][S35][S47][S48][S49]

This is the cleanest general default because it uses insolvency ranking for what ranking is good at while refusing to ask ranking alone to solve predictable collapse-risk design problems.

### Option D — controller-backstop presumption for high-risk stacks

Where a sector is systemically thin-shelled, presume controller guarantees, groupwide reserves, or look-through responsibility from the start. This is justified in some recurring patterns, but taken as the universal default it can overshoot and erase genuinely separate firms too quickly.[S34][S35][S47]

## Provisional recommendation

Adopt a **five-stage practical ladder**:

| Stage | When it should apply | Failure-ordering answer | Main guardrail |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. ordinary tax lane | routine assessed income, corporate, property, or similar tax debts with no trust-like collection feature and no unusual downside gap | **keep within ordinary insolvency ranking** | do not smuggle in treasury superpriority just because the claimant is public |
| 1. custodial / trust-tax lane | withheld payroll taxes, collected VAT/GST, escrowed levies, or similar amounts held on behalf of others | **allow narrow segregation, tracing, or stronger priority** | keep the category tight so it does not swallow all tax debts |
| 2. floor-and-repair shield | unpaid wages, estate-preserving administration, and serious cleanup or victim-repair claims compete with routine treasury recovery | **shield these claims from casual subordination to ordinary taxes** | do not let the treasury collect by turning workers or localities into involuntary fiscal lenders |
| 3. prefunded-risk lane | hazardous, extractive, infrastructure-heavy, or frontier-AI operations where predictable downside can outrun the estate | **require ex ante bonds, reserves, insurance, escrow, or decommissioning funds** | size the security to real downside and reset it as scale, concentration, or hazard changes |
| 4. controller-backstop lane | recurring undercapitalization, upstream extraction, shell splitting, affiliate games, or AI-stack fragmentation make the operating entity structurally unable to bear the burden | **move to controller guarantees, regrouping, look-through, or denial of limited-liability shelter for the relevant downside** | escalate only on real control / extraction facts, not mere corporate complexity |

The archive's provisional setting is therefore **stage 0 for routine tax debts, stage 1 only for genuine custodial collections, stage 2 where wages / administration / repair would otherwise be crowded out, stage 3 as the default answer for foreseeable large downside, and stage 4 when the shell pattern itself shows that prefunding and ordinary ranking are being gamed**.[S15][S16][S17][S34][S35][S47][S48][S49]

For present AI stacks, the practical consequence is direct: **do not wait for a failed model lab, data-centre SPV, or deployment subsidiary to reveal that public-repair or worker claims were unfunded. Where concentrated compute, energy, safety, or decommissioning burdens are predictable, the archive prefers stage-3 security or stage-4 controller backstops over broad post-collapse tax priority and over theatrical respect for shells that were never capitalized to carry the downside.**[S15][S16][S17][S19][S20][S34][S35]

## Anti-patterns

- **Treasury leapfrogging** — ordinary taxes outrank wages, cleanup, or victim-repair by default.[S47][S48][S49]
- **Trust-tax creep** — a narrow custodial category becomes a general fiscal superpriority.[S47][S49]
- **Bond-later politics** — known downside is left unfunded until failure makes bargaining panicked and distributively cruel.[S15][S16][S17][S47]
- **Shell sacrifice** — the operating entity bears the risk on paper while the controller harvests upside elsewhere.[S34][S35][S47][S48]
- **AI-stack partition theater** — compute, facility, deployment, and energy exposure are split across affiliates mainly to keep each node below any serious security requirement.[S15][S16][S17][S34][S35]

## What would change the recommendation

This ladder should be tightened, loosened, or partly replaced if any of the following become clearer:

- insolvency practice develops a reliably narrow and administrable boundary around custodial tax claims without spillover into general superpriority,[S47][S49]
- evidence shows some sectors can fund large downside through ordinary insurance markets or pooled compensation funds more cleanly than through bespoke bonds or reserves,[S47][S48]
- controller-chain disclosure and anti-fragmentation enforcement become strong enough that stage-4 backstops can be used more sparingly because stage-3 prefunding is no longer easily dodged,[S34][S35]
- or AI, energy, or infrastructure projects generate larger or more correlated public-repair risks than first estimated, in which case the archive should move earlier from stage 3 toward stage 4 and away from reliance on ordinary failure ordering.[S15][S16][S17][S34][S35]

## Rev0298 accountability map

- **Primary actor:** the parent, controller, project sponsor, regulator, insurer, surety, or licensing authority that can require prefunding, bond, reserve, insurance, segregation, or controller guarantee before failure.
- **Beneficiary to trace:** the equity holder, lender, parent controller, or sponsor extracting upside while leaving cleanup, wage, trust-tax, victim, or public-backstop claims undersecured.
- **Required packet:** downside risk and failure-probability records, bond/reserve/insurance files, controller guarantees, corporate-group cash sweeps and asset transfers, wage/trust/cleanup/victim claim inventory, and insolvency waterfall ledger.
- **Default move:** require ex ante security for foreseeable downside; do not rely on heroic ex post insolvency priority when an empty shell was predictable.
