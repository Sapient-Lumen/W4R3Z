# Public-input reciprocity and commons-maintenance routing

This note answers a question that sits between AI-trigger design, subsidy discipline, and proceeds routing: **if a controller group builds large private AI rents on top of publicly financed research, open science infrastructure, public compute, public education pipelines, or other shared knowledge systems, when does a special reciprocity contribution become morally justified, and what shape should it take?** The archive's stable answer is narrow. Sometimes yes — but only as a **bounded controller-side commons-maintenance claim** tied to concentrated commercial enclosure of publicly built inputs, never as a universal toll on learning, never as retroactive state ownership of every output, and never as a substitute for ordinary taxation, subsidy cleanup, or direct governance.[S10][S11][S15][S22][S29][S30][S71][S107][S108]

## Core rule

Route these cases in order. For settings on activation thresholds, intensifiers, and review strength, use [`../20-calibration/public-input-reciprocity-contribution-ladder.md`](../20-calibration/public-input-reciprocity-contribution-ladder.md).

1. **Start with ordinary taxation and ordinary subsidy discipline.** Profit tax, minimum-tax floors, land and energy taxation, corrective taxes, procurement rules, grant conditions, and explicit budget review all come first.[S1][S9][S21][S29][S30][S71]
2. **Name a qualifying public-input bundle at the class level.** Use public research, open science infrastructure, public-interest compute, public datasets, public education and training pipelines, or similar shared-capacity bundles — not atomized tracing of each output back to each paper, datum, or line of code.[S10][S22][S107][S108]
3. **Ask whether the actor is a large commercial encloser or bottleneck beneficiary.** Ordinary learning, open publication, research reuse, nonprofit work, and small-firm adoption do not by themselves justify a special levy; concentrated proprietary enclosure at scale is the live trigger.[S10][S11][S22][S107]
4. **Check whether equivalent direct public-return channels already exist.** If the actor is already providing clear, auditable support for public-interest compute, open research maintenance, shared education, or comparable common capacity, credit that before layering on a new contribution.[S22][S71][S107][S108]
5. **Keep the contribution controller-side and aggregate.** Prefer revenue bands, deployment tiers, covered beneficiary classes, or comparable controller-side aggregates. Reject per-token, per-output, per-paper, or per-fragment billing fantasies.[S10][S16][S17][S22][S107]
6. **Protect open and public-interest circulation.** Open, nonprofit, educational, civic, and genuinely small-scale uses should usually stay in the ordinary-tax default or receive very light treatment; the archive taxes concentrated enclosure and rent before it taxes broad capability diffusion.[S11][S22][S107][S108]
7. **Route proceeds visibly to commons maintenance.** Public-interest compute, open science infrastructure, educational access, broad capability diffusion, and burdened local repair are the normal destinations; hidden incumbent subsidy loops are not.[S22][S29][S30][S68][S71][S107][S108]

## Default routing map

| Case | Best answer | Why this usually wins | Main warning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Student, teacher, researcher, ordinary worker, or ordinary household learning from public knowledge or using general-purpose AI tools | ordinary taxation only | public knowledge is supposed to diffuse; ordinary use is not concentrated enclosure | do not turn learning itself into a taxable privilege.[S11][S22][S107] |
| Small firm, startup, civic lab, or nonprofit using public research and off-the-shelf models without visible bottleneck power | ordinary taxation first; special reciprocity usually absent or light | the archive protects experimentation and plural entry unless concentrated enclosure appears | do not impose big-incumbent paperwork on weak actors merely because they use open or public inputs.[S11][S21][S22][S107][S108] |
| Large commercial frontier-model controller building proprietary access on top of public research, public-interest compute, or shared education pipelines | bounded controller-side reciprocity contribution can be justified | concentrated monetization of public inputs can create a fair-return claim beyond ordinary taxes when public return is visibly thin | do not confuse reciprocity with ownership of all outputs or with a universal public-data royalty.[S10][S11][S22][S107][S108] |
| Dominant cloud or distribution gatekeeper drawing on public inputs while also receiving major tax expenditures, discounted infrastructure, or other hidden support | reciprocity plus net-fiscal-stack scrutiny | the public claim may arise both from underreturned public inputs and from hidden negative taxation | do not let the same actor collect subsidy on the way in and call any return obligation anti-innovation on the way out.[S10][S29][S30][S71][S107][S108] |
| Public or nonprofit research infrastructure, open model project, or community-governed open science service | direct budget support, grant conditions, and governance first; reciprocity contribution usually absent | the object here is shared capacity itself, not private enclosure of it | do not tax the commons for being common.[S22][S107][S108] |
| Large controller group already funding open research maintenance, public-interest compute, or broad capability access in verified outside form | credit or narrow any extra reciprocity contribution | reciprocity should target underreturned extraction, not collect twice for the same real public support | do not count marketing, closed ecosystem sponsorship, or affiliate recycling as outside public return.[S22][S71][S107][S108] |

## Four routing rules that matter most

### 1. Reciprocity is about upkeep and fair return, not ownership fantasy

The fact that public institutions, publicly funded science, and open infrastructures helped create today's AI capabilities does **not** imply that the state owns every downstream output or that every model interaction should carry a royalty. The cleaner claim is narrower: when concentrated controllers build durable private rents on top of publicly built inputs, they may owe a bounded contribution to help maintain and widen the shared capacity on which they rely.[S10][S11][S22][S107][S108]

### 2. Class-level bundles beat impossible micro-accounting

The archive rejects per-paper, per-dataset-fragment, or per-output tracing as both morally confused and administratively unserious. The workable object is a qualifying **input bundle** or **beneficiary class**: public science and open research infrastructure, public-interest compute, broad public education and training pipelines, or similar shared systems that can be named and reviewed without pretending that thought can be itemized like a meter reading.[S10][S16][S17][S107][S108]

### 3. Open and public-interest use should not be taxed like enclosure

Open science is meant to reduce knowledge divides, support broad access, and widen capability rather than privatize it behind a single tollbooth.[S107] A reciprocity contribution therefore belongs, if anywhere, on large-scale controller-side enclosure, not on ordinary reuse, nonprofit research, education, civic deployment, or small-firm learning.[S11][S22][S107][S108]

### 4. Credit real direct return before imposing an extra layer

Some covered actors will already fund outside open research infrastructure, public-interest compute access, or comparable shared-capacity institutions. Where that support is real, outside, auditable, and not simply affiliate recycling or brand theater, it should narrow or offset any additional reciprocity contribution. Otherwise the archive would turn reciprocity into duplication instead of fair return.[S22][S71][S107][S108]

## Five anti-patterns

1. **universal toll-road drift** — treating open knowledge as if every ordinary use should be billed;  
2. **retroactive public-ownership theater** — acting as if public contribution gives the state a claim on every downstream output;  
3. **micro-accounting fantasy** — trying to price each output by tracing it through atomized public inputs;  
4. **incumbent subsidy loop** — collecting reciprocity revenue only to channel it back into the same dominant players through opaque support;  
5. **weak-actor overreach** — imposing concentrated-enclosure rules on students, open projects, ordinary researchers, or small firms.[S10][S11][S22][S29][S30][S71][S107][S108]
