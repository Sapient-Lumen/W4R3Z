# Incidence and protected burden routing

This note answers the question that comes **after** the tax base, nominal subject, and first remittance anchor look workable but **before** the proposal can count as morally justified: **who really ends up poorer once prices, wages, rents, contract terms, and bargaining power adjust?** The archive's stable answer is that statutory remittance is only a first pass. A tax passes only if its **final incidence** is acceptably routed toward rents, bottlenecks, concentrated beneficiaries, or correctable harms rather than quietly onto protected workers, low-income households, tenants, or weak suppliers.[S2][S3][S4][S5][S8][S9][S10][S15][S16][S17][S20][S21]

For post-waist operating calibration on when presumed incidence is good enough, when evidence has to be stronger, and when protected-burden risk forces redesign, keep [`../20-calibration/incidence-evidence-and-protected-burden-ladder.md`](../20-calibration/incidence-evidence-and-protected-burden-ladder.md) nearby.

## Core rule

Judge incidence in five steps:

1. **name the legal remitter**,
2. **map the obvious pass-through channels**,
3. **locate bargaining power and inelastic positions**,
4. **identify who is hardest to protect if the burden moves**,
5. and **redesign until the predictable final bearer is morally tolerable**.[S4][S21]

A proposal fails even when its legal payer looks attractive if the predictable final burden lands mainly on protected humans or on actors the tax was supposed to support rather than squeeze.

## Default incidence routing by base or context

| Base or context | Where the burden should mainly stick | Why that routing is usually more defensible | Main warning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary wages and payroll-linked labor taxes | as little as possible below the protected floor; any remaining burden should be visible and paired with strong floor protection | routine labor is usually the least shielded and most basic source of livelihood | labor-heavy extraction often compresses take-home pay, weakens bargaining position, and makes subsistence shoulder public finance.[S2][S4][S21] |
| Broad consumption taxes | broad consumers only after visible rebate, dividend, or service offset; luxury-heavy consumption may carry more | transaction visibility is strong, but raw incidence is often regressive | a technically clean VAT or sales tax still fails if low-income households pay first and compensation arrives late, incompletely, or only by separate claim.[S3][S4][S64][S65][S66] |
| Land, location rent, natural-resource rents | landholders, concession holders, and extractive rent claimants | these bases are unusually inelastic and less likely to flee or compress wages directly | local housing or utility scarcity can still shift burdens toward tenants unless the design distinguishes site rent from improvement or household floor effects.[S5][S6][S21][S25] |
| Inheritance, gifts, and dynastic transfer | large recipients, donor estates, and dynastic wealth chains | these flows are concentrated and not ordinary work effort | sympathy for heirs should not hide the incidence question or turn staged payment into effective exemption.[S8][S18][S21] |
| Monopoly profit, bottleneck rent, and dominant-platform surplus | owners and controllers with true market power | above-normal rents can often bear tax with less pass-through than competitive-margin firms | accounting incidence can mislead when dominant firms squeeze suppliers or workers instead of consumers.[S1][S9][S21][S28] |
| Carbon, congestion, water stress, and other recurring harms | the harm-creating activity and its rent or convenience beneficiary, with floor protection layered in | corrective taxes are strongest when they change conduct and price harm instead of exploiting weak demand | household energy, transport, or heating burdens can become regressive if dividends, transit alternatives, or transition help lag behind.[S6][S7][S15][S39][S64] |
| Frontier AI stacks and automated windfall contexts | controller groups, bottleneck owners, large deployers, major commercial beneficiaries, and excess-profit recipients | current AI burdens are morally routed through control, rent, and benefit, not through model personification | a controller-side levy still fails if it predictably reappears as worker displacement without support, developer squeeze, or price hikes on low-income users.[S9][S10][S15][S16][S17][S20][S21][S22][S67] |
| Open or public-interest research and weak counterparties | should usually be shielded rather than treated as the residual tax absorber | low-margin public-interest actors are poor hidden tax bases | visibility and contractual weakness can make them easy to charge even when they are not the morally intended bearer.[S10][S15][S17][S21] |
| Future rights-bearing artificial person, if threshold is actually crossed | itself under ordinary person-like reciprocity rules, not an owner-side substitute burden | once a system becomes a rights-bearing subject, incidence should follow person-level rules rather than equipment logic | do not retain a substrate surcharge or use the new subject merely to protect legacy owners from burden-bearing.[S18][S19][S20][S42][S44] |

## Routing rules

### 1. Remittance labels never finish the argument

Who files or wires the payment matters for administration, but it does not settle who sacrifices. A corporation, platform, landlord, cloud provider, or AI deployer can be the remitter while workers, tenants, consumers, or dependent suppliers bear much of the cost.[S4][S21]

### 2. Prefer bases whose burden sticks to rents, bottlenecks, and inelastic positions

Land value, monopoly rents, inherited concentration, excess profits, and some corrective bases remain attractive partly because they are harder to shift cleanly onto protected people than ordinary labor or thin-margin trade.[S5][S8][S9][S21]

### 3. Where pass-through is plausible, build protection into the design rather than treating it as a footnote

Use rebates, payroll relief, direct transfers, competition remedies, rent regulation where appropriate, or narrower excess-rent targeting when those moves are needed to stop a nominally well-aimed tax from being borne mainly by weak parties.[S3][S4][S9][S21]

### 4. Ask who has the weakest bargaining position in the chain

If a proposal sits inside a chain with dependent contractors, captive tenants, fragile municipalities, or workers facing low mobility, expect those actors to absorb more burden than glossy statutory diagrams suggest. Bargaining asymmetry is part of incidence, not a separate topic.[S4][S15][S21]

### 5. Check local burden separately from national incidence

A tax can be acceptable in national distribution yet still overload a host locality through utility stress, rent pressure, land assembly, water scarcity, or cleanup exposure. AI facilities, extractive sites, ports, and major logistics nodes often require this second check.[S6][S15][S17][S28][S70]

### 6. Controller-first AI taxation is necessary but not sufficient

For present LLM systems, the archive routes tax liability through facilities, labs, deployers, and large beneficiaries. That is the right starting point, but only a starting point: every serious AI levy still needs a pass-through review for worker displacement, supplier squeeze, user pricing, municipal burden, and beneficiary escape.[S10][S15][S16][S17][S20][S21][S22]

### 7. Visibility is not the same as good incidence

Token levies, app-store surcharges, utility add-ons, or retail-level fees may be easy to meter. They are not justified merely because they are visible. If they fall most heavily on weak downstream users while concentrated owners keep rents intact, the incidence routing is wrong even if compliance looks elegant.[S10][S15][S20][S21]

## Five anti-patterns

1. **Corporate-name moral laundering** — assuming that because a firm remits, labor or households are not the real payer.
2. **Correction on paper only** — relying on rebates or payroll relief that arrive late, are hard to claim, or miss the intended population.
3. **Rent-blind corrective taxation** — pricing a harm without checking whether monopoly, landlord, or utility power lets the wrong actor pass it on.
4. **Local-burden denial** — treating national revenue as success while host communities absorb infrastructure, housing, or cleanup pressure.
5. **Meter-first design** — choosing the most visible taxable event instead of the point where burden-bearing best matches the moral target.[S4][S10][S15][S17][S21][S64][S65][S66]

## Order of preference

When two taxes pursue the same moral target, the archive usually prefers the one whose burden is more likely to remain on:

1. landholders, rentiers, monopolists, and above-normal beneficiaries,
2. harmful activity or scarce-resource users with room to adjust,
3. high-margin controller groups and large commercial beneficiaries,
4. affluent households and dynastic recipients,
5. and only last, ordinary consumption or labor that needs visible floor protection.

This is why the archive remains incidence-aware even when a proposal looks elegant in rates, subject, and administration.
