# 108 — LLMs are not yet tax persons: tax controllers, compute, and deployment

## One-line thesis

Current LLM systems should usually change the map of taxable rents and externalities, not become a new species of direct taxpayer.

## Why this matters

Present AI systems are economically important but morally ambiguous. They are trained, owned, deployed, and monetized by humans and firms. Treating them as direct tax persons too early would smuggle in a false image of agency and could obscure responsibility for harms and profits.[S19][S20]

## Design rule

For current AI, attach liability to the humans and institutions with control, benefit, and capacity to internalize harm.

## Pattern pack

### 1. No model poll tax

Do not tax:

- parameter count as such,
- token throughput as such,
- model existence as such.

Those measures track scale, not moral personhood.

### 2. Tax compute-linked externalities

Use taxes, fees, or levies on:

- electricity and carbon intensity,
- cooling water stress where relevant,
- data-centre congestion,
- dedicated frontier-training capacity in scarce grids,
- and risk-heavy deployment classes.

### 3. Tax AI-enabled rents and windfalls

Capture excess returns where AI sharpens:

- labor displacement without sharing gains,
- platform lock-in,
- privileged data access,
- cloud bottleneck power,
- or superstar profit concentration.[S10][S11][S15]

### 4. Keep rent taxes and harm charges distinct from one-way gross-activity claims

Where special AI taxes are proposed, preserve the distinction between:

- externality charges that can apply even in loss years because harm is real now,
- rent or windfall taxes that should usually wait until rent appears,
- and public-purpose support that should not evaporate just because a young or loss-making project has no current tax liability.

A controller-first regime should not quietly become a gross-throughput trap that ignores genuine downside or pre-profit development phases.[S15][S20][S50][S51]

### 5. Require bonds or insurance for high-risk uses

Especially for critical infrastructure, public administration, health, or frontier deployment with systemic downside.

### 5A. Do not use taxes as blanket permission for tail risk

Where frontier deployment presents catastrophic, irreversible, or otherwise non-compensable risk, taxation should be a sidecar to approval, tiering, red-teaming, incident response, and possible decommissioning or refusal — not a substitute for them.[S17][S53][S54]

### 6. Meter proxies without worshipping them

Electricity, accelerator-hours, revenue bands, deployment tier, and incident class can help price AI-related burdens because they are observable at the controller side. They should not be mistaken for a full accounting of value, cognition, or moral status.[S10][S15][S16]

### 7. Keep liability with controllers

When in doubt, tax and regulate the entity that can stop, redesign, insure, or decommission the system.


## Guardrails

- Do not let firms evade responsibility by saying “the model did it.”
- Do not tax open-source research prototypes like frontier commercial monopolies.
- Keep public-interest and scientific uses on differentiated tracks where justified.


## Failure modes

- **Anthropomorphic leakage**: policy language quietly treats models as civic persons before evidence exists.
- **Responsibility laundering**: owners shift blame to the tool they built.
- **Token fetishism**: visible AI activity is taxed while monopoly and energy use remain undertaxed.
- **Gross-throughput trap**: pre-profit or public-interest activity is taxed as if gross activity itself were the moral target, while real downside is ignored.


## Practical tests

1. Can the taxed actor actually change the risky or harmful behavior?
2. Is the tax tied to externality, rent, or windfall rather than aesthetic panic about AI?
3. Would the design still make sense if the system were just very advanced software?
