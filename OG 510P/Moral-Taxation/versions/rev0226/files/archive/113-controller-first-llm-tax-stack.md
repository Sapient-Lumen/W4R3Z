
# 113 — Controller-first LLM tax stack

## One-line thesis

The ideal present-day LLM tax stack is not a machine poll tax. It is a layered set of taxes, fees, reserves, and rent-capture instruments applied to the **facilities, firms, deployers, and beneficiaries** that control the systems.

## Why this matters

Current LLM systems sit inside concentrated infrastructure markets, consume scarce electricity and often water, create deployment risks, and can generate unusually sharp profit concentration. A morally serious tax regime should hit those levers directly rather than pretending the model itself is already a civic taxpayer.[S9][S10][S11][S15][S20]

## Design rule

For current LLMs, tax the stack in the order **facility -> lab -> deployer -> beneficiary**, while following real control across related affiliates rather than stopping at the first invoice boundary.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Facility layer

Tax or charge:

- electricity and carbon intensity,
- cooling-water stress where material,
- local grid congestion,
- and scarce siting advantages for major data-centre clusters.

### 1A. Ownership map

Before applying the stack, map the real group:

- captive landlord or data-centre SPV,
- utility-contract holder,
- cloud or accelerator affiliate,
- model lab,
- deployment shell or reseller,
- and final profit-capturing parent.

If the same controller sits behind multiple layers, treat shell splitting as an anti-avoidance problem, not as proof of genuine decentralization.[S34][S35]

### 1B. Jurisdiction ladder

- local or metropolitan jurisdictions should claim site-value capture, utility-burden charges, and local adaptation finance where facilities impose place-bound costs,
- national jurisdictions should claim corporate, labor, transfer, and regulatory obligations tied to resident controllers and deployers,
- market jurisdictions should receive destination-style consumption claims and a coordinated share of large cross-border residual profits,
- and treaty or club layers should hold the minimum floors for mobile rents that no one state can police alone.[S3][S9][S15][S28]

### 2. Frontier-training layer

Above a clearly stated compute or capability threshold, require a registry fee or scarce-capacity levy for very large training runs. Where liability is material before monetisation, staged payment, reserve requirements, or bounded carry-forward treatment are preferable to surprise retrospective lump sums. This is not a tax on thought; it is a charge on exceptional use of scarce industrial infrastructure.

### 3. Model-lab layer

Apply ordinary corporate tax, global minimum-tax backstops, and excess-profit capture where AI infrastructure concentration or privileged data access creates outsized rents.[S9][S10]

### 4. Deployment layer

For health, critical infrastructure, public administration, finance, and other high-risk uses, require licensing, incident reserves, bonding, or insurance so that downside is funded before crisis losses are socialised. Where catastrophic or irreversible risk outruns the possibility of ordinary compensation, use taxes only as sidecars to approval, conditional approval, or disapproval rather than as permission prices.[S16][S17][S53][S54]

### 5. Beneficiary layer

When AI materially raises margins while shrinking payroll or bargaining power, trigger an automation dividend or windfall contribution to fund transition insurance, public compute, and shared capability access.[S11][S13][S14]

Where no such margin or rent is yet visible, do not smuggle in a windfall levy through a gross-activity surrogate that ignores genuine downside years. Externality charges may still apply, but rent capture should track rent.[S15][S50][S51]

### 6. Meter what can be metered honestly

When AI-specific taxes are used, prefer observable controller-side proxies such as facility electricity draw, contracted accelerator-hours, deployment tier, or revenue bands. Do not pretend tokens, parameters, or benchmark scores give a morally complete measure of social value or harm.[S10][S15][S16]

### 7. Public-interest differentiation

Keep distinct tracks for open research, nonprofit science, and genuinely public-interest compute. The ideal stack is harder on concentrated commercial bottlenecks than on small open research communities. That harder edge should be evaluated net of local abatements, tax holidays, accelerated depreciation, subsidised utility contracts, and similar hidden negative taxes.[S29][S30]

## Incidence guardrail

A controller-first stack is only morally serious if the burden stays substantially with controllers, bottleneck owners, and large commercial beneficiaries. If the likely result is a wage haircut for workers, a supplier squeeze on small developers, or a broad price increase for low-income users, the stack needs redesign or offset.[S4][S21]

## Equality guardrail

Do not let AI taxation or AI-linked rebates ride on status proxies. Local burdens, repair funds, filing access, and enforcement channels should be checked so that geography, documentation, disability access, or automated scoring do not quietly shift the effective burden toward already marginalised groups. Future artificial persons, if they ever exist, should not face a substrate surcharge merely for being machine-based.

## Guardrails

- Do not impose a universal per-token levy on low-risk inference.
- Do not launch AI-specific charges without published review cadence, capability-drift triggers, and a path to recalibration, conversion, or sunset if the burden map or risk profile changes materially.[S15][S54][S62]
- Do not let firms escape liability by claiming autonomous model behaviour.
- Do not let one AI group duck thresholds or levies by scattering facilities, labs, and deployers across thin affiliates with common control.[S34][S35]
- Do not confuse frontier-scale commercial infrastructure with ordinary open-source experimentation.
- Do not claim to tax AI bottlenecks heavily while canceling the stack with long abatements or subsidised public inputs.[S29][S30]

## Failure modes

- **Token-tax theater**: visible API activity is taxed while grid strain and monopoly rent escape.
- **Responsibility laundering**: deployers push blame onto models.
- **Anti-commons overreach**: open research is taxed like an oligopoly utility.

## Practical tests

1. Does each levy hit an actor that can actually change the risky or extractive behaviour?
2. Are open and public-interest uses clearly separated from concentrated commercial bottlenecks?
3. Would the stack still make sense if the same harms were produced by non-LLM software at similar scale?
4. After abatements, credits, and public-input concessions are netted out, is the stack still actually harder on concentrated commercial bottlenecks than on the public?
