# 140 — Regulators, licensors, auditors, and certifiers do not by themselves become the controller

## One-line thesis

Public rule-setting, licensing, inspection, auditing, certification, and similar oversight can constrain or gate AI deployment without thereby making the state, standards body, auditor, or certifier the morally primary controller. Oversight is not ownership, rent capture, or ordinary operational governance by itself.[S15][S17][S20][S21][S27][S53][S54][S83]

## Why this matters

Once the archive distinguishes users, passive infrastructure, open publication, downstream co-control, and delegated operators, another avoidance path appears: the real controller can say that because deployment needed a license, audit, certification, or public approval, the overseer has now become the true decision-maker and should bear the special AI-side fiscal burden. That would socialize private controller liability whenever the state or an external checker does its job, and it would punish oversight bodies merely for supervising risky activity.[S15][S17][S20][S21][S27][S53][S54][S83]

## Design rule

Treat **oversight powers** and **controller powers** as distinct unless they materially merge.

Ask separately:

- who set the system's standing objectives, budget, and deployment scope,
- who captures the concentrated gains or avoidance value,
- who can change the model, retrieval, tools, safety posture, or rollout design in ordinary operation,
- whether the overseer is mainly testing, approving, or constraining someone else's deployment,
- and whether the supposed overseer has crossed into actually directing live deployment choices rather than setting external guardrails.[S15][S17][S20][S21][S27][S44][S53][S54][S83]

Where the strategic levers and gains remain with the lab, deployer, platform, or controller group, special controller-side AI liability should remain there even if a regulator, licensor, auditor, or certifier can slow, inspect, approve, or stop the activity from outside.

## Boundary ladder

### 1. General rulemaker or standards setter

A legislature, ministry, regulator, or standards body that sets general rules, reporting duties, or approval criteria does **not** thereby become the controller of each regulated deployment. Public rulemaking shapes the legal environment; it does not usually choose the private actor's objectives, rents, or day-to-day operating posture.[S20][S21][S27][S53][S83]

### 2. Licensor or permitter imposing entry conditions

A licensor that grants, denies, suspends, or conditions entry is still ordinarily acting as an overseer rather than as the primary controller. The existence of approval power does not move private deployment liability onto the state just because scaling now depends on permission.[S15][S17][S53][S54]

### 3. Auditor, assessor, or certifier

An outside auditor, conformity assessor, or certifier that tests claims, checks controls, or issues compliance findings does **not** by itself become the controller. External review can be mandatory and consequential without making the reviewer the owner of the system's objectives, benefits, or ordinary operating choices.[S20][S21][S27][S44][S83]

### 4. Public body as actual deployer or principal

The answer changes when the public body is not merely supervising a field but is itself procuring, configuring, operating, or directing the deployment. At that point the public institution is a real deployer or controller for that layer, not because regulation magically created liability, but because the state has entered the chain as principal or operator.[S15][S16][S17][S20][S44][S54]

### 5. Overseer with live operational discretion

An overseer can also become a co-controller if it moves past exogenous guardrails and acquires durable authority over live objectives, persistent instructions, approvals for particular outputs or actions, or ordinary deployment scope. The test is not formal public status; it is whether the actor now materially governs the system from inside the deployment loop.[S15][S16][S17][S20][S44][S54]

## Hard defaults

1. **Do not socialize private controller liability just because approval is required.** Regulated activity can remain privately controlled even when the state may pause, condition, or forbid it.[S15][S17][S53][S54]
2. **Do not confuse exogenous constraint with endogenous governance.** Rules, audits, and certifications sit outside ordinary system operation unless the overseer also starts choosing objectives, tools, or live behavior.[S20][S21][S27][S44][S83]
3. **Do not erase real public-controller cases.** Where a ministry, agency, or public contractor actually procures and governs the system, treat it as a real deployer or co-controller for that layer.[S15][S16][S17][S20][S44]
4. **Keep regulatory fees and controller-side taxes conceptually separate.** Permit fees, supervisory charges, or audit costs may finance oversight, but they do not by themselves absorb or replace controller-side taxes on rents, harms, bottlenecks, or funded downside risk.[S21][S27][S53][S83]

## Failure modes

- **state-liability laundering** — private actors argue that because activity is licensed or supervised, the state has become the real controller.
- **certifier scapegoating** — liability is dumped onto the outside reviewer because it issued or renewed a certificate.
- **oversight nihilism** — any meaningful public gate is misdescribed as control, making effective regulation look fiscally impossible.
- **public-operator blindness** — a government that is actually procuring and directing a deployment hides behind its regulator label even while it functions as a principal.[S15][S17][S20][S27][S44][S53][S54][S83]

## Practical tests

1. Who chose to build, buy, scale, or profit from the deployment?
2. Who can change the model, retrieval, tools, safety posture, or ordinary rollout design?
3. Is the overseer mainly setting guardrails from outside, or governing live operation from inside?
4. Does the overseer capture the concentrated gain, or mainly bear supervisory cost?
5. Would the system still be privately directed if the oversight conditions were met?

If the answers still point to a lab, deployer, platform, or grouped beneficiary, keep controller-side AI taxation there. Oversight can gate or discipline the activity without becoming its primary moral home.
