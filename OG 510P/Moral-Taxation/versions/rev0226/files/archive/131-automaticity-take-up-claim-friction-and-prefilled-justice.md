# 131 — Automaticity, take-up, claim friction, and prefilled justice

## One-line thesis

A morally serious tax system should not hide floor protection, rebates, refunds, or harm repair behind claim mazes. If the state already has enough information to know that relief is due, the default should shift toward **automatic payment, prefilled claiming, one-step confirmation, or other low-friction delivery**, with human and offline fallback rather than paper entitlement that only the organized, digitally fluent, or well-advised actually receive.

## Why this matters

OECD's *Modernising Access to Social Protection* says automatic enrolment using personally identified, linked administrative data can increase the take-up of benefits because it relieves recipients from the burden of applying.[S64] OECD's *Tax Administration 2025* services chapter says tax administrations support compliance by making it easier to understand obligations, report taxable income, and make payments.[S65] The same report's assessment chapter says prefilled returns and electronic channels are reshaping assessment and reducing burdens in high-volume filing and payment processes.[S66]

## Design rule

Where the moral point of the instrument is to **protect the floor, return over-collection, or repair a taxed harm**, relief should usually be easier to receive than liability is to incur.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Automaticity is strongest where the archive protects the floor

Negative-income support, VAT rebates, child-linked supplements, disability-linked tax offsets, and similar floor protections should not depend on intricate annual applications when existing payroll, tax, or benefits data already identify likely eligibility. Prefill, default enrolment, rolling confirmation, or payment-by-default are often morally superior to a statute that looks generous but relies on non-take-up.[S64][S65]

### 2. Put friction on privileges before putting it on necessities

A high-friction approval path may be tolerable for negotiated abatements, special sector incentives, or exceptional relief for powerful actors. It is much less defensible for subsistence protection, routine refunds, or broadly owed rebates. The archive therefore treats claim friction asymmetrically: **more tolerable for carve-outs, less tolerable for floors**.

### 3. Prefill ordinary liability and ordinary relief together

If administrations can prefill wage income, routine deductions, or VAT information, they should also look for ways to prefill ordinary credits, rebates, and refund claims. Otherwise administrative sophistication is used mainly to collect while leaving ordinary people to self-discover the routes to relief.[S65][S66]

### 4. Keep human, offline, and accessible lanes open

Automaticity does not justify a single brittle digital channel. A morally serious system still needs plain-language notices, human help, translation, accessibility support, and offline or assisted routes for people facing disability, unstable housing, migration friction, digital exclusion, or complex family situations.[S64][S65]

### 5. Automation should assist claiming, not erase contestability

Low-friction delivery should not become silent machine paternalism. Prefilled or automatic relief still needs:

- visible explanation,
- simple correction paths,
- opt-out where appropriate,
- appeal for underpayment or exclusion,
- and auditable logic when administrative data trigger or deny the payment.

### 6. Treat non-take-up as evidence of design failure, not private shame

If large numbers of eligible people fail to claim a protected rebate, credit, or refund, the archive treats that as a policy defect rather than as proof that the underlying support was unnecessary. Non-take-up is especially alarming when it is patterned by poverty, care load, disability, migration status, geography, or digital exclusion.[S64][S65]

## Guardrails

- Do not use automaticity as a pretext for coercive data consolidation unrelated to the tax purpose.
- Do not make correction or appeal harder just because the initial payment was automated.
- Do not let prefill silently lock in wrong household assumptions, stale addresses, or outdated care/dependency data.
- Do not confuse low take-up of a burdensome claim process with low moral need.

## Failure modes

- **Paper-floor failure**: the law promises protection that many eligible people never receive.
- **Collection-first automation**: the state automates withholding and enforcement while leaving refunds and rebates manual and obscure.
- **Digital choke point**: relief depends on a single online identity, device, language, or bank-account channel.
- **Silent misclassification**: automatic logic denies support because of stale records, household assumptions, or inaccessible correction paths.
- **Elite-assisted claiming**: sophisticated households and large firms capture relief reliably while weaker claimants face friction, delay, or non-take-up.

## Practical tests

1. If the state already has the data to know relief is probably due, why is a fresh full claim still required?
2. Is floor protection easier to receive than rentier privilege is to secure?
3. Can people correct a bad prefill or missed payment without entering a legal maze?
4. Are translation, disability, offline, and human-help channels real rather than ceremonial?
5. Is non-take-up measured and treated as a review trigger?

## Archive judgment

The archive therefore adds a simple warning: **a rebate or floor that exists only for high-take-up claimants is only partly real**. Justice is not only about the statutory amount; it is also about whether the intended people can actually receive it without heroic procedural effort.
