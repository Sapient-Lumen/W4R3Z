# 138 — Material customization, orchestration, retrieval governance, and tool permissioning can make a downstream actor a real co-controller

## One-line thesis

A downstream actor stops being a mere user when it can materially **shape how the model behaves in the world**. Fine-tuning, durable system scaffolding, retrieval-corpus governance, tool-permission control, approval gates, or safety-policy tuning can make a firm or institution a real **co-controller** for its deployment layer even when upstream labs, hosts, or major beneficiaries still share responsibility.[S10][S15][S16][S17][S19][S20][S21][S27][S34][S35][S83]

## Why this matters

The archive has already closed three adjacent mistakes: treating ordinary users as controllers, treating passive infrastructure as controllers, and treating static publication as endless controller liability. The remaining mistake runs the other way: a downstream wrapper, integrator, or enterprise stack can claim to be “just a user” even while it materially rewrites behavior, chooses what the system may see or do, and decides how risk is governed in practice. That is not ordinary use. It is a real layer of control.[S15][S16][S17][S20][S21][S34][S35][S83]

## Design rule

Ask whether the downstream actor has acquired **durable behavioral discretion** over the deployed system.

That usually points to co-controller treatment when the actor can materially:

- fine-tune, adapter-tune, or otherwise alter persistent model behavior,
- set durable system prompts, routing logic, or agent orchestration that governs consequential behavior at scale,
- choose, curate, or suppress retrieval corpora or policy layers that decide what information the system may rely on,
- grant, revoke, or constrain tool permissions, approval gates, or action authority,
- and define safety thresholds, escalation rules, human-override design, or incident posture for the deployment layer.[S15][S16][S17][S20][S21][S27]

Where those levers are real and ongoing, the actor may remain an ordinary legal person or firm for general taxation while also becoming a proper home for **deployment-layer AI liability** such as reserve duties, risk charges, local-burden charges, or other controller-side obligations.

## Boundary ladder

### 1. Ordinary prompting or session-level steering

Ad hoc prompts, one-off task descriptions, and ordinary interactive use do **not** by themselves create controller status. The behavior change is too shallow, too local, and too reversible to justify special controller-side AI liability.[S19][S20][S21][S83]

### 2. Reusable wrapper without material policy power

A thin UI layer, billing wrapper, or convenience integration that does not materially choose retrieval, tools, approval logic, or safety posture usually remains a reseller or service layer rather than a real controller. It may still owe ordinary tax or narrow remittance duties, but not the full moral load of controller-side AI taxation.[S21][S27][S83]

### 3. Managed orchestration or retrieval governance

Once an actor controls system instructions, agent routing, memory, retrieval scope, ranking, or tool access in a persistent way, it is shaping the real deployed behavior rather than merely describing a task. That normally justifies co-controller treatment for the deployment layer, even if the upstream model provider still shares responsibility for base-model properties and infrastructure burdens.[S15][S16][S17][S20][S21]

### 4. Fine-tuning, adapter tuning, or durable policy shaping

A firm that materially fine-tunes weights, trains adapters, curates reinforcement policy, or builds a standing control layer that predictably changes downstream action should usually be treated as a controller for that layer. It has crossed from use into authored operational behavior.[S15][S16][S17][S19][S20]

### 5. Enterprise or institutional deployment with delegated authority

Where a downstream institution lets the system screen claims, move money, approve content, triage people, trigger purchases, or act through tools under institutional authority, the institution cannot hide behind the upstream API contract. It becomes a real co-controller because it has chosen the behavioral envelope, action rights, and incident-governance structure under which the system operates.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S27]

## Hard defaults

1. **Do not confuse customization with personhood.** Becoming a co-controller does not make the model a tax person; it identifies which human or firm layer should carry deployment-side liability.[S19][S20]
2. **Do not let “just a wrapper” language erase durable policy control.** Retrieval gates, tool permissions, system scaffolds, and approval logic can matter more morally than raw weight ownership.[S15][S16][S17][S20]
3. **Do not erase upstream duties either.** A downstream co-controller does not cancel base-model, infrastructure, hub, or major-beneficiary obligations where those remain real.[S10][S15][S21][S34][S35]
4. **Follow the behavior-shaping layer that could have chosen differently.** The decisive question is not who touched the interface last, but who had meaningful power to alter the deployed system’s durable conduct or risk posture.[S20][S21][S83]

## Failure modes

- **wrapper laundering** — a firm claims to be “just an integrator” while it actually governs prompts, retrieval, tools, and approval rules at scale.
- **policy invisibility** — tax analysis sees only the base model and misses the downstream layer that made the harmful or gain-producing behavior operational.
- **double erasure** — upstream actors disclaim responsibility because the downstream stack changed behavior, while the downstream stack disclaims responsibility because it did not own the base model.
- **session-theater overreach** — every ordinary prompt or one-off instruction is misread as controller conduct, which would collapse the archive back into user taxation.[S19][S20][S21][S34][S35][S83]

## Practical tests

1. Who could materially change what the system was allowed to know, do, or trigger?
2. Who could alter the persistent instructions, retrieval scope, tools, or approval gates?
3. Who set the safety thresholds, escalation path, or human-override design for this deployment?
4. Who could slow, suspend, or redesign the risky behavior without needing someone else’s permission?
5. Is the actor’s discretion durable enough that a tax or reserve duty at that layer would actually change behavior?

If the answer is mostly “yes,” the actor is probably more than a user or reseller and should be treated as a real co-controller for that deployment layer.
