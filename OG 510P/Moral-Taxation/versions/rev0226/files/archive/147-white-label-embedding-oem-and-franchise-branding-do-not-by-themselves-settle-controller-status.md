# White-label embedding, OEM distribution, and franchise branding do not by themselves settle controller status

When an AI capability is embedded inside another product, sold white-label, distributed as an OEM component, or surfaced through franchise-style branding, do **not** let the customer-facing label decide who the morally primary controller is. Branding, packaging, and contract frontage can matter for remittance, customer remedies, and governed workflow duties, but controller status still follows durable governance over model updates, safety posture, permissions, continuation, incident handling, and residual upside.[S10][S16][S17][S20][S21][S27][S34][S35][S83]

## Why this note exists

The archive already distinguishes ordinary users, thin resellers, channels, tools, input suppliers, and successor chains. What remained too implicit was the branded middle case: one firm owns the customer relationship or interface while another still governs the underlying model, updates, or safety defaults. White-label and OEM packaging can otherwise be used either to overstate downstream control or to make upstream control disappear behind a prettier label.[S10][S16][S17][S20][S21][S27][S34][S35][S83]

## Hard defaults

1. **Branding is not governance.** A downstream seller does not become the sole controller merely because the customer sees its logo, invoice, or dashboard first.[S20][S21][S27][S83]
2. **Upstream maintenance does not disappear behind an OEM shell.** If an upstream actor still governs weights, updates, safety defaults, or ordinary continuation, upstream controller duties remain real.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S83]
3. **Customer-facing integration can still create downstream co-controller duties.** A branded layer that governs workflow, approvals, tool access, escalation, support promises, or incident handling may become a real co-controller for that layer even if it is not the only controller in the stack.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S27][S83]
4. **Follow the franchise principal when local outlets lack real discretion.** A network of branded vendors or local operators should not multiply into many independent controllers when the standing model, policy, and updates remain centrally governed.[S20][S21][S27][S34][S35][S83]

## Practical tests

1. Who controls the underlying model weights, updates, rollback, or safety posture?
2. Who sets the customer-facing workflow, approval rules, tool permissions, or escalation path?
3. Who can continue, pause, or redesign the service without asking permission elsewhere?
4. Who holds the primary incident, support, and remediation relationship with users?
5. Who captures the residual upside after ordinary distribution or service fees are paid?

If the answers split by layer, keep one primary controller for the highest strategic layer and assign only narrow co-controller duties to the branded layer it actually governs.[S16][S17][S20][S21][S34][S35][S83]

## Failure modes

- **brand laundering** — upstream control is hidden behind a downstream logo so the real maintainer seems to disappear.
- **interface capture illusion** — the customer-facing seller is treated as the whole controller story even though a different actor governs updates, defaults, or continuation.
- **franchise multiplication** — centrally governed branded outlets are misread as many independent controller chains.
- **OEM invisibility** — persistent upstream update or safety governance is ignored because the component ships inside another product.[S20][S21][S27][S34][S35][S83]
