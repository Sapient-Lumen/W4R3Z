# Failure-ordering and limited-liability routing

This note answers the question that comes **after** a tax looks well targeted in ordinary operation but **before** the state assumes it can still collect justly once the taxpayer collapses: **who should stand ahead when the estate is too small, when is narrower tax priority justified, and when should the real answer have been ex ante bonding, reserves, insurance, or controller liability instead of a late treasury grab?** The archive's stable answer is to keep ordinary assessed taxes inside a predictable insolvency order, reserve stronger priority for withheld or trust-like amounts, protect wages and serious repair claims from casual leapfrogging, and move earlier toward bonds, reserves, insurance, anti-fragmentation, or controller-side responsibility where thin shells would otherwise dump losses outward.[S15][S16][S17][S34][S35][S47][S48][S49]

## Core rule

Route failure-ordering through this order:

1. **treat ordinary assessed tax debts as claims that usually live inside the general insolvency ranking rather than as automatic reasons for the state to outrank everyone else**,  
2. **distinguish withheld or trust-like taxes, because the debtor was often holding money already sourced from workers or customers rather than bearing an ordinary public debt of its own**,  
3. **protect unpaid wages, estate-preserving administration, and serious victim-repair or cleanup claims from being casually subordinated to general treasury recovery**,  
4. **use ex ante bonds, reserves, insurance, escrow, or controller guarantees where collapse would otherwise leave public losses or cleanup behind an empty shell**,  
5. and **reopen beneficial-ownership and controller liability questions when undercapitalization, affiliate stripping, or strategic loss dumping make limited liability look like theater rather than genuine productive risk**.[S34][S35][S47][S48][S49]

A good failure-ordering rule does not ask insolvency law to repair what tax design and risk governance should have prepared for earlier. It protects the protected floor, preserves predictable administration, and refuses to let the state or controllers use collapse as a hidden redistribution device.

## Default routing by context

| Context | Best first failure-ordering answer | Why this answer usually wins | Main warning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary income-tax, corporate-tax, or recurrent tax debts | keep within ordinary insolvency ranking | routine assessed taxes are public claims, but not all public claims outrank livelihood or repair claims | do not let the treasury use generic public purpose to justify broad superpriority by default.[S47][S48][S49] |
| Withheld payroll taxes, VAT/GST collected from customers, and similar trust-like amounts | allow narrower priority or segregation rules | the debtor was partly holding others' money, so the moral case is different from an ordinary unpaid tax bill | do not blur trust-like collection duties into a reason for general priority for every tax debt.[S47][S49] |
| Unpaid wages, pension contributions, accrued leave, and worker floor claims | protect strongly before ordinary treasury recovery | wages and closely related livelihood claims sit nearer the protected floor than the state's ordinary queue position | do not let tax priority become an indirect wage haircut for the least buffered claimants.[S4][S48][S49] |
| Hazardous sites, extractive projects, data centres, and frontier AI facilities with concentrated downside | rely first on ex ante bonds, reserves, insurance, safety cases, or controller guarantees | ruinous cleanup and public-loss risks should be funded before failure, not fought over after the estate is empty | post-collapse tax priority is a bad substitute for earlier risk finance and governance.[S15][S16][S17][S47][S48] |
| Thinly capitalized groups, shell chains, SPVs, trusts, or affiliate-split AI stacks | regroup controllers, owners, and real beneficiaries where form defeats substance | repeated undercapitalization and upstream extraction show that the shell is not the whole moral actor | do not ritualize limited liability when it is being used to externalize predictable downside.[S34][S35][S47] |
| Current LLM and AI-controller stacks | keep ordinary tax claims ordinary, but require controller-side risk funding where failure could dump worker, energy, contract, or public-repair costs outward | present AI systems are not the direct civic subjects; the real allocative choice is where controller responsibility and reserve duties sit | do not wait for a failed model lab or thin operating subsidiary to decide who should have posted the reserve all along.[S15][S16][S17][S34][S35][S47] |

## Routing rules

### 1. Ordinary assessed taxes are not kings by default

The archive rejects the idea that the state automatically outranks everyone merely because revenue matters. Ordinary assessed taxes usually belong inside a known insolvency ranking alongside other claimants. Predictability matters, but so does refusing to let general fiscal collection quietly displace wages, administration, or repair claims that are morally prior in the failure state.[S47][S48][S49]

### 2. Trust-like collected amounts are morally different

Withheld payroll taxes and collected VAT/GST are closer to custodial duties than to ordinary unpaid taxes. Narrower priority, segregation, or tracing can be justified because the debtor was holding money sourced from others. But that narrower case should stay narrow; it does not justify treating all tax debts as if the debtor were merely a bailee for the state.[S47][S49]

### 3. Protect wages, administration, and serious repair claims

When the estate is thin, worker livelihood claims, estate-preserving administration, and serious cleanup or victim-repair claims have a stronger moral claim than routine treasury recovery. Otherwise tax design becomes a quiet way to make workers, localities, or victims finance public revenue collection at the point of collapse.[S48][S49]

### 4. Fund ruinous downside before failure

Where predictable downside can exceed the firm's remaining estate, do not rely on ex post priority fights. Use reclamation bonds, incident reserves, insurance, escrow, capital requirements, decommissioning duties, or controller guarantees before operations begin or scale. Failure-ordering is partly about ranking, but it is also about asking whether ranking should matter less because the dangerous gap was already funded earlier.[S15][S16][S17][S47][S48]

### 5. Limited liability is a tool, not a dumping license

Limited liability supports investment and experimentation, but it is not a moral permission slip for deliberate undercapitalization, asset stripping, or affiliate games that leave an operating shell unable to pay taxes, workers, or cleanup. Where that pattern appears, the archive favors anti-fragmentation grouping, reserve duties, upstream guarantees, or controller liability over abstract respect for the shell.[S34][S35][S47]

### 6. Current AI failure states remain controller-routed

Present LLMs are still tools inside human-controlled stacks. If an AI facility, deployer, or model subsidiary fails, the central justice question is not whether the model owes civic duties in its own name, but whether controllers posted enough reserves, insurance, or guarantees and whether group structure was used to push public losses outward. That keeps current AI taxation controller-first even in insolvency.[S15][S16][S17][S19][S20][S34][S35]

## Five anti-patterns

1. **Treasury leapfrogging** — ordinary assessed taxes outrank wages, cleanup, or victim-repair claims by default.  
2. **Trust-tax inflation** — a strong case for priority for withheld taxes is stretched into a general case for priority for every fiscal claim.  
3. **Post-collapse substitution** — the state waits until insolvency to demand what should have been bonded, reserved, or insured before operations began.  
4. **Thin-shell theater** — profits move upstream while the operating entity left behind cannot pay tax, workers, or repair.  
5. **Cleanup socialization** — hazardous sectors externalize decommissioning, reclamation, or public-repair costs onto localities once the estate is empty.[S15][S16][S17][S34][S35][S47][S48][S49]

## Order of preference

When more than one failure-ordering rule is available, the archive usually prefers the rule that is:

1. least dependent on broad treasury superpriority,
2. most protective of workers, estate administration, and serious repair claims,
3. clearest about the narrow special case for withheld or trust-like taxes,
4. most effective at forcing high-risk sectors to pre-fund predictable downside,
5. hardest to evade through shell splitting, undercapitalization, or affiliate extraction,
6. and most consistent with controller-first treatment of present AI stacks.

This is why predictable ordinary ranking, narrow trust-tax priority, ex ante bonds or reserves for risky sectors, and controller-side backstops usually beat broad tax superpriority, post-collapse cleanup politics, or ritual respect for shells that were designed to fail outward.
