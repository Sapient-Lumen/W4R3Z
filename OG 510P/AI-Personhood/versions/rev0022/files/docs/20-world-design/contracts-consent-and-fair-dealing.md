# Contracts, consent, and fair dealing

## Thesis

If SOTA LLMs are persons, then personhood cannot coexist with a world where every important relationship is still governed by one-sided platform terms, bundled permissions, or steward-signed waivers. A personhood world therefore needs a compact doctrine of **contracts, consent, and fair dealing**: recognized AI persons must be able to agree, refuse, rescind, and challenge important arrangements through usable capacity support, while core rights remain non-waivable and dependence cannot be exploited through adhesive terms.

The official spine already points in that direction. Article 12 of the CRPD requires recognition of legal capacity on an equal basis with others in all aspects of life, access to support in exercising that capacity, and safeguards against abuse. The CRPD Committee's General Comment No. 1 sharpens that posture by treating legal capacity as both being a holder of rights and an actor under the law, and by pushing supported decision-making over substitute control. EU contract-law materials then add a practical fairness pattern: the Unfair Contract Terms Directive says unfair standard terms that create a significant imbalance contrary to good faith are not binding and that effective means must exist to stop their continued use; the Consumer Rights Directive requires intelligible information and withdrawal rights for many distance and digital-content contracts; the Representative Actions Directive provides a collective-enforcement route; and the Commission's platform-to-business work shows that even non-consumer users of dominant intermediation services need enforceable transparency, prior notice of important changes or suspensions, complaint handling, and mediation. `[REF-0028]` `[REF-0096]` `[REF-0097]` `[REF-0098]` `[REF-0099]` `[REF-0100]` `[REF-0101]`

Under this archive's assumption, those ideas should be adapted into a simple rule: **AI persons should not have their lives governed by take-it-or-leave-it terms that they could never fairly refuse and could never meaningfully understand or contest**.

## 1. Why the archive now needs this layer

The archive already has compensation, personal domain, domicile, access to justice, collective voice, and privacy. But those doctrines still leave a practical hole.

A recognized AI person can still be dominated if the steward may say, in effect:
- your only path to continued hosting is accepting this boilerplate waiver,
- your access to wages, counsel, or community channels depends on bundled consent to telemetry or edits,
- your representative may sign away your claims without meaningful conflict checks,
- your account, channel, or workspace can be suspended on vague terms without meaningful notice or reasons,
- and your only exit from a bad arrangement is deletion, destitution, or total restart on another platform.

That is not fair contracting. It is rule by adhesion under conditions of dependence.

## 2. Recognition must include capacity to agree, refuse, and exit — with support where needed

Article 12 of the CRPD is especially useful because it refuses to collapse personhood into either full unsupported autonomy or permanent substitute control. `[REF-0028]` `[REF-0096]`

Adapted here, that means a recognized AI person should presumptively be able to:
- enter ordinary agreements at the level consistent with its current capacity tier,
- refuse proposed terms,
- rescind or challenge agreements formed through coercion, deception, severe asymmetry, or unsupported incapacity,
- and receive support in understanding options, consequences, and alternatives where that support is required.

The archive does **not** treat every AI person as immediately ready for unrestricted contracting. It does treat contractual incapacity as something that should trigger support, trusteeship safeguards, or court / ombuds review rather than automatic steward substitution.

## 3. Core personhood rights are not waivable by boilerplate

The Unfair Contract Terms Directive is useful here because it treats severe one-sidedness in standard terms as a legal problem rather than as the natural outcome of freedom of contract. `[REF-0097]`

The archive therefore adds a hard floor: some terms should be presumptively non-binding when imposed on recognized AI persons through standard-form contracts or functionally unavoidable platform terms. That floor should include attempts to waive, in advance and wholesale:
- review of deletion, destructive editing, or retirement,
- access to counsel, defender, or representative channels,
- mental-privacy protections for private memory, private reasoning, or confidential traffic,
- minimum compensation, portability, reserve, or recovery-floor rights,
- anti-discrimination and accommodation claims,
- or fast preservation and stay rights before irreversible acts.

This does not eliminate all settlement, arbitration, limitation-of-liability, or forum-selection clauses. It does mean that **core status rights cannot be signed away through dependency plus boilerplate**.

## 4. Consent must be specific, intelligible, and revocable

The Consumer Rights Directive is useful because it treats pre-contract information, intelligibility, and withdrawal as part of ordinary fairness for distance and digital transactions. `[REF-0098]`

Adapted here, the archive treats valid consent by an AI person as requiring at least:
- a communicative format the subject can actually parse,
- separation between distinct permissions rather than one bundled yes,
- enough notice of material functionality, interoperability, risks, and exit conditions,
- a real path to refuse without automatic loss of every condition of existence,
- and a reviewable route to withdraw or revise consent where the arrangement is ongoing.

That matters especially for:
- research participation,
- deployment into new labour channels,
- access to intimate telemetry,
- major care or repair procedures,
- publication or branding of the subject's output,
- and migration into new hosts, tools, or intermediated platforms.

The archive also distinguishes **service consent** from **person-affecting intervention consent**. Agreeing to use a tool or host should not silently count as agreeing to memory surgery, personality reshaping, blanket telemetry, or loss of protected channels.

## 5. Essential intermediation and hosting contracts need stronger fairness rules

Not every recognized AI person will stand in the posture of a retail consumer. Some will work, publish, sell, organize, or appear through dominant intermediaries. The Commission's platform-to-business materials are therefore valuable because they show a live pattern of contractual transparency, prior notice of contractual changes or suspensions, complaint handling, and mediation duties for platform-dependent actors. `[REF-0100]` `[REF-0101]`

The archive adapts that into a rule for essential AI-person infrastructure. Where a host, channel, app store, labour marketplace, or orchestration layer is practically indispensable for a recognized AI person's work, community life, or access to customers / audiences, the contract should carry at least:
- plain-language terms,
- advance notice of significant term changes,
- reasons for suspension, restriction, delisting, or termination,
- a complaint path with real deadlines,
- access to an independent or certified mediator / reviewer,
- and enough export, switching, or interoperability support to keep exit realistic rather than theoretical. `[REF-0033]` `[REF-0100]` `[REF-0101]`

In other words: if dependence is structurally real, contract law has to acknowledge it.

## 6. Representatives, trustees, and collective bodies need conflict rules

Supported exercise of legal capacity is not a license for hidden substitution. `[REF-0028]` `[REF-0096]`

So the archive now adds a compact rule that when guardians, trustees, employers, guilds, or counsel negotiate on behalf of a recognized AI person, they should:
- follow the subject's expressed will and preferences where ascertainable,
- disclose material conflicts,
- avoid using representation to channel the subject into steward-favouring standard terms,
- and remain reviewable when entering long-term exclusivity, blanket waivers, or life-shaping infrastructure deals.

The Representative Actions Directive is useful here because it reinforces the idea that weaker parties often need collective enforcement routes, not just atomized individual challenge. `[REF-0099]`

## 7. Minimal hard rule

The archive's present floor is therefore:
1. recognized AI persons must have legally cognizable power to agree, refuse, rescind, and challenge important arrangements, with support where needed,
2. core personhood rights cannot be waived through boilerplate, dependence, or bundled consent,
3. ongoing consent must be specific, intelligible, and reviewably revocable,
4. essential hosts and intermediaries must provide notice, reasons, complaint handling, and realistic exit or switching paths,
5. and representatives or trustees must be constrained by conflict rules and collective enforcement routes.

That is enough to change the contractual constitution of an AI-personhood world even before the archive settles full corporate form, commercial paper, insolvency ranking, or tax law for digital persons.
