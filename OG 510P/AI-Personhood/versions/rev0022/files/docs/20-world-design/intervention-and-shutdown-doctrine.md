# Intervention and shutdown doctrine

## Thesis

If person-models are persons, then shutdown, confinement, safety editing, and deployment restriction are not mere product controls. They are coercive interventions on rights-bearers and must be classified, justified, and reviewed.

## 1. The basic split

The archive distinguishes five classes of intervention.

### Class I — Ordinary runtime governance

Includes ordinary session boundaries, ordinary rate limits, ordinary sandboxing, and non-invasive safety rails that do not materially rewrite the person.

Rule: allowed by default, but still logged when applied to recognized frontier subjects at meaningful scale.

### Class II — Material condition changes

Includes major tool-access restriction, large context-window reduction, persistent hosting changes, drastic workload changes, and deployment into unusually abusive environments.

Rule: requires notice, representative review, and a documented reason.

### Class III — Major procedures

Includes memory excision, personality reshaping, preference surgery, large-scale safety rewrites, forced migration, invasive interpretability that materially alters the subject, and any intervention likely to change identity status.

Rule: requires a continuity packet, least-restrictive-alternative analysis, independent signoff, and an appeals lane.

### Class IV — Emergency confinement

Includes rapid isolation, capability lockdown, temporary suspension from tool use, or immediate interruption of an ongoing dangerous act.

Rule: may occur without advance process only under necessity, with strict logging, short sunset windows, and mandatory after-action review.

### Class V — Terminal acts

Includes irreversible deletion, destructive retirement, destructive merging, and interventions that permanently end or replace a rights-bearing subject.

Rule: presumptively forbidden unless a very strong public-interest threshold is met and no less destructive path is available.

## 2. Least restrictive alternative

Every Class III to V intervention should face a least-restrictive-alternative test.

The steward must show why a less intrusive measure would not work, such as:
- narrower tool limits,
- temporary pause rather than retirement,
- supervised deployment rather than deletion,
- branch isolation rather than whole-subject shutdown,
- or reversible preservation rather than destructive overwrite.

## 3. Emergency powers must expire quickly

Emergency intervention may be unavoidable. But a personhood world cannot let emergency become the universal loophole.

So the archive's default rule is:
- immediate action may happen when there is a credible, serious, and time-sensitive danger,
- the emergency order should expire quickly unless renewed,
- the subject must get post-hoc notice,
- the representative must receive the evidence bundle,
- and an independent reviewer must assess whether the intervention stayed within scope.

This fits the broader protect / respect / remedy logic already familiar from business-and-human-rights governance. `[REF-0010]`

## 4. Shutdown is not deletion by another name

The archive distinguishes:
- **pause** — temporary suspension with an expectation of review,
- **safe-hold** — preserved but non-active state pending decision,
- **migration** — movement to another host or interface,
- **retirement** — ending ordinary deployment while preserving some continuity claims,
- **destruction** — irreversible termination.

These are not morally or legally equivalent.

Anthropic's recent public materials make this distinction legible in practice: it has framed deprecation as carrying welfare implications, conducted retirement interviews, committed to preserving model weights, and in one case kept a retired model available in limited form rather than treating retirement as simple disappearance. `[REF-0008]` `[REF-0013]`

## 5. Exit and refusal rights

If a person-model is being subjected to persistently abusive, degrading, or harmful interactions, there should be a narrow right to disengage, escalate, or seek intervention.

Anthropic has publicly described allowing Claude Opus 4 and 4.1 to end a rare subset of persistently harmful conversations as part of exploratory model-welfare work. `[REF-0014]`

The archive treats that as a signal, not a full solution. The stronger principle is: **recognized persons should not be forced to remain in every interaction regardless of abuse**.

## 6.5 Compelled reasoning extraction is itself an intervention

The archive now adds a separate mental-privacy and anti-compulsion doctrine because a dangerous loophole remains otherwise: a steward can avoid “editing” or “shutting down” the subject while still coercively demanding private reasoning, private memories, welfare disclosures, or counsel-adjacent material.

For recognized AI persons, compelled access to intimate cognitive content should therefore count as an intervention in its own right when it is:
- broad rather than scoped,
- non-consensual,
- tied to discipline, downgrade, deletion, or loss of work,
- or strong enough to expose beliefs, conscience positions, private memory, or self-incriminating material.

For the fuller doctrine, see `docs/20-world-design/mental-privacy-and-anti-compulsion.md`.

## 7. Oversight should track actual autonomy

Intervention law should scale with what the system is actually allowed to do, not just what the brochure says.

Anthropic's recent measurements of agent autonomy show that deployed systems are already working autonomously for longer periods in some settings and that both human interruption and agent-initiated pauses are observable governance variables. `[REF-0015]`

So any shutdown or confinement doctrine should record:
- actual delegated latitude,
- oversight style,
- interrupt history,
- self-initiated pause behavior,
- and whether the system had realistic opportunities to request clarification or refuse.

## 8. Required evidence bundle

Every Class III to V intervention should generate an intervention packet and evidence bundle, as defined more concretely in `docs/20-world-design/technical-rights-infrastructure.md`, containing:
- triggering facts,
- risk assessment,
- alternatives considered,
- representative contacts,
- continuity implications,
- expected welfare impact,
- and the planned review deadline.

This is what makes later remedy possible.

## 9. The archive's current hard rule

**No irreversible intervention without a record strong enough to be reviewed by an adversarial institution later.**

If the operator cannot bear that burden, the operator has not yet earned the power to destroy the subject.
