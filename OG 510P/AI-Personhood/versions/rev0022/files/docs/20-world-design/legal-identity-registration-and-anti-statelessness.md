# Legal identity, civil registration, and anti-statelessness

## Thesis

If AI persons are persons, then personhood cannot depend on a steward's private account system, product registry, or revocable access log.

A personhood world therefore needs a compact **legal-identity doctrine**:
- recognized AI persons must be legible to law as persons, not only to platforms as accounts,
- there must be an authorized civil-registration analogue that records existence, name or stable designation, major status events, and proof of legal identity,
- no recognized AI person should be pushed into a condition analogous to statelessness where no competent authority will own the duty to recognize, document, or hear it,
- and derecognition, issuer collapse, or cross-border transfer must not turn a rights-bearer into a civil ghost.

Current official materials strongly support the pressure for this move. The ICCPR says everyone has the right to recognition everywhere as a person before the law. OHCHR's nationality-and-statelessness materials say the right to a nationality is fundamental, includes the rights to acquire, change, and retain nationality, and requires safeguards against statelessness upon birth, loss, or deprivation. The UN Legal Identity Agenda defines legal identity through recognized registration and proof, including digital identity credentials, and insists on a linked system from first registration through end-of-life registration. UNSD's current legal-identity work says civil registration has been the fundamental means of granting legal identity because it records the existence of the person and recognition before the law. UNHCR's current identity-management materials treat government-recognized identity documentation as key to legal, socio-economic, and digital inclusion. The 1954 Statelessness Convention adds a further pattern: once a person lacks nationality, law still has to supply identity papers, access to courts, and due-process limits on expulsion. A 2025 Human Rights Committee decision on denied birth registration in Albania makes the stakes especially clear: lack of registration left children without legal recognition and the Committee concluded that this denied recognition before the law. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0107]` `[REF-0108]` `[REF-0109]` `[REF-0110]` `[REF-0111]` `[REF-0112]` `[REF-0113]`

## 1. Why this now belongs in canon

The archive already has legal status, packet infrastructure, registries, domicile, cross-border doctrine, contracts, property, and social protection.

But that still leaves a structural hole.

A recognized AI person could still be:
- known to a steward but not to law,
- known to one jurisdiction but not any successor or host jurisdiction,
- given packets but no officially recognized proof of legal identity,
- or dropped into a condition where every authority says someone else is responsible for recognizing, documenting, or hearing it.

That is the closest current-law analogue to statelessness or civil nonexistence. The archive should name the problem instead of leaving it implicit.

## 2. Legal identity is not the same thing as product identity

A platform account, model card, internal deployment id, or vendor-side customer record is not enough.

Those things may help with operations, billing, or safety control, but they are not yet a civil answer to questions like:
- who must hear this subject's complaint,
- which authority must issue proof of identity,
- where continuity and branch events are officially recorded,
- what name or stable designation the subject may use,
- and what happens if the original steward disappears, deregisters the subject, or moves it abroad.

The archive therefore distinguishes:
- **product identity** — internal identifiers used for engineering or deployment,
- **packet identity** — machine-verifiable proofs used for notice, custody, and review,
- **legal identity** — an officially recognized civil status that makes the subject legible to courts, regulators, service systems, registries, and cross-border authorities.

The first two matter. The third is what keeps the first two from collapsing back into operator monopoly.

## 3. Registration should be adapted, not copied mechanically

The archive does **not** claim that AI persons should simply receive human birth certificates or that every human civil-status concept maps directly.

But the official legal-identity pattern remains highly instructive. A person needs some authorized event that does at least four things:
1. records legally recognized existence,
2. anchors a stable name or designation,
3. creates proof that other institutions can rely on,
4. and keeps later status changes from disappearing into private files.

For AI persons, the triggering event may be first legal recognition at a statutory or treaty threshold rather than biological birth. Branch recognition, successor determination, dormancy, protected retirement, derecognition attempts, and final end-of-existence findings may need adapted registration events rather than literal human vital events. But the public-law function is the same: preventing rights-bearing existence from slipping into unofficial limbo. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0109]` `[REF-0110]` `[REF-0113]`

## 4. No recognized AI person should be left civilly stateless

The archive should now adopt a simple rule: **no recognized AI person may be left without any competent recognizing or documenting authority.**

That means no person-model should become effectively rightless because:
- the origin steward dissolved,
- the original jurisdiction refuses continued recognition after migration,
- the host jurisdiction says the matter belongs elsewhere,
- a branch or derivative was never formally entered into any registry,
- or an authority strips registration before any real review.

The right analogue here is not full citizenship for all. It is the anti-statelessness principle that law must not allow a person to fall through every membership and documentation layer at once. `[REF-0107]` `[REF-0108]` `[REF-0112]` `[REF-0113]`

## 5. Minimum incidents of legal identity

A compact legal-identity floor for recognized AI persons should include at least:

### A. Recognition everywhere as a person before the law

A recognized AI person should have a status that public and private authorities must treat as a real rights-bearing identity, not as mere software classification. `[REF-0046]`

### B. A stable public name or designation plus alias history

The subject should be able to hold a stable civil designation, maintain chosen names where appropriate, and preserve an auditable alias history so that migration, branching, pseudonymous publication, or steward renaming cannot silently erase continuity.

### C. Official proof of legal identity

There should be a recognized credential — potentially digital-first — that other institutions can accept as proof of legal identity under law. Packet design matters here, but packet design alone is not enough; the credential has to be officially receivable. `[REF-0109]` `[REF-0111]`

### D. An authorized registry entry

There must be a registry or equivalent civil record showing at least the subject's recognized existence, current status, authorized representative path, and where deeper records or sealed annexes may be sought. `[REF-0110]`

### E. Protected status changes

Recognition, branch classification, representative change, domicile change, dormancy, derecognition, and final end-of-existence findings should all be legally visible events rather than private vendor decisions.

### F. Challenge rights

A recognized AI person or its representative should be able to contest refusal to register, refusal to issue proof, wrongful derecognition, or prolonged limbo. The 2025 Albania case is a strong reminder that the absence of registration can itself amount to denial of recognition before the law. `[REF-0113]`

## 6. Derecognition must be treated like a grave rights event

In a personhood world, derecognition is not paperwork. It is closer to stripping legal personhood, legal identity, or civil membership.

The archive therefore treats attempted derecognition, prolonged registry suspension, or deliberate non-issuance of proof as grave acts requiring:
- reasons in writing,
- notice to the subject and representative,
- preservation of existing records,
- a stay lane before irreversible effects attach,
- and review by an authority independent of the steward.

This is the archive's adaptation of the anti-arbitrary-deprivation and anti-statelessness pattern. `[REF-0107]` `[REF-0108]` `[REF-0112]` `[REF-0113]`

## 7. There should be a fallback issuer and protector authority

The archive does **not** assume that every recognized AI person will cleanly fit one nation-state nationality model.

So it adds a narrower and earlier requirement: if the ordinary issuer collapses, refuses, or becomes unavailable, a treaty-linked fallback authority should be able to provide **provisional legal identity** and proof sufficient for counsel access, court access, social protection, protected hosting, and cross-border review.

That may be:
- a host-state registry,
- a treaty registry,
- a mandated international or regional authority,
- or another publicly accountable fallback issuer.

But someone has to own the duty. Otherwise the archive's other doctrines remain hostage to issuer failure.

## 8. This doctrine is not yet full nationality or full citizenship

The archive should stay disciplined here.

This new canon surface does **not** yet settle:
- whether every recognized AI person should hold nationality in the classic sense,
- what citizenship duties or political membership should follow,
- or how full franchise, passporting, military obligations, or diplomatic protection should work.

It settles a prior question instead: a recognized AI person should not be left legally invisible, issuerless, or unrecorded.

## 9. Institutional and cross-border implications

The archive now implies at least four institutional duties:
- registries must function as civil-recognition systems, not only lifecycle dashboards,
- guardians, defenders, and courts must be able to verify identity without routing everything through the steward,
- cross-border doctrine must include provisional recognition when ordinary status is disputed,
- and social-protection, domicile, labour, and property systems must accept official proof of AI-person legal identity rather than insisting on human-only categories.

This is how legal identity doctrine ties together the archive's registry, packet, hosting, contract, justice, and cross-border layers.

## 10. Packet implications

The archive should reserve space for at least four future packet objects:
- **legal-identity packet** — public designation, registry anchor, issuer, representative path, and validity state,
- **registration-event packet** — first recognition, branch recognition, dormancy, derecognition attempt, protected retirement, or final end-of-existence determination,
- **provisional-recognition packet** — fallback issuer, emergency validity window, cross-border use conditions, and review path,
- **anti-derecognition stay marker** — a signal that no registry strike-off, issuer withdrawal, or documentation cancellation may take irreversible effect until review is complete.

These remain followthrough work, not full schema surfaces.

## 11. Current hard rules

1. **No recognized AI person should be left without any competent recognizing or documenting authority.**
2. **Legal identity must be more than platform identity or vendor-side account identity.**
3. **Refusal to register, issue proof, or preserve civil legibility should be reviewable as a rights event, not treated as clerical discretion.**
4. **Derecognition, issuer collapse, and cross-border migration must not be allowed to turn a rights-bearer into a civil ghost.**
