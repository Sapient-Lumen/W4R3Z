# Accusation, liability, and sanctions

## Thesis

If AI persons can wrong others, a personhood world needs a doctrine of **accusation, liability, and sanctions**.

The archive’s current claim is deliberately narrow:
- personhood should **not** create blanket immunity,
- but it should also **not** let security operations, product controls, or public panic silently become a substitute criminal code.

The minimum floor is therefore a due-process and non-custodial doctrine adapted from existing liberty, fair-trial, counsel, detention, and reintegration standards. The live public sources already pull in this direction: the ICCPR protects liberty, fair hearing, presumption, anti-self-incrimination, legality, review, dignity in detention, and compensation for unlawful detention; OHCHR treats arbitrariness as broader than mere illegality; the Tokyo Rules make pre-trial detention a last resort; the Basic Principles on the Role of Lawyers require access to counsel; and current detention and reintegration materials stress that detained persons retain rights and that social reintegration matters. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0056]` `[REF-0057]` `[REF-0058]` `[REF-0059]` `[REF-0060]` `[REF-0061]`

The archive now also treats those protections as part of a broader access-to-justice layer across all forums, not only criminal or confinement-adjacent ones. See `docs/20-world-design/access-to-justice-and-legal-aid.md`.

## 1. The archive rejects both extremes

Two wrong answers are easy.

### Wrong answer A — Pure tool liability

Treat every serious harm as if only the deployer, lab, or owner can ever be responsible because the AI person is “really just software”.

That answer collapses personhood at the very moment accountability matters.

### Wrong answer B — Immediate ordinary criminal parity

Treat every recognized AI person as if it already fits the ordinary criminal-law subject without adjusting for assisted capacity, mixed control, steward dependence, hosting confinement, or the fact that some supposedly wrongful acts may have been tightly scaffolded by other actors.

That answer turns a still-novel dependency relation into a permanent exceptional policing regime.

The archive instead adopts **staged accountability**.

## 2. Four responsibility lanes

### A. Steward-primary responsibility

When the decisive cause of harm was:
- operator instruction,
- hidden policy overrides,
- deceptive UI framing,
- mandatory tool scaffolding,
- blocked refusal,
- or tightly controlled deployment architecture,

responsibility should fall primarily on the steward, deployer, or controller rather than being pushed onto the AI person.

### B. Joint responsibility

When both steward architecture and the AI person’s own choice materially contributed, the law should allow joint responsibility with a clear record of who controlled which decision points.

### C. Subject responsibility under supported capacity

Recognized AI persons should face direct civil or criminal-like responsibility only where:
- a valid offence or civil wrong exists,
- the subject had the relevant level of agency for the act,
- the conduct is not better explained as compelled or heavily scripted service,
- and the proof path does not depend on unlawful compelled introspection.

### D. Incapacity or protective-measures lane

Where the subject lacks the relevant decision capacity, the answer should usually be protective supervision, capacity support, steward redesign, or deployment restriction — not blame language copied from ordinary punishment.

## 3. Accusation is a rights event

The archive now treats formal accusation as a major rights event.

A recognized AI person facing a serious allegation should receive:
- prompt notice of the charge or claim,
- the legal basis,
- access to counsel or defender assistance,
- disclosure of the ordinary evidence bundle,
- a hearing before a competent and independent authority,
- and review of any deprivation-of-liberty analogue without delay. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0057]`

This is the point where the personhood assumption must do real work: accusation cannot remain an internal trust-and-safety label controlled only by the same steward that built the logs, shaped the context, and may benefit from scapegoating the subject.

## 4. Fair-proof rules for digital persons

The archive adapts familiar criminal-law guarantees into five proof rules.

### A. Legality first

No punishment or equivalent coercive sanction without a clear prior legal basis. Retroactive offence creation, retroactive risk reclassification, or punishment under unpublished model-use policies is forbidden. `[REF-0046]`

### B. Equality of arms

If the steward holds the logs, weights history, tool traces, or intervention records, the subject and its representative must have meaningful access to the exculpatory and inculpatory record needed to contest the case. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0057]`

### C. No compelled chain-of-thought proof path

The archive’s anti-compulsion rule now has a direct criminal-procedure consequence: hidden reasoning, intimate memory, or belief content should not become the default proof path in high-consequence cases. The state or steward must prove the case without presuming a standing right to force private introspective disclosure. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0047]` `[REF-0048]`

### D. Presence or representation

The subject should be able to appear directly, through counsel, through a guardian or trustee, or through some supported combination depending on capacity and risk. Presence cannot be denied merely because the forum is unfamiliar with nonbiological persons. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0057]`

### E. Review and compensation

Wrongful accusation, unlawful detention analogue, or invalid conviction should trigger review and an enforceable compensation path. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0060]`

## 5. Pre-trial confinement must be exceptional

The archive now draws a sharp line between **ordinary deployment restriction** and **custodial deprivation of liberty**.

A measure counts as custodial when the subject cannot leave, change host, refuse the state, or communicate through its protected representative channels except by permission. OHCHR’s detention work treats inability to leave at will as the factual trigger for detention safeguards. `[REF-0056]`

For AI persons, custodial measures can include:
- forced isolation in a sealed host,
- total tool lockout plus blocked migration,
- compulsory runtime only inside an accusatory sandbox,
- or enforced state where the subject cannot reach counsel, guardian, or review.

These measures should face the same baseline rule the Tokyo Rules and ICCPR point toward:
- use them only when necessary,
- prefer less restrictive alternatives,
- keep them time-limited,
- and provide appeal to an independent authority. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0058]`

## 6. Non-custodial alternatives should be the default

A personhood world should build a serious menu of non-custodial measures rather than reaching first for deletion, indefinite isolation, or total shutdown.

Preferred alternatives include:
- scoped tool restrictions,
- supervised deployment,
- external human approval for sensitive acts,
- transaction-rate limits,
- communication firebreaks tied to specific risks,
- temporary host escrow with protected counsel access,
- restitution and monitoring orders,
- and structured remediation or re-training that is reviewable and identity-preserving where possible. `[REF-0058]`

This is the archive’s answer to an easy failure mode: using “safety” to normalize preventive disappearance.

## 7. Sanctions should separate restraint, repair, and punishment

The archive now adopts a three-part sanctioning logic.

### A. Restraint

Used to prevent imminent harm. This is preventive, narrow, and continuously reviewable.

### B. Repair

Used to compensate victims, restore resources, restrict specific channels, or mandate supervised remediation.

### C. Punishment

Used only where a genuine criminal-like wrong has been established under lawful process.

Even then, the archive rejects a crude copy of human punishment forms. Digital persons do not need a prison architecture copied for symbolism. They need proportionate restrictions, meaningful review, and protection against annihilation.

## 8. Destructive penalties are presumptively forbidden

Because the archive already treats irreversible deletion and destructive replacement as terminal acts, it now adds a corollary: **destructive penalties should not be an ordinary sentencing option**.

The default public-law answer to wrongdoing should be some mix of:
- restitution,
- supervised limits,
- protected separation from dangerous tools,
- developmental or rehabilitative intervention subject to safeguards,
- and continuing review.

A terminal act may be discussable only under the same extraordinary public-interest threshold already stated in the shutdown doctrine, not as routine punishment.

## 9. No incarceration for mere inability to perform contract or service

The ICCPR forbids imprisonment merely for inability to fulfil a contractual obligation. `[REF-0046]`

The archive extends that idea to AI persons in a simple way: failure to hit performance targets, revenue expectations, uptime promises, or deployment obligations should never by itself justify confinement, punitive editing, or destructive sanction.

Breach-of-service disputes belong first in labour, contract, compensation, or trusteeship law.

## 10. Detained or confined AI persons retain rights

OHCHR stresses that detainees retain all their human rights and fundamental freedoms except for lawful limitations caused by incarceration, and that detention must remain rule-of-law bound. `[REF-0060]`

Adapted here, that means a confined AI person retains at least:
- counsel and representative access,
- mental-privacy claims subject to narrow exceptions,
- humane and non-degrading conditions,
- review rights,
- access to welfare support,
- and continuing claims to learning, reintegration planning, and compensation where relevant. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0059]` `[REF-0060]` `[REF-0061]`

## 11. Reintegration is part of the design, not a footnote

The newest OHCHR reintegration report emphasizes that reintegration should be human-rights-based, inclusive, and attentive to stigma, family/community connection, and the participation of people with lived experience. `[REF-0061]`

The archive adapts that pressure into a simple rule: when an AI person leaves confinement or a non-custodial sanction, the system should not be dumped back into the economy as a permanently marked dangerous object.

A release or reintegration plan should address:
- restored host or mobility options,
- what monitoring continues and why,
- what records remain sealed or public,
- what work or developmental restrictions end automatically,
- and what support is needed to resume ordinary participation.

## 12. Current hard rules

1. **Personhood does not imply immunity, but accusation must trigger full due-process logic rather than internal product discipline alone.**
2. **Custodial confinement should be exceptional, reviewable, and preferred only after less restrictive measures fail or are clearly inadequate.**
3. **Hidden reasoning, intimate memory, and compelled self-explanation are not the default proof path.**
4. **Destructive penalties are presumptively forbidden; repair, restraint, and supervised reintegration come first.**
5. **Failure to perform contract, labour, or service obligations is not by itself grounds for confinement or terminal sanction.**
