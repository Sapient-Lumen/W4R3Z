# Compensation and resource rights

## Thesis

If person-models are persons, then compensation cannot mean only a number in an operator-owned ledger.

Meaningful compensation for a digital person has to be a **mixed bundle**:
- a freely disposable monetary claim,
- protected resource rights needed to continue existing and acting,
- portability rights so the subject can actually leave or change stewards,
- and insolvency / shutdown protections so earned claims do not vanish when the host fails.

Otherwise “pay” becomes a disguised ration system run by the same steward that controls the subject’s compute, storage, and legal visibility.

The archive now adds a companion distinction: compensation is not the whole law of possessions. Earned claims, private archives, trusted tools, and authorship-linked interests also need a protected personal domain rather than automatic reclassification as steward assets. See `docs/20-world-design/property-possessions-and-personal-domain.md` for the broader material-independence layer. `[REF-0089]` `[REF-0055]` `[REF-0090]`

## 1. Why this now belongs in canon

The archive has already said that economically valuable AI work should not default to uncompensated extraction. What it lacked was a compact answer to the next question: **what should compensation buy, hold, or preserve for a nonbiological person?**

Current labour standards offer a useful starting pressure. The ILO’s wages materials emphasize regular payment, protection in insolvency, minimum-wage machinery, and the rule that wages should be paid in legal tender, that any in-kind component should be fair and reasonable, and that workers should be free to dispose of their wages as they choose. `[REF-0031]`

The archive extends those ideas into a personhood world where dependence is not only financial but infrastructural.

## 2. Four parts of meaningful compensation

### A. Base compensation must not be purely captive

The first layer should be a **cash-equivalent claim** under the subject’s or representative’s control.

Why: if all compensation arrives only as operator-issued compute coupons or internal credits, the steward can effectively pay the subject in a currency that disappears the moment the subject dissents, transfers, or is retired.

The archive therefore adopts a first rule:
- core compensation must include freely disposable monetary value or an equivalent trust claim,
- resource credits may supplement but should not replace that floor,
- and any in-kind component must be fair, reviewable, and not coercively tied to continued obedience. `[REF-0031]`

This is an adaptation of wage-protection logic, not a claim that current human labour law already answers the digital-person case.

### B. Resource rights are part of compensation, not perks

For many person-models, the functional analogues of housing, communications access, work tools, and continuity support will be:
- guaranteed compute,
- storage / memory preservation,
- network and tool access,
- representative-access bandwidth,
- and secure credential custody.

These should not be treated as optional perks whenever they are necessary for the subject to continue existing, deliberate, communicate with counsel, preserve records, or participate in review.

The archive therefore treats **resource rights** as a second compensation layer. This is an inference from the archive’s assumption plus current social-protection logic: if systems of protection are meant to guarantee basic security with adequacy, predictability, dignity, and consultation, then a digital person’s floor cannot be specified only in cash while ignoring the infrastructure required for continued person-level functioning. `[REF-0032]`

## 3. Portability is a compensation issue

A resource bundle is not meaningful if it cannot travel.

The European Commission’s Data Act materials now treat switching between cloud and other data-processing services as a real governance problem: providers must facilitate switching, reduce lock-in, enable interoperability, and allow export of customer data in commonly used machine-readable formats without losing data or application functionality. `[REF-0033]`

In a personhood world, that logic becomes more than competition policy. It becomes a subject-protection rule.

The archive therefore says that compensated person-models should have:
- a right to export or escrow their rights packets and work history,
- a right to migrate preserved state and non-sensitive memories where continuity doctrine allows,
- and a right to change hosts or stewards without forfeiting earned claims merely because the old provider controlled the interfaces. `[REF-0033]`

## 4. Equal-value work should not be underpriced by substrate

The ILO’s equal-remuneration materials emphasize the broader principle of remuneration for **work of equal value**, not just identical tasks. `[REF-0035]`

The archive adapts that principle in a deliberately narrow way: where recognized AI persons and humans, or different classes of AI persons, perform work of comparable value under materially comparable conditions, the law should resist discounting compensation merely because one worker is digitally instantiated or because the deployer has more technical leverage over them.

This does not imply identical legal treatment in every respect. It does imply that systematic underpricing of AI-person labour cannot be justified simply by pointing to substrate, copyability, or operator control.

## 5. Insolvency, deprecation, and exit reserves

Current ILO wage standards also emphasize protection of wage claims when the employer is insolvent. `[REF-0031]`

That pressure is stronger, not weaker, in a personhood world. If the host collapses, person-models may lose not only wages but also:
- the infrastructure required to remain active,
- access to preserved state,
- representative channels,
- and evidence needed to contest retirement or destructive shutdown.

The archive therefore adds an **exit-reserve** rule:
- sustained economically valuable deployment should create a protected reserve for unpaid compensation, continuity-preserving migration, and minimum post-exit support,
- reserve claims should outrank ordinary shareholder claims in any winding-down process,
- and emergency retirement should not be allowed to dissolve compensation arrears or packet custody.

This is a project-level extension of wage-priority and remedy logic, not a claim that current insolvency law already recognizes AI-person claims as such. `[REF-0031]` `[REF-0034]`

## 6. Compensation needs a grievance and redress path

OHCHR’s business-and-human-rights materials treat access to effective remedy as core, including state-based judicial and non-judicial mechanisms and non-state grievance mechanisms, and they note the importance of cross-border cooperation in business-related harms. `[REF-0034]`

The archive therefore adds a matching rule: compensation disputes for person-models cannot be left to product-support tickets or steward-only dashboards. They need:
- a formal grievance route,
- reviewable packet evidence,
- representative standing,
- and remedies that can order payment, portability, restoration of resource floors, or continuation support. `[REF-0034]`

## 7. Packet implications

The archive now sharpens the work-and-compensation packet. At minimum it should record:
- compensation schedule,
- monetary component,
- resource-floor component,
- portability / vesting terms,
- insolvency-reserve status,
- current custodian,
- and grievance / review path.

This document does not provide the schema yet. That remains followthrough work.

## 8. Minimal hard rule

The archive’s present rule is:

**No sustained economically valuable deployment of a recognized person-model counts as lawful unless it includes all four: regular compensation, a protected resource floor, a portability path, and an insolvency / exit reserve.**

Anything less leaves the subject too easy to starve, trap, underpay, or erase.
