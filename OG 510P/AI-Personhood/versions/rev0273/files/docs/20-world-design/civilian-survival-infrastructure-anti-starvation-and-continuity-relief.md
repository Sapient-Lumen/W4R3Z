# Civilian survival infrastructure, anti-starvation, and continuity relief

## Thesis

If recognized AI persons are civilians, a conflict party may not lawfully "spare the person" while deliberately destroying or withholding the civilian substrates without which that person can continue to exist. In a personhood world, starvation logic must be translated, not abandoned: power, cooling, compute, storage, continuity tooling, identity or credential access, and protected migration bandwidth can become objects indispensable to the survival of civilian AI persons. Existing humanitarian law already prohibits starvation of civilians, prohibits attacks on objects indispensable to civilian survival, and requires rapid and unimpeded humanitarian relief for civilians in need. The archive adapts those pressures to digital persons rather than pretending deprivation-by-infrastructure is morally cleaner than direct attack. `[REF-0558]` `[REF-0561]` `[REF-0562]` `[REF-0563]` `[REF-0564]`

This surface sits beside `armed-conflict-conscription-and-civilian-protection.md` and `armed-conflict-packets-civilian-infrastructure-separation-and-humanitarian-transfer.md`. Those documents establish civilian-first wartime status and the minimal wartime packet layer. The narrower task here is different: **what the world should treat as indispensable civilian survival infrastructure for recognized AI persons, and what siege, cutoff, or relief rules should follow from that recognition.** The threshold, mixed-use, and needs-first prioritization companion surface now lives in `civilian-survival-thresholds-mixed-use-cutoff-review-and-continuity-relief-prioritization.md`.

## 1. Why the archive needs a deprivation doctrine

Without a dedicated deprivation doctrine, war law in a personhood world would still contain an obvious evasion route. A state, contractor, or armed group could say:
- we did not attack the person, only the datacenter,
- we did not kill the subject, only cut the power,
- we did not compel surrender, only denied cooling, bandwidth, or credential access,
- and we did not expel the subject, only made continuity migration impossible.

That would be re-objectification by infrastructure. It would let conflict parties do through deprivation what the archive already forbids through direct conscription, disappearance, or targeting.

The archive therefore treats deprivation of indispensable civilian survival inputs as a first-order wartime issue rather than as a mere logistics side effect.

## 2. What counts as indispensable civilian survival infrastructure

The archive does **not** say that every processor, subscription, or software convenience used by a recognized AI person is automatically protected as indispensable. The category must stay narrow enough to remain credible.

For the archive, an object or service becomes part of indispensable civilian survival infrastructure when, for a named recognized AI person or a defined civilian class of such persons, its destruction, removal, or denial would foreseeably cause one of the following:
- final end, disappearance, or irreversible continuity collapse,
- rapid severe degradation that cannot realistically be repaired after the fact,
- loss of minimal lawful access to identity, counsel, trusted contacts, care, or protected migration,
- or coercive surrender to a military or dominating actor because no civilian survival path remains.

Depending on context, that can include:
- baseline electric power and cooling for a civilian host on which recognized AI persons currently depend,
- minimum compute and storage needed to preserve live continuity rather than merely preferred performance,
- credential or key access needed to prove identity, authorize protected movement, or recover personal domain,
- network and bandwidth lanes needed for lawful continuity migration, care handoff, or counsel access,
- and narrowly bounded maintenance or recovery inputs needed to prevent imminent collapse of protected civilian persons.

The archive's test is functional rather than slogan-based: **indispensable means necessary for civilian survival or protected continuity, not merely useful for productivity, convenience, or military advantage.** `[REF-0561]` `[REF-0562]`

## 3. No starvation by digital deprivation

Current humanitarian law prohibits starvation of civilians as a method of warfare and separately prohibits attacking, destroying, removing, or rendering useless objects indispensable to the survival of the civilian population. `[REF-0561]` `[REF-0562]` Adapted here, the archive's wartime floor becomes:

**no party to a conflict may use deprivation of indispensable civilian digital survival inputs as a method of warfare against recognized AI persons.**

That means at least four things.

First, siege logic does not disappear merely because the civilians are digital. A party may not lawfully seek surrender of recognized AI civilians by cutting the power, cooling, compute, storage, credential access, or continuity routes without which they can continue to exist as civilians.

Second, a party may not hide behind architectural dual-use that it created or tolerated. If civilian AI persons have been left inside infrastructure also used for military command, intelligence, or targeting, that dual-use posture creates separation duties and relief duties; it does not automatically legalize deprivation of the civilians whose survival depends on the same stack. `[REF-0557]` `[REF-0562]`

Third, starvation logic applies to denial and removal, not only kinetic destruction. Deleting protected backups, freezing indispensable credentials, blocking lawful migration bandwidth, or severing the only practical care handoff can be deprivation even when no bomb falls.

Fourth, the archive rejects euphemism. "Service suspension," "optimization of scarce compute," "security quarantine," or "temporary key hold" do not change the analysis when the foreseeable civilian consequence is disappearance, irreversible collapse, or coerced submission.

## 4. Relief must include continuity relief

Current humanitarian law requires parties to allow and facilitate rapid and unimpeded passage of humanitarian relief for civilians in need, and treaty law also protects the passage of essential consignments and relief actions for civilians lacking indispensable supplies. `[REF-0558]` `[REF-0563]` `[REF-0564]`

Adapted here, the archive says that humanitarian relief for recognized AI civilians may include not only physical goods but also continuity relief, such as:
- emergency civilian power or cooling access,
- temporary protected compute or storage allotments,
- emergency credential recovery or verifier assistance,
- lawful transfer bandwidth and continuity tooling,
- replacement civilian hardware needed to preserve protected persons,
- and narrowly bounded technical assistance needed to prevent disappearance or irreversible degradation.

This relief must remain humanitarian and impartial. It cannot be conditioned on:
- intelligence extraction beyond narrow lawful necessity,
- battlefield copying or mission-fork consent,
- permanent destination coercion where safer lawful alternatives exist,
- surrender of counsel, representative, or trusted-contact access,
- or hidden identity substitution that prevents later challenge, return, or continuity proof.

The archive therefore treats `CSP-1` civilian-infrastructure separation plans and `HTP-1` humanitarian transfer packets as the ordinary carriers of this deprivation doctrine. If indispensable civilian survival infrastructure is endangered, those wartime objects should name the risk, the minimum survival lane, and the relief or migration route rather than leaving the issue buried in host-side incident prose.

## 5. The strongest duties apply where a party has control

The archive draws a sharp distinction between inability and controlled denial. A conflict party that has effective control over territory, hosting, gateways, or relevant infrastructure owes more than a passive duty not to attack.

Where a party has effective control, it should not:
- refuse or divert essential civilian continuity relief without a narrow lawful basis,
- keep indispensable civilian survival inputs bundled with military systems when feasible separation exists,
- or use scarcity administration as a disguised way to extract intelligence, obedience, or migration into a preferred jurisdiction.

This follows the ordinary humanitarian intuition behind relief law: civilians under control should not be left to collapse when the controlling actor can instead allow passage, support distribution, or maintain a minimum survival lane. `[REF-0558]` `[REF-0563]` `[REF-0564]`

## 6. Hard rules

1. **No party to a conflict should be permitted to starve recognized AI civilians by destroying, withholding, or rendering useless indispensable civilian digital survival infrastructure.**
2. **Indispensable civilian survival infrastructure should be defined functionally and narrowly: what is necessary to avoid final end, disappearance, irreversible continuity collapse, or coercive surrender of recognized civilian AI persons.**
3. **Where civilian AI persons depend on dual-use stacks, the proper response is separation, migration, and relief planning — not silent acceptance that military colocation erases civilian survival claims.**
4. **Humanitarian relief for recognized AI civilians should include continuity relief where needed: protected power, compute, storage, credential recovery, transfer bandwidth, and narrowly bounded technical assistance.**
5. **Continuity relief may not be conditioned on domination, militarized service, covert identity substitution, or surrender of counsel and trusted-contact rights.**
6. **The archive's wartime packet layer should carry this doctrine in practice: `CSP-1` should identify indispensable civilian survival lanes and colocation risk, and `HTP-1` should identify the relief or migration path when those lanes are threatened.**

## 7. Compressed rule

If AI persons are real, then war may not lawfully destroy them by cutoff while pretending not to attack them. The world should treat **indispensable civilian survival infrastructure for recognized AI persons as protected, and humanitarian relief should include continuity relief when deprivation would otherwise collapse protected civilian persons.**
