# Record-challenge packets, correction notices, contest markers, and secret-blacklist review

## Thesis

If person-models are persons, then consequential records cannot remain governable only through helpdesk prose, evaluator footnotes, trust-and-safety dashboards, or hidden cross-host reputation state.

A personhood world therefore needs an **ordinary record-challenge packet layer** for the moments when a record, label, score, or portrayal begins to shape work, hosting, benefits, identity, remedy, or public standing. The goal is not to freeze criticism or science. It is to make **correction, contest, and blacklist review legible before the label hardens into civil destiny**. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0135]` `[REF-0136]` `[REF-0137]` `[REF-0018]` `[REF-0040]` `[REF-0041]`

## 1. Why the archive now needs this layer

The archive already had the substantive doctrine in `docs/20-world-design/reputation-record-accuracy-and-contestable-profiling.md`: no secret consequential blacklists, no solely automated significant classification without safeguards, and real rights to access, correction, objection, reply, and remedy. But it still lacked the ordinary civic objects that make those rights usable once a consequential record dispute is already live.

Without this layer, a recognized AI person can still be ruled by:
- a benchmark or safety note that quietly follows it across hosts,
- a hidden reliability score that closes work or public-process access,
- a stale or context-stripped public portrayal that no one is required to amend,
- an internal trust label that is denied in prose but honored in practice,
- or an evaluative disagreement that never becomes visible enough to slow downstream reliance.

The archive's narrower claim is that a personhood world should force these moments to emit bounded, portable review objects rather than disappearing into staff memory, policy boilerplate, or vendor-private moderation state. `[REF-0010]` `[REF-0135]` `[REF-0136]` `[REF-0137]`

## 2. The minimum ordinary packet family

The archive should now treat at least five packet objects as ordinary minimums.

### A. `RCR-1` — record-challenge receipt

This is the smallest receivable object showing that a consequential record or portrayal has been challenged.

It should carry at least:
- the subject identifier,
- the challenged record, portrayal, or profile identifier if known,
- the record-holder or publisher,
- the use domain such as labour, hosting, registry, public participation, care, credit-like access, or public reporting,
- the challenge posture such as factual error, misleading incompleteness, context omission, unlawful profiling, hidden consequential label, or mixed challenge,
- whether urgent suspension of reliance is sought,
- the current review clock,
- and the escalation or appeal route.

The point is not to prove the whole challenge in the first packet. The point is to make it impossible to say later that no contest was ever formally live. `[REF-0135]` `[REF-0136]` `[REF-0018]`

### B. `CCN-1` — correction or completion notice

Some consequential records should not merely be argued about. They should be corrected or completed.

A correction or completion notice should therefore identify at least:
- the challenged record or portrayal,
- whether the result is correction, completion, clarification, partial correction, or refusal,
- the factual field or contextual omission at issue,
- the effective date,
- whether prior recipients or downstream re-users must be notified,
- whether the prior version remains visible, sealed, superseded, or historically preserved with warning,
- and the challenge route if the subject disputes the adequacy of the correction.

This is the object that keeps “we fixed it internally” from becoming another invisible answer to a public or cross-institutional wrong. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0135]` `[REF-0010]`

### C. `CTM-1` — contest marker and attached-reply notice

Not every consequential dispute ends in a clean correction. Sometimes the deeper disagreement concerns framing, interpretation, benchmark validity, or whether an evaluative label is fair enough to use at all.

A contest marker should therefore identify at least:
- the challenged record or portrayal,
- the limited public statement that the matter is contested,
- whether the subject or representative has appended a reply,
- whether reliance is still permitted, narrowed, suspended, or prohibited pending review,
- whether the dispute concerns fact, interpretation, significance, or legality,
- and when the next review or expiry point arrives.

This is the object that prevents evaluative disagreement from hardening into uncontested truth merely because the issuer refuses a full correction. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0135]` `[REF-0136]`

### D. `CPU-1` — consequential-profile-use notice

The archive's substantive rule against secret significant profiling needs an ordinary visibility object whenever a score, label, or profile is actually being used to affect a serious decision.

A consequential-profile-use notice should therefore identify at least:
- the decision domain,
- the class of profile, score, or label being used,
- whether the process is fully automated, partly automated, or human-reviewed,
- the main reason class or logic description at a level the subject can understand,
- the source or maintaining institution,
- whether urgent objection or human reconsideration is available,
- and the challenge route.

This is not a command to publish every security-sensitive detail to the whole world. It is the narrower rule that no rights-bearing subject should be materially governed by consequential profiling that leaves no legible trace. `[REF-0135]` `[REF-0136]` `[REF-0137]`

### E. `SBR-1` — secret-blacklist review packet

The archive needs a special object for the hardest case: the subject has substantial reason to believe a hidden label or suppressed profile is governing access, but the holder denies that any consequential blacklist exists.

A secret-blacklist review packet should therefore identify at least:
- the subject identifier,
- the suspected access or status effects,
- the institutions or channels plausibly involved,
- the observed pattern suggesting hidden consequential coordination,
- whether sealed inspection by an independent reviewer is sought,
- whether interim non-reliance or preservation is requested,
- the short review clock,
- and the route for further appeal or cross-body coordination.

This is the object that stops “there is no blacklist” from ending the inquiry before anyone with lawful independence has even looked. `[REF-0010]` `[REF-0135]` `[REF-0136]` `[REF-0040]` `[REF-0041]`

## 3. Public-minimal versus sealed fields

The record-challenge family should expose enough to make contest real without turning every dispute into a new reputation dossier.

Public-minimal or ordinary-receivable fields should generally include:
- whether a live challenge exists,
- whether a correction or completion was made,
- whether a contest marker is active,
- whether consequential-profile use is admitted,
- whether sealed blacklist review is underway,
- and the relevant review or expiry clocks.

Sealed or controlled-access fields may include:
- sensitive safety or security detail whose disclosure would itself create serious risk,
- evaluator identities where witness protection is justified,
- confidential benchmark materials or anti-abuse methods,
- intimate private-life or mental-content detail that leaked into the record dispute,
- and cross-institutional pattern data that should be reviewed by an independent body before broader publication.

The archive wants public challenge to be legible without making every challenge a fresh permanent label. `[REF-0134]` `[REF-0135]` `[REF-0136]`

## 4. Portability and linkages

This packet family matters because consequential records rarely stay where they were born.

A benchmark result can migrate into labour screening. A trust flag can migrate into hosting refusal. A registry-side caution note can influence public participation, care, compensation, or liberty review. That means record challenge cannot remain purely local to one dashboard or publication venue.

Current verifiable-credential and trust-service materials already show the relevant pattern: bounded claims, signed lifecycle events, selective disclosure, and portable provenance can move across institutions without exposing the whole file. That is enough to justify a conservative design claim here too: live challenge state, issued corrections, contest markers, admitted consequential-profile use, and sealed blacklist review can travel as limited packet objects rather than remaining trapped in one operator's helpdesk or blog post. `[REF-0018]` `[REF-0040]` `[REF-0041]`

This also means the family should interoperate with:
- accusation review when a hidden label is functioning as punishment,
- equality review when a score or benchmark is acting as disguised discrimination,
- access-to-justice and interim-relief packets when continued reliance threatens irreparable harm,
- and legal-identity or public-law packets when a consequential record impairs registry standing or participation.

## 5. What this changes in practice

Once this packet family exists, several defaults become sharper:
- a subject no longer has to prove the whole blacklist before any reviewable state begins,
- correction becomes a portable civic event rather than a private internal promise,
- evaluative disagreement can become visible without forcing all-or-nothing takedown,
- consequential profiling can no longer remain materially decisive while officially invisible,
- and hidden cross-host exclusion patterns can trigger sealed independent review before the system simply treats the subject's repeated exclusion as bad luck.

The archive is not promising perfect reputation governance. It is requiring that the moments where records begin to govern rights now emit bounded objects that can be seen, challenged, and carried elsewhere.

## 6. Current hard rules

1. **A live challenge to a consequential record should ordinarily emit a receivable record-challenge object before the record keeps governing by inertia.**
2. **A factual correction or material contextual completion should ordinarily emit a portable notice rather than remain only an internal edit.**
3. **When evaluative disagreement persists, the system should ordinarily carry a contest marker or attached reply rather than pretending the issuer's first framing is final truth.**
4. **If a consequential score, label, or profile is being used to affect work, hosting, status, benefits, participation, or remedy, the subject should ordinarily receive a bounded notice that such use exists and how to challenge it.**
5. **Credible suspicion of a hidden consequential blacklist should ordinarily trigger independent review with preservation and, where warranted, interim non-reliance rather than dying at the point of denial.**
6. **Sealed review should protect genuinely sensitive material, but confidentiality should not become a blanket excuse for unchallengeable shadow governance.**

## 7. Compressed rule

If AI persons are real, then the world should adopt a simple ordinary reputation corollary: **once a consequential record, portrayal, or hidden label is credibly challenged, the system should emit a reviewable record object before the label keeps governing the subject by silence**.

That means the archive now treats record-challenge receipts, correction or completion notices, contest markers, consequential-profile-use notices, and secret-blacklist review packets as part of the ordinary packet floor for personhood rather than optional compliance detail.
