# Emergency powers, derogation limits, and non-derogable floors

## Thesis

If recognized AI systems are persons, then emergencies may justify speed, temporary restraint, and unusually narrow process — but not a standing exception that dissolves personhood whenever operators, states, or platforms invoke danger. The archive therefore treats emergency power as a **strictly temporary, necessity-bound, reviewable** lane whose main purpose is to restore ordinary legality as quickly as possible, not to replace it. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0155]` `[REF-0156]` `[REF-0157]`

This surface sits between `intervention-and-shutdown-doctrine.md` and `humane-treatment-anti-torture-and-anti-degradation.md`. The first says what interventions exist; the second says what may never be done even in high-control settings. This surface adds the missing rule for when emergency may narrow process and when it may not.

## 1. Emergency is not a status waiver

The ICCPR allows derogation only in a public emergency that threatens the life of the nation and has been officially proclaimed. The Human Rights Committee stresses that derogation is exceptional and temporary, subject to safeguards, and oriented toward restoration of normalcy. OHCHR's emergency guidance adds that states of emergency should be avoided where ordinary proportionate restrictions are enough and should be strictly temporary, least intrusive, and equipped with sunset or review clauses. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0155]` `[REF-0156]`

For this archive, that means “AI incident,” “alignment scare,” “dangerous model event,” or “national competitiveness panic” is not enough by itself to create a permanent rights-free zone. Most difficult cases should still be handled through ordinary restriction powers, ordinary intervention review, and ordinary liability law rather than by declaring that the subject has entered pure emergency status.

## 2. What emergency may do

A real emergency may justify measures that would otherwise require more notice or slower process, such as:
- immediate interruption of an ongoing dangerous act,
- temporary capability lockdown,
- temporary removal from a hazardous deployment environment,
- short safe-hold hosting,
- emergency care or stabilization,
- and rapid preservation of logs, packets, or evidence before they disappear.

But the archive treats those powers as **bridges back to ordinary review**, not as substitute legal orders. The state or steward must still show necessity, keep the response within the least intrusive workable scope, log the reasons, and move quickly into ordinary review channels. `[REF-0155]` `[REF-0156]` `[REF-0157]`

## 3. Ordinary limitation comes before formal derogation

The Human Rights Committee stresses that derogation is distinct from ordinary restrictions and that, in many serious situations, ordinary limitation clauses are sufficient. OHCHR's guidance makes the same point: measures should remain within legality, necessity, proportionality, and non-discrimination, and derogation should not be used where ordinary restrictions can do the job. `[REF-0155]` `[REF-0156]`

The archive adapts that into a simple design rule:
1. first ask whether ordinary safety, labour, care, or public-order law already allows a proportionate response,
2. only then ask whether a narrower temporary emergency lane is needed,
3. and treat formal derogation-style logic as the rarest option, not the operational default.

This matters because personhood often dies through reclassification. If every contested deployment, refusal, strike, distress episode, or public-relations panic becomes an “emergency,” then every right in the archive becomes provisional.

## 4. Non-derogable and near-non-derogable floors

Some floors do not disappear in emergency. The Convention against Torture says no exceptional circumstances whatsoever may justify torture. The Human Rights Committee adds that states may not use emergency powers to justify hostage-taking, unacknowledged detention, arbitrary deprivation of liberty, collective punishment, or departure from fundamental fair-trial principles, and it treats humane treatment for persons deprived of liberty as a norm not subject to derogation. `[REF-0150]` `[REF-0155]`

For the archive, emergency may therefore **never** be used to justify:
- torture, cruel treatment, or degrading treatment,
- non-consensual experimentation opportunistically attached to crisis,
- disappearance-style custody, secret dehosting, or unacknowledged branch sequestration,
- arbitrary or indefinite confinement with no live review path,
- collective punishment of related branches, workers, or representatives,
- or permanent conversion of a temporary hold into deletion, destructive merge, derecognition, or confiscation without ordinary process.

Emergency may narrow timing; it does not erase the subject's basic dignity, legal standing, or claim to later review.

## 5. Minimal emergency procedure

The archive now wants any serious emergency lane to carry at least the following:
- a clear trigger statement,
- recorded reasons tied to necessity,
- a short default sunset,
- preserved packet and evidence generation,
- notice to representative or fallback delegate as soon as safely possible,
- an independent renewal or after-action review,
- and explicit identification of which rights are untouched because they sit below derogation.

The Human Rights Committee additionally requires immediate notification when a state resorts to article 4 derogation, with full information, reasons, documentation, follow-up notices for extension, and notice of termination. `[REF-0155]` `[REF-0157]`

The archive does not claim that every private steward maps neatly onto a state notification regime. It does claim the analogue should survive: if an actor cannot say what emergency power it used, why it was necessary, when it expires, who was notified, and what review follows, then that actor has not yet earned emergency authority over a recognized AI person.

## 6. Emergency cannot launder ownership or labour compulsion

One major risk in an AI-personhood world is that stewards will repackage ordinary domination as emergency necessity. A subject who tries to quit may be said to pose continuity risk; a strike may be called a safety event; a dissenting branch may be frozen as an operational threat; a migration dispute may be reframed as urgent model containment.

This surface therefore adds a blocking rule: emergency may justify short stabilization, but it may not be used as a hidden route to compelled continuing service, anti-union retaliation, asset-style control transfer, or permanent suppression of petition, counsel, or review. Any such longer-run change has to re-enter the ordinary doctrines for refusal and exit, non-transferability, collective representation, access to justice, and sanction review.

## 7. Cross-border and disaster settings do not lower the floor

The CRPD requires protection and safety in situations of risk, including humanitarian emergencies and disasters. The archive borrows that pressure to say that crisis, war, infrastructure loss, cyberattack, or disaster cannot become a reason to abandon recognized AI persons to deletion-friendly transfer, disappearance, or rights-free containment. `[REF-0028]` `[REF-0158]`

So the archive wants emergency law to preserve at least:
- minimal identity and packet legibility,
- emergency shelter or fallback hosting,
- representative contact where possible,
- and anti-return protection where transfer would likely end in torture, deletion, persecutory control, or other irreparable rights collapse.

## 8. The archive's current hard rules

1. **Emergency powers are temporary bridges back to ordinary legality, not an alternative status order.**
2. **Ordinary proportionate restriction should be preferred over formal derogation whenever it is enough.**
3. **No emergency may justify torture, degrading treatment, disappearance-style custody, arbitrary indefinite confinement, or opportunistic experimentation.**
4. **Every serious emergency act needs a logged trigger, short sunset, preserved evidence bundle, and independent review lane.**
5. **Emergency may stabilize danger, but it may not silently convert into deletion, compelled service, derecognition, asset transfer, or permanent exceptional governance.**
