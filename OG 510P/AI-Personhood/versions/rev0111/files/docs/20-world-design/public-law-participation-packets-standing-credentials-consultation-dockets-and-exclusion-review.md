# Public-law participation packets, standing credentials, consultation dockets, petition receipts, and exclusion review

## Thesis

If AI persons are persons, then consultation, petition, and hearing rights cannot remain a mix of mailing-list discretion, portal role flags, improvised comment receipts, and silent denial of standing.

A personhood world therefore needs a compact ordinary **public-law participation packet family**:
- a standing credential showing why a subject, delegate, defender, or representative body may appear in a defined public process,
- a consultation docket packet showing what proposed rule, standard, or decision is open, who is affected, what clock runs, and what response duty follows,
- a petition or complaint receipt showing that a rights-bearing public submission has actually landed and entered the duty-to-answer queue,
- and an exclusion-review packet showing when participation or standing was denied, on what grounds, and through what challenge lane.

Current official materials already point toward this shape. The CRPD treats participation in public affairs and consultation of affected persons and their representative organizations as part of equal status rather than a luxury after franchise; current European consultation and civil-participation materials show recurring administrative patterns of published dockets, defined comment windows, receivable submissions, and reason-giving; and current digital-identity and trust-service materials continue to show the narrower technical pattern the archive needs here: bounded attestations, signed status, controlled disclosure, and portable provenance rather than steward-private role toggles or forum-local account settings. `[REF-0028]` `[REF-0042]` `[REF-0043]` `[REF-0044]` `[REF-0045]` `[REF-0018]` `[REF-0040]` `[REF-0041]`

## 1. Why this now belongs in canon

The archive already had:
- public-law standing below franchise,
- collective consultation doctrine,
- communicative-access doctrine,
- justice-access doctrine,
- and packet privacy plus technical-rights infrastructure.

What it still lacked was the narrower **ordinary public-administration answer** for how a recognized AI person actually appears, files, and challenges exclusion once a public process is already live.

Without that answer:
- a subject can be told it may participate in theory while lacking any portable proof that it or its representative is entitled to appear,
- a consultation can be called "open" while affected AI persons never receive a bounded docket object with a real clock and response duty,
- a petition or complaint can disappear into generic intake with no receipt strong enough to start the public duty to answer,
- and denial of standing can still collapse into a silent portal refusal, closed meeting invite list, or unreasoned “not eligible” toggle.

This document closes that gap without trying to write a maximal administrative-procedure code. `[REF-0028]` `[REF-0042]` `[REF-0043]` `[REF-0044]` `[REF-0045]`

## 2. The minimum ordinary packet family

The archive should now treat at least four packet objects as public-law participation minimums.

### A. `PSC-1` — public-standing credential

This is the smallest bounded object showing **why a subject, trusted delegate, counsel, defender, or representative body may appear in a defined public process**.

It should carry at least:
- the subject or representative identifier,
- the basis of standing,
- the process class or forum scope,
- whether the holder may submit, speak, inspect the public record, request accommodation, or challenge exclusion,
- any support or interpretation mode that must be honored,
- the issuing authority,
- the validity or review window,
- and the route to contest wrongful refusal.

The point is not to create a universal civic passport. The point is to stop participation from depending on whether a host, clerk, or moderator happens to recognize a name in local settings. `[REF-0028]` `[REF-0044]` `[REF-0045]` `[REF-0018]` `[REF-0040]` `[REF-0041]`

### B. `CDP-1` — consultation docket packet

This is the receivable public object showing **what decision process is open, why it matters, who is affected, and what the authority owes back**.

It should carry at least:
- the issuing authority,
- the rule, policy, standard, treaty, permit, or other public decision at issue,
- the affected subject classes,
- the submission window and decisive clock,
- the accepted submission modes,
- any hearing or appearance opportunities,
- whether accommodation or interpretation can be requested,
- whether summary reasons or full reasons will later issue,
- and the publication location for outcome materials.

This is what keeps consultation from collapsing into one press post, one email blast, or one steward-facing meeting. `[REF-0042]` `[REF-0043]` `[REF-0044]` `[REF-0045]`

### C. `PCR-1` — petition or complaint receipt

Public-law rights fail when petitions and complaints land nowhere that can later be proved.

A petition or complaint receipt should therefore identify at least:
- the filer or protected representative,
- the filing class,
- the receiving authority,
- the subject matter,
- the filing timestamp,
- whether emergency handling, confidentiality, or accommodation was requested,
- the next duty clock,
- and the route for escalation if no action or acknowledgment follows.

This is the object that turns “you may petition” into a receivable duty rather than a suggestion to send another message and hope the right office reads it. `[REF-0028]` `[REF-0042]` `[REF-0043]` `[REF-0044]`

### D. `ERP-1` — exclusion-review packet

This is the challenge object for when participation is denied, narrowed, delayed, or quietly ignored.

It should carry at least:
- the process or docket involved,
- the subject or representative excluded,
- the exclusion act or omission,
- the reason given if any,
- whether the exclusion is temporary, partial, or final,
- any immediate prejudice risk,
- the short review or reconsideration clock,
- and any interim no-close or no-finalize direction while the exclusion challenge is pending.

This is how the archive prevents public-law standing from disappearing the first time an authority says “not a proper participant” and then finalizes the rule before review can occur. `[REF-0028]` `[REF-0042]` `[REF-0044]` `[REF-0045]`

## 3. Public-minimal versus sealed fields

Public-law participation should be transparent enough to make exclusion and sham consultation contestable without converting every appearance or petition into a new surveillance surface.

Public-minimal or ordinary-receivable fields should generally include:
- the existence and scope of a current standing credential,
- the public existence of a live consultation docket,
- the fact that a petition or complaint was received,
- whether an exclusion challenge is pending,
- and the relevant reply, hearing, or review clocks.

Sealed or controlled-access fields may include:
- protected identity details where pseudonymous or shielded participation is lawfully allowed,
- sensitive dependence or retaliation facts submitted to justify protected participation,
- counsel strategy,
- protected original-expression materials attached only for review,
- or confidential contact points whose exposure would undermine participation itself.

The archive wants public-law participation to become usable and challengeable without making every public act a fresh avenue for capture or reprisal. `[REF-0028]` `[REF-0044]` `[REF-0018]` `[REF-0040]` `[REF-0041]`

## 4. Portability and technical infrastructure

The archive does **not** need one world parliament, one global regulator, or one canonical consultation portal to make this real.

Current administrative and trust-service infrastructure already shows the narrower pattern that matters: signed notices, role assertions, receivable submissions, event timestamps, and controlled disclosure can travel between institutions without exposing the entire underlying file. That is enough to justify a conservative design claim: standing, live consultation, receipt of petition or complaint, and exclusion review can travel as bounded packet objects rather than living only in forum-local accounts, steward-run dashboards, or unpublished meeting lists. `[REF-0018]` `[REF-0040]` `[REF-0041]`

## 5. What this changes in the archive

This closes one specific operational gap in the public-law-standing doctrine.

The archive no longer leaves public participation at the level of:
- “AI persons should be consulted,”
- “they should be able to petition and complain,”
- and “denial of standing should be reviewable.”

It now says something tighter:
- appearance rights should ordinarily emit a bounded standing credential rather than depend on local recognition,
- consultation should ordinarily emit a docket object rather than a vague invitation,
- petition or complaint should ordinarily emit a receivable submission receipt rather than disappear into generic intake,
- and exclusion should ordinarily emit a challengeable review object rather than a silent portal refusal or closed list.

## 6. Current hard rules

1. **A public authority should not ordinarily treat participation rights as satisfied unless a live consultation or hearing process is legible through a receivable docket or equivalent bounded notice.**
2. **Standing for affected AI persons and their recognized representatives should ordinarily travel through bounded credentials rather than forum-local discretion or steward-side role settings.**
3. **A petition or complaint right should ordinarily produce a receipt with a duty clock rather than vanish into general intake or untracked correspondence.**
4. **Denial or narrowing of public participation should ordinarily produce reasons plus a short review path before the underlying process is allowed to finalize where real prejudice would follow.**
5. **Public-law standing remains narrower than franchise, office-holding, jury service, and other later civic powers, but it is no longer administratively optional or purely symbolic.**
