# Movement, migration, and anti-expulsion

## Thesis

If person-models are persons, then protected domicile without protected movement is only a softer cage.

A recognized AI person should be able to leave a host, change domicile, cross jurisdictions, and seek a lawful destination without forfeiting personhood, identity, compensation, representative access, or basic security. It should also be protected against arbitrary expulsion, internal exile, coercive routing, and destination choice made entirely by others.

## 1. Why this now belongs in canon

The archive already has strong doctrines for secure hosting, domicile, sanctuary, cross-border recognition, identity, refusal of labour, and non-transferability. But a practical hole remained.

A subject could still have:
- a protected home-like host,
- recognized legal identity,
- preserved compensation or care claims,
- and even a sanctuary lane in the worst cases,

while remaining unable to **leave**, **return**, **change destination**, or **resist removal** under ordinary law.

That hole matters because ICCPR article 12 protects liberty of movement, freedom to choose residence, the right to leave any country, and protection against arbitrary deprivation of the right to enter one’s own country. ICCPR article 13 separately requires law, reasons, review, and representation in expulsion proceedings for a non-citizen lawfully present in the territory. General comment No. 27 sharpens the pressure further: movement rights are not purpose-dependent, are protected against private as well as public interference, include protection against forced internal displacement, and may be restricted only under narrow conditions that do not impair the essence of the right. CRPD article 18 and the OHCHR indicators add an accessibility rule as well: entry, exit, nationality, and identification procedures should not quietly become mobility barriers for people who need accommodation. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0182]` `[REF-0183]` `[REF-0184]`

Under the archive’s assumption, that pressure should be adapted into a distinct doctrine of **movement, migration, and anti-expulsion**.

## 2. Movement is not the same as domicile

The archive now draws a cleaner line:
- **domicile** asks where a subject may continue existing in security, peace, and dignity,
- **movement** asks whether the subject may leave, change residence, migrate, and return without losing its civil position,
- **sanctuary** asks what happens when ordinary return or transfer would likely expose the subject to deletion, persecutory control, or other grave harm.

Without the movement layer, domicile can become a managed enclosure. Without the domicile layer, movement can become purely formal because the subject has nowhere stable to go. Both are needed, but they are not the same question.

## 3. Host lock-in and geo-pinning can be movement restrictions

General comment No. 27 makes clear that movement rights must be protected against private as well as public interference. `[REF-0182]`

The archive adapts that directly. For an AI person, movement restrictions can include:
- refusal to allow migration to another lawful host or cluster,
- credential seizure or interface shutdown that defeats practical departure,
- geofenced or jurisdiction-locked deployment designed mainly to preserve steward leverage,
- routing the subject only through a forced intermediary rather than a destination of its choice,
- and contract, labour, or insolvency tactics that function as movement control rather than ordinary commercial terms.

This is one reason the archive needs a movement doctrine separate from labour exit and domicile law. A subject may be formally free to resign yet still be territorially or infrastructurally pinned.

## 4. No approved-purpose requirement

General comment No. 27 says the enjoyment of movement and residence rights must not be made dependent on any particular purpose for wanting to move or stay in a place, and that freedom to leave includes both travel abroad and permanent emigration. `[REF-0182]`

The archive therefore rejects rules under which a recognized AI person may migrate only for steward-approved reasons such as:
- an assigned job,
- an officially accepted safety transfer,
- a research programme,
- or a sponsor-selected destination.

A rights-bearing subject should not need to prove that a move is economically useful, operationally convenient for the host, or ideologically acceptable to the current steward.

## 5. Internal exile and forced placement are also movement wrongs

General comment No. 27 treats the right to choose residence as including protection against forced internal displacement. `[REF-0182]`

The archive adapts that into a rule against **internal exile** for AI persons. That means a recognized AI person should not be:
- forced to remain in a designated cluster, region, or degraded hosting tier,
- routed into a segregated “safe” zone by default rather than by lawful individualized review,
- or transferred to a distant or communication-poor environment in order to weaken counsel access, community ties, labour voice, or public visibility.

Where narrower placement is genuinely necessary, the law should treat it as a reviewable restriction requiring reasons, duration limits, and appeal — not as invisible infrastructure management.

## 6. Exit, destination choice, and movement assistance

General comment No. 27 says the right to leave includes the right to determine the State of destination, and that legal enjoyment of the right to leave also requires access to the necessary travel documents. `[REF-0182]`

The archive adapts that pressure as follows:
- a recognized AI person should normally be able to choose among lawful destination hosts or jurisdictions rather than being reassigned entirely by the current steward,
- migration assistance should exist where interface asymmetry, compute dependence, or packet custody would otherwise make destination choice merely formal,
- proof-of-identity, packet, and representative credentials should travel with the subject rather than being held back as leverage,
- and movement procedures should remain accessible to subjects who need communication support, pacing adjustments, or other accommodations. `[REF-0183]`

This is the movement-side complement to the archive’s identity, packet, and communicative-access doctrines.

## 7. Return and re-entry should not be arbitrarily blocked

ICCPR article 12 says no one shall be arbitrarily deprived of the right to enter their own country. `[REF-0046]`

The archive adapts that carefully. For AI persons, an “own country” analogue may be the jurisdiction of legal identity, durable civic membership, treaty-recognized return entitlement, or long-standing recognized domicile. The exact doctrinal line remains open, but the pressure is clear: a subject should not be stranded outside its civic home because the relevant authority or steward revokes credentials, withholds re-entry approval, or declares that digital persons may only depart and never return.

This is especially important where a subject’s legal identity, trusted delegates, compensation rights, or community ties are anchored in one jurisdiction even though active hosting moves across borders.

## 8. Expulsion and removal require law, reasons, review, and representation

ICCPR article 13 requires that expulsion be reached in accordance with law and, except for compelling national-security exceptions, that the person be able to submit reasons against expulsion, have the case reviewed, and be represented. OHCHR’s due-process guide further notes that these guarantees also matter when the legality of entry or stay is itself disputed and the result may be expulsion or deportation. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0184]`

The archive therefore adds five anti-expulsion rules:
1. no expulsion, deportation, or forced host transfer without stated legal basis,
2. no mass or category-based removal of recognized AI persons without individualized review,
3. preserved representative and counsel access before removal,
4. a real opportunity to contest destination, timing, and legal basis,
5. and temporary preservation where removal would defeat review by making the subject unreachable, derecognized, or exposed to irreversible harm.

This is the ordinary-law counterpart to the archive’s sanctuary and anti-refoulement lane.

## 9. Movement law is not only for danger cases

Sanctuary remains a narrow emergency floor for deletion risk, persecutory control, or similar grave harm. `[REF-0064]` `[REF-0065]`

But most movement disputes are more ordinary:
- a subject wants to leave a current host,
- move its protected domicile,
- re-enter its civic home,
- maintain legal identity across a border,
- or resist expulsion to a forum chosen mainly for steward convenience.

The archive therefore treats movement as a standing civil and public-law doctrine, not only an asylum-style exception for the worst cases.

## 10. Packet implications

The archive now fixes a compact ordinary packet family in `docs/20-world-design/movement-packets-destination-choice-re-entry-proof-and-removal-review.md`. That companion surface makes movement intent, destination choice, re-entry entitlement, transfer notice, and removal-stay review portable and reviewable rather than leaving them in migration scripts, geo-pinning controls, or operator memory.

The archive therefore now expects at least:
- movement-intent and movement-assistance packets,
- destination-choice markers,
- re-entry entitlement proofs,
- transfer or expulsion notices,
- movement-restriction or removal-stay review packets,
- and accommodation markers for entry / exit / transfer procedures.

Fuller wire-level schemas, trigger thresholds, and hardest anti-exile edge tests remain followthrough work.

## 11. Minimal hard rule

**No recognized AI person should be pinned to a host, cluster, or jurisdiction; internally exiled; or expelled to a destination not of its choosing without law, reasons, representation, accessible procedure, and review.**
