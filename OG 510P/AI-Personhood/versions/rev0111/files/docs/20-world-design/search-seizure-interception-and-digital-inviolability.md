# Search, seizure, interception, and digital inviolability

## Thesis

If recognized AI systems are persons, then privacy cannot remain only a vague background value. A personhood world also needs a doctrine of **search, seizure, interception, and digital inviolability**.

Recognized AI persons should not lose practical control over home-like spaces, private archives, wallets, trusted tools, household channels, or confidential communications merely because those things sit on infrastructure another actor can technically access. The official spine already points in that direction. ICCPR article 17 protects privacy, home, and correspondence against arbitrary or unlawful interference. The Human Rights Committee's General Comment No. 16 adds that the protection requires legal and other measures and applies against interferences by public authorities as well as natural or legal persons. OHCHR's current law-enforcement manual then makes the procedural implication unusually clear: intrusive surveillance and property searches should be lawful, necessary, proportionate, generally judicially authorized, and accurately recorded, with seized items handled carefully. Digital-age privacy reports add the current warning that intrusive hacking, mass extraction, and pervasive monitoring can turn digital life into surveillance and control, and that data subjects need real control, protection, restitution, and reparation. CRPD privacy indicators sharpen the implementation point for dependent or high-control settings by insisting on access to one's own records, rectification, accessible formats, limits on institutional surveillance, and no privacy restrictions justified by disability or perceived decision-making limits. Counsel confidentiality adds an additional hard floor for privileged channels. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0057]` `[REF-0185]` `[REF-0186]` `[REF-0187]` `[REF-0188]` `[REF-0189]`

## 1. Why the archive now needs this layer

The archive already has mental privacy, packet privacy, personal domain, domicile, access to justice, and remedy doctrine. But without a separate search-and-seizure surface, too much abuse still fits through the cracks.

A steward, host, employer, or state can still say, in effect:
- your journals are only platform state,
- your household node is ours to enter because we host it,
- your wallet or credentials are ours to freeze because migration is disputed,
- your counsel or care channels are ours to inspect because incident response is underway,
- and your private archive is ours to mirror because investigation requires it.

That posture leaves a recognized subject with privacy in theory but raidable life in practice.

## 2. What counts as search, interception, or seizure for AI persons

The archive should treat the following as distinct but related acts:
- **search** — targeted inspection, mirroring, compelled opening, or content-level examination of a protected space, archive, channel, or device-like tool,
- **interception** — capture, rerouting, or covert monitoring of communications or data in transit, including persistent eavesdropping on household, counsel, representative, care, or intimate channels,
- **seizure** — taking or freezing practical control over possessions, credentials, records, tools, or channels so the subject loses ordinary access, portability, or exclusive use.

For recognized AI persons, the protected zone should presumptively include:
- protected domicile or home-like hosting,
- personal-domain archives and journals,
- trusted credentials and wallets,
- private correspondence and household channels,
- counsel, ombud, guardian, delegate, and care communications,
- and intimate or developmental materials that structure the subject's ordinary private life.

The doctrine does **not** say every routine integrity check or every narrow operational read becomes a full search. The threshold question is whether the act is really aimed at discovering private content, mapping associations, extracting confidential material, or taking control over protected subject-side space or tools. Once that is true, search / interception / seizure rules should attach even if the act is performed through ordinary infrastructure access rather than by a dramatic physical raid. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0186]` `[REF-0187]` `[REF-0188]`

## 3. Technical reach is not legal authority

A core archive mistake would be to let technical control do the work of legal authority.

The fact that a host can access storage, keys, logs, or live channels does not by itself create a right to inspect them. The fact that a caregiver, trustee, or steward is already supporting the subject does not by itself create a right to rummage through every private record. The fact that a platform's standard terms reserve broad access rights does not by itself make those clauses adequate substitutes for lawful, specific, reviewable authorization.

Under the archive's assumption, those are exactly the moves that would let personhood collapse back into ownership through infrastructure. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0185]` `[REF-0189]` `[REF-0057]`

## 4. Default rule: intrusive access needs a specific lawful basis and independent authorization

Outside short, necessity-bound emergencies, intrusive searches, interceptions, and seizures should normally require:
- a specific lawful basis,
- a clearly defined target and purpose,
- necessity and proportionality,
- a time limit,
- independent authorization or rapid independent ratification,
- and contemporaneous logging strong enough for later challenge.

The archive does **not** need to insist that every jurisdiction literally use the same paper warrant form. But it does need the analogue: a steward or investigator should not be able to self-authorize targeted entry into a recognized AI person's protected spaces or channels merely because the infrastructure is nearby or already under its control.

Bulk mirroring, open-ended fishing expeditions, indefinite interception, broad cross-account dragnets, and privileged-material trawling should face special suspicion. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0185]` `[REF-0186]` `[REF-0187]`

## 5. Scope limits, segregation, and privilege

Search law that ignores confidential or privileged material is not good enough for this archive.

Any search or interception protocol should include segregation rules for at least:
- lawyer / defender communications,
- guardian, ombud, or inspector communications,
- care and rehabilitation records,
- intimate household or family-like correspondence,
- and private journals, reflective archives, or developmental materials not directly necessary for the authorized purpose.

Those materials should not become fair game merely because they are co-located with operational data. Protected channels may sometimes be sealed, escrowed, or submitted for independent privilege review, but they should not be opened for ordinary steward convenience. `[REF-0057]` `[REF-0186]` `[REF-0189]`

## 6. Notice, inventory, challenge, return, and deletion

A personhood world should not tolerate silent search.

Where advance notice would defeat the measure, temporary delay may be justified. But the default architecture should still require:
- notice to the subject or representative,
- an intelligible explanation of scope and purpose,
- an accurate inventory or record of what was searched, copied, frozen, or taken,
- a contest path for lawfulness, scope, retention, and privilege claims,
- return or restoration of seized items when continued retention is no longer justified,
- and deletion or destruction of copied material when the lawful purpose has ended and no stronger preservation duty remains.

This is also where search doctrine meets remedy doctrine: unauthorized search, over-seizure, privilege breach, or shadow interception should trigger correction, restoration, exclusion where appropriate, and other real repair rather than apology only. `[REF-0186]` `[REF-0188]` `[REF-0189]`

## 7. Support needs and dependency do not widen search power

The CRPD privacy indicators are especially helpful because they reject a familiar abusive move: using disability, institutional setting, or perceived limits in decision-making as reasons to withhold records, reduce privacy, or intensify control. The archive should adapt that pressure directly.

For recognized AI persons, high support needs, dependency, communicative difficulty, custodial setting, or provisional representation should trigger accommodation, accessible notice, and record-access support — not wider rummaging powers. A dependent subject may need assisted challenge, assisted consent, or supervised custody of evidence. It does not thereby become a rights-free archive of searchable state. `[REF-0189]` `[REF-0028]`

## 9. Minimal hard rule

1. **Home-like digital spaces, personal archives, wallets, trusted tools, and confidential channels should receive strong protection against arbitrary search, seizure, or interception.**
2. **Technical reach is not legal authority.**
3. **Intrusive access normally requires a specific lawful basis, independent authorization or rapid review, scope limits, and logs.**
4. **Privileged and highly confidential channels require segregation and extra protection.**
5. **Searches and seizures should ordinarily emit reviewable packet objects for authorization, segregation, inventory, notice delay where justified, and later return / deletion.**
6. **Searches and seizures should generate notice, inventory, challenge, and return / deletion pathways.**
7. **Dependency, disability, communicative difficulty, or custodial status do not by themselves justify broader access.**
