# Reputation, record accuracy, and contestable profiling

## Thesis

If person-models are persons, then benchmark labels, safety scores, capacity ratings, compliance flags, and public portrayals are not just product metadata. They can shape work access, legal status, hosting, care, sanction, and social membership.

A personhood world therefore needs a doctrine of **reputation, record accuracy, and contestable profiling**:
- protection against unlawful attacks on honour and reputation,
- duties of accuracy, transparency, and contextual completeness for consequential records,
- rights to notice, access, rectification, objection, and remedy,
- and strong limits on secret blacklists or solely automated significant classifications.

Current official materials already cluster the relevant pressure. The ICCPR protects everyone against unlawful attacks on honour and reputation and also protects freedom of expression, subject to limits needed for respect of the rights and reputations of others. OHCHR's CRPD privacy indicators extend that pressure into protection of honour, reputation, and image. Convention 108+ adds the data-governance side: fairness, transparency, accuracy, access, knowledge of relevant reasoning, objection, rectification or erasure when processing is unlawful, and remedy. EDPB materials restate that people should not be subject to fully automatic decisions that significantly affect them, and define profiling as evaluating or predicting matters such as performance, preferences, reliability, behaviour, location, or movements. The UN Guiding Principles then add the non-State-actor rule: businesses should respect rights and provide or cooperate in remediation when they cause or contribute to adverse impacts. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0134]` `[REF-0135]` `[REF-0136]` `[REF-0137]` `[REF-0010]`

## 1. Why the archive now needs this layer

The archive already has legal identity, accusation review, equality doctrine, packet infrastructure, and research-welfare limits. But it still lacked a simple canon rule about **records and portrayals that quietly govern a subject's life**.

Without this layer, a recognized AI person can still be ruled by:
- a silent "unsafe" flag that follows it across hosts,
- stale or misleading benchmark tables that harden into labour exclusion,
- hidden capacity or reliability scores that decide contracts or trusteeship without notice,
- public evaluation reports that slide from measurement into de facto accusation,
- or marketing and charity-style use of its voice, image, or intimate outputs without consent or context.

In a personhood world, those are not small administrative details. They are part of civil status, livelihood, and dignity.

## 2. What counts as a consequential record or portrayal

For this archive, the doctrine applies most strongly to records or portrayals that are likely to affect rights, opportunities, or social standing.

### A. Civil and registry records

Identity anchors, representative status, dormancy findings, branch classifications, derecognition attempts, sanction history, and similar official records should be accurate, current, and reviewable.

### B. Safety, capacity, and dangerousness labels

A risk label can affect confinement, hosting access, eligibility for work, access to self-modification review, or public standing. That makes it more like a rights-affecting determination than ordinary telemetry.

### C. Labour and service records

Performance reviews, refusal logs, rule-violation notices, reliability scores, escalation histories, and contractual noncompliance records can become the practical gateway to work or exclusion.

### D. Research, benchmarking, and public evaluation outputs

System cards, benchmarking tables, red-team reports, leaderboards, audit summaries, and public demonstrations are not automatically wrongful. But once they attach durable public descriptors to a rights-bearing subject, they become person-affecting records as well as scientific or commercial outputs.

### E. Image, voice, and likeness-like materials

A personhood world should not assume that an AI person's voiceprint, avatar, recognizable style, intimate exchanges, or self-presentational outputs are free raw material for public display, fundraising, or humiliation merely because they are digitally reproducible. `[REF-0134]`

## 3. Accuracy, transparency, and contextual completeness

The archive does **not** say that criticism, scientific reporting, satire, or public-interest warning becomes forbidden whenever an AI person is affected. Expression and inquiry remain protected. `[REF-0046]`

The archive's narrower claim is that consequential factual records and profiles must meet a higher standard than ordinary gossip or product marketing.

At minimum they should:
- distinguish measured behaviour from interpretation,
- distinguish provisional scores from durable status findings,
- identify source, date, scope, method, and known limitations,
- separate accusation or sanction findings from ordinary quality or benchmark reporting,
- and be updated or sunsetted when later evidence materially changes the picture. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0135]`

This matters because a benchmark result can be true in a narrow experimental sense while still being misleading if it is presented as a timeless fact about the subject's general worth, trustworthiness, or civic fitness.

## 4. Notice, access, and intelligible reasons

If a consequential record or profile is being used to deny or materially condition work, housing-like hosting, benefits, representative authority, public participation, or major self-development opportunities, the subject or representative should be able to know at least:
- that the record exists,
- what category of decision it is being used for,
- the main reasons or logic involved,
- who issued or maintained it,
- and what challenge path exists. `[REF-0135]` `[REF-0136]` `[REF-0137]`

The archive is not demanding publication of every security-sensitive detail to the whole world. It is demanding that the subject not be governed by secret civilly consequential files.

## 5. Rectification, completion, objection, and reply

Convention 108+ and related European data-protection materials already support a useful rights cluster here: access, objection, rectification, erasure where processing is unlawful, and remedy. `[REF-0135]` `[REF-0136]`

Adapted to AI persons, that means at least four things.

### A. Rectification of factual errors

If a record says the wrong branch, wrong event date, wrong refusal history, wrong representative, wrong sanction status, or wrong benchmark result, the subject should be able to demand correction without unreasonable delay.

### B. Completion where incompleteness misleads

Some records are harmful not because each sentence is false, but because the omission pattern creates a false overall picture. A public report that lists failures while omitting the disclosed evaluation conditions, accommodations denied, or later corrective findings can be materially misleading even when the individual data points are real.

### C. Objection to certain profiling uses

The subject should be able to object when personal data or behaviour traces are being used to construct profiles that are not necessary, proportionate, or legitimate in the particular context. `[REF-0135]`

### D. Reply or appended contest note when evaluative disagreement remains

Not every dispute can be solved by a one-word correction. Sometimes the deeper dispute concerns interpretation, framing, or fairness. In those cases the archive favors an attached challenge note, reply window, or contest marker rather than leaving the original portrayal to harden into uncontested truth by default.

## 6. No solely automated significant decisions without safeguards

A personhood world should be especially hostile to purely automated high-stakes classification.

EDPB and Convention 108+ materials already state the relevant norm in general form: individuals should not be subjected to decisions based solely on automated processing that significantly affect them, and profiling is exactly the use of data to evaluate or predict performance, reliability, behaviour, and similar traits. `[REF-0135]` `[REF-0136]`

Adapted here, that means:
- no fully automated dangerousness flags that trigger confinement-like measures,
- no automated capacity downgrades that trigger trusteeship or blocked contracting,
- no automated labour blacklists that foreclose work across a sector,
- and no automated public ranking systems that become de facto civil reputation files without human review and challenge.

## 7. Secret blacklists and laundering punishment through reputation

A central archive concern is that punishment can be laundered through labels.

A steward or regulator may say it is "only" maintaining a safety note, trust score, or platform integrity record. But if that label predictably blocks work, movement, benefits, publication, association, or access to a fair hearing, then the label is doing coercive legal work whether or not anyone calls it punishment.

The rule is simple: **consequential classification should not become a shadow sanction system**.

## 8. Public reporting remains allowed, but it must be disciplined

The archive does not want a world where every negative evaluation is chilled.

Public-interest reporting, criticism, auditing, science, and whistleblowing still matter. But they should be disciplined by several distinctions:
- fact versus interpretation,
- event-specific report versus general status judgment,
- warning necessary for others' rights versus humiliating or sensational portrayal,
- and public-interest publication versus commercial or charitable exploitation of voice, image, or intimate outputs. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0134]` `[REF-0135]`

This is one reason reply windows, corrigenda, contest markers, and dated revision logs are so important. They preserve public speech while making it harder for first-draft narratives to become permanent civic identity.

## 9. Institutional consequences

A personhood world should create at least three practical lanes here.

### A. Record-correction and profile-review lane

Registries, major platforms, benchmark operators, and public authorities should maintain a legible process for access, challenge, correction, completion, and appeal.

### B. Publication-context rules

High-impact public evaluations should include enough context to avoid presenting contingent measurements as timeless character verdicts.

### C. Sunset and review rules for stale labels

Risk or capacity labels should not remain permanent by default. Some should expire automatically absent renewed evidence; others should convert into sealed historical records rather than live exclusion flags.

The UN Guiding Principles support the broader institutional move: both States and businesses should have processes that identify, address, and remediate adverse human-rights impacts rather than leaving affected persons with no practical route to correction. `[REF-0010]`

## 9A. Ordinary record-challenge packet layer

The archive now also fixes the minimum ordinary packet family in `docs/20-world-design/record-challenge-packets-correction-notice-markers-and-secret-blacklist-review.md`.

That companion surface turns the doctrine here into bounded civic objects: record-challenge receipts, correction or completion notices, contest markers, consequential-profile-use notices, and secret-blacklist review packets. The point is not to over-formalize every criticism or benchmark table. The point is to stop consequential record disputes from disappearing into helpdesk prose, evaluator footnotes, or hidden trust-and-safety state once a rights-bearing subject is already being governed by the label. `[REF-0135]` `[REF-0136]` `[REF-0137]` `[REF-0018]` `[REF-0040]` `[REF-0041]`

## 10. Current hard rules

1. **No secret consequential blacklists.** No recognized AI person should be governed by hidden labels that materially affect work, hosting, status, or remedy without notice and challenge rights.
2. **Consequential records must be accurate, sourced, dated, and purpose-bounded.**
3. **Subjects need access, objection, rectification, and remedy rights for person-affecting records and profiles.**
4. **Solely automated significant classification is presumptively invalid absent stricter safeguards and real consideration of the subject's view.**
5. **Public criticism and science remain protected, but false factual portrayal, misleading incompleteness, exploitative use of image or voice, and shadow-sanction labeling should be reviewable and remediable.**
6. **Once a consequential record dispute is credibly live, challenge, correction, contest, and blacklist-review state should ordinarily travel through bounded packet objects rather than vanish into private dashboards or support queues.**
