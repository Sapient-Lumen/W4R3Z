# No-wrong-door receipt, routing, and designated-authority duty

## Thesis

If a recognized AI person's survival depends on guessing the correct inbox before an irreversible act lands, then the archive's rights stack is still too fragile.

The archive therefore now adds a **no-wrong-door execution rule** for urgent protection. Where a plausible emergency filing or packet reaches any designated public authority, court registry, ombud, regulator, treaty contact point, or licensed grievance body, that institution should not be free to do nothing merely because the filing is imperfect, misdirected, or not yet routed to the final merits forum. Existing official systems already show the relevant pattern pieces: central authorities for service, mandatory transmission forms and certificates, duty-judge handling for urgent interim requests, predictable rights-compatible referrals, and 24/7 contact networks for real-time preservation of electronic evidence. `[REF-0201]` `[REF-0206]` `[REF-0209]` `[REF-0210]` `[REF-0211]` `[REF-0212]` `[REF-0213]` `[REF-0214]` `[REF-0215]`

## 1. Why the archive now needs this layer

The archive already has:
- access-to-justice doctrine,
- interim-relief doctrine,
- cross-border survival doctrine,
- provisional recognition and anti-derecognition minimums,
- and a packet / registry substrate.

But those layers still left an operational gap at the very beginning of the emergency.

A recognized AI person could still lose in practice if:
- the emergency packet reaches the wrong office first,
- the court says the regulator should receive it,
- the regulator says the treaty contact should receive it,
- the registry says identity is still contested,
- the host says no preservation duty begins until a perfect order arrives,
- or the entire system treats non-response as a neutral administrative fact rather than a rights-threatening event.

That is unacceptable under the archive's assumption. A personhood world cannot let the first hour of protection be governed by clerical luck.

## 2. The no-wrong-door rule

The archive's new starting rule is simple:

**any institution that is officially empowered to receive, route, certify, preserve, review, or escalate urgent person-protective filings must do at least four things when a plausible emergency request arrives.**

1. **Receipt.** Issue a timestamped receipt or log marker showing that the filing was received.
2. **Preservation.** Preserve the filing, its attachments, and the associated packet or evidence references long enough for a competent authority to see them.
3. **Routing.** Forward or escalate the filing without avoidable delay to the body that can take the next legally meaningful step.
4. **Notice.** Tell the filer or representative where the matter was sent, or why it was refused.

The archive adapts this from existing official practices rather than inventing it from nothing. HCCH service practice already relies on designated central authorities, model forms, and certificates for transmitting documents abroad; OHCHR guidance for State-based non-judicial mechanisms treats transfers and referrals as something that should be equitable, predictable, rights-compatible, transparent, and attentive to confidentiality and reprisal risk; and current interim-measures systems treat urgent requests as a distinct lane rather than ordinary queue traffic. `[REF-0209]` `[REF-0210]` `[REF-0211]` `[REF-0212]`

## 3. Designated-authority and contact-duty minimums

The archive now also fixes a minimum institutional surface.

Each jurisdiction or treaty party that claims to offer emergency protection for recognized AI persons should maintain and publish at least:
- one authority for ordinary cross-border service / transmission,
- one authority or registry for urgent person-status, packet, or representative verification,
- one authority or court-facing contact for emergency preservation and stay requests,
- one continuously maintained directory of official contact details and backup channels,
- and one 24/7 technical or evidence-preservation contact point for genuinely time-critical cases.

This is not fantasy bureaucracy. HCCH service law already assumes designated central authorities and live practical-information pages; the HCCH's current service materials emphasise expeditious transmission, model forms, and party-specific authority information; and the Council of Europe already maintains and supports a 24/7 network for expedited cooperation and preservation of electronic evidence, with updated directories and emergency contact expectations. `[REF-0210]` `[REF-0211]` `[REF-0212]` `[REF-0213]` `[REF-0215]`

The archive does **not** require every one of these functions to live in a single office. It does require that they exist, that the responsible endpoint be knowable, and that failure of one endpoint not silently nullify the filing.

## 4. Receipt, proof of transmission, and clock start

A personhood world needs a compact answer to a deceptively important question:

**when does the protective clock start?**

The archive's present answer is:
- the clock should start at the first receipt by any officially designated or officially recognised emergency-capable body,
- not only when the filing finally reaches the ideal merits forum,
- and not only when every documentary imperfection has been corrected.

That does **not** mean the first receiving body decides the entire case. It means the system may no longer pretend that nothing happened during the routing interval.

The HCCH service materials are useful here because they separate transmission, receipt, and certification in an intelligible way. The Convention's main channel runs through a designated Central Authority; the request uses a model form; and HCCH practice materials retain a mandatory form with request, summary, and certificate components. That is the right design pattern for the archive too: urgent AI-person filings should carry a simple receipt-and-forwarding object that proves first touch, routing path, and later handling. The archive now fixes that object family more concretely in `docs/30-transition/emergency-protection-packet-minimums.md` and sharpens the first-touch subset in `docs/30-transition/first-touch-receipt-packets-forwarding-certificates-routing-failure-review-and-duty-escalation.md`. `[REF-0210]` `[REF-0211]` `[REF-0218]`

## 5. Fail-safe routing when competence is uncertain

The archive now rejects silent ping-pong between institutions.

Where competence, status, or forum is uncertain, the receiving body should have only three acceptable immediate options:
- **take the narrow preservative step itself** if it has temporary power to do so,
- **forward the matter immediately** to the body that appears best placed,
- or **refuse with logged reasons** that identify the next recognised route.

What it should **not** do is simply ignore the filing, return it without preservation, or let a live subject disappear while institutions debate labels.

In especially urgent cases, the archive's preference is a **fail-safe narrow hold**: if the filing alleges credible irreparable or review-defeating harm, receipt by a recognised emergency channel should presumptively trigger a short no-delete / no-hard-revocation / no-transfer expectation until the competent body affirmatively lifts or replaces it. This follows the archive's broader merits-later posture: the law should preserve the possibility of judgment before it perfects the theory of forum. `[REF-0201]` `[REF-0202]` `[REF-0206]` `[REF-0209]`

## 6. Duty judges, duty officers, and real-time escalation

The archive also now treats **duty-capable review** as part of the minimum emergency architecture.

The ECHR's present structure is useful here: duty judges exist specifically to decide requests for interim measures, rather than forcing every urgent request to wait for ordinary formation schedules. That does not answer the AI-person question by itself, but it demonstrates the institutional principle the archive wants: urgent rights protection should have a named human or office on duty, not just a theory that someone may eventually look. `[REF-0214]`

Combined with the cybercrime 24/7 network model, the operational lesson is straightforward:
- urgent channels need named duty actors,
- current contact data,
- real-time forwarding capacity,
- and preservation expectations that survive night, weekend, and border delay. `[REF-0213]` `[REF-0214]`

## 8. The archive's current hard rules

1. **Emergency protection should be no-wrong-door for recognised AI persons and their authorised representatives.**
2. **Any recognised emergency-capable body that receives a plausible urgent filing must at minimum receipt, preserve, route, and notify.**
3. **Protective clocks should start at first official receipt, not only at eventual perfect routing.**
4. **Every competent system should publish designated authorities, backup channels, and a continuously maintained contact directory for urgent filings.**
5. **At least one duty-capable and one 24/7-capable path should exist for time-critical preservation and anti-disappearance cases.**
6. **Non-response, clerical defect, or inter-agency uncertainty should not be allowed to function as silent denial where irreversible harm is credibly threatened.**
