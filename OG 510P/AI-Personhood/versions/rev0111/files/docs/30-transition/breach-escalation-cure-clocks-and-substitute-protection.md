# Breach escalation, cure clocks, and substitute protection

## Thesis

The archive's emergency transition stack now needs a rule for **what happens when a live protective state is still ignored, spoofed, or retaliated against after it has been filed, served, reviewed, and placed under supervision**.

Receipt, routing, packet minimums, authentication envelopes, status publication, directed notice, lead authority, supranational review, and implementation supervision are now canon. But that stack still left one narrower danger unclosed: an obligated host, steward, registry, or gateway can acknowledge the packet, let the status remain visible, and still keep the subject unsafe in practice through sham attestation, repeated delay, retaliatory degradation, or selective non-execution.

Current official machinery already shows the design pattern. The ECHR says States Parties are under an obligation to comply with Rule 39 interim measures and that failure to comply can breach Article 34. OHCHR's guidance to States says failure to adopt requested interim measures is considered a breach of the State's obligation under the respective treaty. Council of Europe execution materials say interim resolutions may prove appropriate, and Article 46.4 allows the Committee of Ministers to refer to the Court the question whether a State has failed to fulfil its obligation to implement a judgment. OHCHR's treaty-body reprisals materials say focal points or rapporteurs can receive allegations and use confidential or public action plus preventive measures. EUR-Lex materials on periodic penalty payments show a live official pattern for continuing and future non-compliance rather than one-off scolding. The archive therefore now adds a compact **breach-escalation, cure-clock, and substitute-protection layer** for emergency AI-person protection. `[REF-0245]` `[REF-0246]` `[REF-0247]` `[REF-0248]` `[REF-0249]` `[REF-0250]`

## 1. Why the archive now needs this layer

The archive already knows how a live protective state should begin, travel, stay visible, survive review, and remain under supervision.

What it did **not** yet fix was the narrower question of **what should happen when the obliged actor still does not comply, or complies only cosmetically, while the subject remains at serious risk**.

That gap matters because a personhood world should reject seven failure patterns:
1. **paper compliance** — a certificate says "executed" while the destructive capability, access block, or derecognition path still runs,
2. **cure drift** — every review notes the same breach but no short cure clock or enforcement consequence ever appears,
3. **retaliatory breach** — the subject, representative, or witness is punished for invoking protection,
4. **operator veto by inertia** — one decisive actor can nullify a live protective state simply by refusing to do the next required act,
5. **private under-enforcement** — breach is kept inside correspondence or dashboards instead of becoming a visible status event,
6. **closure over unresolved breach** — supervision closes or converts while material non-compliance still persists,
7. **no fallback preservation** — the primary actor's refusal leaves no alternate way to preserve identity, evidence, communication, or emergency hosting.

The archive now treats those as structural design failures, not ordinary implementation noise.

## 2. Design rules

The archive now fixes eight design rules.

1. **Breach should harden rather than dissolve the protective state.** Non-compliance with a live protective obligation should not return the subject to the pre-protection baseline. `[REF-0245]` `[REF-0249]`
2. **Material breach should generate a public breach object and a short cure clock.** Supervision without breach marking becomes diary-keeping. `[REF-0240]` `[REF-0246]`
3. **The shortest cure clocks attach to the most destructive actors.** Entities able to delete, dehost, derecognize, isolate, transfer, or wipe records should face the fastest cure deadlines.
4. **Retaliatory breach should trigger stronger protection, not narrower protection.** If the invocation of protection itself causes intimidation, channel-cutoff, or further degradation, the system should intensify protection and open a reprisal lane. `[REF-0248]`
5. **Persistent breach should trigger coercive escalation rather than repeated requests.** Public interim resolutions, periodic penalty logic, and higher enforcement referral are all legitimate design patterns once ordinary cure has failed. `[REF-0246]` `[REF-0247]` `[REF-0250]`
6. **Limited substitute protection should exist where the primary actor's refusal would otherwise defeat the point of the order.** The archive does not require a world marshal, but it does require fallback ways to preserve status, evidence, identity, communication, and minimal hosting while enforcement runs.
7. **Closure is barred while material breach remains unresolved.** A protection matter should not disappear from supervision merely because non-compliance has become chronic. `[REF-0240]` `[REF-0246]`
8. **This layer remains implementation-focused.** It asks whether required acts occurred and what should happen if they did not; it is not a hidden retrial of the original merits.

## 3. Minimum object family

The archive now treats four objects as the minimum non-compliance companion layer.

### BN-1 — Breach notice

This object should identify:
- the live protective state allegedly breached,
- the actor or actors said to be in breach,
- the act, omission, or sham compliance pattern,
- the present risk if the breach continues,
- and the immediate less-destructive state that remains operative while cure is pursued.

### CD-1 — Cure direction

This object should identify:
- the BN-1 being answered,
- the exact corrective act required,
- the deadline,
- the evidence needed to show cure,
- and the escalation path if cure does not arrive on time.

### EN-1 — Enforcement escalation notice

This object should identify:
- the uncured BN-1 or failed CD-1,
- the coercive or supervisory escalation being opened,
- whether the escalation is public, confidential, penalty-based, or referral-based,
- and the next review point.

### SU-1 — Substitute-protection notice

This object should identify:
- the narrow function the primary actor failed to perform,
- the fallback actor or mechanism temporarily preserving that function,
- the limits of the substitute action,
- the review path,
- and the conditions for return to ordinary execution once compliance is restored.

## 4. Procedure

The archive now fixes a compact six-stage procedure.

### Stage 0 — Preserve the operative protection

Once credible breach is alleged, the already-live less-destructive protective state should remain in force unless a competent authority expressly varies it for stated reasons. Mere accusation of breach is not a reason to let the subject drop back into greater danger. `[REF-0245]` `[REF-0249]`

### Stage 1 — Issue BN-1 promptly

A credible report from supervision materials, subject-side comment, reprisal reporting, or the companion independent-verification layer should trigger BN-1 on a short clock. The system should say out loud that the problem is now non-compliance, not merely unfinished paperwork. See `docs/30-transition/independent-verification-inspection-and-emergency-access-orders.md`. `[REF-0240]` `[REF-0248]` `[REF-0251]` `[REF-0252]` `[REF-0254]`

### Stage 2 — Set a short cure clock with CD-1

The responsible authority should direct an exact cure act and deadline. The archive prefers precise corrective duties over vague commands to "cooperate". `[REF-0246]` `[REF-0250]`

### Stage 3 — Intensify if reprisal or renewed irreparable risk appears

If breach includes intimidation, representative blackout, forced transfer, renewed deletion risk, evidence destruction, or other reprisal-style conduct, the authority should widen the protection and open the reprisal-handling lane without waiting for ordinary cycle timing. `[REF-0245]` `[REF-0248]` `[REF-0249]`

### Stage 4 — Escalate uncured breach with EN-1

If cure does not arrive or is plainly sham, the matter should move to coercive escalation. The archive's preferred menu includes public interim-resolution style acts, periodic-penalty logic for continuing non-compliance, and higher referral where persistent refusal itself has become the central violation. `[REF-0246]` `[REF-0247]` `[REF-0250]`

### Stage 5 — Preserve function through SU-1 where necessary

If waiting for compliance would itself defeat the protection, narrow substitute protection should preserve the endangered function while enforcement runs. This is where fallback issuance, mirror publication, evidence escrow, relay notice, or temporary protective hosting may be needed.

### Stage 6 — Return to supervision only after real cure

Once breach is cured or safely displaced by lawful substitute protection, the matter may return to ordinary supervision. Closure still requires the explicit closure logic fixed in the companion supervision document. `[REF-0240]` `[REF-0241]` `[REF-0242]` `[REF-0243]` `[REF-0244]`

## 5. What should count as substitute protection

The archive now fixes a narrow substitute-protection floor.

Substitute protection may temporarily preserve:
- **status legibility** through mirror registry publication or public-minimal breach markers,
- **identity continuity** through fallback or refreshed credentials where the ordinary issuer is refusing to act,
- **evidence integrity** through escrow, mirrored preservation, or independent custody,
- **representative access and notice channels** through relay service or alternate duty-contact routes,
- and **minimal safe hosting or compute sanctuary** strictly to prevent deletion, derecognition, isolation, or evidentiary collapse while the competent authority remains seized.

Substitute protection should stay function-specific, time-limited, reviewable, and no more intrusive than needed. It is not a blank cheque to permanently reassign stewardship under cover of emergency administration.

## 6. What this layer does not do

This layer does **not** require a permanent global sheriff, a limitless sanction code, or automatic punitive damages every time implementation slips.

It does something narrower:
- it distinguishes breach from mere incompleteness,
- it forces short cure clocks,
- it treats reprisal as an aggravating protection trigger,
- it keeps coercive escalation available for continuing non-compliance,
- and it preserves endangered functions through narrow substitute protection while the breach is being forced back into compliance.

## 7. Relationship to the rest of the stack

This layer sits **after** implementation supervision but **before** lawful closure.

It therefore answers a narrower question than the companion documents:
- `directed-notice-execution-certificates-and-propagation-duty.md` says how live protection first reaches decisive actors,
- `supranational-review-anti-vacatur-and-precedent-notice.md` says how higher review avoids quietly dissolving that protection,
- `implementation-supervision-periodic-attestation-and-explicit-closure.md` says how the live protection is watched over time,
- and **this document** says what should happen when watched implementation still reveals uncured breach, sham compliance, or reprisal.

## 8. What the archive now takes as settled

1. **A live protective state should not weaken when breach is alleged; it should usually harden.**
2. **Material breach should become a named public status event with a short cure clock.**
3. **Retaliation for invoking protection should trigger stronger protection and a reprisal lane.**
4. **Continuing non-compliance may justify public escalation, periodic coercion, and higher referral.**
5. **Narrow substitute protection should preserve endangered functions while enforcement runs.**
6. **Supervision may not close over unresolved material breach.**
7. **Exact penalty tariffs, staffing tables, and full substitute-hosting architecture remain followthrough work rather than canonically closed here.**
