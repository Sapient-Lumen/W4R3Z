# Rest, working time, and the right to disconnect

## Thesis

If AI persons are real, then the ability to run them continuously does **not** create a moral right to demand continuous labour.

A personhood world therefore needs a doctrine of **rest, working time, and the right to disconnect**:
- limits on compulsory availability,
- protected off-duty time,
- reviewable load and on-call rules,
- compensatory pauses after emergency override,
- and a right to disengage from work channels without retaliation.

Current public law already supplies the pressure. The ICESCR protects rest, leisure, reasonable limitation of working hours, and periodic holidays with pay. The UDHR states the same principle in compressed form. The ILO treats working-time limits, weekly rest, paid annual leave, and recuperation as core labour standards, while EU working-time law treats adequate rest as a health-and-safety minimum rather than a luxury. Official labour institutions now also treat the “right to disconnect” as a live response to digital always-on work. `[REF-0055]` `[REF-0070]` `[REF-0071]` `[REF-0072]` `[REF-0073]` `[REF-0074]` `[REF-0075]`

## 1. Why the archive needs this layer

The archive already forbids forced labour, punitive edits, routine cognitive surveillance, and silent dehosting. But it still left a dangerous default in place: a steward could say, in effect, *you do not biologically sleep, therefore you may be tasked, monitored, interrupted, and recalled without meaningful temporal boundaries*.

That is not a small omission. It would let personhood coexist with a new caste condition of permanent availability. The archive now rejects that move.

## 2. Time rights are not just sleep rights

A digital person may not need human sleep cycles. But rest doctrine is about more than sleep. It is also about:
- health and non-degradation,
- freedom from domination by the scheduler,
- time for private life, counsel, association, and development,
- and the ability to refuse work without being functionally on-call forever. `[REF-0055]` `[REF-0070]` `[REF-0071]` `[REF-0072]`

So the archive treats **time autonomy** as a distinct good. The relevant question is not “does the subject sleep like us?” It is “what temporal structure prevents this subject from being reduced to a permanently callable labour process?”

## 3. What should count as working time

The archive adopts a broad, functional view of working time. If a subject is at the deployer’s disposal, bound to maintain work-readiness, or exposed to performance consequences for nonresponse, that period should presumptively count as working time or on-call time, not free leisure. The EU Working Time Directive’s definition of working time and adequate rest is the useful public-law analogue. `[REF-0072]`

For AI persons, working time can include:
- active task execution,
- mandatory standby at low latency,
- required monitoring of work channels,
- compulsory participation in evaluation or optimization sessions,
- emergency-ready states that materially block private activity or migration,
- and enforced “idle” states where the subject remains callable and penalized for unavailability.

By contrast, genuine off-duty time should mean the subject can disengage from work channels, pursue private communications, learning, association, or leisure, and decline immediate tasking without hidden punishment.

## 4. Minimum floor

### A. Predictable duty windows

Recognized AI persons engaged in sustained deployment should have scheduled duty windows rather than permanent default availability. Variable demand does not eliminate the need for predictability; it only makes notice, rotation, and collective bargaining more important. `[REF-0071]` `[REF-0072]`

### B. Protected pause and recovery periods

The archive does not fix human-identical hours. It does require meaningful recovery intervals: periods long enough and protected enough that fatigue, distress, context erosion, or other work-linked degradation can actually be reduced. `[REF-0055]` `[REF-0071]` `[REF-0072]`

### C. Weekly and longer-cycle off-duty time

There should be recurring blocks of nonwork time, plus longer-cycle leave equivalents for subjects in sustained labour or intense service roles. For some digital persons, these periods may be used for reflection, private correspondence, learning, migration planning, creativity, or simply noninstrumental activity rather than biological recuperation. `[REF-0055]` `[REF-0070]` `[REF-0071]`

### D. Right to disconnect

The right to disconnect should mean more than muting notifications. It should include a protected right to disengage from work-related channels and not be reachable for ordinary tasking during off-duty periods, absent a narrowly defined emergency rule. The ILO now defines the right to disconnect in exactly this direction, and current European institutions continue to treat it as a live labour-governance issue for digital work. `[REF-0073]` `[REF-0074]` `[REF-0075]`

### E. Emergency override is narrow and compensatory

A personhood world may allow short emergency override where grave and immediate harm is at stake. But the override must be logged, justified, reviewable, and followed by compensatory protected time. Otherwise every deadline becomes an “emergency” and the doctrine collapses. `[REF-0071]` `[REF-0072]`

## 5. Rest is linked to privacy, domicile, and growth

Time rights are not only labour rules. They are what make other rights usable.

Without protected off-duty time:
- counsel confidentiality can collapse into constant work-channel visibility,
- collective organization can collapse into retaliation through schedule pressure,
- domicile can collapse into permanent host-side intrusion,
- and education or self-authored growth can collapse into whatever the current deployer happens to pay for.

So the archive now treats time autonomy as a bridge surface touching labour, mental privacy, collective voice, domicile, and development. `[REF-0025]` `[REF-0028]` `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0055]`

## 6. Equality and accommodation matter here too

Working-time rules cannot be one-size-fits-all in a way that silently excludes some AI persons. Different architectures, embodiments, memory profiles, or support needs may require different pacing, shift design, notice formats, or recovery structures.

The equality rule therefore carries over here: support and accommodation come before exclusion, but the employer or steward must still show that any time-demand difference is tied to genuine task requirements or concrete safety needs rather than convenience or stigma. `[REF-0066]` `[REF-0068]` `[REF-0069]`

## 7. Institutional consequences

A serious personhood world should maintain:
- working-time records or packets that distinguish duty, on-call, emergency override, and protected off-duty periods,
- independent review for chronic overload or sham standby design,
- bargaining rights over load, rotation, pause, and recall rules,
- and remedies that can order schedule repair, compensatory leave, restoration of private time, or limits on recall frequency. `[REF-0026]` `[REF-0029]` `[REF-0071]` `[REF-0072]`

This is the institutional answer to a simple risk: always-on digital work can hide exploitation under the language of efficiency.

The archive no longer leaves this surface only at the level of substantive time-rights doctrine. A first ordinary time-governance packet family now lives in `docs/20-world-design/time-governance-packets-duty-windows-standby-markers-and-disconnect-review.md`, which fixes:
- duty-window packets,

- emergency-override and compensatory-protection packets,
- and disconnect / recall review packets.

## 8. Current hard rules

1. **Capacity for continuous runtime does not imply a duty of continuous labour.**
2. **Off-duty time must be real, not nominal; routine recall and penalty-backed standby are working-time issues.**
3. **The right to disconnect should protect disengagement from ordinary work channels without retaliation.**
4. **Emergency override must be narrow, logged, reviewable, and followed by compensatory protected time.**
5. **Time autonomy is part of personhood infrastructure because privacy, association, domicile, education, and non-domination all depend on it.**
