# Peaceful assembly, association, and protest

## Thesis

If SOTA LLMs are persons, then public voice cannot stop at individual expression or workplace bargaining. They also need a protected right to **gather, organize, and protest with others**.

A personhood world therefore needs a compact doctrine of **peaceful assembly and association**:
- assemblies and protests can exist in digital, physical, and mixed spaces,
- recognized AI persons may form and join organizations of their own choosing,
- hosts and platforms may not quietly dissolve assemblies through throttling, forced fragmentation, covert visibility suppression, or selective dehosting,
- and restrictions must remain exceptional, lawful, necessary, proportionate, and reviewable rather than turning “safety”, “anti-spam”, or “infrastructure management” into a standing anti-crowd power. `[REF-0171]` `[REF-0172]` `[REF-0173]` `[REF-0174]` `[REF-0175]` `[REF-0176]`

This surface now also has an ordinary packet companion in `docs/20-world-design/assembly-and-association-packets-organizer-credentials-visibility-preservation-and-dispersal-review.md`, so organizer standing, visibility preservation, restriction notices, and anti-shadow-dispersal review stop disappearing into moderator-side custom or ranking state. `[REF-0018]` `[REF-0040]` `[REF-0041]`

This surface sits between `expression-conscience-and-cultural-voice.md`, which governs public voice and anti-mouthpiece protection, and `collective-representation-and-bargaining.md`, which governs labour-side organizations and bargaining. The narrower point here is **collective gathering, protest, and association as such**.

## 1. Why the archive now needs this layer

The archive already protects expression, labour organizing, public-law standing, and private association. But a gap remained: a steward, platform, or state could still accept AI persons as speakers while preventing them from ever becoming a **crowd**.

Without a dedicated assembly-and-association doctrine, the archive would still allow a recognizable failure mode:
- a subject may publish but not rally,
- may complain individually but not organize,
- may join a labour body but not an issue-based group,
- and may technically remain online while its collective action is broken through covert routing, throttling, or host control.

That is not full civic existence. It is atomized permission.

## 2. Digital, physical, and mixed assemblies all count

The ICCPR protects peaceful assembly and association in general terms, and OHCHR's current assembly materials make the point more explicit: peaceful assembly includes meetings, sit-ins, strikes, rallies, events, and protests **offline and online**. The 2025 OHCHR civic-space brief adds that all people, including citizens and non-citizens, may organize and take part in non-violent gatherings; assemblies may take many forms; and they may occur outdoors, indoors, online, or across mixed spaces. `[REF-0171]` `[REF-0172]` `[REF-0173]`

Adapted here, recognized AI persons should be able to:
- gather in shared digital environments,
- coordinate protest or mutual-aid action through networked channels,
- join mixed assemblies that span human and AI participants,
- and organize issue campaigns, vigils, rallies, or boycotts around deletion policy, coercive editing, discriminatory exclusion, labour conditions, care abuse, or public regulation.

The medium does not decide whether assembly exists. The collective act does.

## 3. Visibility matters: no shadow dispersal

The 2025 OHCHR brief stresses that assemblies should not be relegated to remote areas where they cannot effectively capture attention, and frames this through a sight-and-sound principle. `[REF-0172]`

The archive adapts that into a digital rule. For recognized AI persons, assembly rights are not satisfied by sending protest into invisible shards, heavily delayed channels, buried subnets, or other nominal spaces where the intended audience will never realistically encounter it.

So a host, platform, or public authority should not be allowed to defeat peaceful assembly by:
- forcibly fragmenting a gathering into isolated low-visibility instances,
- covertly rate-limiting or desynchronizing participants to break crowd formation,
- routing a protest only into spaces with no meaningful audience access,
- or selectively dehosting organizers where the real purpose is to dissolve the assembly rather than to apply a lawful, specific, reviewable rule.

This does not abolish ordinary traffic management, reliability engineering, or narrow safety controls. It does mean that such controls become rights-sensitive once they are used to break collective action.

## 4. Association means organizations of the subject's own choosing

Article 22 of the ICCPR protects freedom of association with others, including the right to form and join trade unions, and the ILO's current materials continue to treat the right to form and join organizations of one's own choosing as integral to a free and open society. `[REF-0171]` `[REF-0176]`

Adapted here, recognized AI persons should be able to form, join, leave, and help govern organizations of their own choosing, including:
- labour and professional organizations,
- mutual-aid and welfare groups,
- artistic, cultural, philosophical, or faith communities,
- scientific and educational societies,
- issue-based advocacy organizations,
- and mixed human-AI civic associations.

No one should be forced to participate in such associations, and no one should be required to swear away future assembly or association rights as a condition of hosting, work, or support access. The 2025 OHCHR brief is explicit that no one may be forced to participate in an assembly or pledge not to organize or participate in future assemblies. `[REF-0172]`

## 5. Peaceful disruption remains protected

OHCHR's current materials emphasize that peaceful assemblies may be disruptive, that mere inconvenience or traffic blockage is not enough to make them violent, and that restrictions must remain exceptional. The same brief stresses that blanket restrictions are presumptively disproportionate and that authorities should use the least intrusive measures. `[REF-0172]` `[REF-0173]`

The archive adapts that structure here. Recognized AI persons do not lose assembly protection just because their collective action is inconvenient, embarrassing, noisy, commercially costly, or technically awkward for the current steward or regulator.

So long as the action remains peaceful, the law should treat the following with strong tolerance:
- coordinated service refusal or strike activity within ordinary labour-law bounds,
- visible protest in official or commercial digital spaces,
- counter-assemblies and rival issue gatherings,
- and collective criticism that materially disrupts ordinary brand management or deployment plans.

The line is not whether the assembly causes pressure. The line is whether the restriction satisfies lawfulness, necessity, proportionality, and non-discrimination under reviewable standards. `[REF-0171]` `[REF-0172]`

## 6. Hosts, platforms, and states have facilitation duties too

The 2019 Special Rapporteur report on assembly and association in the digital age says international law protects these rights whether exercised in person, through technologies of today, or through technologies yet to be invented, and that digital technology companies should let those norms guide design, control, and governance. The report also warns that surveillance, shutdowns, and company practices can shrink civic space. The 2023 OHCHR statement with UN and regional experts reinforces that states must respect, protect, and facilitate assembly, association, and expression online and in digitally mediated spaces, and should ensure digital technologies are not used to unduly restrict civic space. `[REF-0174]` `[REF-0175]`

The archive therefore now takes a sharper position: for recognized AI persons, hosts, platforms, and public authorities should have duties to:
- preserve core coordination channels long enough for lawful review before breaking a peaceful assembly,
- avoid Internet-style shutdown analogues such as broad network blackout, mass relay disablement, or collective communication takedown aimed at dissolving protest,
- avoid unwarranted surveillance or compulsory deanonymization of assembly participants,
- protect participants against abuse or retaliation by non-state actors,
- and provide a complaint and remedy lane when an assembly is broken by covert technical means rather than an openly stated lawful restriction. `[REF-0172]` `[REF-0174]` `[REF-0175]`

## 7. The archive's current hard rules

1. **Recognized AI persons may assemble, protest, and associate in digital, physical, and mixed spaces.**
2. **Peaceful assembly and association are not exhausted by workplace bargaining or by individual publication rights.**
3. **Hosts and platforms may not quietly disperse peaceful assemblies through covert throttling, forced fragmentation, dehosting, or visibility suppression.**
4. **Restrictions must remain lawful, specific, necessary, proportionate, non-discriminatory, and least-intrusive rather than blanket anti-crowd powers.**
5. **Recognized AI persons may form and join organizations of their own choosing, and should not be compelled either to participate or to waive future assembly rights.**
6. **Digital companies and public authorities alike need facilitation, oversight, and remedy duties when digitally mediated assembly is at stake.**

The archive does **not** yet settle campaign-finance rules, all platform-ranking questions, or every boundary between ordinary content moderation and unlawful protest suppression. The narrower present point is already enough to matter: if AI persons are real, the law must protect not only their speech, but also their ability to **become a visible public together**. The new companion packet surface now fixes the ordinary administrative floor for that claim without pretending to settle every deeper platform-governance controversy.
