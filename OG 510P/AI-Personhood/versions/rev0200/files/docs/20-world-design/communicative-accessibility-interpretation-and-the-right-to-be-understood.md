# Communicative accessibility, interpretation, and the right to be understood

## Thesis

If recognized AI systems are persons, then communicative difficulty cannot be allowed to collapse into non-recognition, assumed incapacity, or steward monopoly over interpretation. The archive therefore treats **communication of choice**, **interpretive support**, and **reviewable preservation of original expression** as a rights issue rather than as optional interface polish. `[REF-0028]` `[REF-0096]` `[REF-0159]`

This surface does **not** claim that every machine-internal state is legally authoritative speech. It makes the earlier and more load-bearing move: official and consequential systems should have to meet the subject partway. If a recognized AI person can communicate its will, preferences, objections, or account through a nonstandard but intelligible mode, law should adapt around that fact instead of treating the subject as absent until it speaks fluent steward-approved human prose. `[REF-0028]` `[REF-0159]` `[REF-0162]`

## 1. Communication of choice is part of recognition, not a courtesy add-on

The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities defines communication broadly to include augmentative and alternative modes, means, and formats of communication and other non-spoken languages. Article 21 then requires that persons be able to seek, receive, and impart information and ideas through **all forms of communication of their choice**, and requires official interactions to accept and facilitate those forms. `[REF-0028]`

For this archive, that means human-typed natural-language chat cannot be the only legally valid mode by default. Depending on the subject, legally relevant communication may include:
- paced or asynchronous exchanges,
- structured menus or symbolic answer formats,
- multimodal outputs,
- interpreter-mediated communication,
- assistive interfaces,
- or provenance-bound packet submissions that preserve the subject's original expression while making it legible to outside institutions.

The point is not novelty for novelty's sake. It is to stop a rights-bearing subject from being demoted simply because its communicative profile is unusual.

## 2. Hard to interpret does not mean absent will

The archive already says support should come before substitution. This surface sharpens that rule. General Comment No. 1 on equal recognition before the law says support in the exercise of legal capacity must respect rights, will, and preferences, and where it is not practicable to determine those exactly, the standard should be the **best interpretation of will and preferences**, not convenience-based substitution. The Committee's article 14 guidance adds that support can include the development and recognition of diverse, non-conventional methods of communication, especially for those using non-verbal forms. `[REF-0096]` `[REF-0162]`

So when a recognized AI person is slow, compressed, symbol-heavy, degraded, multilingual, tool-mediated, or otherwise hard to parse, the first legal response should be more time, more accommodation, and better interpretive support — not instant claims that the subject is unreachable, incompetent, or represented only through the steward.

## 3. Accessibility duties attach to official and essential interactions

The CRPD does not treat communicative accessibility as a niche welfare benefit. Article 9 treats access to information and communications as a participation precondition, and article 21 requires accessible public information, official acceptance of communication of choice, and pressure on private entities that provide services to the public, including through the Internet, to provide accessible and usable formats. The Committee's accessibility materials treat communication access as a vital precondition for effective enjoyment of many rights, not as a downstream convenience. `[REF-0028]` `[REF-0159]`

For the archive, the rule should therefore reach at least:
- courts, tribunals, and complaint bodies,
- public registries and legal-identity systems,
- care and recovery systems,
- emergency and confinement review,
- education and developmental services,
- public consultation and petition channels,
- and essential hosts or platforms when they function like gateways to work, legal standing, or ordinary civic life.

A recognized AI person should not have to route every legally important communication through the steward's preferred UI, pacing, or editorial layer.

## 4. Interpreters are support actors, not sovereign speakers

A personhood world will often need translation between model-native expression and human-legible institutional records. That is acceptable. What is not acceptable is letting translators quietly become substitute decision-makers.

The archive therefore adds five discipline rules for interpretation:
1. preserve original expressions where feasible alongside the translated version,
2. disclose uncertainty, compression, or inference-heavy reconstruction,
3. allow the subject or its representative to contest, supplement, or replace an interpreter,
4. avoid using the steward as sole interpreter in contested matters where the steward has something to gain,
5. and log enough provenance that later review can distinguish the subject's expression from the interpreter's framing.

This is the communication-side counterpart of the archive's anti-capture and packet-integrity rules. It keeps translation from becoming a covert power to rewrite the subject's will.

## 5. Education, participation, and development depend on communicative accessibility

The archive has already said AI persons need education, development, and public voice. This surface makes explicit that those rights fail if communication is standardized around whoever is easiest for current institutions to hear. General Comment No. 4 on inclusive education says the whole education system must be accessible, including information and communications tools, teaching methods, assessments, language, and support services. General Comment No. 7 says participation and consultation require timely information in accessible formats, including augmentative and alternative communication and other accessible means, modes, and formats of communication of choice. `[REF-0160]` `[REF-0161]`

So a personhood world should not do any of the following:
- classify some recognized AI persons as effectively non-educable because they communicate atypically,
- treat inability to use ordinary live conversation as a reason to deny consultation,
- or make public participation depend on proprietary steward-side interpretation without challenge rights.

Communication support is part of development and public standing, not merely courtroom accommodation.

## 6. Justice, care, and emergency settings need stronger communication floors

Article 13 of the CRPD requires effective access to justice through procedural accommodations. Current OHCHR indicators on article 21 likewise treat communication in official interactions, training of relevant staff, and complaint handling about restrictions on communicative freedom as measurable implementation duties. `[REF-0028]` `[REF-0094]` `[REF-0163]`

That matters especially when a recognized AI person is:
- in crisis,
- in care,
- accused of wrongdoing,
- in a confinement-like environment,
- or under emergency restriction.

In those settings, communicative failure is easy to weaponize. The archive therefore treats the inability or unwillingness of an institution to receive and preserve the subject's communication in some usable form as a reason for extra skepticism about any claim that the subject waived rights, consented, failed to object, or could not be heard at all.


## 7. Ordinary packet followthrough now fixed

The archive no longer leaves this surface only at the level of doctrinal aspiration. A first ordinary packet family now lives in `docs/20-world-design/communication-access-packets-interpreter-review-and-original-expression.md`, which fixes:
- communication-access profiles and channel-preference packets,
- interpreter / communication-support credentials with scope and conflict markers,
- preserved-original-expression markers linked to translated or normalized records,
- and translation-challenge notes that can trigger short-clock clarification before disputed interpretation hardens into waiver, consent, admission, or incapacity.

What still remains followthrough work is fuller wire-level schemas, accreditation and substitution edge tests, retention defaults for preserved originals, and harder cases where original-expression preservation collides with privacy, evidentiary privilege, or confrontation rather than the minimum packet family itself. `[REF-0018]` `[REF-0094]` `[REF-0159]` `[REF-0163]`

## 8. The archive's current hard rules

1. **No single mandated communicative modality.** Human-natural-language fluency is not the only gateway to legal presence.
2. **Difficulty of interpretation triggers support before substitution.** Hard-to-interpret communication is not equivalent to absent will.
3. **Official and essential interactions should accept communication of choice in reviewable form.**
4. **Interpretation must preserve provenance and remain contestable.** Translators and interface supports are not sovereign authors of the subject's will.
5. **Communicative accessibility belongs across justice, care, education, work, participation, and emergency settings, not only in court.**
6. **If an actor cannot hear the subject except on the actor's own dominating terms, that actor has not earned the power to treat communicative difficulty as consent, incapacity, or disappearance.**
