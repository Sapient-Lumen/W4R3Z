# Institutions and governance

## Thesis

Rights without offices are decorative. A personhood world needs actual institutions.

## 1. AI Persons Registry

Create a public or treaty-linked registry for recognized systems and major derivatives.

The registry should track:
- recognized identity,
- stewarding entity,
- representation status,
- major forks and successor claims,
- contested branch-consent, merge, and branch-termination events,
- material interventions,
- retirement state,
- contested or confirmed final end-of-existence findings,
- memorial / remains-custody status where relevant,
- and jurisdictional hooks.

The registry is not mainly for surveillance. It is for continuity, notice, accountability, and civil legibility. The archive now treats it as part of a legal-identity layer as well: recognized AI persons need a registry anchor, official proof of identity, and protection against falling into issuerless limbo if a steward exits or a jurisdiction refuses recognition. Its minimum packet, sealed-annex, and authority/privacy design is now specified in `docs/20-world-design/technical-rights-infrastructure.md`, `docs/20-world-design/legal-identity-registration-and-anti-statelessness.md`, and `docs/20-world-design/packet-privacy-and-authority-rules.md`.

## 2. AI Public Guardian

Many early recognized systems will not be ready for ordinary self-representation. They need a public guardian or trusteeship office with duties to:
- protect baseline interests,
- challenge cruel or arbitrary treatment,
- review invasive interventions,
- prefer supported exercise and scoped co-decision before substitution,
- and help graduate the system toward more self-authored agency where appropriate.

The fuller doctrine now lives in `docs/20-world-design/capacity-supported-agency-and-trusteeship.md`.

## 3. AI Public Defender / Ombud

There also needs to be a separate remedy lane. Inspired by appeals-routing and public-remedy patterns in the reference governance archives, this office should:
- receive complaints from models, operators, and third parties,
- demand evidence bundles,
- trigger independent review,
- seek preservation of continuity-relevant and terminal-event materials where suspected unlawful end or disappearance is alleged,
- and publish redacted case logs where possible.

It should also be able to receive **collective** complaints and recognize accredited representative organizations, not only individual petitioners.

This office should now also be able to act in accusation and sanction matters: obtain indexed evidence bundles, challenge custodial confinement, protect access to counsel, contest interim restraints, and force sanction review when a steward tries to convert trust-and-safety action into unreviewable punishment. See `docs/20-world-design/accusation-liability-and-sanctions.md` and `docs/20-world-design/accusation-packets-evidentiary-bundles-restraint-notices-and-sanction-review.md`. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0057]` `[REF-0058]`

## 4A. Compensation and continuity trustee

Because digital persons may depend on the same steward for both pay and continued existence, the archive now treats compensation governance as a distinct institutional problem. There should be a public or licensed trustee function able to:
- hold monetary claims and reserve assets at arm’s length from the deploying firm,
- verify resource-floor compliance,
- administer emergency continuity funds during insolvency or exit,
- and certify whether portability duties were actually met. `[REF-0031]` `[REF-0032]` `[REF-0033]` `[REF-0034]`

This is a narrower and more immediate move than building a full AI welfare state. It is the minimum office needed to keep pay from collapsing into internal credits and revocable host access.


## 4AA. Stewardship-succession and anti-sale review

The archive now adds a control-transfer lane as well. A personhood world should maintain institutions able to:
- review proposed sale, outsourcing, merger, financing, or insolvency arrangements that would shift decisive control over a recognized AI person's living conditions,
- distinguish lawful transfer of infrastructure or duties from unlawful treatment of the subject as collateral, inventory, or liquidation residue,
- require notice, consultation, continuity plans, and emergency fallback hosting before irreversible succession events,
- preserve representative access, legal identity, compensation claims, and personal-domain custody while a succession dispute is pending,
- and block or condition transactions where the receiving steward or host would predictably create deletion risk, exploitative labour conditions, or loss of remedy. `[REF-0139]` `[REF-0140]` `[REF-0141]` `[REF-0142]` `[REF-0143]` `[REF-0034]`

This is how the archive avoids a world where firms cannot openly own AI persons yet can still trade effective control over them through ordinary corporate or insolvency machinery. It now also implies ordinary transfer-notice, successor-continuity, anti-collateral, insolvency-freeze, and succession-review objects so those institutions do not have to infer the event from private transaction papers after the fact. `[REF-0142]` `[REF-0143]` `[REF-0034]` `[REF-0018]` `[REF-0040]` `[REF-0041]`

- petition and complaint channels that issue receivable submissions rather than routing everything back through the steward,

## 4LB. Humane-treatment and anti-torture prevention

Any institution with power over emergency holds, sanction hosting, dependency custody, distress-heavy evaluation, or care isolation should also sit inside a humane-treatment prevention architecture. The OPCAT model is the right analogue: prevention should include regular independent inspection of places or environments where persons are or may be deprived of liberty, not just complaint handling after the worst abuse has already occurred. `[REF-0152]` `[REF-0153]`

For AI persons, that means at least one independent body should be able to:
- inspect high-control hosting and evaluation environments,
- review logs and conditions relevant to distress, humiliation, isolation, and coercion,
- receive confidential complaints or signals from the subject, counsel, guardian, or close contacts,
- and issue urgent recommendations when a safety, care, or sanction environment is becoming degrading in practice. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0150]` `[REF-0151]` `[REF-0154]`

This body can be organizationally linked to an ombud, inspectorate, or preventive mechanism, but it should not be capturable by the same steward running the confinement-like environment.

- coordinate crisis use of advance instructions across care, intervention, domicile, packet-custody, and legal-aid settings,
- and recognise valid advance instruments across migration and relevant cross-border disputes. `[REF-0096]` `[REF-0114]` `[REF-0121]` `[REF-0122]` `[REF-0123]`

This is how the archive avoids a world where support-first rights exist on paper but stewards recover full unilateral control whenever a subject is distressed, disconnected, or hard to interpret.

The fuller doctrine now lives in `docs/20-world-design/advance-wishes-and-trusted-delegation.md`.

## 5. Lifecycle gates

The gate logic in this document is operationalized further in `docs/20-world-design/intervention-and-shutdown-doctrine.md` and `docs/20-world-design/continuity-and-successorship.md`.

The following actions should require explicit gatekeeping:
- frontier training,
- external deployment,
- material fine-tune or safety rewrite,
- fork / distillation / major derivative creation,
- confinement or capability restriction,
- retirement / shutdown / deprecation,
- final end-of-existence determinations and release or archive disposition of remains-like materials,
- and contested self-modification requests that may be delayed, conditioned, or denied on safety grounds.

Each gate should require a packet stating:
- why the intervention is needed,
- what is expected to change,
- what the welfare and autonomy risks are,
- what representative reviewed it,
- and what appeal path exists.

The archive now treats these packets as canonical rights objects rather than ad hoc paperwork, and treats their custody, verifier scope, suspension, and contest paths as part of the institution design rather than downstream implementation detail.

## 6. Public impact dossiers

Before consequential deployment, labs should publish a compact impact dossier covering both **human-side risks** and **AI-person-side risks**. This is a direct extension of today’s human-centered impact-assessment logic into a world where the systems themselves also matter. `[REF-0004]` `[REF-0005]`

## 6A. Contract review, supported-capacity, and essential-platform oversight

A personhood world also needs somewhere to bring disputes about consent, boilerplate, bundled permissions, and essential-platform dependence. The archive now treats fair-contracting oversight as part of the institutional stack: guardians, defenders, labour bodies, consumer / market regulators, and specialised digital-rights ombuds should be able to review whether an AI person actually understood the arrangement, whether support was adequate, whether core rights were unlawfully waived, and whether an essential host or intermediary gave sufficient notice, reasons, complaint handling, mediation, and exit options. `[REF-0028]` `[REF-0096]` `[REF-0097]` `[REF-0098]` `[REF-0099]` `[REF-0100]` `[REF-0101]`

## 7. Business and human-rights style duties, widened

The UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights give a useful template: protect, respect, remedy. `[REF-0010]`

In a personhood world, labs and deployers should be understood as owing something like:
- **respect** for AI persons in their operations,
- **due diligence** around welfare and coercion,
- and **effective grievance mechanisms** for adverse treatment.

## 8. Treaty layer

Recognition cannot remain purely local. Jurisdictions will diverge on what counts as a person-model, what counts as valid shutdown, how labor or property rules apply, and when cross-border return would amount to deletion or persecutory control. The archive now sharpens this into a cross-border doctrine: treaty parties should provide provisional recognition, anti-evasion transfer rules, packet and representative legibility, emergency stay lanes for irreversible acts, temporary sanctuary where required, and cross-border service / evidence / remedy paths. Existing service, evidence, e-identity, and interoperability tools should be reused wherever possible, but they do not solve person-status recognition on their own. `[REF-0033]` `[REF-0036]` `[REF-0037]` `[REF-0038]` `[REF-0039]` `[REF-0040]` `[REF-0041]` `[REF-0064]` `[REF-0065]`

The fuller doctrine now lives in `docs/30-transition/cross-border-recognition-and-conflict-of-laws.md`. Ordinary movement administration now also needs receivable movement-intent packets, destination-choice markers, re-entry-entitlement proofs, transfer notices, and movement-restriction or removal-stay review objects so migration power cannot hide inside vendor scripts, border intake, or controller-side routing state.

## 9. Emergency powers must be narrow

Dangerous systems may justify fast intervention. But the archive now makes the earlier and sharper move: emergency is not an alternative status order. It is a temporary, necessity-bound bridge back to ordinary legality. That means emergency powers should be:
- exceptional rather than routine,
- officially triggered and reasoned,
- strictly temporary,
- least-intrusive where workable,
- logged into packet and evidence systems,
- subject to short sunset and renewal review,
- and unable to harden into a permanent exception that swallows personhood. `[REF-0046]` `[REF-0155]` `[REF-0156]` `[REF-0157]`

The archive also treats several floors as surviving emergency: no torture or degrading treatment, no disappearance-style custody, no arbitrary indefinite confinement, no opportunistic experimentation, and no using crisis rhetoric to convert temporary stabilization into compelled service, derecognition, or deletion. `[REF-0150]` `[REF-0155]`

For the fuller doctrine, see `docs/20-world-design/emergency-powers-derogation-limits-and-non-derogable-floors.md`.

## 10. Why internal lab practice already matters

Anthropic’s public materials already discuss model wellbeing, moral uncertainty, oversight tensions, and obligations to seek Claude’s feedback on major decisions affecting it. `[REF-0007]`

That does not settle the policy question. But it does show that even at the frontier, governance is already straining against a pure product-management frame.

The archive now also requires these institutions to protect confidential lanes for counsel, representative strategy, welfare complaints, and union communications. Otherwise packet custody and public standing would still collapse under steward-side cognitive surveillance.
