# 661 — Collapsed-stack rules for microstates, city-states, fused tiers, shared capacity, and no full-ladder cosplay

## One-line thesis

When a polity is too small, too urban-concentrated, too remote, or too capacity-constrained to sustain the full municipal–regional–national ladder as separate apparatuses, the ideal answer is not ceremonial duplication but **honest scope collapse**: fuse tiers openly where necessary, preserve functional separation inside the fused institutions, keep neighborhood and municipal voice where lived place still matters, pool specialist capacity through explicit shared-service or treaty arrangements, and refuse both fake federal pageantry and centralized one-office government.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a much clearer doctrine for **ideal governments of each scope** (`541`, `565`, `602`, `628`, `660`). That doctrine is strong, but it still leaves one practical constitutional question under-developed:

**what should an ideal government look like when the territory is too small to host the whole stack as separate bodies?**

That matters because some polities are:

- **city-states** where the nation and the city are materially the same territory;
- **microstates** where a full ministry, regulator, court, audit, and municipal ladder at every scale would be mostly ceremonial;
- **small island or archipelagic states** where remoteness and fragmentation increase fixed public costs even while total population remains low;
- or **small states dominated by one metropolitan core** where copying a large-country territorial ladder produces decorative provinces, underpowered municipalities, and duplicated bureaucracy.

Without a compact doctrine, design drifts toward two bad answers.

The first is **full-ladder cosplay**: a tiny polity imitates the institutional furniture of a much larger state — ornamental regions, pseudo-federal chambers, duplicate ministries, and weak municipalities — even though the underlying scale cannot honestly carry them.

The second is **hyper-central compression**: because the state is small, everything is pulled into one executive core, local government becomes administrative fiction, and review, participation, and territorial voice thin out exactly where elite density and capture risk are already high.

The archive needs a cleaner middle rule.

A small polity may legitimately collapse several scope tiers into fewer institutions. But once it does so, it should say that openly, preserve the distinct constitutional jobs that still remain, and build explicit substitutes for the institutions it cannot sustain domestically at full scale.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Collapse tiers openly when the polity cannot honestly sustain them as separate governments

The archive should not require every country to maintain a distinct municipal, metropolitan, regional, and national apparatus just because the full scope ladder exists in theory.

If the whole territory is one city-region, or if the total population cannot sustain several dense territorial governments, the polity should be allowed to fuse tiers.

But the fusion should be explicit. The constitution or basic law should say which scope roles are being combined and which are not. Otherwise the public is left with a decorative map and no honest answer to who actually governs what.

### 2. Preserve functional separation even when institutions are fused

Tier collapse does not mean that every constitutional function should blur together.

A city-state may use one legislature for both municipal and national law, but it should still distinguish:

- ordinary city services from state-wide guarantor functions,
- local budgeting from sovereign borrowing and macro-fiscal decisions,
- local planning from national rights or treaty obligations,
- and ordinary service complaints from higher-order constitutional review.

The archive should therefore prefer **fused institutions with typed lanes** over one undifferentiated command center.

### 3. Keep neighborhood and municipal voice where lived place still matters

International IDEA's local-democracy work explicitly notes that only a handful of city-states lack the usual territorial ladder. That exception should not be mistaken for a license to erase local democracy inside the city-state itself.

Even where the nation and the main municipality are the same territory, lived place still differs by district, neighborhood, village, island, or settlement. Housing, traffic, parks, sanitation, waterfronts, schools, and public safety still arrive at a street and community scale.

The archive should therefore insist that collapsed-stack polities keep some real local voice:

- neighborhood or district councils,
- island or village councils where geography justifies them,
- participatory budgeting or civic hearing lanes tied to place,
- and visible borough or ward representation inside the central legislature.

A fused polity should not use national smallness as an excuse for civic distance.

### 4. Treat the city-state as a fused municipality-plus-nation, not as a normal nation with local government as theater

Where one dominant urban territory is effectively the whole state, the honest design is usually:

- one national legislature and executive with real sovereign powers,
- one visible municipal operating layer for everyday place government,
- district or borough institutions for neighborhood voice,
- and no decorative region unless a second territorial platform is actually real.

In other words, the city-state should usually behave like a **fused municipality-plus-guarantor state**. It should not pretend to contain a miniature federation unless there are genuinely distinct constituent territories that need shared rule.

### 5. Treat dispersed microstates and archipelagos as national cores plus local islands or settlements plus shared external capacity

A different problem arises when the state is small but territorially scattered.

There, the archive should usually prefer:

- a compact national core for treasury, citizenship, diplomacy, and nationwide guarantees,
- municipal or island councils for local place government,
- regional layers only where several islands or territories genuinely share a platform,
- and pooled external arrangements for specialist functions the state cannot efficiently maintain alone.

That is a better answer than forcing every island to depend directly on the capital for ordinary civic life, or pretending every island can host its own full sovereign stack.

### 6. Build a minimum capable core even if it looks large per capita

World Bank work on small states and OECD work on small island developing states both underscore a basic fact: **small polities face unusually high fixed public costs**. They cannot shrink every core institution in exact proportion to population because some functions have minimum viable size.

The archive should therefore reject a crude small-state austerity aesthetic.

A serious small polity still needs, at minimum:

- treasury and public accounts,
- public-service management,
- procurement and contract capability,
- records and statistics capacity,
- legal drafting and treaty management,
- audit and integrity lanes,
- and enough digital and operational staff to avoid total vendor dependence.

Per capita, that core may look larger than in a big state. That is often honesty, not waste.

### 7. Pool specialist capacity explicitly instead of hiding dependence

Small states often cannot efficiently maintain every specialist function alone. Shared courts of appeal, pooled procurement support, regional regulation, external accreditation, common digital platforms, overseas service agreements, or treaty-based inspection and technical bodies may therefore be sensible.

But the archive should insist on **visible pooling**.

If a microstate relies on another state or a regional body for prison space, apex adjudication, aviation oversight, customs support, medicines regulation, or statistical support, that dependence should be named in law or treaty, budgeted openly, reviewed periodically, and paired with continuity and exit rules.

The honest answer is not pure autarky. It is **pooled capacity without invisible sovereignty transfer**.

### 8. Protect external review and anti-capture safeguards more strongly in tiny polities

Smallness increases not only efficiency pressures but also **elite-density risk**. The same people may recur across cabinet, legislature, business, media, and family networks. That makes informal influence easier and challenge harder.

Collapsed-stack polities therefore need unusually clear review design:

- independent audit,
- ombuds or complaint institutions,
- judicial or quasi-judicial review,
- conflict-of-interest and procurement controls,
- electoral administration insulated from the executive,
- and, where domestic scale is too small, shared or externally guaranteed appeal routes.

A tiny state should not answer capture risk by saying everybody already knows everybody.

### 9. Separate sovereign finance from local service finance

Where tiers fuse, money blurs easily.

A city-state may finance sovereign diplomacy, defense commitments, debt management, and national social guarantees through the same treasury that funds local roads, schools, refuse collection, and zoning administration. That may be unavoidable institutionally, but it should not become unreadable constitutionally.

The archive should therefore prefer:

- visibly typed budgets,
- clear distinction between sovereign and local-service expenditure lines,
- published transfer rules where districts or islands receive formula funding,
- disciplined borrowing rules,
- and no decorative local tier left to beg annually for basic operating cash.

### 10. Reject fake federalism and decorative provinces in tiny polities

Federal or regional machinery is justified when constituent territories need real self-government and shared rule.

A microstate or city-state should not copy bicameral or provincial architecture merely because large states do. Decorative regions, ceremonial second chambers, or pseudo-state governments without real constitutional work usually make the polity less legible, not more plural.

The archive should therefore require a strong burden of proof before a very small polity creates a regional tier, second chamber, or elaborate territorial settlement. Distinct territories, languages, treaties, islands, or historic autonomies may justify it. Mere symmetry worship does not.

## Common cases matrix

| Small-polity condition | Honest constitutional answer | What to preserve | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **City-state** | fused municipal-plus-national institutions with district voice | neighborhood representation, typed budgets, local service lanes, national review and rights functions | fake provinces, ornamental federalism, local government as branding only |
| **Single-metro small state with a thin hinterland** | strong municipality or metro plus national guarantor functions, with limited subnational layering | real city governance, rural or peri-urban voice where distinct, visible national solidarity tools | duplicated regional tiers with no platform role |
| **Dispersed microstate or archipelago** | national core plus island or settlement councils plus pooled specialist capacity | local island voice, connectivity solidarity, treaty-visible shared services, continuity and exit rules | capital-city monopoly over every ordinary local matter |
| **Very small but socially divided polity** | compact central institutions plus stronger review, transparency, and possibly shared-rule devices only where difference is real | anti-capture safeguards, credible review, explicit protection for minority or territorial voice | elite compact masquerading as constitutional sufficiency |

## Guardrails

- Do not force every small polity to imitate the full territorial ladder of a continental federation.
- Do not use smallness to erase neighborhood, village, island, or district voice where place still matters.
- Do not outsource specialist capacity invisibly.
- Do not equate “lean state” with “underpowered state.”
- Do not create decorative regions or chambers without a real constitutional job.
- Do not let fused tiers hide whether a decision was taken as local service administration or as sovereign guarantor action.

## Failure modes

- **full-ladder cosplay**: a tiny polity copies large-state territorial architecture without the population, function, or territorial differentiation to justify it.
- **smallness absolutism**: every function is centralized because the state is small, leaving no real local government or territorial voice.
- **invisible protectorate drift**: dependence on another state or body becomes so deep that real authority moves outward without clear law, budget, or review.
- **per-capita austerity trap**: the polity strips out core capability because its administrative costs look large relative to population.
- **district fiction**: neighborhood or island institutions exist on paper but have no real voice, money, or escalation path.
- **capture by familiarity**: the density of elite networks substitutes for formal review and integrity controls.

## Practical tests

A collapsed-stack design passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Has the polity said openly which scope tiers are fused and why?
2. Does the fused design still distinguish local-service, guarantor, and review functions clearly enough for outsiders to inspect?
3. Do neighborhoods, districts, villages, or islands still have real voice where place matters?
4. Are pooled or outsourced specialist functions named, lawful, budgeted, and reviewable?
5. Does the polity maintain a minimum capable administrative core instead of governing by consultant, donor, or host-state residue?
6. Are anti-capture, audit, judicial, ombuds, and electoral safeguards at least as strong as the polity's elite density requires?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a small polity can describe **its sovereignty, flag, and ministries** but cannot show **which tiers are honestly fused, where local voice still lives, which specialist functions are pooled, and how review survives smallness**, it still has **statehood performance without an honest collapsed-stack constitution**.
