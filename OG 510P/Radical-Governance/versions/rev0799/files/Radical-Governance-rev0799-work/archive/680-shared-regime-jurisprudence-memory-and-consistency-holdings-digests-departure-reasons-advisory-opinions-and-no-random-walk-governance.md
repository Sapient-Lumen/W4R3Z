# 680 — Shared-regime jurisprudence memory and consistency: holdings, digests, departure reasons, advisory opinions, and no random-walk governance

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed jurisprudence-memory constitution** that separates binding force, persuasive reasoning, holdings, summaries, digests, advisory opinions, and departure rules — so interpretation develops through visible continuity rather than random walk, citation theater, or stealth precedent.

## Why this matters

Once a regime has a real interpretation constitution (`679`), a second problem appears immediately:

**how does the regime remember and reuse its own legal reasoning over time?**

Many regimes do not operate under a simple domestic-court model of strict stare decisis. Some judgments bind only the parties. Some opinions are advisory. Some committees publish general comments or digests that help systematize case law but do not themselves bind. Some chambers or panels speak for a whole court only indirectly. Some secretariats maintain repertoires, analytical indexes, or case-law guides that are indispensable in practice but not authoritative in themselves.

Without a typed jurisprudence-memory constitution, six familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **random-walk governance**: similar questions receive materially different answers because no one must identify, distinguish, or depart from prior reasoning.

The second is **citation theater**: actors shower a body with old cases, summaries, or dicta without saying what legal weight any of them actually carry.

The third is **digest confusion**: an index, guide, or staff summary is treated as if it were the holding.

The fourth is **hidden overruling**: a body departs from prior reasoning without admitting it, leaving the regime’s legal baseline unclear.

The fifth is **advisory overreach**: advisory or interpretive-support products are treated as if they had the same force as judgments in contentious matters.

The sixth is **memory collapse**: the regime accumulates decisions faster than participants can reconstruct doctrine, so practical meaning migrates to insiders and repeat players.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **jurisprudence memory and consistency discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Publish one hierarchy of jurisprudential weight

A serious regime should distinguish at least:

- decisions binding only on the parties and in the case,
- decisions or holdings with broader institution-wide effect where the legal order says so,
- persuasive but non-binding reasoning,
- advisory opinions,
- officially maintained digests or case-law guides,
- and unofficial commentary or practitioner tools.

If all citations arrive in one pile, doctrine will be governed by confidence and repetition rather than by law.

### 2. Separate holding, reasoning, and summary

The archive should prefer a rule that every decision record can show:

- the formal disposition or operative holding,
- the key reasons necessary to reach it,
- and any later official summary, digest, or guide that describes it.

This keeps the regime from treating helpful secondary syntheses as the law itself.

### 3. Require explicit treatment of materially relevant prior decisions

A body should not need to repeat every prior citation. It should have to name and handle the ones that materially matter.

That means a serious regime should require decision-makers to say whether earlier reasoning is being:

- followed,
- distinguished,
- limited,
- left open,
- or departed from.

Continuity does not mean immobility. It means departures travel under their own name.

### 4. Publish departure reasons when doctrine moves

If a regime changes course, it should say why.

The archive should therefore prefer a rule that doctrinal movement names the trigger, such as:

- new text,
- authoritative interpretation,
- explicit overruling,
- materially different facts,
- institutional error correction,
- or demonstrated incompatibility with higher-order norms.

Unexplained movement is what turns legal development into random walk.

### 5. Give advisory opinions and reference answers a typed lane

Some regimes can issue advisory opinions or answer abstract legal questions. Those products can be extremely important. They should not be confused with contentious judgments unless the legal order says so.

The regime should therefore state:

- who may request an advisory opinion,
- on what questions,
- what legal weight the opinion has,
- whether later contentious bodies may depart from it,
- and how it enters the public jurisprudence register.

### 6. Maintain official digests, guides, and analytical indexes without pretending they bind

Large jurisprudential systems need compression tools.

Official case-law guides, analytical indexes, repertories, and subject digests can improve access and consistency. They should also say plainly that they organize, synthesize, or summarize doctrine rather than replace the underlying decisions.

### 7. Build chamber and panel alignment rules

Where multiple chambers, panels, committees, or divisions may speak, the regime should say:

- when ordinary panels must follow settled lines,
- when escalation to a grand chamber, plenary body, or authoritative organ is required,
- and how conflicts across lines of authority are resolved.

Otherwise the regime will slowly create parallel constitutions inside one legal order.

### 8. Keep one public jurisprudence register with doctrine status fields

A serious regime should be able to show, in one place:

- case or opinion identifier,
- date,
- body,
- holding type,
- weight class,
- issue tags,
- relationship to earlier cases,
- and whether later law, interpretation, or judgment displaced it.

This is the minimum memory spine for non-insider legal navigation.

### 9. Separate precedent management from citation prestige

Not every old decision deserves the same practical weight. Not every famous decision is still current.

The regime should therefore favor doctrine tracking by explicit status fields and later treatment rather than by age, fame, or rhetorical grandeur.

### 10. Preserve accessibility without diluting legal exactness

Summaries, digests, and thematic guides are often the only way many actors can navigate a large jurisprudence. They should be accessible, regularly updated, and visibly linked back to the underlying decision texts.

Accessibility is part of legal equality. But it should never depend on pretending the digest is the judgment.

## Working matrix

| Jurisprudence object | Honest rule | Why | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| contentious decision | identify holding, necessary reasoning, and party-specific binding force | preserves legal precision | treating all paragraphs as universal amendment |
| advisory opinion or reference answer | publish request basis, legal weight, and relation to later contentious cases | prevents advisory overreach or underuse | pretending advisory and contentious outputs are identical |
| official digest or guide | summarize and link back, but label as non-binding unless law says otherwise | makes doctrine navigable | digest confusion or law by summary |
| later decision confronting earlier line | follow, distinguish, limit, or depart expressly | keeps doctrine legible | hidden overruling |
| multi-chamber or multi-panel system | publish alignment and escalation rules | avoids parallel constitutions | leaving conflicts to happenstance |
| doctrine registry | track status, issue tags, and later treatment | prevents insider-only memory | citation by prestige or rumor |

## Guardrails

- Do not let a digest, repertory, or guide displace the underlying holding.
- Do not pretend that every past decision has the same legal force.
- Do not depart from materially relevant prior reasoning without saying so.
- Do not let advisory outputs float without a stated weight class.
- Do not let multiple panels or chambers quietly generate inconsistent constitutions.
- Do not make doctrine navigable only to repeat players with institutional memory.

## Failure modes

- **random-walk governance**: doctrine changes because nobody must identify or justify departure.
- **citation theater**: old cases and staff summaries are waved around without legal-weight discipline.
- **digest confusion**: a helpful guide becomes the de facto law.
- **hidden overruling**: the baseline moves but no official record says it moved.
- **advisory inflation**: non-contentious answers are treated as if they bound every later case automatically.
- **memory collapse**: practical understanding survives only in insider folklore.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it publish a hierarchy of jurisprudential weight classes?
2. Can users distinguish the operative holding from summaries, guides, and commentary?
3. Must decision-makers explicitly follow, distinguish, limit, or depart from materially relevant prior decisions?
4. When doctrine moves, are departure reasons published?
5. Are advisory opinions or abstract reference answers given an explicit legal status?
6. Do official digests and case-law guides link back to the underlying decisions and disclaim independent binding force unless law says otherwise?
7. Are chamber, panel, or division conflicts governed through an escalation or alignment rule?
8. Is there one public register showing issue tags, later treatment, and current doctrine status?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can produce **cases, opinions, and summaries** but cannot say **what binds whom, what merely persuades, how prior decisions must be handled, when doctrine has moved, and where the live jurisprudence record sits**, it still has **legal outputs without an honest jurisprudence-memory constitution**.
