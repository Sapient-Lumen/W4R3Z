# 659 — Scope evidentiary-break presumptions, burden allocation, and no stack change by missing file

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should treat any **unrepaired evidentiary break inside a materially stack-altering reform** as a burden-allocation event rather than a neutral accident — keeping the burden on the authority that claims the scope change, continuity, legality, or harmlessness, and refusing to let missing files, broken migration, or lost metadata manufacture constitutional title by opacity.

## Why this matters

The archive now says that when functions move, the polity should preserve **evidentiary continuity** (`658`): stable identifiers, timestamps, authorship, transformation logs, and certified retrieval routes should survive record transfer and system migration.

That is already a strong rule.

But it still leaves one dangerous question unresolved:

**what happens when continuity fails anyway?**

A ministry takes over a regional inspection power and says the transition was lawful, but key override logs are gone. A metropolitan authority inherits a transport file and produces only flattened exports with new timestamps and no author history. A state claims a municipality validly consented to transfer, but the signed instrument cannot now be authenticated. A successor body says there is no way to reconstruct who acted on a pending matter before cutover, whether notice was sent, or whether an appeal clock was still open. A court or ombud is told that the old system has been retired, the vendor contract ended, and the historical audit trail is no longer recoverable.

In all of these cases the state may still argue:

- the function moved,
- the public should trust the surviving summary,
- the challenger cannot prove the defect,
- and continuity concerns make reversal unrealistic.

That is exactly where a serious constitutional stack must refuse fake neutrality.

When the state or another public authority controlled the system, the migration, the retention rule, the publication surface, the certification route, or the records custody, **missing proof is not just empty space**. It changes who should bear evidentiary risk.

Without that rule, many earlier notes can be neutralised in practice.

- successor accountability (`657`) becomes thinner because the successor can say it inherited only partial files;
- evidentiary continuity (`658`) becomes aspirational because nobody pays a price when it fails;
- source hierarchy (`653`) becomes unstable because lower-quality artifacts can dominate once richer evidence disappears;
- pending-matter protection (`656`) weakens because queue position, service, filing, or handoff events cannot be verified;
- nullity, remand, severability, and standstill (`644`-`647`) become harder because the challenger is asked to prove defects from a record the state itself degraded.

The archive therefore needs one more compact doctrine.

If a materially stack-bearing act or transition produces an unrepaired evidentiary break, the polity should not silently convert that break into a presumption for the sponsor.

It should do the opposite.

That is the role of **scope evidentiary-break presumptions**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat an evidentiary break as a governance fact, not a mere technical inconvenience

When a public authority cannot produce the record needed to prove a stack-bearing claim, that absence should itself become part of the constitutional analysis.

The archive should distinguish at least three situations:

- **ordinary uncertainty**: the record is incomplete for reasons not attributable to the sponsor or custodian;
- **declared evidentiary break**: the authority openly states that some required element of provenance, completeness, or retrievability has failed;
- **culpable or sponsor-controlled break**: the missing proof follows from migration, publication, retention, procurement, redaction, or custody choices that the authority controlled or should have governed.

These are not equivalent. A polity should not pretend they are.

### 2. The burden should stay with the authority that claims the scope consequence

If an authority claims that a function moved lawfully, consent was valid, notice was given, a pending matter transferred cleanly, or a historical defect is harmless, it should bear the burden of proving the facts necessary for that claim where the underlying records were within public control.

A challenger should not be forced to prove the negative from a file the state lost, flattened, hid, failed to publish, or made practically irretrievable.

The archive should therefore start from a simple rule:

**missing public proof does not automatically defeat the challenger; it weakens the public authority's claim.**

### 3. Every declared evidentiary break should carry one burden-and-presumptions notice

Whenever the polity issues the declared evidentiary-break notice anticipated by `658`, it should also say what follows for proof.

At minimum that notice should state:

- what element of proof is missing,
- which authority controlled or inherited that proof,
- whether the loss is sponsor-controlled, neutral, or contested,
- which claims can no longer rely on ordinary presumptions of regularity,
- which interim protections apply while reconstruction is attempted,
- and which issues now resolve against the sponsor unless fresh proof appears.

Without that second step, candour about the break does not yet protect anyone from it.

### 4. Do not let presumptions of regular administration survive a record failure unchanged

Many systems give ordinary weight to the idea that officials acted regularly unless shown otherwise.

That presumption may make sense when records are intact and inspection is possible.

It should weaken sharply when the same authority:

- controlled the record,
- controlled the migration,
- controlled publication,
- controlled retention,
- or now asks a reviewer to trust a thinner derivative because the richer evidence disappeared.

The archive should reject a familiar abuse: **presumption stacking**, where the authority first loses the proof and then demands deference because the proof is gone.

### 5. Distinguish continuity protection from validity inference

Sometimes public services cannot simply stop while an evidentiary break is repaired.

That may justify temporary continuity measures:

- preserving current service delivery,
- keeping a payment channel open,
- maintaining a filing intake,
- or freezing disruptive unwind until reconstruction is attempted.

But those continuity measures should not be mistaken for proof that the underlying scope claim was valid.

The archive should keep two questions separate:

- what must continue for fairness and public safety,
- and what has actually been proven about who lawfully governs what.

### 6. The broken element should usually be construed against the actor who needed it to widen power

Not every missing file has the same significance.

A serious polity should ask what the missing proof was supposed to establish.

If the missing evidence was needed to prove:

- valid consent to transfer,
- lawful promulgation,
- timely notice,
- authentic handback,
- completeness of a migration,
- closure of an exception lane,
- or the exact boundary of delegated power,

then the loss should usually work **against** the actor who relies on that proof to widen, preserve, or defend the stack change.

Ambiguity should not default upward merely because upward actors had better systems access.

### 7. Reconstruction is allowed, but reconstruction should remain visibly second-best

Authorities should be allowed — and sometimes required — to reconstruct broken records through certified exports, witness statements, mirrored logs, ledger comparisons, or predecessor backups.

But reconstructed proof should remain visibly classified as reconstructed.

The archive should require the polity to say:

- what was reconstructed,
- from which materials,
- by whom,
- under what method,
- with what known gaps,
- and whether the reconstruction is sufficient only for continuity purposes or also for validity judgment.

Reconstruction should repair uncertainty where possible, not erase the fact that uncertainty existed.

### 8. The challenger should get the benefit of disclosure help when the authority's record failed

If the public body lost track of the authoritative file, it should owe more help, not less.

That may include:

- identifying possible substitute repositories,
- cross-walking old and new identifiers,
- certifying what searches were actually performed,
- disclosing transformation history,
- and providing review bodies with privileged access routes where public access cannot be complete.

No polity should answer a migration-caused proof failure by telling the individual to search harder.

### 9. Review bodies should be able to issue partial presumptions instead of fake all-or-nothing outcomes

Sometimes only part of the record fails.

A serious review lane should be able to say things like:

- the current service channel may continue,
- but the claimed historical notice is not proven;
- the successor may hold the file,
- but the validity of the predecessor's transfer consent is not established;
- the pending queue can stay frozen,
- but the claimed expiry date is not accepted;
- the authority may reconstruct payment operations,
- but not claim a broader transfer of policy discretion.

Partial presumptions are often more honest than either total collapse or total deference.

### 10. Evidentiary-break presumptions should close through repair, candid settlement, or explicit narrowing

A polity should not leave burden allocation in suspense forever.

The break should close in one of three ways:

- **repair**: enough certified proof is recovered to restore ordinary evaluation;
- **candid settlement**: the polity openly concedes uncertainty and adopts a new lawful arrangement prospectively;
- **explicit narrowing**: the polity keeps only the part of the claim that can still be proven and abandons the rest.

What it should not do is let ambiguity ripen into title.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat a public authority's missing record as neutral when the authority controlled the record or the migration.
- Do not let ordinary presumptions of regularity survive unchanged after sponsor-controlled proof loss.
- Do not confuse temporary continuity protection with proven constitutional validity.
- Do not let reconstruction impersonate original proof without visible caveats.
- Do not force challengers to prove negatives from systems they cannot inspect.
- Do not let derivative summaries outrank missing but legally required originals by mere convenience.
- Do not let unresolved ambiguity default toward the actor claiming broader power.

## Failure modes

- **proof inversion**: the public loses the record and then demands the challenger prove what the record would have shown.
- **presumption stacking**: first the evidence disappears, then the sponsor claims deference because nothing disproves its story.
- **continuity laundering**: a service-continuity order is misdescribed as proof that the scope change was valid.
- **reconstruction theater**: a best-effort rebuild is presented as though no break ever occurred.
- **summary capture**: the thinnest surviving artifact becomes decisive because richer evidence has vanished.
- **upward ambiguity drift**: uncertainty quietly benefits the actor seeking wider or more centralised authority.
- **search burden dumping**: the individual is told to perform system archaeology after public recordkeeping failed.

## Anti-theater tests

1. What exact proposition does the missing record matter to — validity, notice, consent, timing, continuity, or liability?
2. Who controlled the lost or degraded evidence when it was still intact?
3. Is the authority asking for an ordinary presumption of regularity after a sponsor-controlled proof failure?
4. Has the polity separated temporary continuity protection from judgment about lawful scope?
5. Are reconstructed artifacts visibly marked as reconstructed, with method and gaps declared?
6. Has the challenger been given help locating substitute repositories, identifiers, or certification routes?
7. Does the unresolved ambiguity currently benefit the actor claiming broader power?
8. If the missing record had cut the other way, would the polity still call its absence neutral?
9. What part of the sponsor's claim could still be proven if deference were removed?
10. If the answer is not much, why should the stack treat the break as harmless at all?

## Closing image

A serious constitutional stack should not let public power say: **we moved the function, lost the proof, and therefore you cannot question the move**. When the state breaks the record it depends on, the break should not harden into authority. It should instead make the state's claim travel on a shorter leash until proof is repaired, narrowed, or candidly abandoned.
