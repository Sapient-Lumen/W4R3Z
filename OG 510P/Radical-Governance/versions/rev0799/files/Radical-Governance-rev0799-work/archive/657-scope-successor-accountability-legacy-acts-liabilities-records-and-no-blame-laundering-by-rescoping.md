# 657 — Scope successor accountability, legacy acts, inherited liabilities, records, and no blame laundering by rescoping

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should treat **future competence, predecessor accountability, successor service continuity, records custody, litigation defence, and fiscal liability as separate questions that must each be typed explicitly whenever a function moves**, so rescoping cannot become a way to enjoy new power while laundering blame for old acts, omissions, debts, broken files, or unremedied harms.

## Why this matters

The archive now says much more clearly **how stack-bearing reforms are assigned, cleared, published, timed, reviewed, cured, and transitioned** (`602`, `628`-`656`).

It has doctrines for fit, burden of proof, lawful rescoping authority, audit triggers, registers, jurisprudence digests, diff packets, opinions, overrides, clear statements, pre-go-live conformance, runtime observability, settlement, retirement, reactivation, nullity, standstill, severability, remand, functional-equivalence review, waiver, estoppel, prescription, desuetude, source hierarchy, promulgation, retroactivity, and pending-matters schedules.

That already blocks many ways of rewriting the multilevel bargain too cheaply.

But one common loophole still remains: **blame laundering by rescoping**.

A state recentralises inspections and says old municipal failures are now a dead local story even though the state inherited the full records and continues the same enforcement line. A region takes over permitting and starts issuing new decisions, but when applicants challenge earlier unlawful delay, the region says the municipality alone is responsible while the municipality says it no longer controls the file. A metro body inherits a transport function and the budget, but not the formal duty to explain old route choices, disabled-access failures, or procurement defects. A ministry abolishes an agency, takes its powers, and then claims the dissolved body alone must answer for missing reasons, bad data, or unpaid obligations. Two authorities each deny ownership of a compensation claim because the act happened before transfer but the harm continues after it.

All of these maneuvers exploit the same weakness.

A polity may say clearly **who now exercises the function** while leaving murky:

- who answers for predecessor acts,
- who carries forward access to records,
- who must defend old decisions in court,
- who pays compensation or completes corrective action,
- who owns legacy contract duties,
- and who owes the public a truthful explanation of institutional lineage.

That ambiguity is dangerous for at least five reasons.

First, it undermines remedy. People can know that a function moved yet still be unable to find the body that must answer for past wrong.

Second, it rewards institutional restructuring as an accountability shield. Governments can fix the chart while escaping the bill.

Third, it corrodes legal certainty and legitimate expectations. The person affected by an old decision should not have to reverse-engineer internal succession law just to learn who can explain, correct, defend, or pay.

Fourth, it damages records integrity. Files, reasons, and author history become orphaned when future competence is moved without backward accountability rules.

Fifth, it weakens democratic memory. If every rescoping lets sponsors say “that was the previous body,” the archive of responsibility becomes thinner each time government reorganises.

The archive already has nearby instincts in its notes on records transfer (`585`), conformance before go-live (`639`), settlement and retirement (`641`-`642`), source hierarchy (`653`), promulgation (`654`), retroactivity (`655`), and pending-matters transition (`656`). What it still lacked was one compact rule saying: **when authority moves, accountability for the past must be typed as carefully as authority for the future**.

That is the role of **scope successor accountability**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate future competence from backward accountability before pretending the transition is complete

A stack change should never assume that the body which now holds the function automatically owns every question about the past.

A polity should explicitly distinguish at least these questions:

- who exercises the function going forward,
- who answers for acts or omissions taken before cutover,
- who holds and authenticates the historical record,
- who defends old decisions in administrative or judicial review,
- who pays legacy liabilities,
- who must perform corrective action for continuing harm,
- and who gives the public a readable successor crosswalk.

If those questions are collapsed into one vague phrase like the region now takes over, the archive is already drifting.

### 2. Every materially stack-altering act should carry one successor-accountability schedule

A polity should require one compact successor-accountability schedule whenever a function moves materially across the stack.

At minimum it should say:

- whether predecessor acts remain attributable to the predecessor,
- whether a statutory successor is named for defence, payment, or records purposes,
- who receives service of challenge for old acts,
- where claimants send compensation or correction demands,
- who preserves and certifies the file,
- how ongoing harm from earlier acts is allocated,
- and whether internal indemnity or reimbursement exists between the bodies.

Without that schedule, the rescoping packet is still incomplete.

### 3. The default should preserve accountability for predecessor acts unless law reallocates it openly and without weakening remedy

As a baseline, a public body should remain answerable for its own past acts and omissions even after the function later moves.

Sometimes the polity may instead choose to designate a statutory successor that stands in the predecessor's place because the predecessor is merged, dissolved, or stripped of operational capacity.

That can be legitimate. But it should happen openly, by law or equally authoritative act, and only if the public remedy remains at least as reachable as before.

The archive should therefore reject a familiar trick: **a predecessor keeps the blame in theory while the successor keeps the records, staff, money, and public interface in practice**. That is not accountability. It is jurisdictional hide-and-seek.

### 4. Successor custody of records should carry successor duties of access, explanation, and co-operation

Even where legal responsibility for an old act formally remains with the predecessor, the body that inherits the file, staff memory, or system of record should owe concrete duties.

Those duties should include:

- preserving the historical file,
- authenticating its completeness,
- making it inspectable through the current public route,
- assisting defence or review,
- providing factual clarifications,
- and preserving author/action lineage when platforms change.

No polity should allow the response “we are not the liable body” to double as permission to bury the evidence.

### 5. The public should not have to guess the right door for legacy complaints, claims, or explanations

Where authority has moved, the polity should give outsiders one plain answer to a practical question: **who do I contact now about what happened then?**

That does not always mean there is only one legally responsible body. But it should mean there is one visible front door that can:

- receive the complaint or claim,
- route it without clock loss,
- identify the proper respondent,
- and tell the person whether the issue concerns old act, continuing harm, inherited duty, or new decision.

A reorganisation should increase administrative burden inside government, not outside it.

### 6. Internal indemnity, reimbursement, or budget settlement should not weaken the public remedy

Governments often need internal financial rules after reorganisation.

One body may pay claims first and recover from another later. A ministry may fund inherited liabilities for a dissolved agency. A new metro authority may defend the case but charge the old municipal member under a closing account.

Those arrangements can be sensible.

But they should remain internal. The person affected by the old act should not lose speed, clarity, or remedy because two public bodies are still bargaining over who ultimately bears the cost.

The archive should therefore keep two questions visibly separate:

- **who owes the public response now**, and
- **how public bodies settle the burden among themselves later**.

### 7. Distinguish liability for the old act from responsibility for continuing or post-cutover harm

Some harms are frozen in the past. Others continue across the cutover.

A bad permit denial may be complete on the day it was issued. But inaccessible service design, neglected infrastructure, unlawful data retention, or an unremedied inspection backlog may continue after the function moves.

The archive should therefore distinguish:

- liability for the predecessor act itself,
- responsibility to stop or mitigate continuing harm after cutover,
- responsibility to comply with orders or judgments already issued,
- and responsibility for fresh harms created by the successor's own post-cutover choices.

Without that typing, each body blames the other and the harm keeps running.

### 8. Legacy litigation, appeals, audits, and investigations should have explicit respondent and cooperation rules

When a function moves, there are often live review lanes concerning old conduct:

- judicial review,
- administrative appeals,
- ombuds inquiries,
- audit follow-up,
- legislative scrutiny,
- integrity investigations,
- or international complaint mechanisms.

A successor-accountability schedule should state who:

- appears as respondent or representative,
- provides the record,
- signs affidavits or certifications,
- implements orders,
- and reports completion.

Otherwise review becomes performative because the forum can still identify the defect but cannot find the body that must carry the fix.

### 9. Dissolution, merger, and renaming should never orphan obligations

Where a predecessor disappears, the law should name a statutory successor for at least five things:

- records,
- representation,
- payment,
- compliance,
- and public contact.

Renaming is not succession policy. Merger is not a substitute for a liability rule. Abolition is not a defence.

The archive should treat any dissolved-or-renamed body without a visible successor-accountability rule as a constitutional defect, not an administrative footnote.

### 10. Historical authorship and institutional lineage should stay attached to the record even when the live holder changes

A successor may need to carry the file, but it should not rewrite who did what.

The archive should preserve:

- which body authored the original act,
- which officials or offices signed it,
- when the function moved,
- which body now holds the record,
- and which body now owes future service.

That keeps the public memory honest. Otherwise the live holder is tempted either to deny all connection to the past or to overwrite the past as though it had always been the acting authority.

### 11. Successor-accountability arrangements should end through visible closure rather than folklore

Not every transition needs an immortal special regime.

The polity should therefore say how successor-accountability arrangements end:

- when all legacy liabilities are exhausted,
- when the predecessor is formally wound up,
- when all files are transferred and certified,
- or when a dated closure certificate shows that old obligations have either been resolved or assigned to a named permanent successor.

Without a visible end state, transitional accountability bodies become permanent and confusing shadow institutions.

## Guardrails

- Do not assume the new holder of a function automatically answers every question about the past.
- Do not let the old holder keep theoretical liability while the new holder keeps the only usable records and public interface.
- Do not force claimants to guess which body owns legacy complaint, compensation, correction, or explanation duties.
- Do not let merger, dissolution, renaming, or platform migration orphan historical obligations.
- Do not use internal reimbursement disputes to delay or weaken the public remedy.
- Do not let continuing harm go unowned because the original act predated cutover.
- Do not let historical authorship disappear just because the live holder changed.

## Failure modes

- **blame laundering**: the successor takes the power but disowns the predecessor's mistakes.
- **orphan file**: no body will certify, disclose, or explain the historical record.
- **wrong-door exhaustion**: the claimant loses time or rights trying several authorities that each disclaim responsibility.
- **liability shell game**: bodies argue over internal reimbursement while no one pays, corrects, or complies.
- **continuing-harm gap**: each authority says the other is responsible because the act was old but the injury is current.
- **dissolution amnesia**: abolition or merger is used to erase respondent identity and public memory.
- **lineage rewrite**: the successor cleans the record so the public can no longer tell who actually acted.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Does the reform say only who now exercises the function, or does it also say who answers for the past?
2. If the predecessor remains legally responsible, can outsiders still reach the record, the respondent, and the remedy through one visible route?
3. If a successor is named, does the shift preserve or improve practical access to review, compensation, correction, and explanation?
4. Are continuing harms clearly allocated, or is each body free to blame the other?
5. If the predecessor is dissolved, has the polity named a real statutory successor for records, defence, payment, and compliance?
6. Can internal indemnity fights delay the external remedy, or are those questions kept behind the public-facing duty line?
7. Do the records preserve institutional lineage clearly enough that future courts, auditors, and residents can tell who actually acted?
8. Does the transition say how the special accountability arrangement ends, or has it created a permanent shadow regime?
9. Would a resident with no inside access know where to complain now about something the old authority did then?
10. If the answer to that last question is no, has the polity really completed the rescoping at all?

## Closing image

A serious constitutional stack should not let government keep **the powers of the successor** and none of **the burdens of the predecessor**. When a function moves, the public should still be able to say, without solving an institutional puzzle: **this is who governs now, this is who acted then, this is who holds the file, and this is who must answer**.
