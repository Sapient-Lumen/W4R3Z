# 487 — Subsidiarity by default, burden of proof for upward scope, and recallable delegation

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should allocate power by a rebuttable subsidiarity rule: default to the smallest scope that can solve the problem, move upward only when spillovers, pooling, rights floors, common-pool integrity, or peace-and-standards needs require it, and keep upward delegation explicit, reviewable, and reversible where possible.

## Why this matters

A recurring governance mistake is to argue about **which level should rule** before asking **what kind of problem is actually being governed**. Some failures are deeply local. They depend on street geometry, local trust, land parcels, shared spaces, or the texture of one neighborhood. Other failures are obviously larger: contagion, rivers, labor markets, fiscal shocks, carbon, war, or infrastructure that only works if many parties obey one interface. A serious political order needs both truths at once.

The archive has mostly approached this from the side of consequential public systems: name the authority, name the scope, name the review route, and do not let one layer impersonate another. What it still lacked was one compact constitutional rule for **scope allocation itself**.

That rule should be neither maximal centralization nor romantic localism. The better default is subsidiarity with teeth: keep authority close to where information is richest and correction is fastest, but require upward delegation when the smaller unit cannot internalize the harm, fund the floor, or protect the commons on its own.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start with a default-local presumption

When assigning a public function, begin from the smallest scope that can plausibly carry it with competence, transparency, and contestability. That default matters because local knowledge is often real knowledge: who is affected, what the terrain is, which tradeoffs are tolerable, and which institutions people can actually reach.

### 2. Move upward only when a named scale test is met

The burden of proof for moving power upward should be explicit. A higher level should have to show at least one of these tests is met:

- **spillover test** — actions in one jurisdiction impose material harms or costs on others,
- **pooling test** — insurance, risk, or capital pooling works much better at larger scale,
- **rights-floor test** — equal citizenship or basic rights would otherwise depend on postcode luck,
- **commons test** — the resource being governed crosses boundaries or is vulnerable to overuse without wider coordination,
- **standards test** — interoperability, market access, or shared infrastructure require one common rule set,
- **peace-and-security test** — the stakes involve external defense, internal peace between units, or conflict restraint beyond local reach.

### 3. Keep upward delegation enumerated rather than ambient

When higher-level authority is justified, it should be described as a bounded delegation or reservation of competence, not as an invitation to absorb every adjacent question. The archive should prefer competence maps over prestige maps.

### 4. Prefer floors and interfaces over total monoculture

Where upper-tier action is justified, the higher level should usually set:

- minimum rights and service floors,
- redistribution and equalization rules,
- shared standards and interoperability constraints,
- common measurement and disclosure rules,
- and backstop remedies when lower levels fail.

It should not automatically seize every implementation detail. A constitutional floor is different from administrative micromanagement.

### 5. Preserve local over-performance above common floors

If a municipality, region, or member state wants to exceed the common floor on housing, health, climate adaptation, participation, or transparency, the presumption should generally favor permission unless it creates material external harm or destroys portability for everyone else.

### 6. Require reviewable reasons and sunset for emergency centralization

Crises often justify temporary upward shifts in authority. They do not justify **scope ratchet by adrenaline**. Emergency centralization should therefore carry:

- a named trigger,
- an explicit time horizon,
- a stated review point,
- and a visible rule for what moves back down when the emergency posture ends.

### 7. Build a downward-reconsideration path, not only an escalation path

A serious federation needs a route for **returning** powers downward when conditions change. New technology, stronger local capacity, regional compacts, or better transparency can make formerly central functions safe to devolve. If the archive only knows how to move upward, it will quietly convert every temporary necessity into permanent hierarchy.

### 8. Treat intermediate coordination as a first-class option

The choice is often not simply local versus national. Cross-municipal boards, metropolitan authorities, watershed compacts, provincial regimes, and treaty-based regional bodies can solve many coordination problems better than either tiny fragmentation or distant central monopoly.

## Guardrails

- Do not centralize merely because a problem is important.
- Do not localize merely because a function feels intimate.
- Do not treat emergency upward delegation as self-renewing.
- Do not confuse fiscal dependence with democratic legitimacy.
- Do not let one level silently redefine another level’s residual powers by habit.

## Failure modes

- **scope ratchet**: exceptional upward delegation never comes back down.
- **postcode absolutism**: basic rights or minimum public goods vary wildly because every place is told to fend for itself.
- **prestige allocation**: high-status levels collect powers that would work better closer to the ground.
- **mapless federalism**: nobody can say which level is actually responsible for what.
- **false binary**: the polity overlooks metro, regional, or treaty-level institutions that fit the problem better than either village or nation alone.

## Practical tests

A subsidiarity discipline passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is there a visible default in favor of the smallest workable scale?
2. If a power moved upward, is the reason stated in spillover, pooling, rights-floor, commons, standards, or peace terms rather than prestige terms?
3. Are upper-tier powers described as bounded competences rather than ambient supremacy?
4. Can lower levels exceed common floors where that does not break portability or impose external harm?
5. Is there a visible path to reconsider and return powers downward when conditions improve?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **which level currently holds a power** but cannot say **why that level is the smallest scale that can safely carry it—or what would justify moving it back down**, then it still has **hierarchy without a scope theory**.
