# 872 — Capacity floor and fragile-jurisdiction implementation dockets: security, administration, donor dependence, records, and no authority by mandate print

## One-line thesis

In fragile or low-capacity jurisdictions, the archive must not treat a mandate, compact, donor pledge, emergency authorization, stabilization plan, technical-assistance project, or international support mission as operative authority until the docket proves minimum implementability: security access, staffed operating lanes, money that actually arrives, records that can survive crisis, rights and accountability routes, and a handoff path back to ordinary local institutions.

## Why this matters

The archive has learned to classify forms: thick metropolitan authority, shared-service backbone, basin platform, utility authority, concession bridge, inspection regime, global alert waist, procedural digital waist, soft-law waist, database-alert waist, plural-order authority, supplier-dependent platform, distress handback monitor, and symbolic-authority mismatch. But a separate failure mode remains: the form may be legally or conceptually right while the operating substrate cannot carry it.

A low-capacity jurisdiction makes this visible. It may have a constitution, ministries, local authorities, courts, police, procurement rules, elections machinery, public finance law, and treaty obligations. It may also have blocked roads, unsafe offices, unpaid staff, destroyed registries, captured checkpoints, absent judges, donor shortfalls, missing fuel, no secure data room, contested interim officials, displacement, and humanitarian dependence. In that condition, a design that looks legitimate in a normal administrative state can become mandate theater.

This note does not say fragile jurisdictions should be governed from outside. It says the opposite: outside support is illegitimate when it pretends that authorization, logistics, or donor funding equals self-government. A support mission, trust fund, technical-assistance office, emergency coordinator, or donor compact is honest only if it names what the local state retains, what outside actors temporarily supply, how affected people can complain, how records will be preserved, and what capacity must exist before handback is real.

The docket's core question is therefore not merely "which level of government should act?" It is: **what is the minimum operating floor below which the chosen authority form becomes fiction?**

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate legal mandate from operating capacity

Start with the mandate, but do not stop there.

| Layer | Question | Theater signal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| legal authority | who is authorized to act? | the mandate is cited as if it proves capacity |
| operating access | can staff safely reach the places, people, roads, courts, depots, warehouses, hospitals, or registries involved? | dashboards show national coverage while access is corridor-only |
| personnel | are there enough trained, paid, supervised people? | pledges or nominal positions substitute for deployed staff |
| logistics | are fuel, vehicles, radios, body armor, medical evacuation, housing, rations, power, connectivity, and maintenance available? | plans assume equipment that is delayed, unfunded, or unusable |
| public finance | does money arrive through a controllable channel on a usable clock? | voluntary contributions are counted as if appropriated |
| records | can decisions, incidents, payments, complaints, detentions, assets, and transfers be reconstructed? | emergency action creates no durable public file |
| rights route | can affected people complain, appeal, obtain correction, or trigger investigation? | hotline, liaison, or protection language exists without remedy power |
| local owner | which domestic authority owns the continuing duty? | support actor becomes the visible state while local institutions hollow out |
| exit and handback | what conditions end or reduce external support? | mandate renewal becomes the default success metric |

The archive should issue no final design verdict until the capacity floor is visible.

### 2. Treat donor dependence as a governance field

Donor support is not a neutral appendage. It changes what power is possible and who controls the clock.

A capacity docket must therefore name:

- pledged amount;
- received amount;
- disbursed amount;
- restricted purpose;
- procurement and delivery bottleneck;
- trust fund or reimbursement route;
- domestic budget counterpart;
- who can reallocate money;
- what happens if funds do not arrive;
- which public claims depend on unreceived funds.

A voluntary trust fund can be a useful narrow waist. It can also become a false budget. The archive should mark every plan that depends on voluntary contributions as **funding-contingent**, not funded, until money and goods are delivered.

### 3. Use a security-access map before assigning administrative duties

Fragile-jurisdiction design often uses normal administrative nouns: school, clinic, court, registry, municipality, police station, market, road, border, warehouse, port, water point, polling center. Those nouns can hide the fact that the state cannot safely reach them.

The docket should map each function by access class.

| Access class | Meaning | Governance consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| normal access | ordinary staff can travel, work, inspect, and return | use ordinary administrative controls |
| escorted access | operations require police, military, or negotiated escort | add security-risk and rights logs |
| corridor access | state or partners can reach only certain routes or facilities | block national coverage claims |
| remote access | work is done by phone, local partner, satellite, or dashboard | mark records as mediated and verify source integrity |
| no access | duty exists but cannot be exercised | declare a blocked-service condition and escalation clock |

A state duty may remain legally real even when physically blocked. But performance claims must not ignore access class.

### 4. Do not let emergency support become shadow sovereignty

Outside support can supply logistics, money, personnel, technical systems, protection, airlift, procurement, training, intelligence, or coordination. The archive should ask whether that support has crossed from assistance into substitute government.

The crossing risk rises when the outside actor controls:

- targeting or prioritization;
- detention or arrest;
- checkpoint access;
- payment of local staff;
- fuel or mobility;
- registry access;
- procurement lists;
- public communication;
- complaint filtering;
- incident classification;
- handback criteria.

When these are live, the docket must name the domestic legal owner, the external operational holder, the review route, and the sunset / transfer condition. "At the request of the government" is not enough if the requesting government itself lacks electoral, territorial, fiscal, or operational reach.

### 5. Split capacity-building from capacity-proof

Training, mentoring, equipment donation, institution-building, technical assistance, and reform strategies are not capacity proof.

Capacity proof requires observable operating evidence:

- deployed staff, not pledged staff;
- working depots, not procurement announcements;
- paid salaries, not payroll reform promises;
- functioning complaint files, not accountability policies;
- open courts or substitute lawful review channels, not justice-sector strategies;
- incident investigations, not human-rights clauses;
- repeatable public-service delivery, not pilot demonstrations;
- local budget execution, not donor project documents;
- handback drills, not exit language.

The archive should label capacity-building as an input and capacity-proof as an output.

### 6. Preserve emergency records before optimizing institutions

A fragile setting often loses public memory: destroyed files, inaccessible offices, offline systems, displaced staff, absent minutes, unlogged detentions, missing procurement files, cash advances, and unverified casualty records. That record loss becomes future impunity and future fiscal fog.

A capacity-floor docket should require a minimum emergency record spine:

| Record | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| mandate file | proves who was authorized to do what |
| incident log | reconstructs harm, use of force, access denial, and security events |
| asset register | tracks vehicles, weapons, fuel, medical stocks, communications gear, and facilities |
| payment ledger | distinguishes donor money, state money, reimbursements, advances, and arrears |
| staff roster | shows who was deployed, paid, trained, suspended, or transferred |
| complaint log | preserves affected-party claims even before full remedy is possible |
| detention / custody log | prevents people from disappearing into support operations |
| handoff log | records transfer from international, national, local, humanitarian, or private actor to another |
| blocked-service ledger | admits where the state could not act |

Without this spine, later audits become archaeology.

### 7. Add a handback ladder, not only an exit date

Fragile-jurisdiction interventions frequently say they will support local ownership, restore order, build capacity, and exit. That is too abstract. The archive should require a handback ladder with measurable rungs.

| Rung | Evidence |
| --- | --- |
| basic access restored | roads, facilities, courts, depots, or service points reachable without extraordinary escort |
| local staff available | named domestic units staffed, paid, trained, and supervised |
| domestic budget line active | state funds, not only donor funds, carry recurring functions |
| record custody transferred | logs, assets, cases, contracts, complaints, and data have a domestic home |
| rights route working | complaints and investigations continue without external gatekeeper |
| ordinary oversight resumes | parliament, council, court, audit body, inspectorate, ombuds, or local committee can review |
| external role narrows | support actor loses functions rather than merely changing title |
| reactivation rule declared | future escalation requires a fresh trigger, not habit |

The endpoint is not the departure ceremony. It is the public receipt that ordinary authority can carry the duty.

### 8. Classify mission theater separately from mission failure

A support mission can fail because the problem is too large, because resources never arrive, because host institutions are captured or absent, because donors underfund it, because the mandate is incoherent, because armed actors adapt, because accountability is weak, or because external actors pursue rival objectives. These are different failures.

The docket should separate:

- **mandate mismatch**: the legal task exceeds the powers granted;
- **capacity mismatch**: powers exist but personnel, equipment, money, access, or records do not;
- **legitimacy mismatch**: the population, affected parties, domestic institutions, or opposition do not accept the arrangement;
- **accountability mismatch**: power is exercised without remedy or investigation;
- **handoff mismatch**: temporary support does not build the institution that must inherit the function;
- **metric mismatch**: operations produce activity counts without control, service, rights, or state-capacity gains.

A finding may say the mission was necessary, underbuilt, overbroad, rights-fragile, donor-dependent, and still not a substitute government. The archive should preserve that complexity.

## Failure modes

- **mandate print**: legal authorization is treated as proof that authority can operate.
- **pledge sovereignty**: donors announce support and the plan counts it as delivered capacity.
- **corridor state**: officials claim national authority while actual access is limited to protected roads, compounds, ports, or airports.
- **support-office government**: logistics and coordination become the practical state without domestic accountability.
- **capacity-building by brochure**: training, workshops, and equipment announcements substitute for repeatable service or lawful control.
- **humanitarian-security fusion**: humanitarian access, protection, and service delivery are made dependent on a security actor's legitimacy.
- **private-force fog**: contractors, advisors, drones, or security vendors act inside state functions without public record, remedy, or command clarity.
- **hotline accountability**: complaint channels exist but cannot produce investigation, remedy, sanction, compensation, or public findings.
- **exit-date theater**: a mandate expires or changes name without a handback receipt.
- **activity metric capture**: patrols, trainings, seizures, meetings, workshops, deliveries, and dashboards are reported as success while territory, courts, services, rights, and records remain broken.

## Anti-theater tests

1. **Access honesty**: every claimed function must carry an access class.
2. **Pledge honesty**: every plan must distinguish pledged, received, disbursed, and usable support.
3. **Local-owner honesty**: every external function must name the domestic duty holder and the ordinary institution that should inherit it.
4. **Record honesty**: emergency action must preserve incident, payment, asset, custody, complaint, and handoff files.
5. **Rights honesty**: force, detention, deprivation, exclusion, and aid denial must have complaint and investigation routes.
6. **Handoff honesty**: renewal, transition, or exit must be judged by capacity receipt, not calendar or mission title.

## Claim-status note

This note is a **strong default** for future fragile-jurisdiction packets. It should be treated as a docket discipline, not a universal argument against intervention or external support. The falsifier is a case where urgent external support demonstrably preserves rights and services while maintaining clear domestic ownership, public records, complaint routes, and measurable handback.
