# 620 — Workforce homes for shared territorial power: employer status, recruitment, training, labor relations, and no government by borrowed payroll

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, it needs an honest **workforce home** as well as democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, participatory, knowledge, service, planning, capital, regulatory, and contract ones: a visible employer of record; merit-based recruitment and progression rules; pay, classification, and training systems aligned to the territory’s real functions; bounded use of secondment and shared-service support; explicit labor-relations, discipline, and grievance routes; and a workforce-data spine that can see vacancies, turnover, time-to-hire, and critical skills — so the territory stops being governed through borrowed payroll, host-city HR custom, and consultant memory.

## One-line thesis

Real metropolitan or regional government needs its own **employer constitution**: a named employer of record, merit-based staffing and progression, explicit pay and labor-relations rules, bounded reliance on secondment, visible workforce metrics, and a transition path out of borrowed payroll.

## Why this matters

The archive now says when shared territorial power becomes real government, what democratic form it should have, what fiscal base and operating core it needs, how it should be held accountable, what legal and intergovernmental standing it requires, how residents should participate, what knowledge spine it needs, how services, plans, capital, regulation, and contracts should be organised, and why all of that should be visible rather than left to consortium habit.

What it still lacked was the **workforce analogue**.

That absence matters because a metropolitan or regional tier may already have an assembly, budget, staff establishment, service powers, contracts, and plans, yet still remain practically half-built if the people who actually recruit, pay, classify, train, discipline, promote, reassign, bargain, and dismiss its workforce all sit somewhere else. In that situation the polity may look administratively real, but its operational sovereignty still runs through host-city payrolls, member-government secondments, ministry borrowing, temporary project units, or contractors who quietly hold the institutional memory.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine for the **workforce home of shared territorial government**: when a real metropolitan or regional tier must stop living on borrowed payroll and become an employer in its own right.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish the workforce constitution from the administrative constitution

A metro or region should not be treated as having a workforce home merely because it has an executive office or some staff desks. The administrative constitution says **that the territory has an operating core**; the workforce constitution says **how people enter, develop within, are paid by, are disciplined by, and remain accountable to that core**.

If the real people system is still run through another government’s HR, payroll, bargaining structures, or career ladders, then the shared tier remains only partially built even if it has an office and a logo.

### 2. Give the shared tier a visible employer of record once powers are real

Once shared territorial power becomes real, the archive should prefer one visible public answer to the question: **who employs the people doing this work?** That means a named employer of record with authority for establishment control, recruitment, appointment, classification, pay administration, leave, performance, discipline, and lawful separation.

This does not require that every support function be duplicated. It does require that the territory’s workforce is not legally or practically a ghost inside some other government’s payroll.

### 3. Keep secondment, shared-service HR, and borrowed specialists bounded and declared

Secondments, shared payroll services, or temporary borrowing of scarce specialists can be useful, especially during transition or for highly technical roles. But the archive should treat them as **bridges**, not constitutions.

A shared tier becomes dishonest when secondment is the permanent answer to staffing a body that is already making recurrent territorial bargains. Borrowed people may advise, seed a new function, or cover bounded gaps. They should not remain the hidden sovereign of ordinary territorial government.

### 4. Use merit-based recruitment and professional standards matched to territorial function

The workforce home should be matched to what the territory actually does. A shared tier that plans transport, commissions networks, regulates operators, manages data, or runs capital programmes needs recruitment standards and professional ladders appropriate to those functions.

The archive should therefore prefer merit-based hiring, role profiles tied to real territorial competences, transparent selection criteria, and enough professional specificity that the body can recruit planners, analysts, engineers, finance officers, inspectors, contract managers, records staff, engagement leads, and equivalent specialists as required by its actual constitutional kit.

### 5. Build pay, classification, and progression rules that support retention without creating a mini-sovereign court

A real shared tier should not be forced to choose between two bad options: dependence on host-government pay scales that make critical recruitment impossible, or prestige inflation that turns the body into a tiny court of over-titled executives. The archive should prefer visible pay bands, classification logic, and progression rules calibrated to the territory’s functions, labour market, and fiscal base.

The point is neither maximal uniformity nor managerial discretion. It is an honest, legible employment order that can attract and retain capable staff without turning payroll into patronage or symbolic empire-building.

### 6. Keep political appointment narrow and the merit core broad

A workforce home becomes constitutionally unsafe when a shared territorial tier treats most consequential roles as political spoils, personal appointments, or coalition trophies. The archive should therefore prefer a narrow strategic appointment layer around elected leadership and a broad professional merit core for continuing administrative, analytical, regulatory, operational, and commercial work.

Otherwise every election or mayoral bargain becomes a partial liquidation of the tier’s capacity to remember, advise, and deliver.

### 7. Give the territory its own labor-relations, grievance, ethics, and discipline lanes

A real employer needs more than hiring forms. The archive should prefer explicit routes for collective representation where applicable, workforce consultation, grievance handling, protected disclosure, conflicts-of-interest obligations, performance management, discipline, and appeal or review where law provides it.

If those routes remain borrowed, confused, or contested, the territory’s workforce constitution is still hiding inside another employer’s institutional life.

### 8. Treat training, mobility, and succession as constitutional maintenance, not HR garnish

A shared tier that governs real systems needs more than first-round hiring. The archive should prefer visible induction, continuing training, digital and analytical capability-building, managed mobility where helpful, succession planning for scarce functions, and explicit plans for replacing single-point expertise.

Without that, the polity becomes dependent on heroic individuals, incumbent consultants, or long-lived secondees who carry the real memory of the institution.

### 9. Publish workforce indicators that make hollowness visible

The archive should prefer a minimum workforce-data spine for real shared territorial government: authorised headcount, filled posts, vacancy rates, time to hire, turnover, first-year attrition, skills gaps, training uptake, and reliance on secondments, temporary labour, and consultants for core functions.

This is not managerial vanity. It is how the polity sees whether it is actually staffing the powers it claims to possess.

### 10. Make transition from co-operation to government include an employer transition plan

When a consortium, joint committee, or shared-service body crosses the threshold into real metropolitan or regional government, the archive should prefer an explicit employer transition pack: which staff transfer, which remain with member governments, which secondments expire, which collective arrangements continue, how records and personnel files move, how continuity is preserved, and when the new tier becomes the clear employer of record.

Otherwise the polity constitutionalises the tier in public law while leaving its human operating system unresolved.

## Workforce-home matrix

| Question | Thin or dishonest arrangement | Honest workforce home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Who employs the people doing the work?** | host-government payroll, project shells, or permanent secondment | visible employer of record for the shared tier |
| **How do people enter roles?** | informal borrowing, political sponsorship, consultant substitution | merit-based recruitment tied to territorial functions |
| **How are specialist roles sustained?** | one-off heroic hires or perpetual borrowed experts | professional ladders, training, and succession planning |
| **How are pay and grades set?** | host-city residue or prestige inflation | explicit classification, pay bands, and progression rules |
| **What remains political?** | broad spoils or patronage turnover | narrow strategic appointments, broad merit core |
| **How are grievances and discipline handled?** | borrowed or contested routes | explicit labor-relations, ethics, grievance, and discipline lanes |
| **How much hollowing is visible?** | no workforce indicators beyond budget totals | vacancy, turnover, skills, hiring, and dependency metrics |
| **How does transition happen?** | constitutional upgrade without staff settlement | explicit employer transition pack with clocks and file transfer |

## Failure modes

- **government by borrowed payroll**: the shared tier looks real in public law but its workforce still belongs to somebody else.
- **secondment as constitution**: temporary staff borrowing becomes the permanent operating model of a body making recurrent territorial choices.
- **ghost employer syndrome**: nobody can state clearly who controls appointments, classification, discipline, or separation.
- **patronage metro**: political appointments spread far beyond the strategic layer and hollow out professional continuity.
- **consultant memory state**: institutional knowledge lives mainly in outside advisers because the tier never builds and retains its own people.
- **grade inflation principality**: compensation and titles become a prestige project detached from function and fiscal capacity.
- **skills blindness**: the polity can describe its powers but not the actual skills, vacancies, or succession risks needed to carry them.
- **unsettled transfer**: a body is constitutionalised before employer-of-record, bargaining, payroll, and personnel-file questions are resolved.

## Practical tests

A shared territorial workforce constitution passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can the territory state clearly who the employer of record is for the people carrying its real powers?
2. Are secondments, shared-service support, consultants, and temporary labour visibly bounded rather than acting as the permanent constitutional core?
3. Are recruitment, classification, pay, progression, and separation rules explicit enough to attract and retain staff for the territory’s actual functions?
4. Is the strategic political appointment layer narrow enough that professional continuity survives electoral and coalition change?
5. Are grievance, labor-relations, ethics, discipline, whistleblowing, and review routes visible enough that workforce accountability does not disappear into another employer’s machinery?
6. Does the tier publish enough workforce indicators to reveal vacancy, turnover, skills gaps, training, and dependence on borrowed capacity?
7. When co-operation hardens into government, is there an explicit employer transition plan rather than a vague promise that staffing will sort itself out later?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a metropolitan or regional body can describe **its powers, contracts, services, and plans** but cannot show **a named employer of record, merit-based recruitment and progression, explicit pay and discipline rules, bounded use of secondment, visible labor-relations and grievance lanes, workforce indicators, and a real transition path out of borrowed payroll**, then it still lacks **an honest workforce home for shared territorial power**.
