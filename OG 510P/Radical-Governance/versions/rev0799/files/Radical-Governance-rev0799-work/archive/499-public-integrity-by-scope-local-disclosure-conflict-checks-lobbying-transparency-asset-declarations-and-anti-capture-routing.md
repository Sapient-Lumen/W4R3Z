# 499 — Public integrity by scope: local disclosure, conflict checks, lobbying transparency, asset declarations, and anti-capture routing

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat integrity as institutional infrastructure rather than moral decoration: small governments need simple, enforceable disclosure and procurement rules; larger governments need stronger conflict controls, lobbying transparency, asset declarations, cooling-off periods, and independent verification; and audit, prevention, prosecution, and adjudication should remain distinct enough that no one office becomes both ethics mascot and political weapon.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a stronger answer to what each level should govern, how each level should be represented, how the executive stack should work, how remedies should travel, and how constitutional change should happen. It still needs one compact answer to a simpler and more dangerous question: **how does a multi-level polity avoid becoming privately owned in practice?**

Capture rarely arrives as a single dramatic coup. More often it enters through procurement favoritism, undisclosed conflicts, opaque lobbying, revolving-door dependency, selective enforcement, shell financing, or local patronage that scales upward before anyone names it as constitutional drift. Small jurisdictions are vulnerable because everyone knows everyone and institutional capacity is thin. Large jurisdictions are vulnerable because influence markets become professionalized, law is dense, and oversight can be fragmented or politicized.

An ideal government therefore needs integrity controls that are **matched to scale and function**. Tiny institutions should not drown in impossible compliance theatre, but they also cannot be left to pure trust. National institutions should not pretend that codes of conduct alone will defeat concentrated money or organized influence. And global or cross-border layers should help prevent arbitrage rather than becoming a safe exit ramp for dirty influence.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate prevention, audit, investigation, prosecution, and adjudication

A sound integrity system should not collapse every anti-corruption task into one heroic commission. Prevention bodies, internal controls, supreme audit institutions, inspectors general, police, prosecutors, ethics commissioners, and courts solve different problems. The archive should prefer a differentiated integrity stack in which failures can be surfaced, investigated, sanctioned, and corrected without one office becoming an all-purpose political cudgel.

### 2. Fit disclosure burden to office power and market exposure

The right question is not whether every public role gets the same disclosure form. It is whether the obligations match the corruption and capture risk carried by the role. Small local offices may need plain-language declarations of interests, gifts, outside employment, recusals, and contract links. Senior executives, ministers, legislators, top regulators, judges, procurement authorities, and state-owned-enterprise leaders usually justify stronger and more frequently updated asset and interest disclosure, verification, and public visibility.

### 3. Make local and regional integrity usable rather than ornamental

At municipal and regional scale, integrity often fails not because standards are unknown, but because the controls are too weak, too informal, or too impractical for ordinary use. Ideal local government should therefore maintain workable public registers for contracts, beneficial ownership where lawful, gifts and hospitality above defined thresholds, recusals on conflicted items, and procurement awards. Simpler systems honestly used beat elegant systems nobody can operate.

### 4. Treat conflicts of interest as a live governance problem, not a yearly paperwork ritual

Disclosure without recusal, screening, advice, and enforcement is integrity theater. A good polity should identify vulnerable roles, require ad hoc declarations where specific matters arise, bind conflicted officials out of the decision path, and preserve a record of the recusal or mitigation taken. The archive should treat unmanaged conflicts as a distortion of public authority even when no criminal bribery can be proved.

### 5. Make lobbying and influence legible to the public before decisions harden

Lobbying is not illegitimate by definition; hidden influence is. The archive should prefer rules that disclose who is trying to influence public decisions, on whose behalf, about what, using which channels, and with what material support or advisory role. This should extend beyond classic lobby firms where needed to cover consultants, trade associations, law firms, and influence on behalf of foreign state interests or state-linked entities. The principle is not to criminalize representation. It is to stop invisible policymaking.

### 6. Use cooling-off, incompatibility, and revolving-door rules where public-private boundary risk is acute

Senior executive, regulatory, procurement, defense, and sensitive licensing roles often justify post-employment restrictions, pre-employment screening, or both. A government that ignores revolving-door incentives teaches officials to govern for their next employer. The ideal archive should prefer visible, reviewable cooling-off rules tied to role sensitivity rather than pure etiquette.

### 7. Verify high-risk declarations and disclosures rather than archiving them unread

A declaration regime that nobody verifies becomes a ritual laundering device. Higher-risk offices should face risk-based review, discrepancy checks, complaint-triggered scrutiny, and sanctions for non-filing or false filing. The same is true of lobbying and political-finance disclosure: visibility without verification invites strategic lying.

### 8. Protect whistleblowing, complaint intake, and local escalation

People closest to corruption risk often see it first. The integrity stack should therefore include protected reporting channels, anti-retaliation safeguards, and escalation routes that do not dead-end in the same hierarchy accused of wrongdoing. This matters especially in small governments where social and employment dependency can silence obvious red flags.

### 9. Use the national and cross-border layers to prevent arbitrage, not to monopolize virtue

National government should set baseline integrity rules where equal citizenship, procurement markets, tax systems, foreign influence, and asset recovery require common discipline. Cross-border cooperation matters for beneficial ownership, foreign bribery, asset tracing, mutual legal assistance, and sanctions evasion. But the center should not use integrity as a pretext to seize every local decision. The goal is common floors, interoperable tracing, and escalation capacity — not ethics-flavored recentralization.

## Guardrails

- Do not build one super-agency that is supposed to educate, audit, investigate, prosecute, and judge everything.
- Do not force tiny jurisdictions into compliance architectures only a capital city ministry can operate.
- Do not confuse filing a form with managing a conflict.
- Do not let lobbying rules cover only registered lobbyists while influence migrates through consultants, donors, or advisory intermediaries.
- Do not publish disclosures without any credible verification or enforcement path.

## Failure modes

- **ethics theater**: codes, forms, and speeches substitute for enforceable controls.
- **capture by informality**: local and regional decisions are steered through personal ties, undeclared interests, and procurement favoritism.
- **visibility without consequence**: disclosures exist but are not verified, audited, or sanctioned.
- **anti-corruption centralism**: the center uses integrity rhetoric to swallow constitutionally local discretion.
- **revolving-door government**: public office becomes a staging ground for private influence markets.

## Practical tests

A scale-matched integrity system passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Are prevention, audit, investigation, prosecution, and adjudication visibly distinct enough to check one another?
2. Do disclosure and conflict rules track the actual power and corruption risk of the office rather than pretending every role is identical?
3. Can local and regional governments run the integrity controls expected of them without impossible compliance load?
4. Are lobbying, gifts, recusals, and high-risk asset or interest declarations disclosed early enough, and verified credibly enough, to matter?
5. Do whistleblowers, complainants, and affected people have escalation routes that do not terminate inside the same patronage network they are challenging?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can describe **who is formally in office** but cannot show **which conflicts, influence attempts, disclosures, recusals, audits, and escalation routes keep that office from becoming privately owned in practice**, then it still has **a constitution without an integrity system**.
