# 489 — Fiscal equalization, universal floors, and no postcode lottery

## One-line thesis

Decentralization is only legitimate when paired with upper-tier equalization and universal social floors, so local self-government can shape place without turning basic rights, public goods, and life chances into a lottery of inherited tax base.

## Why this matters

Local government is attractive for good reasons: it can adapt to context, surface preference differences, and let people visibly influence the places where they actually live. But localism without fiscal design quickly curdles into hierarchy by geography. A rich district can fund parks, schools, clinics, transit, and competent administration; a poor district is told to innovate with scarcity. Citizens are then said to enjoy local democracy while the real determinant is local tax luck.

The archive therefore should not romanticize decentralization in the abstract. OECD work on fiscal federalism and equalization makes the core point clearly: bringing services closer to people can improve fit and accountability, but unless intergovernmental finance is well designed it can also reinforce territorial inequality. Equalization exists to prevent that drift.

An ideal multi-level order therefore combines three things at once:

- local discretion where local knowledge matters,
- universal floors where equal citizenship matters,
- and redistributive equalization where fiscal capacity is uneven.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate local autonomy from local revenue luck

A municipality should be able to choose among lawful local priorities without having its residents’ life chances determined by the taxable wealth trapped inside its borders. The archive should treat these as different questions:

- who decides,
- who pays,
- who is guaranteed a floor,
- and who absorbs asymmetric shocks.

### 2. Use equalization formulas based on both capacity and need

Upper-tier transfers should not be pure political bargaining. They should account for at least:

- **fiscal capacity** — what a jurisdiction could reasonably raise,
- **expenditure need** — the cost of serving its population well,
- **structural burdens** — remoteness, age profile, disability prevalence, infrastructure legacy, climate exposure, or service sparsity where relevant.

### 3. Guarantee universal floors that travel with citizenship, not postal code

Some goods should not depend on the local balance sheet. The exact bundle varies by polity, but ideal candidates include:

- core healthcare access,
- basic education quality,
- minimum income protection,
- due process and legal aid,
- and baseline water, sanitation, and safety obligations.

The higher level may fund or co-fund these even when lower levels help deliver them.

### 4. Keep some local tax authority for accountability and experimentation

Equalization should not erase local responsibility. Local institutions should still control some meaningful revenue instruments and some discretionary spending, so citizens can connect preferences to consequences and places can experiment above the floor.

### 5. Make countercyclical stabilization a higher-tier function

When recession, disaster, or sectoral shock hits, local governments are usually badly placed to run macro stabilization. If they are forced to cut precisely when needs rise, decentralization becomes pro-cyclical cruelty. Upper tiers should therefore carry the main stabilization burden and protect lower-tier service continuity during shocks.

### 6. Fund metropolitan and regional systems as shared systems

Where transport, housing, water, grid, and ecological systems cross borders, finance should cross borders too. A government should not demand metro-scale performance from institutions financed as if they were still isolated municipalities.

### 7. Publish formulas, appeals, and exception pathways

Because equalization is politically sensitive, it should be unusually legible. The archive should prefer published formulas, stable update rules, visible exceptions, and review paths over ad hoc bargaining behind closed doors.

### 8. Allow local topping-up above the common floor where rights stay intact

A good equalization system does not require total sameness. Places may spend more, organize differently, or pursue higher ambition above the floor, provided the common guarantee remains intact and richer jurisdictions are not allowed to convert shared citizenship into gated sovereignty.

## Guardrails

- Do not pair decentralized mandates with inadequate revenue or opaque grants.
- Do not use equalization to force uniformity in every local choice.
- Do not let richer places veto solidarity by claiming complete local ownership of structural advantages.
- Do not confuse one-time bailout politics with durable equalization design.
- Do not let universal floors exist only on paper while delivery capacity collapses in poor regions.

## Failure modes

- **postcode lottery**: basic goods vary mainly with local tax base.
- **unfunded decentralization**: local units receive duties without the means to perform them.
- **equalization fog**: nobody can explain why one place receives more or less.
- **pro-cyclical localism**: downturns force the weakest places to cut hardest.
- **solidarity ceiling**: equalization is framed as illegitimate redistribution rather than as the companion of shared citizenship.

## Practical tests

An equalized multi-level order passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can it distinguish local choice from local fiscal luck?
2. Do transfers account for both fiscal capacity and expenditure need?
3. Are there universal floors that do not depend on municipal wealth?
4. Does the higher tier absorb most macro stabilization and asymmetric-shock capacity?
5. Can localities still choose, tax, and innovate above the floor without breaking equal citizenship?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity praises **local autonomy** but cannot show **how poor places can deliver a real common floor at a reasonable tax burden**, then it is not describing decentralization; it is describing **territorialized inequality with nicer language**.
