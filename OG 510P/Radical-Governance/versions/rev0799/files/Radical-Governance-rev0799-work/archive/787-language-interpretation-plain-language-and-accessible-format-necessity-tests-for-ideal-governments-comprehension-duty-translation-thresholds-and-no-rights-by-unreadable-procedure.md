# 787 — Language, interpretation, plain-language, and accessible-format necessity tests for ideal governments: comprehension duty, translation thresholds, and no rights by unreadable procedure

## One-line thesis

Ideal governments should require that **administrative procedure be intelligible to the people it governs**: binding notices, adverse cases, required actions, deadlines, hearing opportunities, and remedies should be communicated in **plain language, understandable form, and accessible modes**, with **interpretation, translation, and disability accommodation triggered by stakes and necessity** rather than treated as optional courtesy.

## Why this matters

The archive already says that notice and service are constitutionally important (`575`, `721`), that reasons and file access matter in appeals (`773`), that delay and evidentiary burden can quietly destroy rights (`778`, `785`), and that guidance should be publicly visible rather than hidden inside administrative folklore (`782`). What it still lacked as one compact operating rule was the simplest and most easily forgotten question of all:

**can the person actually understand what the state is saying and what the state expects next?**

Many governments satisfy themselves with formal transmission. A decision is “served” but written in dense legal prose that an ordinary reader cannot parse. A portal exists, but only in the dominant language. An interpreter is available only after sanction. A deaf person receives a hearing notice without sign-language planning. A blind person gets a scanned image file. A migrant entrepreneur receives a licensing demand in a language they cannot use and then loses for non-response. A person with an intellectual disability is technically given reasons, but not in a format they can meaningfully follow. In such systems, the state often insists that rights exist while designing procedure so that only specialists can read the map.

The opposite mistake is also real. Governments sometimes promise full translation of everything for everyone in every case, then fail operationally and produce backlogs, false expectations, and strategic opacity. Ideal design therefore needs honest thresholds: what must always be plain, what must be translated, when interpretation is necessary, which channels must be accessible by default, and which accommodations are triggered by the seriousness of the decision, the vulnerability of the person, or the state’s knowledge that comprehension is otherwise not realistic.

Ideal-government design therefore needs a doctrine of **comprehension necessity**. The point is not to abolish official languages or require every administrative memo in every language. It is to insist that legally significant procedure should be understandable enough to act on, and that when comprehension is not realistically present, the state owes additional explanation, accessible format, language support, or accommodation before it can honestly say the person had notice, was heard, or failed to respond.

## Necessity table

| Necessity test | What counts as proof | Honest design consequence | Default answer if proof is weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Need to make rights-bearing procedure intelligible** | people can receive formal notice without understanding obligation, deadline, or remedy | require plain-language core notices and decision summaries for legally significant acts | equate transmission with comprehension |
| **Need to distinguish ordinary service information from cases requiring translation or interpretation** | some matters are routine and others are severe, urgent, or high-stakes | type translation and interpretation triggers by stakes, adversity, vulnerability, and state knowledge | promise everything or provide almost nothing |
| **Need to include persons with disabilities on equal terms** | procedure depends on formats, channels, or hearing modes some people cannot use | require accessible formats, communication modes, and reasonable procedural accommodation | leave disability accommodation to informal goodwill |
| **Need to keep multilingual access realistic in diverse or cross-border administration** | one-language systems exclude users from basic compliance and remedy pathways | provide multilingual front-door information and translated key action prompts, with deeper support where stakes justify it | treat language opacity as the user’s private problem |
| **Need to preserve legal precision without making decisions unreadable** | legal drafting culture rewards density and boilerplate | separate precise legal grounds from clear summaries of what happened, why, and what comes next | write only for lawyers and then call it due process |
| **Need to stop digital administration from becoming comprehension by portal literacy only** | online systems assume literacy, device access, or interface familiarity that many lack | keep assisted-human routes, channel choice, and accessibility testing | equate usability for insiders with accessibility for everyone |
| **Need to make hearings and interviews genuinely participatory** | people can attend but not understand questions, evidence, or consequences | provide interpretation, communication support, breaks, and accessible materials before and during hearing | count bodily presence as meaningful participation |
| **Need to align accommodation with urgency and scale** | full bespoke translation of every low-stakes communication is impractical, but high-stakes adverse action without support is unfair | tier support by consequence, irreversibility, and known need while keeping core information always understandable | let operational convenience set fairness floors |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat comprehension as part of legality, not a customer-service add-on

An unintelligible administrative act is often only formally lawful. Where the state expects compliance, response, attendance, payment, correction, or appeal, it should assume a duty to communicate in a form that a reasonable affected person can understand well enough to act. Comprehension is not charity. It is part of fair procedure.

### 2. Separate legal precision from plain-language explanation

Decisions can contain their formal legal grounds while also giving a short plain-language explanation of what was decided, why, what evidence mattered, what must happen next, what the deadlines are, and what remedy routes exist. Plain-language summaries should not replace legal reasons; they should make them usable.

### 3. Translate the parts that carry action, risk, and rights

Not every background document must always be translated in full. But notices of proposed adverse action, final decisions, hearing dates, deadlines, remedy information, compliance obligations, and core requests for evidence should be available in a language the person can reasonably use where stakes, context, or state knowledge make that necessary. The more severe the consequence, the stronger the translation duty.

### 4. Keep multilingual front doors even where full-language parity is impossible

Websites, service counters, helplines, and intake forms should at minimum provide multilingual orientation about what the procedure is, what documents are needed, how to get help, and how to avoid default. This front-door layer prevents language opacity from becoming a hidden screening mechanism.

### 5. Provide interpretation for interactive procedure when comprehension otherwise fails

Interviews, hearings, inspections, complaint intake, eligibility assessments, and similar live interactions require interpretation or other communication support when a person cannot meaningfully participate otherwise. This includes sign-language interpretation, augmentative communication, or other modes appropriate to the person’s needs.

### 6. Build accessibility and accommodation into default design, not only after complaint

Accessible PDFs, screen-reader compatibility, captions, easy-read versions where needed, alternate formats, disability-aware scheduling, communication support, and accessible digital interfaces should be designed in upstream. Systems that wait for failure and complaint before accommodating are usually telling people to lose once before they can participate.

### 7. Keep assisted-human routes open alongside digital channels

Even excellent digital services do not remove the need for counters, phone support, community assistance, outreach, or caseworker help for people facing literacy, disability, language, age, poverty, housing instability, or technological barriers. “Available online” is not the same thing as “practically accessible.”

### 8. Trigger stronger support where the state knows or should know comprehension is weak

Where the administration is aware that the person has limited proficiency in the procedure language, a communication disability, low literacy, cognitive impairment, or other serious barrier, the state should not rely on ordinary-form notice alone and later treat non-response as clean default.

### 9. Keep records of what language and accessibility support was offered or provided

Review bodies should be able to see whether translation, interpretation, accessible format, or accommodation was requested, offered, refused, delayed, or provided inadequately. Otherwise comprehension failures disappear into unverifiable administrative memory.

### 10. Design thresholds honestly so accommodation is dependable, not mythical

Ideal systems should define what is always plain-language by default, what core multilingual material must always exist, when interpretation is mandatory, when translation of key documents is required, how urgent cases are handled, and how people request further accommodation. Vague promises of accessibility without typed triggers usually degrade into arbitrary help for the assertive and nothing for everyone else.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat formal delivery as proof of understanding.
- Do not write rights-bearing notices only in dense legal prose and then blame non-response on the user.
- Do not make portals, scanned images, or inaccessible digital forms the only practical route for compliance or appeal.
- Do not treat interpretation and disability accommodation as afterthoughts once default has already occurred.
- Do not promise universal translation in ways the system cannot actually deliver; type the strongest duties where stakes are highest.
- Do not let administrative convenience override the need for plain-language explanation of action, deadline, and remedy.
- Do not let hearing participation count as meaningful when the person could not understand the process.

## Failure modes

- **rights by unreadable procedure**: people formally receive process but cannot understand it well enough to act.
- **translation theater**: the state promises multilingual fairness but delivers only fragments after the decisive deadline.
- **digital legibility gap**: the interface works for insiders while excluding those with literacy, disability, or device barriers.
- **default by misunderstanding**: silence is treated as waiver even though the person never understood the demand.
- **lawyer-only decision writing**: reasons exist, but only specialists can extract the operative meaning.
- **accommodation after defeat**: support appears only once the person has already lost, missed, or defaulted.
- **unrecorded comprehension failure**: later review cannot tell whether support was needed, offered, or denied.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can tell you **that notice was served, evidence was requested, a hearing date was offered, and an appeal exists**, but cannot also show **what part of that procedure is always in plain language, when translation or interpretation is triggered, how disability accommodation and accessible formats are provided, what assisted-human routes remain open, and how non-response is treated when comprehension was not realistic**, then it still has **administrative procedure without a comprehension constitution**.
