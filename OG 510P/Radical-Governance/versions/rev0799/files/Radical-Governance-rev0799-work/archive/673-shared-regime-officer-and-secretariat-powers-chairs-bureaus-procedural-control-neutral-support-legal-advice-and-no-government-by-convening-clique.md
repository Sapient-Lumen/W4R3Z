# 673 — Shared-regime officer and secretariat powers: chairs, bureaus, procedural control, neutral support, legal advice, and no government by convening clique

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed officer-and-secretariat constitution** that separates presiding officers, bureau steering, secretariat support, legal and drafting assistance, document control, and emergency administrative acts — so the regime is not actually governed by whoever controls the agenda email, the microphone, or the drafting room.

## Why this matters

The archive now has notes on entry (`669`), exit (`670`), representation (`671`), and decision rules (`672`).

But a constitutional gap still remains between **who is allowed in the room** and **how the room is procedurally steered**.

In many shared regimes, the formal text describes members, meetings, and votes, yet the real operating constitution lives elsewhere:

- in the chair’s ability to recognize speakers or declare consensus,
- in the bureau’s ability to pre-sort what is mature enough for the floor,
- in the secretariat’s power to draft, cost, translate, circulate, or delay documents,
- and in the legal office’s ability to decide which text is procedurally clean enough to travel.

Without a typed officer-and-secretariat constitution, the regime often drifts into one of three pathologies.

The first is **procedural government by clique**: the people who schedule, sequence, and interpret procedure become the real rulers without visible constitutional authorization.

The second is **secretariat policy entrepreneurship without mandate**: support staff cease to be support staff and become the de facto source of substantive settlement because they alone hold continuity, drafting memory, and timing control.

The third is **chair rule by ambiguity**: points of order, consensus tests, speaker recognition, and referral decisions are treated as personal style rather than bounded authority.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **officer and secretariat discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate presiding authority from member representation

A presiding officer is not just another delegate with a better chair.

The archive should require regimes to say whether the presiding officer acts:

- as a representative of the participant that sent them,
- in a specifically impartial procedural capacity for the duration of office,
- or in a hybrid role with named limits.

If the regime wants a neutral chair, it should say so expressly and define what neutrality requires in recognition, rulings, sequencing, and public communications.

### 2. Separate the chair from the bureau and from the secretariat

These are different constitutional objects.

The **chair** presides. The **bureau** helps steer intersessional preparation, sequencing, and workload. The **secretariat** supports the body with documentation, logistics, translation, recordkeeping, and implementation follow-through as authorized.

The archive should reject any regime that collapses all three into one fuzzy category of “the organizers”.

### 3. Type agenda-shaping powers and pre-floor filtering

Power begins before debate.

Who may prepare a provisional agenda, decide whether an item is complete, request legal or financial implications, combine overlapping drafts, or recommend deferral is exercising real constitutional power.

The regime should therefore publish:

- who holds each pre-floor function,
- what criteria apply,
- what notice or circulation minimums exist,
- and what appeal or override lane exists if an item is delayed, bundled, or refused.

### 4. Keep secretariat support visibly supportive unless a substantive mandate is expressly delegated

Secretariats need real operating authority. They may need to receive and circulate documents, manage translations, prepare background papers, estimate budget impact, track mandates, and co-ordinate implementation.

But the archive should insist on a visible line between:

- support and facilitation,
- delegated technical implementation,
- and substantive political direction.

If the secretariat may propose text, settle language, or act on behalf of the body between sessions, that authority should be explicit, bounded, and reviewable.

### 5. Publish one legal-advice and drafting lane

Shared regimes often blur legal advice, political drafting, and final authentication.

The archive should require one visible drafting-and-legal lane that says:

- who may issue procedural or legal advice,
- whether that advice is binding, rebuttable, or merely advisory,
- who controls consolidated drafts,
- who certifies that a text is procedurally ripe,
- and who authenticates the final adopted text.

No regime should force participants to guess whether “the secretariat’s version” is merely convenient paper or the authoritative draft.

### 6. Bind officer powers to terms, succession, recusal, and replacement rules

An honest constitution does not assume the officer corps will always be present, trusted, and conflict-free.

The regime should therefore say:

- how officers are chosen,
- how long they serve,
- who substitutes when they are absent,
- when recusal is expected,
- and how vacancies are filled.

Temporary convenience should not silently become a new constitutional doctrine.

### 7. Give emergency administrative acts a ratification clock

Between sessions, something still has to happen: rooms must be booked, urgent notices may need to issue, technical deadlines may need clarification, and implementation follow-up may require administrative action.

The archive should allow emergency or interim administrative acts only if the regime also names:

- who may take them,
- what counts as administrative rather than substantive,
- how they are logged,
- and by when the relevant body must ratify, revise, or cancel them.

### 8. Record officer rulings, bureau steering decisions, and secretariat gatekeeping actions

A regime that records only final resolutions will hide the constitution that actually mattered.

The archive should therefore prefer a procedural trace that captures, where relevant:

- rulings on points of order,
- agenda admissions and deferrals,
- document admissibility decisions,
- bundling or referral decisions,
- and interim administrative acts taken between sessions.

This need not mean maximal transcript culture. It means the constitutional control points are visible after the fact.

### 9. Do not let continuity memory become hidden constitutional supremacy

Secretariats and long-serving officers usually know more than rotating delegates. That continuity is useful.

But the archive should resist letting continuity memory become a substitute constitution. If an operating practice matters repeatedly, it should be promoted into the visible rulebook, decision map, standing guidance, or officer terms of reference.

### 10. Publish one officer-and-secretariat map by body

A serious regime should maintain one visible map showing, for each body and session type:

- who chairs,
- what the bureau may do,
- what the secretariat may do,
- who issues legal or drafting advice,
- what interim acts are possible between sessions,
- and what review or appeal lane exists.

The archive should reject any regime where members must reconstruct real authority from protocol habits, staff memory, and who happened to send the email.

## Working matrix

| Function | Honest owner | Why | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| presiding, speaker recognition, points of order | chair under standing rules | keeps debate governable | treating personal style as constitutional law |
| intersessional sequencing and workload steering | bureau or equivalent under mandate | allows preparation without floor overload | letting bureau convenience become substantive agenda veto |
| document receipt, circulation, translation, records | secretariat | maintains continuity and administrative reliability | confusing clerical custody with substantive authorship |
| procedural or legal advice | named legal/advisory lane | lets members know what kind of authority they are hearing | secret legal authority by hallway interpretation |
| emergency administrative follow-up | secretariat or officers under narrow interim power | keeps the regime operational between sessions | using “administration” to smuggle substantive policy change |
| final adoption and authentication | competent body plus named certifier | preserves the difference between draft and adopted text | treating the latest working draft as law |

## Guardrails

- Do not hide agenda-shaping power inside “preparation”.
- Do not let the same actor preside, filter, draft, authenticate, and interpret without explicit authorization.
- Do not describe secretariat support as neutral if it includes unreviewable substantive settlement power.
- Do not permit emergency interim action without a logging and ratification clock.
- Do not rely on custom where recurring practice should be written down.
- Do not force participants to guess whether an officer ruling or secretariat instruction is binding.

## Failure modes

- **convening-clique government**: a small officer or staff circle becomes the real constitutional core.
- **chair absolutism**: the presiding officer turns recognition, sequencing, and consensus testing into personal prerogative.
- **secretariat drift**: support staff quietly become the main source of policy settlement because the rules never typed their role.
- **procedural laundering**: substantive exclusion is hidden inside admissibility, consolidation, translation timing, or cost-preparation decisions.
- **continuity capture**: long memory and staff access substitute for visible authority.
- **interim power creep**: administrative follow-up becomes substantive rule change between sessions.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it distinguish presiding, bureau, and secretariat roles?
2. Are agenda-preparation and pre-floor filtering powers visible and reviewable?
3. Is there one named lane for legal or procedural advice and draft control?
4. Are officer terms, substitution, vacancy, and recusal rules explicit?
5. Are emergency interim administrative acts typed and time-limited?
6. Are procedural rulings and gatekeeping actions recorded somewhere reviewable?
7. Can members tell the difference between a working draft, a certified draft, and an adopted text?
8. Is there one public map of officer and secretariat authority by body?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can name **members, meetings, and votes** but cannot say **who actually controls agendas, speaker recognition, document flow, legal advice, interim acts, and text authentication**, it still has **constitutional nouns without an honest operating constitution**.
