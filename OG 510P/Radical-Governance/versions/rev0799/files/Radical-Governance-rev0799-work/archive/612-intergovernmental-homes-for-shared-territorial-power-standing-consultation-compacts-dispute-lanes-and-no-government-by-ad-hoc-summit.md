# 612 — Intergovernmental homes for shared territorial power: standing consultation, compacts, dispute lanes, and no government by ad hoc summit

## One-line thesis

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, it needs an honest **intergovernmental home** as well as a democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, and public-law one: a standing place in state decision-making, lawful consultation routes, regular bargaining forums, explicit compact and co-financing lanes, visible conflict-escalation and dispute rules, and no territorial government by occasional summit, ministry favour, or host-city mediation.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a fairly complete ladder for the constitutional upgrade of shared territorial power. It can say when co-operation has become real government, what democratic home should follow, what fiscal base it needs, what operating core it needs, what accountability stack it needs, and what public-law form it should inhabit.

What it still lacked was the **intergovernmental analogue**. A metropolitan or regional tier may have an assembly, a budget, staff, audit, and legal personality, yet still remain constitutionally half-built if its real relationship with the state above and the municipalities below is managed through sporadic leader summits, closed ministry bargains, emergency taskforces, project-by-project deals, or dependence on one host city to speak for the territory.

That gap matters because shared territorial government almost never governs alone. It sits inside a stack of national laws, transfers, sector ministries, local authorities, regulators, and sometimes treaty-facing obligations. If those relationships are not given an honest institutional home, even a formally constituted metropolitan or regional body can end up governing by permission slip rather than by right.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Give real shared territorial government a standing place in the intergovernmental order

A territorial tier that carries real powers should not have to rely on personal access, party alignment, or the political generosity of a minister or capital-city mayor in order to be heard. The archive should therefore prefer **standing intergovernmental position** once metropolitan or regional power is real: a named role in consultation, negotiation, co-ordination, and implementation rather than occasional invitation.

This does not require permanent constitutional drama. It requires that the polity be able to answer a simple question: **where does this tier go when national or upper-tier decisions materially affect its competences, resources, boundaries, or essential interests?**

### 2. Separate three relationships: upward, downward, and lateral

Intergovernmental design gets blurry when every relationship is treated as one generic dialogue field. The archive should distinguish:

- **upward relations** between the shared territorial tier and the state or national sector ministries,
- **downward or member relations** between the shared tier and component municipalities or districts,
- **lateral relations** with neighbouring regions, metros, counties, or transboundary authorities.

Each relationship needs different instruments. Upward relations need consultation, participation, and negotiated implementation channels. Member relations need clear voice, co-operation, and conflict rules that do not erase local self-government. Lateral relations need compact, corridor, watershed, and mutual-adjustment tools.

### 3. Put consultation in due time, by rule, and near the real decision point

A real intergovernmental home is not satisfied by being told about decisions after the bargaining is over. The archive should prefer consultation that is:

- **in due time**, before the meaningful choice is frozen,
- **appropriate in form**, with the information, draft texts, financial numbers, and implementation assumptions needed for real response,
- **tied to matters that directly affect competences, essential interests, resources, or the scope of self-government**.

The point is not to create an all-purpose veto. It is to prevent the shared territorial tier from being treated as an implementation contractor for policies it never had a real chance to shape.

### 4. Prefer standing bodies and regular calendars over summit improvisation

Intergovernmental life should not depend on whether leaders happen to like each other this quarter. The archive should therefore prefer **standing committees, joint conferences, formal dialogue fora, and recurring calendars** over improvised crisis summits or invitation-only mayoral roundtables.

Standing forums make roles visible, reduce access inequality, create routinised preparation, and allow disputes to be surfaced before they harden into constitutional fights. They are the intergovernmental equivalent of moving from personalist rule to institution.

### 5. Use deals, contracts, and compacts below the constitutional layer, not instead of it

Shared territorial government will often need contracts, city deals, framework agreements, investment compacts, and co-financing arrangements. The archive should keep these tools — but refuse to let them substitute for the intergovernmental constitution itself.

A good compact should sit **below** the standing constitutional relationship. It can specify delivery, sequencing, milestones, and finance for a bounded programme. It should not be the only reason a metropolitan or regional tier can enter the room, obtain recognition, or coordinate with the state.

### 6. Make dispute, deadlock, and escalation routes explicit

An intergovernmental relationship is not serious unless it can survive disagreement. The archive should therefore prefer visible rules for:

- ordinary working-level resolution,
- escalation to political principals,
- mediation or arbitration where the system allows it,
- legality review or judicial recourse where rights or competences are at stake,
- and default continuation rules so a temporary dispute does not erase the shared tier in practice.

Without these lanes, every disagreement gets forced into public theatre, fiscal punishment, or quiet ministry override.

### 7. Keep member-government voice visible without reducing the territorial tier to a hostage forum

A shared territorial government usually remains entangled with municipalities and districts whose own democratic standing must not be erased. The archive should therefore preserve visible member-government voice in intergovernmental machinery — often through associations, conference formats, committee representation, or consultation duties.

But member voice should not collapse the territorial tier back into a mere consortium. Once the shared body has its own democratic home, it should be able to speak as a territorial institution in its own right rather than only as a broker of municipal permissions.

### 8. Give the tier a route into national and treaty-facing policy where its competences are implicated

Some of the most consequential choices for metropolitan and regional government are made in national legislation, national infrastructure programmes, sector strategies, and the implementation of international or supranational obligations. The archive should therefore prefer representation or consultation routes where the shared tier’s competences, interests, or scope of self-government are materially affected.

This matters especially for land use, transport, climate adaptation, energy, water, regional development, and large capital-investment regimes. A tier that carries implementation burdens without any route into upstream design is being treated as a delivery arm, not as a government.

### 9. Do not let emergency coordination become the permanent constitution

Crisis taskforces can be useful during floods, pandemics, energy shocks, or major disruptions. But the archive should reject arrangements in which the real intergovernmental life of a territory only works during emergency and then falls back into fragmentation, or worse, where emergency tables become the permanent substitute for lawful standing relations.

Emergency coordination should plug into ordinary constitutional channels and then stand back down. A polity should not discover, after the fact, that its true metropolitan or regional constitution was an emergency cabinet all along.

### 10. Publish the intergovernmental spine as part of the public constitution of the territory

Residents should be able to tell where a metropolitan or regional government goes to bargain, object, negotiate, align programmes, and escalate conflict. The archive should therefore prefer a visible **intergovernmental spine**: named bodies, participation rights, schedules, agenda-setting rules, publication duties, and basic outcome records.

This is not mere transparency hygiene. It is how a territorial tier stops being governed through private access, informal convening power, and constitutional folklore.

## Intergovernmental-home matrix

| Question | Thin or dishonest arrangement | Honest intergovernmental home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **How is the tier heard by the state?** | ad hoc invitations, personal access, host-city mediation | standing consultation or representation route |
| **Where do recurring bargains happen?** | summit improvisation, leader-only bargaining | regular committee, conference, or dialogue forum |
| **How are programmes implemented jointly?** | one-off opaque deals | bounded compacts below a stable constitutional relationship |
| **What happens in conflict?** | override, ghosting, fiscal punishment, public theatre | defined escalation, mediation, legality review, or judicial recourse |
| **How do municipalities remain present?** | either erased or able to veto everything | visible member-government voice without collapsing the territorial tier |
| **How does the tier enter treaty-facing or national reform processes?** | consulted late or not at all | appropriate representation/consultation where competences or essential interests are implicated |

## Failure modes

- **government by ad hoc summit**: the territorial tier exists, but only gains access through episodic leader bargaining.
- **ministry favour constitutionalism**: the shared tier governs by discretionary upper-tier goodwill rather than durable institutional standing.
- **host-city ventriloquism**: one large municipality or capital city speaks for the territory because no proper intergovernmental route exists.
- **deal-only federalism**: every serious project requires a fresh bespoke bargain because no stable intergovernmental lane is built.
- **consultation after lock-in**: the tier is formally consulted, but only after the real choices are already closed.
- **member-veto regression**: municipal participation is designed so strongly that the shared territorial tier can never act as a government in its own right.
- **delivery-arm downgrade**: the tier carries implementation burdens but has no route into upstream policy, legislation, or treaty implementation.
- **permanent emergency table**: crisis co-ordination machinery quietly becomes the territory’s real constitution.

## Practical tests

A shared territorial body has an honest intergovernmental home when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is there a standing route by which the body is involved in state decision-making affecting its competences, essential interests, or the scope of self-government?
2. Are consultation rights timed early enough and supplied with enough information to matter?
3. Do regular forums exist for recurring bargaining, alignment, and review, rather than only episodic summits?
4. Are compacts and co-financing deals used as bounded implementation tools rather than as substitutes for constitutional standing?
5. Are relations with member municipalities or districts structured so they retain voice without reducing the territorial tier to a permission club?
6. Are conflict, deadlock, and escalation routes explicit enough that disagreement does not collapse into override or paralysis?
7. Does the body have an appropriate route into national, supranational, or treaty-implementation processes where its competences are materially implicated?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a metropolitan or regional tier now governs real territory but nobody can plainly say **where it is heard by the state, how it bargains with member governments, how conflicts escalate, and through which lawful channel it enters national or treaty-facing decisions that affect its competences**, then it still lacks **an intergovernmental home for shared territorial power** and is drifting into **government by ad hoc summit**.
