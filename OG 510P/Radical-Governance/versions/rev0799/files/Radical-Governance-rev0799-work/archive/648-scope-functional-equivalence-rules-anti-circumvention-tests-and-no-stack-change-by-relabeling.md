# 648 — Scope functional-equivalence rules, anti-circumvention tests, and no stack change by relabeling

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should classify **materially stack-altering moves by their real effect rather than by their chosen label**: if a grant condition, procurement framework, software default, model contract, coordination protocol, “pilot”, guidance note, or emergency instruction functionally reallocates who decides, who must comply, who pays, who may object, or which tier now governs in practice, it should travel through the same packet, opinion, override, register, and review lanes as any openly named scope change.

## Why this matters

The archive now says much more clearly **how the scope stack should be assigned, maintained, changed, launched, reviewed, and cured** (`602`, `628`-`647`). It has doctrines for burden of proof, lawful rescoping, handback, trigger systems, canonical registers, scope packets, opinions, overrides, clear statements, conformance, observability, settlement, retirement, reactivation, nullity, standstill, severability, and bounded remand.

That is already a major improvement. But one loophole still remains large enough to swallow most of the architecture.

A ministry says it is not reclaiming municipal discretion; it is only attaching “delivery expectations” to a grant. A metro authority says it is not reallocating jurisdiction; it is only standardising forms, dashboards, and escalation paths in the shared platform. A national agency says it is not occupying a field; it is only publishing model contract language that no local authority can safely refuse. A crisis cell says it is not extending emergency command; it is only keeping the incident workflow, notification chain, and approval ladder in place “until systems settle”.

Every actor in those examples reaches for the same defense: **this is not really a scope event**.

Sometimes that defense is honest. Governments really do need nonbinding guidance, technical templates, reference implementations, intergovernmental coordination memos, and temporary operating instructions. Not every shared spreadsheet or budget condition is a constitutional moment.

But sometimes labels are being used to defeat the archive's own discipline. A document called guidance can become binding because funding, software permissions, inspections, reporting, and public blame all make deviation impossible. A “pilot” can operate at territorial scale with no realistic path back. A “shared service” can become practical subordination. A standards body can use interoperability rules to dictate substantive policy choices. A procurement template can decide the legal relation before legislatures or courts ever speak.

The archive already warned, in the preemption note, against **silent override by upper-tier convenience** (`584`). It also already warned, in the clear-statement note, against **rescoping by ambiguity** (`638`). What it still lacked was a compact general doctrine saying that reviewers should **look through form to effect** whenever an instrument is plausibly functioning as a stack change.

Without that doctrine, the archive risks becoming too formalistic. It can require packets and opinions for clearly labeled scope changes while leaving the largest real reallocations to occur through the very instruments most likely to be described as non-constitutional.

An honest archive needs a short rule against that evasion.

That is the role of **scope functional-equivalence rules**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Classify stack-bearing measures by effect, not by label

The first rule should be simple: the polity should ask **what the measure does**, not just what it is called.

If an instrument materially changes:

- who holds effective decision rights,
- which tier may veto, approve, or supervise another,
- which territory or constituency is bound,
- which body bears the fiscal burden or operating risk,
- which review or remedy route is available,
- or which authority becomes the unavoidable practical gatekeeper,

then it should be treated as a potential scope event even if it is styled as guidance, a protocol, a budget condition, a software release, a model term, or a temporary operational note.

### 2. Use one functional-equivalence test for hidden rescoping

A polity should treat an instrument as **functionally equivalent to scope change** where, in the ordinary course of life, affected authorities or residents can no longer exercise the discretion, route, or protected self-government that the formal legal text claims they still retain.

At minimum, reviewers should ask whether the instrument in practice:

- makes deviation practically impossible;
- changes who must obtain permission from whom;
- centralizes the decisive data, workflow, registry, or switch;
- converts optional coordination into operational compulsion;
- narrows a lower tier's lawful room without saying so expressly;
- or creates a new dependency that cannot be exited without major political or service collapse.

Where the answer is yes, the sponsor should not be allowed to hide behind the instrument's softer name.

### 3. Treat money, code, contracts, and workflows as override-capable instruments

The archive should explicitly name the main modern vehicles of disguised rescoping.

These include:

- conditional grants or fiscal rescue packages;
- framework contracts, concessions, and procurement templates;
- shared-service agreements and host-agency terms;
- platform defaults, permissions, workflow design, and release choices;
- registry access rules and interoperability requirements;
- performance regimes, scorecards, escalation ladders, and reporting conditions;
- emergency operating notes and continuity bridges;
- and model legal text or “voluntary” guidance backed by inspection, finance, or reputational pressure.

These devices are not inherently illegitimate. The point is narrower: when they materially rearrange the live stack, they should be reviewed as scope-bearing instruments, not waved away as technicalities.

### 4. Put the burden on the sponsor to prove that the measure is only supportive or coordinative

If a sponsor claims that an instrument is merely advisory, coordinative, temporary, or technical, the sponsor should carry the burden of showing that the affected tier still retains real alternative routes.

That showing should usually include:

- lawful power to decline or vary the instrument;
- no hidden fiscal penalty for disagreement;
- no code or access lock that makes deviation impossible;
- no change to the review, complaint, or appeal route;
- and no public presentation that misdescribes the relationship as already settled.

Where that showing cannot be made, the safer classification is to treat the measure as at least a provisional scope event.

### 5. Distinguish genuine implementation support from hidden constitutional redesign

The archive should not criminalize normal coordination. Many higher-tier instruments are legitimate support: shared templates, technical standards, procurement advice, interoperable schemas, fallback hosting, or temporary incident discipline can all help a fragmented system work better.

The line is crossed when the supportive instrument stops helping another tier exercise its own lawful function and starts **substituting for, narrowing, or commandeering** that function.

A good practical test is this: after the instrument lands, can the lower or parallel tier still make a real governing choice without asking leave from the instrument's owner? If not, the measure is no longer just support.

### 6. Aggregate cumulative small moves instead of pretending each one is trivial alone

Disguised rescoping often happens through accumulation. One grant condition is minor. One dashboard is only reporting. One workflow default is supposedly temporary. One standard contract term is “for consistency”. One review gate is “just risk management”.

Taken one by one, each move looks too small to trigger constitutional attention. Taken together, they may recreate the whole stack.

The archive should therefore require reviewers to consider the **combined operational effect** of linked instruments, especially when they land on the same function, territory, or protected discretion.

No actor should be allowed to say “none of these pieces alone changed the stack” if the package plainly did so in aggregate.

### 7. Force hidden scope claims into the visible packet and opinion chain

When a functional-equivalence concern is credible, the polity should produce one visible classification object — whether a **scope-equivalence opinion**, supplementary packet note, or equivalent.

That object should say:

- which instrument is under examination;
- which real stack consequences are alleged;
- whether the measure is non-material, materially stack-bearing, or temporarily stack-bearing;
- whether a fresh packet or stronger consent route is required;
- and what interim status applies while classification is pending.

This prevents the archive's packet and opinion machinery from being evaded by simply denying that a scope question exists.

### 8. Apply the same remedies to disguised rescoping as to openly mislabeled rescoping

If a measure is found to be functionally equivalent to scope change, the remedy should track its real effect.

Depending on the case, that may mean:

- reclassifying the measure into the proper packet lane;
- freezing or narrowing its live operation;
- rerunning consultation or consent;
- issuing an override statement if the sponsor insists on proceeding;
- updating the canonical scope register;
- or treating the defect under the archive's nullity, severability, standstill, remand, or handback doctrines (`631`, `633`-`647`).

The critical rule is that relabeling should not produce a lighter remedy than an equivalent openly named stack change would face.

### 9. Preserve the difference between interoperability floors and disguised substantive occupation

The archive should be especially careful with standards, schemas, interoperability requirements, and shared infrastructure.

These often do need some upward coordination. But a technical floor should not be allowed to smuggle in substantive policy occupation.

A registry schema may require one field name; it should not silently dictate who owns the underlying competence. A shared payments rail may require timing and message standards; it should not automatically decide budget authority. A case-management platform may require compatible status codes; it should not erase lawful local discretion about review, sequencing, or service design unless that change has openly traveled through the proper scope lane.

### 10. When in doubt, keep the measure provisional, challengeable, and openly typed

The archive should prefer honest provisionality to false innocence.

If there is a serious question whether an instrument is stack-bearing, the polity should not rush to treat it as ordinary background administration. It should classify it visibly, keep it challengeable, and avoid promoting it to settled constitutional fact by elapsed time, deployment inertia, or repeated use.

That approach works with the archive's newer live-stack doctrines: uncertain instruments can stay provisional, observable, and reviewable without forcing immediate collapse (`640`-`645`).

## Guardrails

- Do not let labels such as guidance, pilot, framework, template, protocol, or platform default decide whether a stack event exists.
- Do not treat fiscal dependence, code locks, shared procurement, or registry access rules as neutral merely because they are not statutes.
- Do not require challengers to prove impossibility of all alternative behavior when the sponsor controls the main budget, workflow, or permissions path.
- Do not assess each small instrument in isolation if the combined package plainly reallocates who governs.
- Do not let technical interoperability become a cover for substantive occupation of another tier's lawful discretion.

## Failure modes

- **rescoping by relabeling**: a materially stack-bearing move is denied constitutional status because it carries a softer administrative name.
- **guidance capture**: formally optional guidance becomes practically binding through inspections, finance, or public blame.
- **platform sovereignty drift**: the owner of code, identity, or workflow becomes the real sovereign while legal text still describes distributed authority.
- **pilot imperialism**: a temporary or experimental arrangement quietly becomes territory-wide government without the packet, opinion, or settlement chain.
- **aggregation blindness**: reviewers examine each instrument separately and miss the fact that the live stack changed through cumulative small controls.
- **technical preemption theater**: interoperability or shared infrastructure is used to erase policy discretion while insisting that nothing constitutional happened.

## Anti-theater tests

1. If the instrument were stripped of its current label, would its operational effect still look like a scope change?
2. After the measure lands, can the affected tier still make a real governing choice without asking leave from the sponsor's budget, code, contract, or approval chain?
3. Would refusal or variation carry a hidden fiscal, reputational, service, or access penalty strong enough to make the measure effectively compulsory?
4. Has the review looked at the cumulative effect of linked instruments rather than judging each one in isolation?
5. Does the measure change who really controls the decisive registry, workflow, permission, or complaint route?
6. Is the instrument only setting a narrow floor for interoperability or legality, or is it actually occupying the substantive field?
7. If the measure is truly only guidance or support, can the sponsor demonstrate lawful headroom, live alternatives, and unchanged remedy routes?
8. Has a visible packet, opinion, or equivalent classification object recorded the issue, or is the polity relying on administrative folklore?
9. If the measure is functionally equivalent to a scope change, will it face the same standstill, nullity, remand, handback, or register consequences as an openly named one?
10. If there is real doubt, is the instrument being kept provisional and challengeable rather than allowed to settle by repeated use?

## Closing image

A constitutional stack should not be so naive that it only notices a change when someone politely calls it one. It should be able to say: **this may be guidance in form, but it is government in effect** — and therefore it must travel through the same visible law, review, memory, and handback lanes as any other real change to who governs what.
