# 742 — Tombstone-rerouting packets for retired government bodies, scopes, and shells: successor drift, lookup updates, correction chains, and no historical rewrite by fresh redirect

## One-line thesis

Once the archive can leave a retired authority with a tombstone (`741`), it should also keep one compact **tombstone-rerouting packet** for the later case where the first successor, record home, archive home, or legacy-question lane itself changes: append a dated correction chain, preserve the original death statement and prior routing history, update the lookup map and machine-readable crosswalk, and refuse to let a new redirect rewrite the old constitutional story as if the dead thing had always belonged to the newest holder.

## Why this matters

The archive can now do the hard part of ending an institutional object honestly.

It can say when a scope or body becomes settled (`641`), when it retires (`642`), when it may return only through a fresh reactivation act (`643`), how successor accountability should survive a move (`657`), how public records and official publications should outlive disappearing institutions (`571`), how official notice and service routes should stay explicit (`575`), and how a dead authority should leave behind one public-facing tombstone packet (`741`).

What it still lacked was the next small object for the messier long tail: **what happens when the tombstone’s own routing facts later change**.

That problem is ordinary, not exotic. A dissolved regulator’s first successor is itself merged three years later. A parent department that temporarily answered legacy questions hands that lane to a records office or ombuds team. A former archive home changes repository, catalogue, or URL. A split-successor crosswalk becomes clearer after litigation, legislation, or delayed records sorting. A dead service route keeps resolving, but the redirect now points somewhere else than it did at the moment of closure. A body dies with “no direct successor,” then a later statute assigns one after all. Or the tombstone’s original explanation turns out to have been incomplete, because the public did not yet know which identifiers, datasets, or disclosure duties would remain live.

If the archive has no compact doctrine for that later update, institutions fall into two bad habits.

The first is **historical rewrite by redirect**. Operators silently change the dead page, replace the original successor story, and make it look as though the retired authority had always flowed cleanly into the newest holder. The public loses the intermediate chain, and later accountability questions become harder to reconstruct.

The second is **stale-crosswalk memorialism**. The tombstone remains frozen even after its routes, contacts, or record homes have plainly changed, so the public keeps arriving at broken addresses, obsolete successor claims, or wrong disclosure lanes.

The archive therefore needs one small proof-object between those two failures: **a tombstone-rerouting packet**. It should not reopen live authority, and it should not replace the original death statement. It should simply say: the dead thing remains dead; here is the dated update to successor, archive, record, identifier, or question-routing information; here is the chain back to earlier versions; and here is why the new route does not retroactively erase the older constitutional path. Without that packet, the archive still permits **historical rewrite by fresh redirect**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Trigger a rerouting packet only when a tombstone’s lookup facts change materially

A tombstone-rerouting packet should not be opened for every typo or design refresh. It should be triggered when a person arriving through the dead thing would now need a materially different route than the one previously declared.

At minimum, the archive should treat these as rerouting triggers:

- a **successor drift** event, where the named successor later merges, splits, renames, or hands the function onward;
- a **record-home move**, where authoritative files or disclosure custody move to a different holder or repository;
- an **archive-home move**, where preserved historical web or publication material now lives somewhere else;
- a **legacy-question-lane move**, where the bounded post-death question route changes team, body, or address;
- an **identifier-map expansion**, where the polity later learns that more aliases, prefixes, programme names, or URLs must resolve;
- or a **material correction**, where the original tombstone misdescribed successor structure, legal basis, or route boundaries.

If none of those are present, ordinary page maintenance is enough.

### 2. Preserve the original death statement and original tombstone version

The rerouting packet should be additive, not substitutive.

The original tombstone should remain historically visible with:

- the original death condition;
- the original effective date;
- the original successor or no-successor statement;
- and the original public explanation as it stood when the authority first ceased to be live.

The new packet should then state what changed later. This prevents institutions from laundering a messy or provisional first successor story into a cleaner retrospective myth.

### 3. Classify what kind of rerouting has happened

A tombstone-rerouting packet should say what changed, because later readers need to know whether the polity is correcting the memory object, not reviving the dead authority.

At minimum it should distinguish:

- **forward reroute** — the previously named successor or question lane has changed again;
- **split reroute** — one old route now resolves into several distinct successors or archive homes;
- **consolidation reroute** — several earlier successor routes now collapse into one;
- **correction reroute** — the earlier tombstone was materially wrong or incomplete;
- **route retirement** — a former legacy-question lane has ended and another has lawfully replaced it;
- **archive-only reroute** — the live question lane stays the same but historical or publication lookup has moved.

Without these classes, future readers cannot tell whether they are looking at a correction, a new successor drift, or a disguised reactivation.

### 4. Keep a dated routing chain rather than one overwritten destination

The rerouting packet should not say only where the dead thing points *now*. It should also preserve where it pointed *before* and during which date range.

That routing chain should include, where relevant:

- the first tombstone state;
- each later successor crosswalk state;
- each archive-home or record-home move;
- each legacy-question-lane move;
- and the effective date for each step.

This lets the public ask historically honest questions such as: where should someone have gone in 2027, even if the answer is different in 2031?

### 5. Update one machine-readable lookup map as well as the human-facing tombstone

A dead page that changes without a matching structured lookup surface is only half repaired. The archive should therefore require the rerouting packet to update one machine-readable map covering:

- current and prior successor targets;
- current and prior record and archive homes;
- legacy identifiers, aliases, URLs, and prefixes;
- effective dates for route changes;
- and any route class limits, such as records-only, archive-only, or question-lane-only.

This keeps later merges, search tools, and public-service front doors from depending on folklore inside one editorial team.

### 6. Use a visible correction chain instead of silent replacement

The rerouting packet should visibly link:

- back to the original tombstone packet (`741`);
- back to any earlier rerouting packets;
- and forward from older routing statements where practical.

That means a person who lands on the old dead page or an archived copy should still be able to discover that a later routing update exists, rather than being stranded with stale but plausible instructions.

### 7. Keep the legacy-question lane continuous during rerouting

A tombstone reroute is not only an information-design event. It is also a continuity event.

Whenever the bounded legacy-question lane changes, the packet should state:

- which lane accepts questions during the handoff;
- whether old addresses forward or only explain;
- whether clocks are preserved when a user contacts the earlier lane in good faith;
- and who is responsible for wrong-door routing during the change window.

A dead institution should not become harder to navigate merely because its surviving lookup route moved again.

### 8. Distinguish route repair from constitutional resurrection

A rerouting packet must say plainly that the dead thing remains dead.

It should therefore block:

- new transactional intake through the tombstone;
- fresh present-tense service language that suggests the old body is back;
- successor language so broad that the memorial page starts acting like the newest holder’s live front door;
- and any update that quietly removes the original death condition.

The packet repairs lookup. It does not reopen authority.

### 9. Preserve old identifiers and search paths even after several reroutes

The archive should assume that people arrive late and with imperfect clues. Even after successor drift, the rerouting packet should preserve old search handles such as:

- earlier body names and acronyms;
- programme and scheme names;
- legacy case or permit prefixes;
- obsolete domains, URLs, and subpaths where practical;
- and old letterhead or office names that still appear in uploaded documents.

The point is not to keep every dead surface live. It is to keep enough lookup memory that institutional archaeology does not require insider veterans.

### 10. Give rerouting packets a light but explicit review rule

A tombstone-rerouting packet should not be immortal by neglect. It should carry a light review trigger such as:

- successor reorganisation;
- broken-link detection;
- closure of the current question lane;
- record-home migration;
- or repeated user arrival through misrouted identifiers.

The archive should prefer event-based rereview over arbitrary perpetual maintenance, but it should still name who checks the crosswalk when those events occur.

## Guardrails

- Do not rewrite the original tombstone as if later successor drift had always been true.
- Do not leave a dead authority’s lookup page frozen when its routes, record homes, or archive homes have materially changed.
- Do not use silent redirects alone where a dated public rerouting explanation is needed.
- Do not let a rerouting packet broaden into a live intake or service surface.
- Do not drop old identifiers, aliases, or prior route history just because the newest holder prefers a cleaner narrative.
- Do not treat route maintenance as purely editorial when complaint, disclosure, or legacy-rights clocks still depend on it.

## Failure modes

- **historical rewrite by fresh redirect**: a new successor is inserted silently, erasing the earlier routing chain and making the constitutional story look cleaner than it was.
- **stale-crosswalk memorialism**: the tombstone remains unchanged long after its declared successor, record home, or question lane has moved.
- **route-chain amnesia**: current routing is known, but nobody can later reconstruct what the tombstone said in earlier periods.
- **legacy-question loss on reroute**: the question lane moves, but good-faith arrivals at the old lane lose time, proof, or remedy.
- **memorial expansion**: a rerouting update turns the dead page into a broad live-service hub for the newest successor.
- **identifier pruning theater**: old names and handles are removed for tidiness, making historical lookup depend on internal memory.

## Practical tests

A tombstone-rerouting packet is honest when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it leave the original death statement visible instead of overwriting it?
2. Does it classify the reroute type clearly: successor drift, split, consolidation, correction, route retirement, or archive-only move?
3. Does it preserve a dated chain of prior routing states rather than only the latest destination?
4. Does it update one machine-readable crosswalk as well as the human-facing tombstone?
5. Does it keep the legacy-question lane continuous, with clear wrong-door handling during the change?
6. Does it preserve old aliases, identifiers, and search paths that people still realistically use?
7. Does it distinguish route repair from constitutional resurrection of the dead body?
8. Can a future reader tell what the dead thing pointed to in each relevant period, and why it later changed?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **this authority or scope lane is dead** and can also leave one tombstone for it (`741`), it should still be able to show one compact packet for **later successor drift, route correction, archive-home change, record-home move, and legacy-question-lane movement without rewriting the original death story or resurrecting live authority by memorial page**. Otherwise it still permits **historical rewrite by fresh redirect**.
