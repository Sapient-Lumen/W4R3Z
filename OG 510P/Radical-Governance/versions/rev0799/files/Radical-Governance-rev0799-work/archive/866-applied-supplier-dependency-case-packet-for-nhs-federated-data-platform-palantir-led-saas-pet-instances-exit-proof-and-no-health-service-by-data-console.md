# 866 — Applied supplier-dependency case packet for NHS Federated Data Platform: Palantir-led SaaS, PET, instances, exit proof, and no health service by data console

## One-line thesis

The NHS Federated Data Platform is best treated as a supplier-dependent operational data-platform waist: a federated SaaS service used by NHS England, trusts, and integrated care boards to connect operational health data, where public control, local controller duties, privacy-enhancing technology, audit logs, product boundaries, migration rights, staff trust, patient rights, and exit proof must be visible before the platform is praised as transformation or condemned as outsourced health government.

## Why this matters

This is the archive's first applied test of `859`. It takes a supplier-dependency lens out of abstract cloud / model-provider doctrine and applies it to a high-stakes public service where data, workflow, waiting-list management, discharge coordination, cancer pathways, procurement, privacy, public trust, and supplier reputation all collide.

The case is hard because both easy stories are wrong. It is too thin to say this is merely private software, because the platform can shape operational facts, queues, products, visibility, analytics, training, support, and exit options across public health organisations. It is also too thick to say a supplier becomes the NHS, because NHS England and user organisations remain the legal controllers or joint controllers for personal data, products remain purpose-specific, and the supplier is described as a processor acting on written instructions.

The correct holding is a middle one: the honest form is a governed infrastructure dependency requiring a public review docket. That docket should prove who owns the legal basis, who controls each instance, which products process which categories of data, who can access what, how privacy-enhancing technology is separated, how benefit claims are tested, how local organisations can opt in or pause, and how the NHS could migrate, terminate, or continue service if supplier trust or performance fails.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Trigger and dispatcher table

| Field | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| trigger | operational health data is fragmented across systems, while waiting lists, theatres, discharge, vaccination, supply chain, cancer pathways, and population insight require cross-organisational visibility |
| boundary mismatch | each trust or ICB controls its own instance and data duties, but the platform, products, privacy technology, support, and contract create a national shared infrastructure surface |
| first-form question | whether the right form is local systems only, a national database, an in-house public platform, a Palantir-led SaaS dependency, or a governed multi-supplier platform waist |
| lower-form test | isolated local systems are too thin if they cannot support cross-organisational operational coordination, reusable products, data-flow transparency, or system-wide waiting-list recovery |
| heavier-form test | a national health data government is too thick because local and national controller duties, product purposes, access roles, clinical ownership, and data-protection law remain separate |
| holding | governed supplier-dependent operational data-platform waist under public controller, product, privacy, audit, exit, and benefit-proof records |

### 2. Candidate forms

| Candidate form | Why tempting | Defect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| local data systems only | protects local autonomy and existing trust relationships | can preserve silos, duplicate work, weak reusable products, and poor system-wide operational visibility |
| central national database | makes joining and oversight simple | overcentralizes the fact layer and can flatten local controller duties, product purposes, opt-out / rights boundaries, and clinical context |
| private SaaS as ordinary procurement | treats the platform as a tool purchased under contract | hides vendor-admin, lock-in, data-flow, benefit-claim, support, and exit questions |
| public in-house build only | maximizes public ownership and sovereign capability | may understate delivery risk, transition time, product maturity, and existing supplier dependency |
| AI / operating-system platform | promises a common data-driven health service | risks turning analytics infrastructure into a hidden administrative-law layer |
| governed dependency waist | names the supplier layer as governance-relevant while keeping public controllers and public duties in view | requires hard records, not reassurance language |

### 4. Authority and responsibility map

| Layer | Actor label | Packet rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| public programme owner | NHS England | owns national programme governance, contract management, national instances, publication of programme information, and extension / review evidence |
| local public controllers | NHS trusts and integrated care boards using local instances | remain responsible for deciding products, data placed into their instances, local access controls, local privacy notices, and local DPO routes |
| platform supplier | Palantir-led consortium | processor / service-provider layer, not public controller or independent health-data owner |
| PET contractor | privacy-enhancing technology supplier | separate privacy / de-identification surface; its role must not be collapsed into the main platform supplier |
| support and training layer | Centre of Excellence, service desk, deployment support, supplier support | governance-relevant because training, access, support tickets, and templates can shape practical adoption |
| public and professional trust layer | patients, clinicians, staff, BMA, patient groups, Parliament, civil society | opposition and confidence are evidence, not public-relations noise |
| oversight and rights layer | DPOs, DPIAs, IG framework, audit logs, ICO, Parliament, procurement review, local boards | each route must have a record and cannot be replaced by supplier assurances |

### 7. Form holding

The honest form is **governed supplier-dependent operational data-platform waist**.

That form allows the NHS to use a common operational data platform, reusable products, privacy technology, and supplier support where the evidence shows public benefit. It forbids treating the platform as a private administrative state, a national health-data government, an unreviewable data console, or a magic answer to waiting-list, discharge, workforce, and trust problems.

The holding must be reopened when any of the following occurs: new products use additional categories of confidential patient information; PET begins a materially different de-identification role; local opt-outs or national opt-out boundaries change; external privileged access expands; a product is used for legal or similarly significant automated decisions; a break / extension decision approaches; benefits cannot be independently reconstructed; or exit proof fails.

### 8. Rival interpretations

| Rival rule | Why it is tempting | Why this packet limits it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Palantir runs the NHS | supplier reputation and platform centrality make the story vivid | overstates the legal role if controllers, processors, product purposes, access rules, and local instances remain real |
| this is just software | procurement and processor language make the story comfortable | understates the public-power effect of workflows, dashboards, queues, products, logs, benefit claims, and exit dependence |
| national data lake | the platform joins operational data | misses the instance/product/PET/controller architecture and the claim that patient record systems remain primary |
| local autonomy solves it | each organisation controls its own instance | ignores national contract, shared technology, product templates, adoption pressure, and the programme-wide benefit story |
| security and audit solve it | controls are central and necessary | assurance does not prove public trust, legal basis, benefit claims, exit feasibility, or clinical appropriateness |
| boycott solves it | opposition may be morally serious | refusal alone does not build migration, replacement, data-flow, or continuity proof |

## Failure modes

- **data-console government**: treating the dashboard or product catalogue as the real authority over waiting lists, discharge, theatres, cancer pathways, or resource attention.
- **processor absolution**: assuming that because the supplier is a processor, the supplier layer has no governance significance.
- **NHS-control assertion**: saying the NHS controls the platform without showing export, migration, access, support, model / product, log, and step-in proof.
- **benefit dashboard laundering**: turning uptake and benefit numbers into legitimacy without method, comparator, independent review, and local validation.
- **trust externality**: treating patient, clinician, or staff opposition as politics rather than as a live condition for data quality, adoption, and lawful confidence.
- **PET fog**: citing privacy-enhancing technology without showing when it registers flows, when it de-identifies, what data it processes, and which products depend on it.
- **extension by inertia**: allowing the March 2027 committed-term review or later extensions to occur without current source, performance, trust, exit, and market evidence.
- **local-instance invisibility**: praising or condemning the national platform while hiding which local organisations, products, and data categories are actually live.
- **admin-access creep**: broad privileged roles, supplier support, or emergency access expand faster than the public record can reconstruct.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Can a patient identify which user organisation and product processed their personal data, what rights route applies, and who the DPO is?
2. Can a clinician identify whether a dashboard flag is informational, operational, clinical, or decision-triggering?
3. Can a local trust export an appeal-ready and audit-ready record after supplier exit?
4. Can the NHS show product-level DPIA, legal-basis, data-category, access, PET, and benefit evidence without relying on a generic programme page?
5. Can the public distinguish supplier claims, NHS programme metrics, parliamentary assertions, and independent evaluation?
6. Are privileged supplier and support sessions named, time-boxed, logged, reviewed, and minimised?
7. Does a break or extension decision test market alternatives, migration feasibility, public skills, trust, performance, and exit cost?
8. Are opposition briefs from patients, clinicians, staff, local organisations, and Parliament kept in the decision file rather than treated as noise?
9. Can the platform fail, be paused, or be replaced without losing waiting-list, discharge, cancer, supply-chain, audit, or rights records?
10. Does any future AI use require a new authority, model, data, redress, and source-hierarchy docket rather than inheriting legitimacy from FDP adoption?
