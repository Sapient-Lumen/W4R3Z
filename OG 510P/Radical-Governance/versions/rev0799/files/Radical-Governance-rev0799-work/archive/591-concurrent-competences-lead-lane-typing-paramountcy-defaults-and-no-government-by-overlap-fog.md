# 591 — Concurrent competences, lead-lane typing, paramountcy defaults, and no government by overlap fog

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat **concurrent or shared competences as a typed constitutional settlement rather than as a vague admission that “everyone is involved”**: where more than one level must legitimately act in the same field, the law should split the field into visible lanes — such as rights floors, finance, planning, delivery, inspection, data, and remedy — assign a lead level for each lane, state what happens on conflict, preserve lower-tier headroom where that is the point of the arrangement, and publish a coordination rulebook so citizens are not forced to infer government structure from a pile of overlapping claims.

## Why this matters

The archive now has tighter rules for **allocating powers by scale** (`579`), **handling asymmetry** (`580`), **matching mandates with finance** (`581`), **backstopping weak-capacity jurisdictions** (`582`), **keeping rescue lawful and temporary** (`583`), **making override and preemption explicit** (`584`), **distinguishing delegated tasks from self-government** (`588`), **protecting local initiative** (`589`), and **requiring higher tiers to justify interventions ex ante** (`590`). What it still lacked was one compact doctrine for the stubborn class of fields where more than one level really does need to act at once.

That gap matters because many constitutions and statutes solve difficult allocation problems by declaring a subject “shared”, “concurrent”, “framework”, or “co-ordinated” and then stopping there. Education is shared. Housing is shared. Disaster management is shared. Climate is shared. Skills are shared. Social services are shared. The legal text sounds flexible, but the operating result is often pathological. Everyone claims credit. No one owns the failure. Citizens do not know which office can actually fix the problem. Ministries and subnational governments keep re-litigating the same boundary question. Funding and reporting multiply while discretion shrinks. Coordination bodies proliferate because the competence assignment never really happened.

The result is a recurring pathology: **government by overlap fog**. Responsibility is diluted rather than shared. Cooperation is demanded without a rule for who leads which lane. Conflict is resolved ad hoc by fiscal leverage, political prestige, or emergency improvisation.

A serious constitutional archive should prefer a narrower rule. **Concurrent competence is not an excuse to avoid allocation; it is an instruction to allocate more carefully inside a shared field.**

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish a genuinely shared field from duplicated authority

The archive should reject the lazy habit of calling a field “shared” when the real issue is that the designer did not choose. Some policy areas truly require multiple levels: one level may own rights floors or macro-risk, another may own delivery adaptation, and a third may own network planning or pooled specialist capacity. But that is different from simply allowing multiple actors to issue commands over the same matter without a rule.

A good constitution should therefore ask first: **is this field actually concurrent, or did we fail to assign it?** If the answer is that one level could own it with support from others, the archive should prefer a cleaner assignment. Shared competence should be used for genuinely composite systems, not as a drafting solvent.

### 2. Split the field into lanes rather than allocating the whole subject in one block

A serious shared settlement should not say only that “housing”, “skills”, or “public health” is concurrent. It should type the field into operating lanes such as:

- constitutional rights floors and common legal effect,
- finance and risk-pooling,
- strategic planning,
- territorial implementation and service delivery,
- inspection, monitoring, and data,
- emergency command,
- and complaints, appeal, or judicial remedy.

Different levels may lead different lanes. The point is that concurrency becomes governable once the field is decomposed into functions rather than treated as one undifferentiated blob.

### 3. Assign one lead level for each lane even when several levels participate

Shared fields do not eliminate the need for leadership. They increase it.

The archive should therefore prefer a rule of **one lead level per lane**. In a concurrent field, many actors may contribute, but each lane should still have a named lead for:

- proposing binding rules,
- maintaining the authoritative standard or dataset,
- chairing joint plans,
- convening operational coordination,
- reporting public performance,
- and initiating correction when the settlement fails.

Without lead-lane typing, “joint responsibility” quickly becomes “joint evasion”.

### 4. State the conflict rule explicitly: headroom, paramountcy, or reservation

The archive already has a doctrine of explicit preemption. Shared competences need an even more specific rule. When acts from different levels conflict, the legal order should say whether the shared field uses:

- **common floors with lower-tier headroom**,
- **paramountcy for specified higher-tier instruments**,
- **reservation of narrow subfields**,
- or **mandatory negotiation before displacement**.

What matters is that the conflict rule be visible before the dispute, not improvised after the clash. A concurrency clause without a conflict rule is just delayed constitutional litigation.

### 5. Keep the coercive core less concurrent than the adaptive edge

Some degree of overlap is easier to tolerate in planning, experimentation, co-financing, convening, or place-specific delivery. It is much more dangerous when the field includes arrest powers, detention, taxation, sanctions, civil status, compulsory data extraction, or emergency command.

The archive should therefore prefer **less concurrency at the coercive core** and more concurrency at the adaptive edge. Shared competences may work well where one level sets floors and another adapts delivery. They work badly where several levels can each coerce, punish, or legally bind the same person in different ways without a clear hierarchy.

### 6. Move finance, records, and data lanes with the shared settlement instead of bolting them on later

Concurrent fields often fail because the law names a shared policy space but leaves the real operating machinery elsewhere. Budget lines remain siloed. Records stay fragmented. Data definitions differ. Reporting multiplies. Nobody knows whose numbers count.

The archive should therefore insist that a concurrent settlement specify at the same time:

- who pays for which lane,
- which dataset or registry is authoritative,
- how data moves across levels,
- who carries liability for error,
- and which level must publish public performance information.

Otherwise “shared competence” is just a label pasted over administrative contradiction.

### 7. Create a standing coordination forum only when the overlap is durable and rule-bearing

Not every shared field needs another council. But where concurrency is durable, high-stakes, and recurrent, the archive should prefer a standing rule-bearing forum rather than endless ad hoc summits.

Such a forum should have a narrow charter:

- maintain the concurrency protocol,
- surface disputes before they harden,
- update joint plans or technical annexes,
- publish unresolved conflicts,
- and trigger review when the settlement becomes unworkable.

This should complement, not replace, the ordinary constitutional allocation of powers. The forum exists to keep the shared settlement legible, not to become a shadow legislature.

### 8. Publish a concurrency charter that ordinary operators can actually use

Many shared systems technically have rules, but only constitutional specialists can find them. The archive should prefer a **single public concurrency charter** for each major shared field. It should say, in one usable place:

- which lanes are shared,
- which level leads each lane,
- which legal instruments control on conflict,
- which duties are delegated versus owned,
- which funding streams apply,
- which records or datasets are authoritative,
- and where an affected resident, worker, or firm should complain or appeal.

A shared field that can be understood only by stitching together five statutes, three circulars, and an unpublished memorandum is not honestly governed.

### 9. Keep the citizen-facing front door singular even when the state behind it is plural

Concurrency should not force the public to perform the constitutional analysis. In a well-designed shared field, the internal settlement may be complex while the external route stays simple.

The archive should therefore prefer:

- one lead intake surface,
- clear referral obligations behind the scenes,
- no wrong door for ordinary complaints or applications,
- and a visible remedy map that says who must answer when levels disagree.

The constitution may tolerate internal overlap more than the citizen can. Public legibility is part of the settlement, not an afterthought.

### 10. Put shared settlements on review clocks and simplify them when they drift

Concurrency is often adopted because no actor can yet prove a cleaner assignment. That can be reasonable, but it should not become immortal by habit.

The archive should therefore require review clocks for major shared settlements. The review should ask:

- whether the field still needs concurrency,
- whether lanes should be reassigned,
- whether one level has become the de facto owner anyway,
- whether coordination costs now exceed the benefits,
- and whether citizens can actually navigate the arrangement.

Some concurrent fields will prove durable. Others should collapse into clearer assignment once the polity learns more.

## Guardrails

- Do not call a field concurrent when the real problem is political reluctance to assign it.
- Do not leave conflict rules implicit.
- Do not let several levels coerce, sanction, or command in the same lane without hierarchy.
- Do not create a standing coordination body unless the overlap is durable and specific enough to justify it.
- Do not expect citizens to discover the lead authority by procedural trial and error.
- Do not treat data, finance, and records as downstream technicalities once concurrency is declared.

## Failure modes

- **government by overlap fog**: multiple levels can all speak, spend, or blame in the field, but no one clearly leads.
- **shared without split**: a whole subject is labelled concurrent without decomposition into rule, finance, delivery, data, and remedy lanes.
- **paramountcy by surprise**: higher-tier supremacy appears only after conflict, because the legal order never declared the rule in advance.
- **coordination inflation**: each overlap produces another committee instead of a cleaner lane assignment.
- **citizen-routing burden**: the resident must discover alone whether the municipality, region, ministry, agency, or joint board actually owns the issue.
- **data pluralism without truth**: several levels report performance, but none maintains the authoritative account.
- **permanent provisionality**: a temporary shared settlement survives for years because nobody built in review or simplification.

## Practical tests

A polity is using concurrent competences honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Has the field been decomposed into visible lanes rather than labelled “shared” in one block?
2. Is there one lead level named for each lane?
3. Does the legal order state the conflict rule in advance?
4. Are coercive powers less concurrent than adaptive delivery or planning powers?
5. Are finance, data, records, and liability rules specified as part of the settlement?
6. Is there a usable public concurrency charter or equivalent operational guide?
7. Can an ordinary resident enter through one front door without having to solve the jurisdiction puzzle alone?
8. Is there a standing coordination and dispute route only where the overlap is durable enough to need one?
9. Does the settlement carry a review clock or simplification trigger?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity says a field is **shared** but cannot show **which lane each level leads, what rule controls on conflict, where the public goes first, and who must repair failure when coordination breaks**, then the polity has not designed concurrency. It has designed **overlap fog with downstream blame**.
