# 807 — Assessment-team independence and conflict discipline for scope reviews: recusal rosters, secondment firebreaks, host-picked experts, donor pathways, and no constitutional judgment by interested mission

## One-line thesis

The archive should pair its assessment-mission, evidence-register, contestability, and close-out notes with one compact **mission-integrity grammar**: **every serious scope review should declare who is judging, who is only informing, what interests and funding pathways each reviewer carries, what firebreaks apply to secondees and host-nominated specialists, when recusal is mandatory, and how outsiders can challenge the composition of the team before findings harden into the live constitution, so the archive does not mistake expertise, proximity, or funding convenience for independence**.

## Why this matters

The archive can already say **what the ideal scope stack is** (`790`), **how to benchmark it** (`791`), **how to stress it** (`792`), **how to maintain it** (`793`), **how to move toward it** (`794`), **what proof must stay public** (`795`), **how persistent failure can justify intervention** (`796`), **who may challenge those judgments** (`797`), **which peer class counts before scoring begins** (`798`), **what must happen to temporary constitutional objects** (`799`), **how a serious mission should run** (`800`), **how its findings should be stated** (`801`), **how those findings become owned repair work** (`802`), **how claimed repair is later verified** (`803`), **how one real path through the stack can be walked** (`804`), **how the sample entering the review should be chosen honestly** (`805`), and **how the underlying evidence objects stay typed, versioned, and reusable** (`806`).

What the archive still lacked was one compact rule for a prior question:

**who is allowed to sit inside the judging chain at all, and when does a relationship to the reviewed body make that impossible or at least visible enough to discipline?**

That gap matters because review systems often fail before evidence is weighed. A host ministry nominates “technical advisers” who quietly become scorers. A donor-funded consultant who designed last year’s reform now grades its implementation. A secondee from the reviewed tier participates as if detached. A specialist with crucial expertise has an obvious financial or reputational pathway into the outcome, and the mission treats disclosure as a substitute for structural limits. Official integrity frameworks point toward the missing discipline. GAO’s auditing standards treat independence as a threshold condition and warn against using specialists whose independence is impaired. WHO’s expert-interest rules insist that expert contributions must be actually and ostensibly objective and independent. OECD conflict-of-interest guidance stresses disclosure, consistency, and openness in how conflicts are resolved, especially for functions subject to close scrutiny. Council of Europe conduct materials likewise treat public duty as incompatible with unmanaged private or sectoral interest. The archive should compress that lesson into one note: **serious scope review needs mission firebreaks, not just smart people and a declaration form**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate expertise from judgment power

The archive should not treat all participants in a mission as if they occupy the same constitutional role.

A serious review team may include:

- **judging members**, who help shape findings and scores,
- **specialists**, who explain a technical domain,
- **witnesses or host explainers**, who clarify local structures,
- **observers**, who watch method or fairness,
- and **secretariat or analytic support**, who organize, draft, or maintain the record.

The first rule is to say which role each person actually has. Expertise is valuable. Judgment power is different.

### 2. Screen interests before mission design is finalized

Conflict discipline should begin before itineraries, evidence requests, or assignment of review lanes are locked.

The archive should prefer a compact pre-mission screen covering at least:

- current or recent employment by the reviewed body,
- advisory, contractual, or grant relationships touching the reviewed field,
- prior authorship of the policy or reform now under review,
- close personal relationships with senior decision-makers,
- active litigation, political-office, or campaign ties,
- and direct financial, reputational, or organizational benefit from the mission’s likely result.

The point is not moral theater. It is to decide early whether a person may judge, may advise only, or must be excluded.

### 3. Use a role-and-interest roster, not hidden bilateral vetting

Every serious mission should keep one compact internal roster stating, for each participant:

- role,
- appointing path,
- funding path,
- declared interests,
- restrictions or recusals,
- and whether the participant may join scoring, drafting, contradiction resolution, or close-out review.

`795` may later publish a public-safe version of this roster without revealing protected details.

### 4. Treat host-nominated experts as information channels unless independence is affirmatively cleared

The reviewed body will often know which local specialist understands the system best.

That does not make the specialist a neutral judge.

The archive should prefer a default in which host-nominated experts are treated as:

- evidence explainers,
- local context witnesses,
- or technical briefers,

unless a conflict screen shows they can sit in the judging chain without material impairment.

### 5. Do not let donor funding disappear into the background

A review system that depends on external support often develops quiet loyalties.

The archive should therefore ask, for each mission:

- who paid for the mission,
- who pays each external expert,
- whether that donor funded the underlying reform or institution being reviewed,
- and whether that funding path gives the appearance or reality of interest in a favorable verdict.

Funding does not automatically disqualify a mission member. Hidden funding dependence should.

### 6. Build explicit secondment firebreaks

Secondments and embedded staff often bring indispensable operational knowledge.

They also bring institutional memory, loyalties, and future career pathways.

The archive should prefer a default in which secondees or recently embedded staff:

- do not lead scoring on the host body from which they came,
- do not resolve contradiction disputes in that lane,
- and do not sign off on closure of repairs they previously owned or administered.

They may still serve as technical explainers, drafters under supervision, or evidence facilitators where that is disclosed and bounded.

### 7. Distinguish disclosure from cure

Disclosure is necessary. It is not enough.

The archive should explicitly distinguish:

- **disclosable but manageable interests**, where role limits can contain the risk,
- **restrictive interests**, where the person may participate only in non-judging roles,
- and **disqualifying interests**, where recusal or replacement is the honest answer.

Otherwise disclosure becomes a ritual that launders an interested mission back into legitimacy.

### 8. Apply the same conflict logic to specialists and method staff

It is easy to police the lead reviewers and forget the specialist who shaped the draft, the data analyst who chose exclusions, or the consultant who designed the score anchors.

The archive should not let support roles escape scrutiny merely because they are “technical.” Influence over frames, evidence selection, contradiction treatment, and wording is already influence over judgment.

### 9. Give parties a short route to contest team composition before findings land

`797` provides the broader contestability ladder.

This note adds an earlier use case: before the mission is finalized, reviewed actors and materially affected outsiders should have some short route to say:

- this reviewer has an undeclared tie,
- this expert designed the system under review,
- this donor pathway is too entangled,
- or this secondment makes the team composition unreliable.

The challenge need not block the whole mission. It should force a recorded decision.

### 10. Keep replacement capacity ready instead of treating recusal as mission failure

A mature review system should assume that some recusals will be necessary.

The archive should therefore prefer:

- pre-cleared reserve experts,
- lane-level substitution plans,
- and documented rules for how the team continues if a participant is restricted mid-mission.

This prevents the common excuse that a conflicted reviewer “had to stay” because nobody else could cover the lane.

### 11. Carry conflict discipline through drafting, appeal, and close-out

A mission member who was restricted during fieldwork should not quietly re-enter at the drafting or closure stage.

The roster should therefore say whether each participant may:

- shape the draft findings,
- answer contradiction disputes,
- join contest review,
- assess corrective-action sufficiency,
- or verify closure and reopen decisions.

This is how the archive prevents recusal theater.

### 12. Publish a public-safe mission-integrity note

`795` should often be able to publish a short outward integrity note stating at least:

- who served as judging members,
- whether any participants were restricted or recused,
- whether host-nominated or donor-funded experts were used,
- and whether any composition objections were received and resolved.

The archive does not need to dump every disclosure form into public view. It does need more than “independent experts reviewed the system.”

### 13. Keep the firebreak discipline tight enough to live inside this archive

The archive should prefer:

- short role-and-interest entries,
- categorical restrictions instead of long ethics memos,
- citations to governing standards rather than retaining full forms,
- and public-safe summaries rather than large confidential appendices.

This note is therefore not a license to accumulate personnel dossiers. It is a rule for keeping the review chain honest without bloating the archive.

## Minimum fields for a mission-integrity roster

| Field | What it tells later actors | Minimum form | Typical failure if missing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Participant ID / name** | who occupied the role | short identifier | invisible influence in the judging chain |
| **Role** | judge, specialist, observer, host explainer, secretariat | one label | expertise and judgment power blur together |
| **Appointing / funding path** | who placed and paid the person | one short line | donor or host dependence hides backstage |
| **Declared interests** | what could impair independence | one short line or none declared | disclosure becomes folklore |
| **Restriction status** | full role / limited role / recused | one short label | restrictions vanish after the intake stage |
| **Lane permissions** | where the person may score, draft, or close | short lane list | recused members reappear in other stages |
| **Challenge / resolution note** | whether objections were raised and decided | optional short note | composition disputes disappear off-record |

## Use rule for the archive

When later notes need to say not just **how a scope mission was run** but **why the people running it counted as honest judges**, the archive should usually do these things in order:

1. **use `800` to declare the mission, lanes, and participation architecture**,
2. **use this note to classify mission members by role, disclose interests, apply restrictions, and record any composition challenges**,
3. **use `805` where the conflict question touches who selected the cases, sites, or stakeholders entering the frame**,
4. **use `806` to keep any disclosure forms, restriction memos, or objection notices tied to stable exhibit IDs rather than private inboxes**,
5. **use `801` so findings reflect only the votes, views, or drafting authority of non-impaired reviewers**,
6. **use `797` if a party contests the composition decision or alleges a hidden impairment**,
7. **use `802` and `803` so restricted reviewers do not quietly return as validators of their own repair pathway or closure claim**,
8. **use `795` to publish the compact public-safe integrity note rather than relying on prestige language about experts**.

The archive should not let constitutional judgments ride on interested missions merely because the reviewers are accomplished.

## Guardrails

- Do not let the reviewed body choose its own scorers without an explicit independence decision.
- Do not treat disclosure as a cure where restriction or replacement is needed.
- Do not let secondees, recent advisers, or reform authors sign off on the lanes they recently owned.
- Do not ignore funding pathways simply because the sponsor is respectable.
- Do not let support staff, specialists, or drafters escape conflict screening because they are “technical.”
- Do not hide composition objections off-record or resolve them only informally.

## Failure modes

- **prestige laundering** — famous experts are assumed independent because they are famous.
- **secondment amnesia** — embedded staff are treated as detached reviewers the moment they change badges.
- **disclosure theater** — interests are declared but nothing operational changes.
- **host-picked grading** — the reviewed body quietly shapes the team that will evaluate it.
- **donor halo** — funding dependence is mistaken for benevolence rather than influence.
- **recusal relapse** — a restricted reviewer disappears during intake and reappears during drafting or closure.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Can a reader tell who had judgment power and who only supplied information?
2. Are appointing and funding paths visible enough to show where influence may enter?
3. Would a reasonable outsider know when a declared interest led to restriction, replacement, or recusal rather than only to disclosure?
4. Could a host-nominated or donor-funded expert materially shape the verdict without an explicit integrity decision?
5. Are secondees and recently embedded staff barred from grading or closing the lanes they recently occupied?
6. Is there a recorded route to challenge mission composition before findings harden?
7. Does the public proof pack say more than “independent experts reviewed the system”?
