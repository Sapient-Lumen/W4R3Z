# 646 — Scope severability, fallback baselines, partial invalidity, and no whole-stack collapse by contamination

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should treat a materially stack-altering package as **severable where honest**, preserving only those parts that can still be described as lawful, coherent, and democratically ownable on their own terms, with one visible fallback baseline and one public explanation of what survives, what is stayed, and what must be rebuilt, so a single defect neither launders the whole package nor needlessly detonates the lawful remainder.

## Why this matters

The archive now says much more clearly **how stack-changing proposals should be disclosed and classified** (`635`-`638`), **how they should be checked before launch and during live operation** (`639`-`641`), **how they should leave or return to the stack** (`642`-`643`), **how defects should be typed** (`644`), and **how serious challenges should pause or narrow live operation while review happens** (`645`).

What it still lacked was a compact doctrine for the common case where a stack-changing package is **partly sound and partly unsound**. That gap matters because multilevel reforms often travel as bundles: a law, finance path, transition plan, systems release, transfer pack, supervisory rule, and public explanation all move together. When review reveals a defect, institutions often jump to one of two bad extremes.

One extreme is **contamination logic**: one unlawful clause, one broken consultation lane, or one invalid transfer step is treated as proof that everything surrounding it must fall, even where a narrower remedy could preserve the lawful baseline without inventing new title. The other extreme is **salvage theater**: reviewers save most of the package by rhetorical optimism, even though the surviving remainder no longer matches any honestly approved arrangement and is really a new settlement that never traveled through the proper lane.

An ideal polity should reject both mistakes. It should prefer a compact doctrine of **scope severability**: preserve what can truly stand on its own; fall back to a known lawful baseline where that is the honest answer; and force fresh clearance, repair, or retirement where the package no longer forms an intelligible constitutional whole.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from constitutional honesty, not from a bias either toward collapse or preservation

When a materially stack-altering package is challenged or found defective, the first question should not be "how much can we save?" or "how much must we destroy?" It should be **what arrangement can still be described honestly after the defect is acknowledged**.

That means asking whether the remaining parts still identify a lawful holder of the competence, a coherent territorial footprint, a finance path, a supervision and review lane, and an intelligible public explanation. If the answer is no, then the package is not severable in the constitutional sense even if some technical fragments could keep running.

### 2. Keep one named fallback baseline for every materially stack-altering package

A package that can change the stack should never exist without one visible **fallback baseline** saying what arrangement governs if the package fails in full or in part.

That baseline may be:

- the prior lawful allocation;
- a narrower incumbent arrangement that remains valid without the challenged expansion;
- a temporary continuity bridge already named in law;
- or a staged handback path already linked to `631`, `642`, or `645`.

Without one named fallback baseline, severability becomes improvisation. Reviewers and operators will then "save" whichever pieces seem easiest to keep running, even if those pieces do not add up to any lawful constitutional design.

### 3. Test each major component against stand-alone intelligibility

The archive should prefer severability tests that ask whether each major component can stand **without borrowing hidden validity** from the invalid remainder.

At minimum, reviewers should test whether the surviving component still has:

- a lawful source of authority;
- a clear holder of decision rights;
- a territory or constituency that still makes sense;
- finance and staffing that still match the retained task;
- reachable complaint, reconsideration, appeal, or judicial routes;
- and a public explanation that does not depend on pretending the invalid part still exists.

A clause, budget line, or software module is not constitutionally severable merely because it is textually detachable. It must still make honest governmental sense when standing alone.

### 4. Distinguish partial invalidity from forced constitutional redesign

Sometimes a package can survive after one feature is removed. Sometimes removal changes the package so deeply that the result is a different settlement.

The archive should therefore distinguish:

- **true severability** — the surviving arrangement is still the same basic constitutional design, only narrowed;
- **fallback reversion** — the package cannot survive, but the polity can return to a known lawful baseline;
- **forced redesign** — the surviving pieces no longer form an intelligible arrangement and must go back through packet, opinion, override, and register lanes as a new proposal.

This stops reviewers from inventing a third constitution under the label of "partial remedy."

### 5. Preserve rights and review routes before preserving managerial convenience

Where severability is contested, the polity should first preserve the pieces that keep the stack lawful and challengeable: review routes, notice duties, territorial clarity, non-prejudice continuity language, and the ability to reconstruct what happened.

By contrast, convenience fragments such as a central dashboard, a host-agency shortcut, a preferred reporting line, or a preloaded software default should not receive preservation bias merely because they are already in motion.

The archive should prefer **rights-bearing severability** over **managerial severability**.

### 6. Keep partial survival visible in the same public stack memory as every other scope event

If only part of a stack-changing package survives review, that should update the same public objects the archive already uses:

- the **canonical scope register** (`633`);
- the **scope-jurisprudence digest** (`634`);
- the relevant **packet, opinion, override, and conformance chain** (`635`-`639`);
- any linked **observability, settlement, retirement, reactivation, nullity, or standstill** objects (`640`-`645`).

At minimum the register should be able to say that the package is:

- **fully operative**;
- **partially operative after severance**;
- **reverted to fallback baseline**;
- **running on a continuity bridge without prejudice**;
- or **returned for redesign**.

Otherwise the public record will describe a neat reform while the live stack is actually a patched compromise assembled after litigation or review.

### 7. Do not let temporary bridging fragments harden into the severed settlement by habit

A common failure mode is that temporary continuity pieces survive the invalid part, remain in place for convenience, and are later narrated as the new normal. That is just runtime custom wearing severability language.

Any component preserved only for continuity should therefore carry:

- a clear temporary label;
- a short clock;
- non-prejudice language;
- a named owner for restoration, handback, or redesign;
- and an explicit next event: repair, re-clearance, retirement, or replacement.

Severability is about preserving lawful remainder, not about giving continuity fragments a quiet route to title.

### 8. Require one public severability statement with reasons

Where a materially stack-bearing package is only partly upheld, one short public **severability statement** should say:

- what failed;
- what survives;
- why the surviving arrangement is intelligible on its own;
- what lawful fallback baseline applies where nothing survives;
- what temporary continuity steps remain in place;
- and which future packet or handback lane is now required.

This statement should be linked to the scope register rather than buried in case-specific prose. Without it, operators, affected authorities, and the public will infer different constitutions from the same decision.

### 9. Use the narrowest remedy that preserves lawful self-government and legal certainty

The archive should prefer remedies that are narrow enough to avoid unnecessary constitutional destruction, but not so narrow that the remaining arrangement becomes misleading or incoherent.

That means severing only where the result still protects:

- explicit responsibility assignment;
- proportionate supervision;
- real consultation and consent requirements where applicable;
- intelligible review and recourse;
- and legal certainty about who governs what now.

This is where the doctrine docks into the Council of Europe's proportionality logic and the OECD's insistence on explicit, reviewable, fit-for-purpose responsibility assignment. Narrowness is not the goal by itself; **honest, reviewable residual order** is the goal.

### 10. If severability analysis becomes too creative, reopen the constitutional lane instead

The archive should mistrust heroic severability. If keeping the package alive requires too many judicial, administrative, or operational inventions, the polity should stop calling the remainder severed and start calling it what it is: a new constitutional design requiring a fresh packet, new opinion, and maybe override or consent.

That is especially true where the invalid part supplied the democratic mandate, the finance spine, the territorial map, the review route, or the transition mechanism that made the whole package workable in the first place.

## Guardrails

- Do not let one invalid element contaminate a whole package if a narrower, publicly intelligible remainder can stand lawfully on its own.
- Do not preserve fragments whose legality depends on pretending the invalid part still exists in the background.
- Do not let continuity bridges, host arrangements, or vendor workarounds become the severed settlement by habit.
- Do not keep partial survival off-book; the live scope register should show that the package was narrowed, reverted, or returned for redesign.
- Do not confuse textual separability with constitutional severability.

## Failure modes

- **collapse by contamination**: one defect is used to destroy lawful remainder that could have stood without harming self-government or reviewability.
- **salvage theater**: almost everything is "saved," but only by inventing a remainder that never traveled through the proper democratic or legal lane.
- **fallback amnesia**: nobody can say what arrangement governs after partial failure because no baseline was named in advance.
- **continuity capture**: temporary bridge arrangements quietly become the post-litigation constitution.
- **register fiction**: the public stack memory still shows the original package even though a severed and narrower arrangement is what actually lives.

## Anti-theater tests

1. When a stack-changing package is found partly defective, can the polity name one honest residual arrangement rather than choosing between panic collapse and optimistic salvage?
2. Was there a visible fallback baseline before review, or is partial survival being improvised after the fact?
3. Does each surviving component still have lawful authority, territory, finance, supervision, and review routes without borrowing hidden validity from the invalid part?
4. Is the surviving arrangement truly the same constitutional design, only narrowed, or is it really a redesign that should go back through the clearance chain?
5. Are rights-bearing and review-preserving elements protected before convenience fragments?
6. Are temporary continuity pieces explicitly temporary, clocked, and without prejudice rather than drifting into new title?
7. Has the partial survival or fallback reversion updated the canonical scope register and linked memory surfaces?
8. Can a future reader tell exactly what failed, what survived, why it survived, and what next legal lane is required?
9. Is the remedy narrow enough to avoid needless destruction but not so narrow that it produces a misleading or incoherent remainder?
10. If severability reasoning starts to require creative constitutional engineering, does the polity stop and reopen the constitutional lane instead?

## Closing image

A constitutional stack should not respond to defect with either indiscriminate collapse or decorative salvage. It should be able to say: **this part failed**, **this remainder still stands on its own**, **this is the lawful fallback where it does not**, and **this is the public record that keeps the difference visible**. Anything weaker invites either **whole-stack collapse by contamination** or **new constitutional design by salvage theater**.
