# 660 — Canonical government matrix by scope: legislature, executive, civil service, fiscal base, review, participation, and no full stack rewrite for every tier

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should reuse one **canonical scope matrix** rather than reinventing every tier from scratch: micro-local units for witness and stewardship, municipalities for ordinary place government, metropolitan authorities for functional urban systems, regions for major territorial platforms and shared rule, nations for equal citizenship and redistribution, continental bodies for bounded cross-border compacts, and global institutions for treaty-bound peace, commons, alerting, and interoperability — each with a scope-matched decision forum, executive spine, civil-service density, fiscal base, review lane, and participation channel.

## Why this matters

The archive already contains a serious run on **ideal governments by scope** (`541`, `565`, `628`, `629`, `630`, `631`). That is useful substance, but it also risks a familiar archive problem: the same constitutional idea gets rewritten in slightly different forms every time a new sector or territorial question appears.

What the archive still needed was one tighter object:

**a reusable matrix that says what the ideal government at each scope is, in its minimum complete form, without restating the entire theory every time.**

That matters for two reasons.

First, ideal-government claims become sloppy when they name only a scale and a slogan. "Local control", "regional autonomy", "federal balance", or "global coordination" do not yet say who decides, who administers, who pays, who reviews, and how ordinary people can contest or shape the result.

Second, archive growth becomes noisy when every later note has to restate the same institutional kit. A compact canonical matrix lets future notes say: **this function belongs at the municipal tier** or **this system requires a metropolitan tier** while pointing back to one settled description of what that tier should look like when it is honest.

The aim here is therefore not to replace the earlier notes. It is to compress them into one reusable constitutional instrument.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Judge every scope through the same six design questions

For any level of government, ask six questions:

1. what is the public decision forum,
2. what executive or administrative spine carries the work,
3. how much civil-service density the level honestly needs,
4. what fiscal base funds the mandate,
5. what review and remedy routes correct abuse or drift,
6. and what participation channel lets the governed reach it directly.

An ideal government by scope is the answer that fits those six questions **for that scale's real vocation**, not a decorative copy of another tier.

### 2. Use micro-local institutions for witness, stewardship, and escalation, not miniature sovereignty

The smallest scale should usually be a neighborhood, village, ward, building, or block institution with three core jobs: notice breakdown early, steward tiny commons, and force escalation when higher levels are absent or unresponsive.

Its decision forum should be direct and reachable. Its administration should be light. Its money should be small and visible. Its review lane should mostly run upward. Its participation channel should be open by default. The archive should not ask this layer to carry heavy coercion, complex debt, or full-spectrum service states.

### 3. Treat the municipality as the first complete government of ordinary life

The municipality is where the public first deserves a **full civic front door**: elected representation, a visible executive, professional administration, a real operating budget, local rule-making power where lawful, and reachable complaint or judicial routes.

This is the level for everyday place government: streets, sanitation, permitting, parks, libraries, ordinary land-use administration, local records, and the daily encounter between resident and state. The municipality should therefore be democratic in politics and professional in administration.

### 4. Give metropolitan government direct legitimacy whenever the functional city is real

Where transport, housing pressure, freight, air quality, flood risk, trunk utilities, labour markets, and emergency spillovers already operate at metropolitan scale, the ideal government is not merely a club of mayors or a grant cartel.

It is a publicly legible metropolitan authority with a decision forum proportionate to its power, a specialist executive core, a stable shared fiscal base, and review routes that fit planning, infrastructure, and regional service tradeoffs. Metropolitan government should remain narrow where co-ordination is narrow, but once it makes recurrent distributive bargains for the whole urban system it needs democratic weight equal to that fact.

### 5. Use regions for territorial platforms, major services, and shared rule

The regional or provincial level should exist when a large territorial platform is real: hospital systems, watersheds, grids, corridors, higher education, ecological landscapes, disaster logistics, or constitutionally meaningful self-government inside a federation or plurinational state.

Its ideal form is not map nostalgia. It needs a legislature or comparably real representative forum, an executive with departmental depth, a substantial fiscal settlement, review and audit institutions, and a clear relation to both municipal and national tiers. Regions without real platform work or shared-rule logic should not be constitutionalized just for symmetry.

### 6. Reserve the nation for equal citizenship, solidarity, macro-capacity, and constitutional arbitration

The ideal national government is the guarantor tier. It should secure equal citizenship, rights floors, redistribution, social insurance, macroeconomic stabilisation, national infrastructure backbones, high courts or equivalent constitutional review, independent electoral administration, external audit, integrity bodies, and the legal settlement of conflicts among lower tiers.

That does not make the nation the proper manager of every ordinary service. The archive should keep repeating a simpler distinction: **the nation is indispensable as guarantor, not omnipotent as daily operator**.

### 7. Keep continental bodies bounded, competence-typed, and reviewable

A continental or plurilateral tier is justified when the problem is recurrently cross-border but not truly planetary: movement regimes, basin and corridor governance, common markets, grid communities, research and industrial compacts, or federal-style shared rule across several states.

Its honest form is compact and typed. It should have a named decision forum, a bounded secretariat or agency architecture, a treaty-based fiscal model, and clear compliance, amendment, dispute, and withdrawal rules. It should not become the default dumping ground for unresolved domestic disagreement.

### 8. Keep the global layer thin, real, and treaty-bound

The ideal global layer remains narrow-waisted: peace and conflict restraint, planetary commons, public-health alerting, selected technical and financial standards, interoperable reporting, and comparable functions that plainly outrun any one state.

Its institutions should therefore be assemblies, courts, secretariats, registries, scientific panels, and specialised agencies with explicit mandates. The archive should reject both fantasies at once: that the globe can be governed like a city, and that nothing global should ever be institutionally real.

## Guardrails

- Do not ask any scope to carry a broader kit than it can honestly finance, staff, or review.
- Do not give a tier binding power without a decision forum proportionate to that power.
- Do not let metropolitan or regional government stay constitutionally weak once its real functional load is strong.
- Do not let the national tier absorb every ordinary function merely because it is the guarantor tier.
- Do not let continental and global layers widen by sentiment, crisis drift, or prestige alone.
- Do not describe an institution as a government of a given scope unless its forum, executive spine, fiscal base, review lane, and participation channel are all visible enough to name.

## Failure modes

- **tier by slogan**: the polity names localism, federalism, or global co-operation without specifying the minimum governing kit.
- **forumless power**: binding metropolitan or regional choices are made without a public decision forum equal to the power exercised.
- **borrowed administration**: a tier is constitutionally real but operationally dependent on another government's staff, records, and systems for too long.
- **fiscal cosplay**: a level is called autonomous while surviving on discretionary bargains it cannot predict or contest.
- **guarantor sprawl**: the national state uses rights or solidarity language to occupy ordinary place functions by default.
- **cross-border creep**: continental or global bodies accumulate domestic reach because lower tiers are messy, not because the problem is irreducibly supranational.
- **archive restatement bloat**: every new scope note rewrites the same constitutional grammar instead of pointing back to a stable matrix.

## Compression rule for the archive

When a future note asks **what the ideal government at this scope should look like**, default to this matrix and only add what is genuinely domain-specific. If a later note cannot say how it departs from, narrows, or specifies this canonical matrix, it is probably repeating settled archive doctrine rather than adding new constitutional information.
