# 684 — Shared-regime secretariat workforce constitutions: appointments, merit, geographic balance, secondments, conduct, separation, and no regime by patronage

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed secretariat workforce constitution** that separates political head selection from career staffing, makes merit and integrity the paramount recruitment rule, gives geographic balance a visible but bounded place, constrains secondments and related-party hiring, and types conduct, reassignment, discipline, and separation lanes — so the regime is not governed by patronage, donor-owned posts, or borrowed-capacity fog.

## Why this matters

The archive now says a great deal about secretariat powers (`673`), integrity controls (`678`), representation (`671`), and records (`674`). It still lacked one compact note on the **workforce constitution** of the regime itself.

That omission matters because a shared regime is often only as real as the staff architecture that keeps it alive between meetings.

A governing body may meet lawfully and publish careful decisions, but the institution can still drift if nobody can tell who appoints staff, what appointment categories exist, whether recruitment is merit-based, how geographic representation is weighed, whether seconded personnel are ordinary staff or temporary imports, how conflicts are handled, and what happens when performance fails or misconduct is found.

Without a typed workforce constitution, six familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **regime by patronage**: appointments follow political sponsorship, family connection, or donor leverage more than competence and integrity.

The second is **borrowed-capacity government**: seconded or externally supported personnel become the real administrative mind of the regime without a clear loyalty or exit discipline.

The third is **geographic tokenism**: representation language is used either to excuse weak recruitment or, in the opposite direction, ignored so completely that the secretariat loses legitimacy as an international service.

The fourth is **conduct vagueness**: conflicts, recusals, confidentiality, outside interests, and reporting duties exist only as culture or rumor.

The fifth is **performance limbo**: the regime cannot distinguish expiry of appointment, abolition of post, unsatisfactory performance, unsuitability for international service, and misconduct.

The sixth is **head/staff confusion**: a political or member-appointed executive head is treated as if the whole secretariat were equally political, or ordinary staff are treated as if they shared the head’s appointment logic.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **shared-regime workforce discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Publish one workforce constitution

A serious regime should say in one place:

- who appoints staff,
- who adopts or amends staff regulations,
- who selects or appoints the executive head,
- which posts are political, fixed-term, continuing, temporary, or local,
- and what review or appeal lanes apply.

If the staffing order must be reconstructed from personnel manuals, donor agreements, and custom, the regime is already partly governed by shadow HR law.

### 2. Make efficiency, competence, and integrity the paramount recruitment rule

A shared regime should say plainly that the leading considerations in staff selection are merit, competence, and integrity.

That rule is not anti-political because it is political in the right place: members decide the regime’s mandate, budget, and leadership architecture; the routine staffing constitution should then protect professional execution against factional capture.

### 3. Give geographic balance a real but bounded role

For international or member-wide regimes, broad geographic representation can be a legitimate secondary rule, especially for professional posts and senior staffing. It should not become a euphemism for quota-only hiring, nor should it disappear so fully that the secretariat reads as the property of a few dominant members.

The archive should therefore prefer a rule that states openly:

- where geographic balance matters,
- where local recruitment or specialist scarcity narrows it,
- and how it is weighed relative to merit and integrity.

### 4. Separate career staffing from political-head selection

Many regimes lawfully give members or a governing body a direct role in choosing the executive head. That does not mean every senior appointment should be partisan or intergovernmental bargaining by another name.

The archive should therefore prefer a clear separation between:

- the appointment lane for the director-general, secretary-general, registrar, or equivalent head,
- and the merit-governed staffing lane for the wider secretariat.

Without that separation, the whole administration tends to inherit the political temperature of the head’s selection contest.

### 5. Type appointment categories, probation, and conversion rules

Not every post needs the same employment form.

A serious regime should distinguish temporary, fixed-term, continuing, consultant, intern, seconded, and locally recruited appointments, and should say:

- what each category is for,
- what probation or trial periods apply,
- whether and how conversion is possible,
- and what rights and obligations attach to each status.

This prevents a permanent institution from being run through endless temporary workaround.

### 6. Bound secondments and other borrowed-capacity lanes tightly

Seconded personnel can be useful. They are also a classic route for influence and ambiguity.

The regime should therefore prefer secondments only with:

- written tripartite terms,
- duration limits,
- conflict and confidentiality controls,
- clarity about reporting lines,
- and explicit decisions about renewal or termination.

Borrowed capacity is a tool, not the natural resting state of an international secretariat.

### 7. Prohibit related-party hiring and line-of-authority conflicts

A serious workforce constitution should block family or comparable close-relationship appointments where another equally qualified person is available and should forbid superior/subordinate or selection-authority relationships that create obvious conflict.

This is a minimum anti-patronage rule, not an optional ethics flourish.

### 8. Make conduct, disclosure, recusal, and reporting duties explicit

The regime should publish a staff conduct lane covering:

- declaration of interests,
- recusal,
- outside activities,
- confidentiality,
- reporting of breaches,
- cooperation with audits and investigations,
- and protection against retaliation for good-faith reporting.

A regime whose conduct order exists only in speeches has not actually constitutionalized integrity.

### 9. Distinguish reassignment, promotion, poor performance, and misconduct

Staff movement and separation should travel through typed lanes.

The archive should therefore prefer separate rules for:

- reassignment in the interest of the organization,
- competitive promotion,
- abolition of post or expiry of appointment,
- unsatisfactory performance or unsuitability,
- and misconduct or serious misconduct.

When these states collapse into one vague managerial discretion, the regime acquires arbitrary power and loses professionalism at the same time.

### 10. Keep one public staffing-and-integrity spine

A serious regime should publish, at minimum:

- the staff regulations and rules,
- the executive head selection method,
- the main appointment categories,
- standards-of-conduct materials,
- and any key integrity or disclosure guidance.

The workforce constitution should be inspectable by outsiders, not preserved only in onboarding packets or insider memory.

## Working matrix

| Workforce object | Honest rule | Why | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| executive head | separate political/member selection lane from ordinary staffing | keeps leadership politics from colonizing all appointments | treating the whole secretariat as spoils |
| ordinary staff recruitment | merit, competence, and integrity first | preserves professional execution | patronage hiring |
| geographic balance | apply visibly but as a bounded rule | protects international legitimacy without displacing merit | tokenism or total neglect |
| appointment categories | define temporary, fixed-term, continuing, local, and seconded statuses separately | prevents workaround institutions | permanent government by temporary contract |
| secondments | use written, time-bounded, conflict-screened terms | keeps borrowed capacity honest | donor-owned or member-owned posts |
| conduct and discipline | publish disclosure, recusal, reporting, reassignment, and separation lanes | makes integrity and fairness real | conduct by rumor or arbitrary punishment |

## Guardrails

- Do not let head-of-secretariat politics become the staffing constitution for the whole institution.
- Do not treat geographic balance as either a magic substitute for merit or a slogan to be ignored.
- Do not let seconded or externally funded staff become the regime’s hidden permanent core.
- Do not rely on unwritten ethics where conflicts, recusal, and reporting should be typed duties.
- Do not collapse performance problems, contract expiry, abolition of post, and misconduct into one discretionary exit lane.
- Do not hide staff rules and integrity expectations from the public-facing constitutional record.

## Failure modes

- **regime by patronage**: appointments track power sponsors more than competence and integrity.
- **borrowed-capacity government**: the institution’s real operating intelligence sits in seconded or donor-linked personnel.
- **geographic tokenism**: representational language substitutes for hiring discipline or is ignored until legitimacy collapses.
- **conduct vagueness**: conflicts and reporting duties exist only as culture rather than rule.
- **performance limbo**: managers cannot distinguish weak performance, unsuitability, and misconduct cleanly.
- **head/staff confusion**: the political selection lane for the executive head swallows the rest of the service.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it publish one visible staff constitution naming who appoints staff and who sets staff rules?
2. Are efficiency, competence, and integrity stated as the paramount staffing considerations?
3. Is geographic balance given a visible but bounded place where appropriate?
4. Is the executive-head selection lane kept separate from ordinary career staffing?
5. Are appointment categories, probation, and conversion rules explicitly typed?
6. Are secondments and borrowed-capacity arrangements written, time-bounded, and conflict-screened?
7. Are related-party hiring and superior/subordinate family conflicts clearly constrained?
8. Are disclosure, recusal, confidentiality, breach-reporting, and retaliation protections explicit?
9. Are reassignment, promotion, poor performance, unsuitability, and misconduct separated into distinct lanes?
10. Can outsiders easily find the staff regulations, conduct standards, and executive-head selection rules?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can **hire, direct, rotate, and remove people** but cannot say **which appointments are political, which are professional, how merit and geographic balance interact, when secondments are lawful, what conduct rules apply, and how separation decisions are typed**, it still has **institutional staffing without an honest workforce constitution**.
