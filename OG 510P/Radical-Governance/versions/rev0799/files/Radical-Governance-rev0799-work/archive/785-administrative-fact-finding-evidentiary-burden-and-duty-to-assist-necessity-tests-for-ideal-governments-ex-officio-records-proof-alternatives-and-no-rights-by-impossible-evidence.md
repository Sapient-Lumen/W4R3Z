# 785 — Administrative fact-finding, evidentiary burden, and duty-to-assist necessity tests for ideal governments: ex officio records, proof alternatives, and no rights by impossible evidence

## One-line thesis

Ideal governments should allocate **administrative fact-finding and evidentiary burden** honestly: public authorities should investigate material facts, fetch what the state already knows, disclose the adverse case before deciding, assist people through proof obstacles they cannot reasonably clear alone, and never win by impossible-document demands, evidentiary surprise, or burden shifting disguised as procedure.

## Why this matters

The archive already says that permit systems should define evidence needs and clocks (`573`), that official notice and proof of delivery need typed effects (`575`), that appeals require file access and reviewable reasons (`773`), that delay and interim relief matter because timing can destroy substance (`778`, `779`), and that reliance, silence, and self-correction change the practical stakes of what the administration says or proves (`780`–`783`). What it still lacked as one compact operating rule was the evidentiary grammar sitting underneath all of those notes:

**who has to prove what, using which records, before the state may decide against someone?**

That question is often answered badly in practice. Authorities demand records they already hold. Applicants are told to prove facts that only the state can verify. Vulnerable people lose not because the law is against them, but because they cannot assemble the demanded documentary stack in time. Agencies rely on undisclosed third-party reports or internal notes. Officials request “more evidence” without identifying what issue remains uncertain. Or, conversely, public authorities do too little investigation of their own and then compensate by distrusting everyone who cannot produce immaculate paperwork.

Ideal-government design therefore needs a doctrine of **administrative evidentiary necessity**. The point is not to abolish party responsibility or turn every administrative process into a judicial inquiry. It is to force the polity to separate several questions that are often blurred together: who bears the initial burden of production, who must investigate disputed facts, who controls relevant records, when the authority must assist rather than merely demand, what access the affected party has to the case file, how confidential or third-party material is handled, when proof substitutes or presumptions are justified, and how uncertainty created by the state’s own record failures should be allocated.

## Necessity table

| Necessity test | What counts as proof | Honest design consequence | Default answer if proof is weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Need to avoid impossible-proof governance** | affected people are asked for records only the state controls or can fetch more easily | require ex officio retrieval of state-held evidence and once-only submission where possible | make rights turn on document scavenger hunts |
| **Need to separate party allegation from authority investigation** | procedures blur who must assert, who must prove, and who must inquire further | type burden of production, investigation, and persuasion separately | let the administration offload all uncertainty onto the individual |
| **Need for adversarial fairness before adverse decisions** | authorities rely on reports, data, or complaints not disclosed to the affected person | disclose the adverse case and permit rebuttal before deciding, subject to lawful confidentiality handling | decide first and reveal the file later or never |
| **Need to protect vulnerable or low-capacity parties from procedural defeat by complexity** | people lose because forms, evidence routes, or language demands are unusable | impose a duty to assist, permit alternative proofs, and keep channels human-accessible | treat incapacity as non-compliance |
| **Need to preserve efficiency without sacrificing fairness** | mass procedures or low-stakes decisions cannot sustain full bespoke inquiry every time | use proportional evidence rules by stakes, reversibility, and risk | either over-judicialise every file or under-protect high-stakes cases |
| **Need to handle confidential or third-party material honestly** | some evidence cannot be fully disclosed but still affects the outcome | use summaries, confidentiality rings, or independent review rather than secret determinative evidence | rely on hidden file material the person cannot answer |
| **Need to manage uncertainty caused by state record failure** | missing, contradictory, or degraded official records create proof gaps | place resulting disadvantage on the state unless the party caused the gap, or use rebuttable presumptions plus repair routes | let administrative disorder become an anti-citizen evidentiary weapon |
| **Need to distinguish anti-fraud scrutiny from blanket distrust** | high-fraud areas tempt authorities into universal suspicion and over-documentation | target verification by risk signals and keep documentary demands proportionate | subject everyone to evidentiary siege because some cheat |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate burden of production, burden of investigation, and burden of persuasion

A person may need to identify the claim, explain the requested benefit or contested burden, and submit readily available supporting material. That does not mean the person should also bear the full burden of investigating all relevant facts or disproving every contrary inference. Ideal systems should name these burdens separately so “please tell us the facts you know” does not mutate into “you must solve the whole case for the state.”

### 2. Fetch what the state already knows or can lawfully obtain directly

Where relevant records sit in public registries, prior case files, tax systems, civil-status registers, land records, or other authoritative state sources, the administration should retrieve them ex officio where lawful and technically feasible. Rights should not depend on whether a person can re-export state knowledge back to the state in the right PDF format.

### 3. Use proportionate evidence demands keyed to stakes, reversibility, and risk

High-stakes sanctions, coercive action, deprivation of entitlement, or irreversible permitting choices justify more careful investigation and stronger evidentiary discipline than routine low-risk updates. The proportionality should cut both ways: severe action requires a fuller case from the authority, while low-stakes routine administration should not force people into exhaustive proof theater.

### 4. Disclose the adverse case in time to answer it

Before an adverse decision, the affected person should know the facts, allegations, legal basis, and significant evidence on which the administration proposes to rely. Where confidentiality is genuinely necessary, the design should provide the maximum usable substitute disclosure compatible with the protected interest.

### 5. Require authorities to say what issue remains unproven

“More documents required” is not a reason. When evidence is considered insufficient, the administration should identify what material issue remains unresolved, why the submitted material did not settle it, and what kind of additional proof or alternative substitute could address the gap. That turns evidence requests from ritual into intelligible administration.

### 6. Build a duty to assist into frontline procedure

Duty to assist can include explaining what is needed, helping complete forms, accepting plain-language descriptions before legal characterisation is fixed, allowing correction of curable defects, identifying available alternative proofs, and routing people toward help where disability, language, poverty, or digitisation barriers would otherwise defeat the claim.

### 7. Allow proof alternatives when formal documents are unavailable

Affidavits, witness statements, photographs, geolocation, expert reports, inspection visits, sworn declarations, or other secondary evidence may be necessary when primary documents are unavailable for reasons not attributable to the person. An ideal system does not convert document absence into automatic legal defeat where reality can still be responsibly established.

### 8. Do not let state-caused record gaps become anti-citizen presumptions by default

If official records are lost, contradictory, inaccessible, or never properly created, the resulting uncertainty should not simply be charged to the individual. The system should use repair routes, rebuttable presumptions, burden shifts, or other fairness devices depending on who controlled the records and why the gap exists.

### 9. Preserve a reviewable evidence trail

Case files should show what was requested, what was received, what was disregarded, what was independently verified, and why particular evidence was credited or rejected. Appeals and ombuds lanes cannot correct evidentiary injustice if the underlying file only shows outcome and not reasoning.

### 10. Keep anti-fraud tools targeted and reviewable

Risk scoring, document verification, cross-checks, and enhanced proof requests can be justified in some fields, but they should be triggered by named risk logic, not by the identity or stereotype of the claimant. Otherwise evidentiary design becomes an instrument of hidden discrimination.

## Guardrails

- Do not require people to produce records the state already holds or can retrieve more easily.
- Do not rely on determinative adverse material without giving a meaningful chance to answer it.
- Do not hide behind generic “insufficient evidence” language when the unresolved issue can be named.
- Do not let record failures caused by the administration default against the individual.
- Do not use fraud control as a blanket excuse for impossible-document demands on everyone.
- Do not let digital convenience erase assisted-human routes, alternative proofs, or correction windows.

## Failure modes

- **rights by impossible evidence**: a person loses because the demanded proof is practically unattainable.
- **state memory outsourcing**: government makes people fetch the government’s own records back to it.
- **evidentiary ambush**: adverse reports, data, or claims appear only after or inside the decision.
- **anti-fraud overreach**: universal suspicion produces documentary siege and selective exclusion.
- **file opacity**: nobody can tell what evidence mattered or why contrary material failed.
- **bureaucratic nihilism about missing records**: official disorder is converted into anti-citizen presumptions.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can tell you **which form to file and when a decision is due** but cannot also show **who bears which evidentiary burden, what facts the authority must investigate for itself, when the state must fetch its own records, how the affected person sees and answers the adverse case, what proof substitutes exist when documents are unavailable, and how missing state records are treated**, then it still has **administrative procedure without an evidentiary constitution**.
