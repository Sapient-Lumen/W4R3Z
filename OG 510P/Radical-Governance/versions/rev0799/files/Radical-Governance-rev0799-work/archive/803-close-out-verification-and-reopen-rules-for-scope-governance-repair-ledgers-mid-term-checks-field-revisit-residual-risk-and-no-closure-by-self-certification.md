# 803 — Close-out, verification, and reopen rules for scope-governance repair ledgers: mid-term checks, field revisit, residual risk, and no closure by self-certification

## One-line thesis

The archive should pair its finding-register and management-response notes with one compact **close-out and verification grammar**: **every material scope repair item should face a typed follow-up review that tests closure proof against live conditions, declares residual risk, decides close / continue / reopen / rescope, and names the next verification clock, so corrective-action ledgers do not turn into self-closing spreadsheets or polite fiction**.

## Why this matters

The archive can now say **what the ideal stack is** (`790`), **how to benchmark a real arrangement against it** (`791`), **how to stress-test it** (`792`), **when those checks recur** (`793`), **how to move toward a better stack** (`794`), **what outward proof should stay public** (`795`), **how persistent failure can justify intervention** (`796`), **who may challenge those judgments** (`797`), **which peer class actually counts before scoring begins** (`798`), **what must happen to temporary constitutional objects once “temporary” has to mean something** (`799`), **how a serious assessment mission should be run** (`800`), **how the resulting judgment should be stated lane by lane** (`801`), and **how the host must answer those findings with owned repair work** (`802`).

What the archive still lacked was one short rule for the moment after a corrective-action ledger starts moving:

**who verifies that an item is actually complete, what kind of follow-up review it receives, and when an apparently closed repair has to be reopened instead of ceremonially buried?**

That gap matters because governance review often fails at the close-out layer rather than the diagnostic one. A polity creates a serious response ledger and then lets the same office that promised the fix declare itself finished. Another closes a rights-sensitive item because a policy was issued, even though the complaint queue, appeal clock, or fiscal transfer path still behaves as before. Another treats a bridge arrangement as repaired because a statute passed, while the staffing transfer, data access, or handback discipline never became operational. Another marks a metropolitan defect “complete” at the next committee meeting and only rediscovers it when the next flood, blackout, outbreak, or election exposes the same break again.

The official models already point away from that mistake. The DAC peer review methodology makes follow-up a structured sequence rather than a one-shot response: members are encouraged to submit a management response within six months, then undergo a mid-term review two to three years later based on an action matrix and discussion of implementation with government and outside stakeholders, with conclusions published. OECD’s implementation toolkit treats systematic follow-up as a distinct maturity condition: clear procedures should require management responses, assign responsibilities, and regularly monitor and track follow-up actions rather than just acknowledging findings. The Council of Europe’s post-monitoring dialogue exists to ensure implementation of recommendations through an agreed schedule and a co-signed road map of main steps. WHO’s after-action review guidance treats corrective actions as needed to institutionalize lessons, while NAPHS guidance turns gaps into immediate and long-term priority actions and WHO progress-documentation missions explicitly review progress made on implementation. UNDRR’s scorecard work likewise treats assessment as part of monitoring and reviewing progress and challenges in implementation rather than as an end in itself. The archive should compress that lesson into one note: **serious scope repair needs verified close-out and honest reopen rules, not completion by self-certification**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate management response from close-out verification

`802` says **how the host answers a finding and organizes repair work**.

This note says **how that repair work is later checked before it is allowed to count as closed**.

The archive should keep those jobs distinct. The office that owns a fix may supply evidence and propose closure, but it should not be the final judge of closure for every material item.

### 2. Type the follow-up review before the item is declared done

Each material action should carry a declared **verification route** before work starts.

Typical routes include:

- **desk close-out** for documentary or clerical repairs,
- **spot-check verification** for limited operational changes,
- **field revisit** where the constitutional question lives in practice,
- **mid-term implementation review** for multi-year structural reforms,
- **drill or exercise retest** where continuity or command claims were central,
- **renewal / handback review** for temporary or supervised arrangements,
- or **full reassessment** where the original finding was severe, disputed, or system-wide.

Without a typed route, closure standards drift toward convenience.

### 3. Match the verifier to the stakes

Not every item needs the same verifier.

Low-stakes administrative fixes may close through a secretariat, inspectorate, or review unit. Structural, rights-sensitive, fiscally material, or contested items should usually require a stronger verifier such as:

- the original review body,
- an oversight institution,
- a cross-level review panel,
- a court-facing compliance process,
- a post-monitoring mission,
- or a peer review / external mission with outside hearings.

The archive should reject the anti-pattern where the same delivery office both owns the fix and unilaterally certifies closure for a high-stakes constitutional defect.

### 4. Verify the live condition, not only the paperwork

A repair should not close merely because one artifact exists.

Verification should test the **claimed closure proof** from `802` against the live condition that originally failed. Depending on the item, that may require checking whether:

- the service actually works,
- the fiscal transfer actually executed,
- the appeal route actually hears cases within the clock,
- the handback actually occurred,
- the temporary administrator actually stood down,
- the data feed actually reaches the lower tier,
- or the drill result actually improved.

This is where `800` reconnects. If the original defect was operational, the close-out check should usually include some operational verification too. Where the issue lived in one concrete user journey, fund flow, complaint route, or handback path, later notes should now also treat `804` as the place to go for a typed tracer-route retest rather than broad reassurance. Where the missing problem is instead whether the retest is still using the same evidence spine, handling rules, and support objects rather than a fresh narrative packet, later notes should now also treat `806` as the place to go.

### 5. Allow partial closure and residual-risk states

Many repairs do not move cleanly from open to done.

The archive should allow small typed closure states such as:

- **closed**,
- **closed with monitoring tail**,
- **partly closed**,
- **closed on paper but not yet in practice**,
- **superseded by different remedy path**,
- **reopened**,
- or **escalated to rescoping / intervention / renewal review**.

That preserves honesty where a legal amendment is complete but staffing is still thin, or where a temporary safeguard can stand down but only under a live monitoring tail.

### 6. Declare residual risk explicitly before closing the item

A serious close-out review should name any **residual risk** that remains after the repair.

Residual risk may include:

- dependence on one-off grant support,
- temporary staffing or contractor reliance,
- untested continuity under stress,
- pending secondary legislation,
- fragile political agreement,
- unresolved data-quality issues,
- or continued vulnerability for a specific claimant group or territory.

A repair with non-trivial residual risk may still close, but the ledger should say so and attach the next monitoring or retest clock.

### 7. Give every closure decision a reopen trigger

Every material closure should carry a small set of **reopen triggers**. Typical triggers include:

- the same failure recurring above threshold,
- missed performance or backlog targets after closure,
- a failed drill or adverse incident,
- a court ruling or audit finding that contradicts the closure claim,
- budget withdrawal,
- failure of a dependency that had been treated as stable,
- or a contested close-out decision being overturned under `797`.

This keeps “closed” from becoming constitutional amnesia.

### 8. Use mid-term reviews for structural reforms that cannot honestly be closed quickly

Some actions should not pretend to short-cycle closure.

Large territorial, fiscal, or institutional reforms should normally carry a **mid-term implementation review** rather than a near-term completion claim. OECD DAC peer review practice is instructive here because it expects a matrix on each recommendation and a later mid-term review discussion with stakeholders before published conclusions are issued. The archive should borrow that discipline for serious multilevel repair: when the reform horizon is measured in years, the honest milestone is usually mid-term verification, not “done”.

### 9. Route failed close-out into the right next lane

A failed close-out is not one thing.

Depending on why closure failed, the archive should route the item differently:

- back to `802` if the repair plan itself was weak,
- to `800` for a focused revisit or site verification,
- to `797` if the closure decision is contested,
- to `796` if persistent implementation failure now justifies proportional supervision,
- to `799` if the item concerns renewal, handback, or expiry of a temporary arrangement,
- or to `794` if the attempted repair reveals that a deeper phased redesign is needed.

### 10. Keep a visible closure residue in the public proof pack

`795` should not publish only findings and promises. It should also show enough of the close-out state that outsiders can tell whether a repair is:

- still open,
- proposed for closure,
- verified closed,
- closed with monitoring tail,
- reopened,
- or escalated.

The archive should reject public proof surfaces that show action-plan optimism without the later closure verdict.

### 11. Do not let contestability disappear at close-out

Closure decisions can be as important as findings.

If a rights-sensitive or high-stakes item is proposed for closure, affected actors should have a declared route to object, supply contrary evidence, or challenge the close-out basis. The archive should therefore treat contested closure as a live constitutional event rather than as an internal project-management note.

### 12. Prefer honest carry-forward to fake closure

The archive should bias toward truthful incompletion.

If the verifier cannot tell whether the reform holds in practice, the item should usually stay open, move to **closed with monitoring tail**, or schedule a focused revisit. The archive should prefer visible uncertainty over neat but dishonest closure.

## Minimum fields for a close-out and reopen register

| Field | What it tells later actors | Minimum form | Typical failure if missing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Finding or action reference** | which original problem this close-out addresses | note / lane / action id | closure loses its object and drifts into general optimism |
| **Verification route** | what kind of review is required before closure | typed route | closure standard changes ad hoc |
| **Verifier** | who is allowed to recommend or confirm closure | institution or panel | owner certifies itself finished |
| **Evidence tested** | what proof was actually examined | short list or packet id | closure rests on one symbolic artifact |
| **Live-condition check** | what real-world condition was retested | one short operational claim | paper repair masks unchanged practice |
| **Closure verdict** | close / partly close / monitor / reopen / escalate | typed verdict | status language becomes mushy |
| **Residual risk** | what still worries later actors | one short note or none | fragile fixes look permanent |
| **Next verification clock** | when the item is seen again | date, trigger, or cycle | closed items vanish from view |
| **Reopen trigger** | what would force the item back onto the docket | one threshold or event | relapse becomes rediscovery |

## Minimal close-out grammar

| Action reference | Verification route | Verifier | Closure verdict | Residual risk | Reopen trigger |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Municipal complaint-handling repair** | spot-check verification | ombuds liaison + city audit | closed with monitoring tail | backlog could return if surge staffing lapses | queue exceeds threshold for 2 consecutive months |
| **Metro flood-governance statute** | mid-term implementation review | regional review panel | partly closed | statute enacted, but joint control room not yet staffed | failed wet-season drill or missed staffing milestone |
| **Temporary special administrator handback** | renewal / handback review | interior ministry + independent reviewer | reopened | formal handback announced, but procurement and payroll still captive | host authority lacks signature control by cutover date |
| **Regional data-sharing repair** | field revisit | benchmark mission secretariat | closed | secondary legislation still pending for one dataset | court or audit finding blocks operational access |

## Use rule for the archive

When later notes need to say not just **what the finding was** or **how the host answered it**, but **whether the claimed repair may honestly count as closed and when it must reopen if reality turns**, the archive should usually do these things in order:

1. **use `800` to run the original assessment or focused revisit where needed**,
2. **use `801` to state the finding register lane by lane**,
3. **use `802` to build the management response and corrective-action ledger**,
4. **use `804` where honest close-out requires retracing one live path through the stack rather than only checking paperwork**,
5. **use this note to type the follow-up review, closure verdict, residual risk, and reopen triggers**,
6. **use `795` to publish the public-safe closure state and monitoring tail**,
7. **use `797` if the closure decision itself is contested**,
8. **use `796`, `799`, or `794` if failed close-out changes the constitutional posture from repair into supervision, renewal review, or deeper redesign**.

The archive should not let serious repair end in a spreadsheet cell turning green.

## Guardrails

- Do not let the delivery owner unilaterally certify closure for every material item.
- Do not close operational failures on documentary proof alone when practice is the issue.
- Do not confuse enacted law with implemented change.
- Do not hide residual risk to produce cleaner dashboards.
- Do not close long-horizon structural reforms on short-cycle evidence.
- Do not let contested close-out disappear into project-management language.
- Do not mark an item closed without a next verification clock or a reopen rule.
- Do not let reopened items return as if they were brand-new surprises.

## Failure modes

- **green-cell constitutionalism** — the ledger says “complete” because the status colour changed, not because the defect disappeared.
- **owner-certified closure** — the same office that promised the fix declares itself done.
- **paper implementation** — a law, plan, or memo exists while the live condition still fails.
- **closure without residue** — fragile improvements are narrated as permanent despite obvious tail risk.
- **mid-term denial** — structural reforms are marked complete before the institutional settlement has actually stabilized.
- **reopen amnesia** — the same defect returns but no one acknowledges that a prior close-out failed.
- **silent escalation** — a failed close-out quietly becomes supervision, temporary custody, or redesign without a visible closure verdict.
- **repair burial by dashboard** — the public sees optimistic completion rates but not verified close-out, monitoring tails, or reopened items.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Does every material repair item have a declared verification route before closure is proposed?
2. Is the verifier strong enough for the stakes, or is the owner certifying itself finished?
3. Where the original defect was operational, did the close-out check test the live condition and not just the paperwork?
4. Can the ledger show residual risk and monitoring tail instead of forcing binary closure?
5. Does every closed or partly closed item carry a next verification clock and a reopen trigger?
6. If closure fails, is the next lane visible rather than buried in general delay language?

## Compression rule

If the archive wants to say that a scope repair is **really closed**, **closed but fragile**, **still open**, or **back on the docket**, it should be able to show one short close-out register naming the verification route, verifier, evidence tested, closure verdict, residual risk, and reopen trigger. If it cannot, the archive is still too close to **closure by self-certification**.
