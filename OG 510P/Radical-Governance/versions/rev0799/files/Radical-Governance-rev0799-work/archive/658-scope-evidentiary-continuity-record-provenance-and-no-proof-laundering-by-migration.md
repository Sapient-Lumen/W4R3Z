# 658 — Scope evidentiary continuity, record provenance, and no proof laundering by migration

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should require every **materially stack-altering reform** to preserve **evidentiary continuity** across record transfer, system migration, redaction, archival cutover, and platform replacement — with stable identifiers, provenance metadata, chain-of-custody, transformation logs, and certified retrieval routes — so responsibility can still be proven after a function moves and rescoping cannot dissolve proof through administrative or technical migration.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a strong doctrine for **how stack-bearing reforms move through law and time**.

It says how scope fits levels (`602`, `628`-`630`), when it must be reopened (`632`-`635`), how proposals are classified and cleared (`636`-`639`), how they are watched, settled, retired, and reactivated (`640`-`643`), how defects and review lanes work (`644`-`652`), which sources and publication surfaces count (`653`-`655`), how pending matters should survive cutover (`656`), and how accountability for predecessor acts should remain visible after transfer (`657`).

That is already a much better constitutional operating system than most real governments possess.

But one loophole still remains large enough to swallow the rest: **proof laundering by migration**.

A region takes over a municipal permitting function and imports the old files into a new platform, but the migration strips author history, collapses version lineage, and replaces original timestamps with bulk-upload dates. A ministry inherits an agency's inspection powers and promises successor accountability, but the operational logs remain trapped in a vendor system that no longer has a public retrieval route. A metropolitan authority receives transport complaints, but predecessor records were flattened into PDFs that no longer show internal routing, officer annotations, or withdrawal history. A national emergency platform centralises decisions that were formerly local and then archives the local audit trail in a way that no longer preserves who actually clicked, approved, overrode, or delayed the action. An old identifier disappears, a new one appears, and with it the practical ability to prove continuity vanishes.

In each case the polity may still claim that:

- the function moved lawfully,
- the successor is known,
- the file technically exists,
- and review routes remain open.

Yet the most important thing may no longer be true: **the public, a court, an auditor, or the affected person can no longer reliably prove what happened, who did it, when it happened, what changed, or whether the migrated record is complete**.

That is not a minor administrative failure.

If evidentiary continuity breaks, several earlier archive doctrines begin to hollow out:

- successor accountability (`657`) becomes hard to enforce because the successor can say the record is incomplete;
- pending-matter continuity (`656`) becomes theatrical because queue position and filing dates cannot be verified;
- source hierarchy (`653`) loses force because the authoritative and derivative surfaces can no longer be linked confidently;
- promulgation and effective-date discipline (`654`-`655`) become weaker because timestamps and operative transitions may no longer be trustworthy;
- nullity, severability, remand, and review (`644`-`647`) become harder because the evidentiary basis of the defect may have dissolved.

The archive therefore needs one more compact doctrine.

It is not enough to preserve **custody** of records. The polity must preserve their **provenance and evidentiary value**.

That is the role of **scope evidentiary continuity**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate file custody from evidentiary integrity

A polity should not treat the statement *the successor now holds the records* as the end of the question.

At least five different things may be at issue:

- physical or digital custody,
- legal authority to disclose,
- operational ability to retrieve,
- evidentiary integrity,
- and public intelligibility.

A transferred file can be in the right building or database and still be evidentially broken.

The archive should therefore distinguish between **having the file** and **being able to prove the file's history, completeness, and authenticity**.

### 2. Every materially stack-altering transfer should carry one evidentiary-continuity schedule

Whenever a function moves materially across the stack, the reform should carry one explicit evidentiary-continuity schedule.

At minimum it should say:

- which systems of record are affected,
- which identifiers persist and which are cross-walked,
- which metadata must survive migration,
- what transformation events will occur,
- who certifies completeness,
- how public retrieval continues,
- how review bodies access the pre- and post-cutover record,
- and how long the predecessor environment, export, or evidentiary snapshot remains available.

Without that schedule, a scope transfer is still under-specified.

### 3. Preserve stable identifiers, timestamps, authorship, and version lineage

A migrated record should preserve enough structure that a later reviewer can still answer basic constitutional questions:

- what object this is,
- which earlier object it came from,
- who created it,
- who altered it,
- when each action occurred,
- and which version was operative at the relevant time.

That usually means preserving or cross-walking:

- canonical case or file identifiers,
- filing and receipt timestamps,
- decision timestamps,
- author and signatory identity,
- version numbers,
- amendment and withdrawal markers,
- and relationships among parent, child, annex, appeal, or enforcement objects.

If migration keeps only the latest visible document and destroys the surrounding lineage, it has not honestly preserved the record.

### 4. Treat transformation as an event that must leave its own trace

Format conversion, bulk export, redaction, deduplication, OCR, metadata normalisation, and platform import should each be treated as named events in the record's life rather than invisible technical housekeeping.

The archive should require that transformation logs show at least:

- what process occurred,
- when it occurred,
- who authorised it,
- what tooling or rules were used,
- what fields were added, dropped, mapped, or changed,
- and whether a human attestation of fidelity was made.

A polity should never ask outsiders to trust a transformed record while hiding the fact of transformation itself.

### 5. The public route to an old record should survive the move, not disappear into system archaeology

After a function moves, the public should not need insider knowledge to learn where the historical file now lives.

A serious polity should preserve one readable retrieval path that tells outsiders:

- whether the record remains in the predecessor system,
- whether it has migrated,
- which new identifier corresponds to the old one,
- what access limits now apply,
- and which authority can certify the record for review or complaint purposes.

Wrong-door loss should be treated as a governance defect, not as a user problem.

### 6. Derivative copies should remain visibly derivative

A common migration trick is to destroy evidentiary depth by flattening records into convenient but thinner forms:

- scanned PDFs,
- screenshot bundles,
- spreadsheet extracts,
- dashboard views,
- or operator-facing summaries.

Those may have a legitimate administrative role. But they should remain visibly derivative unless the polity has openly certified them as legally sufficient replacements under a lawful standard that preserves integrity, completeness, and reviewability.

The archive should therefore insist that:

- originals and derivatives stay distinguishable,
- summary views not impersonate the system of record,
- and transformed artifacts not silently replace evidentiary originals merely because the new platform prefers them.

### 7. Redaction, privacy, and retention rules should narrow disclosure without erasing provenance

Sometimes governments must redact, minimise, or retain different elements for lawful privacy, confidentiality, or archival reasons.

That does not justify provenance collapse.

A polity should be able to say:

- what was withheld,
- under what authority,
- whether the hidden material still exists,
- what retention or destruction rule applies,
- and whether a privileged or review-only access route survives.

The archive should reject a familiar abuse: **privacy as an excuse to make public records structurally untraceable**.

### 8. Successor systems should keep predecessor acts and successor acts legibly distinct

Where a successor continues working in an inherited file, the record should show clearly which steps belong to the predecessor and which belong to the successor.

That includes:

- preserving the original issuing authority,
- separating inherited annotations from new annotations,
- marking whether a decision is old, continued, corrected, superseded, or newly made,
- and keeping line-of-authority changes visible in the event history.

Otherwise the successor may unintentionally — or strategically — overwrite authorship and make the past look like its own work.

### 9. Audit trails, access logs, and override history should survive governance migration too

The public-facing document is not always enough.

For many consequential actions, later accountability depends on the surrounding operational evidence:

- access logs,
- override history,
- workflow routing,
- role assignments,
- notice delivery,
- escalation trails,
- and release history.

A reform should therefore say which of these logs are part of the preserved evidentiary package, how long they remain available, and who can inspect them.

No polity should claim to preserve accountability while discarding the traces that show who actually pressed the button.

### 10. Review forums should have a certification route for the migrated record

Courts, auditors, ombuds, and internal review bodies should not have to guess whether a migrated record is complete.

A serious polity should provide one route for certification that can attest:

- that the record is complete or explain its gaps,
- that the migration preserved specified metadata,
- that the derivative view corresponds to the source environment,
- and that any known breaks in custody, readability, or integrity are declared openly.

Silence about record quality is not neutrality. It is often an attempt to move evidentiary risk onto the challenger.

### 11. Evidentiary continuity should close only through an explicit certification or a visible declared break

Like other transition disciplines in this archive, evidentiary continuity should have an end state.

The polity should either issue:

- a migration-complete certification stating that provenance requirements were preserved and the new public route is authoritative,

or

- a declared evidentiary-break notice stating exactly what cannot now be proven, what mitigations exist, and what presumptions or burdens follow from that loss.

The archive should prefer explicit embarrassment to hidden evidentiary decay.

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse successor custody with preserved evidentiary value.
- Do not let platform migration strip identifiers, timestamps, authorship, or version lineage without disclosure.
- Do not let PDF bundles, screenshots, dashboards, or exports impersonate authoritative originals by convenience alone.
- Do not let privacy, retention, or procurement language erase the trace that a record was transformed, narrowed, or destroyed.
- Do not force the public to perform system archaeology to find an inherited file after rescoping.
- Do not let successor annotations overwrite predecessor authorship.
- Do not discard access logs, workflow traces, or override history when those are necessary to prove who acted.

## Failure modes

- **proof laundering**: the file survives in name but not in a form that still proves what happened.
- **identifier breakage**: old and new systems cannot be mapped reliably, so lineage disappears.
- **timestamp collapse**: upload or migration dates replace the original action chronology.
- **summary impersonation**: a thin derivative view becomes the practical record while richer originals vanish from reach.
- **authorship overwrite**: successor systems rewrite predecessor actions as though the new holder had always acted.
- **log amnesia**: decision traces, overrides, or routing history disappear in platform change.
- **wrong-door archaeology**: outsiders cannot tell where the authoritative historical file now lives.
- **privacy pretext deletion**: lawful confidentiality is used to disguise avoidable provenance loss.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Does the reform merely say where the records go, or does it also say how their evidentiary value is preserved?
2. Can a reviewer still map old identifiers to new identifiers without insider help?
3. Do original timestamps survive, or were they replaced by migration timestamps?
4. Can outsiders still tell which authority acted, which authority later inherited the file, and when that handoff occurred?
5. Are derivative formats visibly marked as derivative, or do they now impersonate the system of record?
6. Are audit trails, routing history, and override logs preserved where they matter to accountability?
7. If redaction or retention narrowed the file, is the narrowing itself visible and legally explained?
8. Can a court, ombuds, or auditor obtain a certification of completeness or a candid statement of known evidentiary gaps?
9. If the new platform disappeared tomorrow, would the polity still be able to prove what happened under the old one?
10. If the answer to that last question is no, has the stack really preserved reviewability across the transfer at all?

## Closing image

A serious constitutional stack should not let power move forward while **proof falls through the gap behind it**. When a function moves, the public should still be able to say: **this is the same matter, this is the same lineage, this is who acted then, this is who holds it now, and this is how we can still prove it**.
