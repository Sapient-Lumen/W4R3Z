# 515 — Digital public infrastructure by scope: local access, city service design, national rails, and global open standards

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should govern digital public infrastructure by keeping assisted access, trust-building, and service usability close to people; letting municipalities and metropolitan institutions integrate place-based services and local data uses under strong law; reserving identity, payments, base registries, secure data exchange, notifications, cybersecurity baselines, and universal-access duties to the nation; and keeping the global layer focused on open technical standards and narrow identifier coordination rather than allowing either platform monopolies or a fantasy world digital state to swallow public governance.

## Why this matters

Digital governance often fails in two opposite directions. One is app maximalism: governments imagine that digitizing the surface of a service is the same as building public infrastructure, so every agency or locality buys its own portal, account system, payments flow, and notification stack. The result is duplication, exclusion, and brittle identity sprawl. The other is centralized digital gigantism: the nation or a large vendor builds foundational rails and then treats them as a license for permanent data extraction, silent interlinking, or one forced account for everything.

A serious polity needs a narrower and more constitutional understanding of digital public infrastructure. Shared digital rails are useful because they reduce duplication and make services more coherent, but they are dangerous when they exceed purpose, become mandatory before assisted alternatives exist, or allow one layer of government to know and control too much. The question is not whether a government should have digital identity, payments, data exchange, and base registries. The question is which level should govern which part, under what rights constraints, and for whose convenience.

The archive therefore needs a compact note on digital public infrastructure by scope. A good polity should keep human access and local usability close to daily life, treat city and metro institutions as important digital integrators of place-based services, reserve common rails and universal guarantees to the nation, and keep the global layer thin, open, and standards-bound.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from universal and meaningful access, not from app download counts

Digital public infrastructure should be judged first by whether people can reliably reach services with affordability, usable devices, connectivity, skills support, and safe alternatives, not by how many portals exist or how often agencies announce new features. A digital state that assumes perfect devices, literacy, and connectivity is simply rebuilding exclusion in software.

### 2. Give micro-local institutions assisted-digital and trust roles

Libraries, schools, community organizations, neighborhood offices, and in-person service counters often supply the missing layer between a national digital system and a real person. They can help with onboarding, credential recovery, consent understanding, translation, and escalation. They should not become shadow identity authorities or informal data brokers.

### 3. Treat municipalities as the designers of service experience where place matters

Municipal government is often the right level for the last mile of service design: permits, inspections, local benefits interfaces, bookings, neighborhood reporting, integrated civic accounts where lawful, and practical user support. This is where residents actually feel whether digital government is legible. Cities should be able to build usable interfaces and service bundles without fragmenting the underlying national rails.

### 4. Use metropolitan and regional institutions where mobility, hospitals, utilities, and labor markets cross local boundaries

Many high-value digital workflows are metropolitan or regional: integrated transit ticketing, regional health referrals, utility coordination, emergency messaging, permitting across commuter sheds, and shared data for basin or environmental systems. The archive should therefore prefer regional digital integration where the real service geography outruns one municipality.

### 5. Reserve foundational rails and rights floors to the nation

National government is usually the right level for public digital identity rules, base registries, secure payments interfaces for the state, trusted notification channels, interoperable data-exchange architecture, cybersecurity baselines, procurement standards, privacy law, and universal access obligations. Those rails should be common enough to reduce duplication and rights-bound enough to prevent surveillance by convenience.

### 6. Separate interoperability from universal visibility

Just because systems can talk does not mean every actor should see everything. A good digital constitution permits narrow, logged, purpose-bound exchange rather than building one omniscient database. The archive should prefer minimal disclosure, federated design where possible, and strong role separation over seductive one-pane-of-glass fantasies.

### 7. Keep the global layer narrow: open standards, open protocols, and technical coordination

The global layer should focus on the technical commons that make a plural Internet and interoperable digital systems possible: open standards, shared protocol work, and narrow coordination of unique identifiers. It should not become a catch-all regulator of content, identity, or domestic public services by stealth. Nor should private platforms occupy that coordinating role without public constraint.

### 8. Treat digital public infrastructure as constitutional infrastructure, not vendor architecture

Identity, payments, notifications, and registries shape citizenship, access, and state power. They therefore need the same constitutional discipline as courts or police: lawful purpose, independent oversight, redress, portability where appropriate, clear shutdown and recovery planning, and no coercive digital-only trapdoors.

## Guardrails

- Do not make digital use effectively mandatory before assisted and offline-equivalent routes exist.
- Do not let each agency or locality reinvent identity, payments, and notifications from scratch.
- Do not treat interoperability as permission for universal data pooling.
- Do not outsource foundational public rails so completely that exit, audit, and constitutional control disappear.
- Do not let the global Internet layer drift from technical coordination into unbounded governance of domestic civic life.

## Failure modes

- **portal sprawl**: every institution builds its own stack and users shoulder the integration burden.
- **digital Leviathan**: foundational rails become a pretext for cross-domain surveillance and coercive linkage.
- **last-mile exclusion**: formal digital rights exist but real people cannot recover credentials, afford access, or navigate interfaces.
- **vendor constitution**: key public rules are embedded in proprietary architecture the polity cannot realistically govern.
- **identifier overreach**: narrow technical coordination is misread as authority over content, speech, or every layer of digital life.

## Practical tests

A scale-matched digital constitution passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Are affordability, assisted access, and offline-equivalent routes treated as first-class design duties?
2. Can municipalities and regional bodies design coherent place-based services without fragmenting common rails?
3. Does national government own the foundational identity, payments, registry, exchange, privacy, and cybersecurity baselines?
4. Is interoperability purpose-bound and logged rather than synonymous with universal visibility?
5. Does the global layer stay focused on open standards and narrow coordination instead of sprawling into a hidden digital sovereign?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can show **its apps, portals, and dashboards** but cannot show **who owns identity and payment rails, how assisted access works, which level integrates place-based services, how data exchange is purpose-limited, and what global standards layer it depends on**, it still has **digital services without a digital constitution**.
