# 654 — Scope promulgation, authentic heads, effective dates, and no stack change by unpublished object

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should require every **materially stack-altering act** to become operative through one **authentic public head** — a canonical promulgation object with legal basis, adoption date, effective date, status, and upstream links — so the scope stack cannot be changed by unpublished memo, side letter, private deck, quiet platform switch, or stale mirror.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a strong doctrine for scope honesty.

It says how functions fit levels (`602`, `628`-`630`), when the stack must be reopened (`632`-`635`), how changes are classified and cleared (`636`-`639`), how they are watched, settled, retired, and reactivated (`640`-`643`), how defects and review lanes work (`644`-`652`), and which surfaces outrank which when the artifacts disagree (`653`).

That already closes many routes for changing the stack too cheaply.

But one vital loophole still remains.

Even if a polity knows which source outranks which, it may still fail to say **when a stack-changing source has become public and operative enough to count**.

A ministry signs a side letter with a regional body and begins acting as though competence has shifted. A governor issues an internal circular that field offices treat as binding, but no authentic public promulgation follows. A municipality is told in a meeting that an emergency lane has been extended. A platform release reroutes filings to a different sovereign on Monday morning, while the legal packet does not appear online until Friday. A budget annex quietly assumes a new holder of the function, and staff are told to treat the old arrangement as dead even though the public cannot yet see the authoritative act or its effective date.

All of these moves try to exploit the same weakness: **if the operative object is not forced into one public promulgation lane, practice can move first and publicity can trail behind**.

That is dangerous for at least four reasons.

First, it undermines legal certainty. People, subordinate authorities, courts, auditors, and operators cannot tell what is live, what is merely proposed, what is provisional, and what never became operative at all.

Second, it defeats the source hierarchy note (`653`). A hierarchy cannot protect the stack if the real title-bearing object is hidden, ambiguously dated, privately circulated, or published only after implementation has already moved.

Third, it corrodes review and remedy. A person cannot challenge a scope change honestly if the operative object is scattered across a memo, a budget note, a platform toggle, and a press statement.

Fourth, it rewards government by ambush. Quiet promulgation lets sponsors shift the multilevel bargain first and explain it later.

The archive already has adjacent instincts in its general notes on diffable public rules (`411`), explicit release records (`445`), canonical disclosure heads (`451`), official notice (`575`), public communication (`626`), clear statements (`638`), conformance before go-live (`639`), and source hierarchies (`653`). What it still lacked was one compact scope-specific rule that says: **a materially stack-altering act is not fully operative until it has an authentic public head**.

That is the role of **scope promulgation**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Give every stack-changing act one authentic public head

Every materially stack-altering act should have one canonical public promulgation object.

That head might be a gazette publication, statutory text, officially published judgment, authenticated packet head, or equivalent constitutional publication object, depending on the system.

What matters is not the exact format. What matters is that one public object is designated as the place where outsiders can learn, without hallway access, whether the act exists, what it does, when it starts, and what it supersedes.

### 2. Do not let materially stack-altering acts become normally operative before appropriate publication

A polity should adopt a simple default: **except in genuinely urgent and lawfully justified cases, a materially stack-altering act should not become operative until it has been appropriately published through its authentic head**.

This is stricter than mere adoption. A body may have signed, voted, approved, or configured something internally. That does not yet answer the public question of when the scope stack changed.

The archive should therefore distinguish clearly between:

- adoption,
- publication,
- entry into force,
- operational conformance,
- and settled live status.

Those moments may coincide. They should not be silently collapsed.

### 3. The authentic head should carry the minimum fields needed to orient the public stack

A scope-promulgation head should be compact, but it must carry enough structure to be useful.

At minimum it should state:

- the name and type of the act,
- the adopting authority,
- the legal basis,
- the date of adoption,
- the date and time of publication,
- the effective date or effective window,
- whether the act is ordinary, temporary, urgent, provisional, or settled,
- what prior arrangement it amends, suspends, narrows, widens, retires, or reactivates,
- where the linked scope packet/opinion/override/register entry lives,
- and where challenge, review, or expiry information can be found.

Without those fields, publication becomes performative rather than orienting.

### 4. Side letters, internal circulars, budget assumptions, and platform releases should not count as authentic promulgation by themselves

Some artifacts may still matter a great deal:

- intergovernmental side letters,
- budget annexes,
- ministerial circulars,
- contract schedules,
- implementation plans,
- permissions-table releases,
- service-routing changes,
- internal FAQs,
- or operator instructions.

They may show preparation, intent, drift, or even unlawful early implementation.

But unless the legal system expressly gives one of those forms authentic promulgating force in the relevant lane, they should not be allowed to substitute for the public promulgation head.

Otherwise the polity ends up with **unpublished constitutions plus published explanations after the fact**.

### 5. Derivative mirrors should point back to the authentic head, not impersonate it

Registers, portals, dashboards, manuals, and communications can and should help people navigate the stack. But they should do so by linking back to the authentic head and carrying visible status labels.

A derivative mirror should say, in effect:

- this is a summary,
- this is when it was updated,
- this is the upstream head,
- this is whether the change is pending, operative, provisional, settled, retired, or contested.

That preserves usability without allowing convenience surfaces to mutate into clandestine constitutions.

### 6. Urgent cases should use a short emergency publication lane, not a publicity exemption

Some scope changes really do arise in urgent conditions: natural disaster, war, financial collapse, epidemic, or abrupt institutional failure.

The right response is not to waive publication. It is to shorten the lane.

An urgent stack-bearing act should therefore travel through a minimal emergency publication object that still states:

- who acted,
- under what legal authority,
- what temporary scope effect is being claimed,
- when it starts,
- when it must be reviewed,
- and where the fuller follow-on packet or record will appear.

Emergency speed should compress the publication object, not erase it.

### 7. Publication failure should trigger one of only a few honest legal states

When publication is defective, the polity should resist improvisation.

The archive should allow only a small number of honest descriptions:

- **not yet operative**,
- **temporarily operative but publication-defective under urgent law**, with a short cure clock,
- **operative for internal preparation only, not for public compulsion or routing**, 
- or **void/voidable**, to be handled under the nullity lane (`644`).

The archive should reject the common fiction that something is already fully live because everyone inside government has been told about it.

### 8. Promulgation should update the memory layers immediately

An authentic head should not live alone.

Publication of a stack-changing act should trigger near-immediate updates to:

- the canonical scope register (`633`),
- the jurisprudence digest where relevant (`634`),
- the scope-diff packet status (`635`),
- the opinion or override crosswalk (`636`-`637`),
- and the conformance and observability lanes (`639`-`640`).

This does not mean every surface must update simultaneously. It means the authentic head should create one visible update obligation, so lag becomes governed debt rather than folklore.

### 9. Preserve frozen historical heads and supersession links

Promulgation is not just about the present tense.

The polity should preserve prior authentic heads, with visible supersession links, because later disputes often hinge on what exactly was published at the moment a competence was claimed to have shifted.

Without that preservation, governments can slide from one dangerous habit to another:

- first, changing the stack by unpublished object;
- later, rewriting the publication history so nobody can prove what was or was not publicly operative at the time.

### 10. The more coercive, fiscal, territorial, or rights-bearing the change, the stricter the promulgation rule should be

Not every publication defect has the same stakes.

If the claimed scope change affects:

- who may coerce,
- who may tax, spend, or borrow,
- who hears complaints or appeals,
- who governs territory, planning, emergencies, or policing,
- or which authority residents must obey or challenge,

then the polity should demand the strongest available publication lane and the clearest effective-date discipline.

That is where unpublished government becomes most constitutionally dangerous.

## Guardrails

- Do not let adoption, publication, entry into force, implementation, and settled status blur into one undefined moment.
- Do not let side letters, internal circulars, budget assumptions, procurement annexes, or software releases count as stack-promulgating acts unless law expressly gives them that force.
- Do not let urgent conditions erase publication; use short-form emergency heads instead.
- Do not let derivative mirrors impersonate the authentic promulgation object.
- Do not let a public body route, compel, tax, deny, or redirect people under a materially stack-altering arrangement that lacks a knowable public head.

## Failure modes

- **side-letter constitutionalism**: the real scope deal lives in an intergovernmental letter that outsiders cannot reliably find.
- **meeting-made-it-live**: participants treat a spoken or minuted understanding as the operative constitutional moment.
- **toggle promulgation**: the platform release or routing switch becomes the real publication event.
- **budget promulgation**: appropriations or payroll assumptions are treated as enough to announce a new scope holder.
- **press-before-law**: the political announcement reaches the public before the authentic legal head exists.
- **retroactive clarity theater**: a later publication is used to imply that the stack had already changed earlier in practice.
- **mirror confusion**: the portal or register is mistaken for the authentic promulgation head rather than a derivative public guide.
- **urgent black-box rule**: emergency is invoked to justify unpublished scope change with no short-form public object.

## Anti-theater tests

1. What is the single authentic public head for this claimed scope change?
2. Has the act been adopted only, or also appropriately published?
3. What exact date and time did it become operative, and where is that stated publicly?
4. If the public head disappeared, could an outsider still prove the claimed scope change from an authority-bearing publication object?
5. Are portals, dashboards, budgets, and manuals clearly marked as derivative mirrors rather than as the promulgating act itself?
6. If urgency is claimed, where is the short emergency publication object and its review clock?
7. Does the authentic head link to the relevant packet, opinion, override, register entry, and remedy lane?
8. Would anyone outside the sponsor institution be able to know what changed without private access, meeting attendance, or back-channel explanation?
9. Is the publication history frozen enough that later review can reconstruct what was publicly operative at the time?
10. If the change affects coercion, money, appeals, territory, or emergency powers, has the strictest available publication lane been used?

## Closing image

A good multilevel constitution should not behave like a private group chat whose consequences are revealed only after the platform has already been reconfigured. It should be able to say: **this is the public head of the act, this is when it became operative, this is what it displaced, and this is how you challenge it**. Anything less invites stack change by unpublished object.
