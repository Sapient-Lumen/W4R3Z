# 617 — Capital homes for shared territorial power: multi-year pipelines, asset stewardship, lifecycle discipline, and no government by project list

## One-line thesis

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, it needs an honest **capital home** as well as democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, participatory, knowledge, service, and planning ones: a visible multi-year capital programme, declared prioritisation and appraisal rules, asset-condition and maintenance stewardship, explicit funding-versus-financing discipline, and no territorial government by project list.

## Why this matters

The archive can now say when shared territorial power has become real government and what kinds of institutional home should follow. It can name the democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, participatory, knowledge, service, and planning constitutions that honest territorial government needs.

What it still lacked was the **capital analogue**.

That absence matters because many metropolitan and regional governments become real not only when they tax, plan, or run services, but when they start deciding which large projects get sequenced, which assets are renewed rather than left to decay, which municipalities or corridors receive scarce capital first, how operating burdens will be sustained after construction, and how risk, debt, and maintenance are distributed across the territory.

A shared tier that makes those choices through mayoral summits, ministry deal rounds, operator wish-lists, or consultant project books may already be doing real government. But without a capital home, it still lacks an honest place where investment priorities, appraisal rules, maintenance obligations, rephasing, cancellation, and long-life stewardship are stated and contested in public.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish a capital constitution from generic development rhetoric

A metro or region should not be treated as having a capital constitution merely because it can announce flagship projects, negotiate grants, or publish a glossy infrastructure strategy. The archive should distinguish **real capital government** from generic development rhetoric.

Real capital government is where a shared tier publicly decides which major assets are advanced, delayed, redesigned, maintained, or retired across a territory over time. If that work is real, the territory needs more than an opportunistic deal ledger.

### 2. Give the territory one visible multi-year capital spine

Once shared territorial power is real, the archive should prefer one visible capital spine: a named multi-year capital programme or tightly declared capital-programme family with

- a clear territorial extent,
- a planning horizon,
- stated prioritisation criteria,
- indicative phasing,
- links to plan and service obligations,
- and declared status in budgeting and implementation.

The point is not a single mega-document. It is one public answer to the question: **what capital programme governs this shared territory?**

### 3. Separate funding from financing

A capital home is dishonest when it treats grants, taxes, user charges, reserves, debt, PPP commitments, and other instruments as one undifferentiated pile of “money.” The archive should therefore insist on a visible distinction between **funding** and **financing**.

The territory should say what ultimately pays for assets over time, including operations and maintenance, and what simply advances upfront cost with later repayment or performance obligations. This is how a shared tier avoids borrowing rhetoric that hides future operating stress.

### 4. Keep capital choices tied to whole-of-life cost and service reality

An honest capital home should not choose projects only on ribbon value, territorial symbolism, or grant availability. The archive should prefer appraisal and selection rules that consider construction, operating, maintenance, adaptation, and end-of-life consequences over the asset’s whole life.

This matters especially where metropolitan and regional projects create long operating tails: transit expansions, water systems, flood works, digital backbones, hospitals, waste systems, and major public buildings. A territory that can build but not run, maintain, or replace honestly is still governing by announcement.

### 5. Put maintenance, renewal, and asset condition on the same stage as new build

Shared tiers often drift toward visible expansion while maintenance remains dispersed, politically weak, or hidden in municipal backlogs. The archive should therefore prefer capital constitutions that publish asset condition, renewal need, maintenance backlog, and resilience exposure alongside new capital proposals.

A capital home should be able to say not only what it wants to build, but what it already owns, what condition it is in, what must be renewed, and what service failure follows if renewal is postponed.

### 6. Declare who prioritises, who appraises, who owns, who operates, and who maintains

A capital home becomes murky when planning bodies, ministries, operators, utilities, municipalities, special authorities, and financiers all shape the investment list but no one can say who actually owns each decision. The archive should therefore prefer a declared capital chain.

The territory should be able to identify who proposes projects, who appraises them, who decides sequencing, who owns the resulting assets, who carries operating burdens, who maintains them, and who reports performance. This keeps shared government from hiding behind operator or ministry residue.

### 7. Dock the capital programme to the territorial plan and to the budget

The capital home should not float separately from the planning home or fiscal home. The archive should prefer arrangements where major capital items visibly dock to territorial plans, service authorities, and medium-term budget logic.

This means the shared tier should be able to show how a project serves the territorial plan, where the recurrent costs land, how financing will be repaid, and what trade-offs are imposed on the rest of the capital queue.

### 8. Make prioritisation and rephasing rules public before the stress arrives

Capital queues tighten under fiscal pressure, inflation, climate shock, or supply disruption. The archive should therefore prefer a capital constitution with declared reprioritisation, rephasing, descoping, and cancellation rules before crisis bargaining begins.

Otherwise the real constitution of the territory becomes whoever has proximity to ministers, mayors, or sponsors at the moment of scarcity.

### 9. Keep capital governance territorial, not just municipal aggregation

A real metropolitan or regional capital home should choose projects against the whole territory, not merely distribute money as a peace treaty among component governments. The archive should reject capital arrangements where every municipality must receive a symbolic share regardless of network logic, risk concentration, or system benefit.

Local balance still matters, but territorial capital government should be allowed to sequence by corridor, catchment, vulnerability, demand, and interdependence rather than by permanent municipal equal slices.

### 10. Publish change, slippage, overruns, and ex post learning

A capital home remains incomplete if the public can see only initial announcements. The archive should prefer a capital programme that shows later rephasing, cost movement, scope changes, delay, abandonment, early failure, and ex post review.

This is how a territory stops governing by launch event and starts governing capital as a continuing constitutional responsibility.

## Capital-home matrix

| Question | Thin or dishonest arrangement | Honest capital home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What capital programme governs the territory?** | disconnected project lists and deal rounds | one visible multi-year capital spine |
| **How is money described?** | funding and financing collapsed together | taxes, grants, fees, reserves, debt, and other instruments are distinguished |
| **How are projects chosen?** | prestige, politics, or grant opportunism | declared prioritisation and appraisal rules |
| **How is lifecycle performance handled?** | build now, worry later | operating, maintenance, resilience, and end-of-life costs are considered |
| **How is existing infrastructure treated?** | renewal hidden behind new-build theatre | asset condition, backlog, and renewal sit beside expansion |
| **Who owns the chain?** | proposals, ownership, operation, and maintenance blur together | roles are explicit from project proposal to asset stewardship |
| **How does the programme relate to plan and budget?** | strategy deck on one side, budget fights on the other | capital choices visibly dock to territorial plan and fiscal spine |
| **How does the public learn from drift and failure?** | launch announcements only | change logs, rephasing, overruns, cancellation, and ex post review are published |

## Failure modes

- **government by project list**: the shared tier is really governed through opportunistic project menus without one visible capital constitution.
- **ribbon-capital territorialism**: expansion projects crowd out maintenance, renewal, and resilience.
- **funding-financing blur**: debt, grants, user charges, and recurrent revenue are merged into one soothing story that hides repayment and operating pressure.
- **ownerless asset chain**: nobody can say who will own, run, maintain, or replace the asset once the launch photo is over.
- **municipal quota capital**: the territory allocates major investment mainly as peacekeeping among member governments rather than by system logic.
- **plan-budget split**: territorial plans promise one future while the capital programme and budget quietly build another.
- **launch-event accountability**: delay, descoping, abandonment, and underperformance disappear once procurement or construction begins.

## Practical tests

A shared territorial capital constitution passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is there one visible multi-year capital programme for the shared territory rather than a stack of disconnected project announcements?
2. Are funding and financing described distinctly enough that repayment, operations, and maintenance obligations remain visible?
3. Do appraisal and prioritisation rules consider whole-of-life cost, resilience, and service implications rather than construction only?
4. Are maintenance, renewal, and asset condition published alongside proposals for new build?
5. Can the territory state clearly who proposes, appraises, decides, owns, operates, and maintains each major class of asset?
6. Are rephasing, cancellation, and ex post review treated as normal parts of capital government rather than as embarrassing exceptions?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a metropolitan or regional body can describe **its flagship projects and funding announcements** but cannot show **one visible capital programme, the rules for prioritisation, the lifecycle cost of assets, the maintenance obligations that follow, and the public record of rephasing or failure**, then it still lacks **an honest capital home for shared territorial power**.
