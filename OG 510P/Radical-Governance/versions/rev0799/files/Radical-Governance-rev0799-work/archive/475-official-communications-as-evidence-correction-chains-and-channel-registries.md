# 475 — Official communications as evidence, correction chains, and channel registries

## One-line thesis

Consequential public-AI communications should be treated as first-class evidence surfaces with an official-channel registry, explicit correction linkage, and capture rules for what was said where and when, so public notice cannot drift into silent edits, split-view updates, or screenshot folklore.

## Why this matters

The archive already knows how to freeze public disclosure heads, preserve current authority, and warn on stale copies. What it still lacked was a harder rule for the operational communications layer itself: status posts, banners, press lines, hotline scripts, email blasts, SMS alerts, portal notices, and social-media updates that people actually rely on during incidents, changes, and appeals.

That layer is where governance often goes soft. A public website says one thing while a call center script says another. A social post is corrected by deletion rather than by a linked superseding notice. A screenshot circulates after the underlying page changed. A department cannot later prove which channels carried which message, whether some audiences were omitted, or whether the correction ever reached the same surfaces as the original claim.

If consequential communications remain outside the evidence model, then the archive can preserve beautiful formal records while the real public accountability story happens in untracked fragments.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat operational public communications as evidence surfaces

At minimum, the archive should treat the following as potentially evidence-bearing:

- web banners and service alerts,
- public status pages,
- press statements and incident advisories,
- hotline or assisted-digital scripts,
- email or SMS notices,
- official social-media posts,
- and operator-facing public-answer macros that materially shape what people are told.

The question is not whether every communication is equally important. The question is whether the communication carried consequential institutional meaning.

### 2. Keep an official-channel registry

An institution should maintain one registry of channels it treats as official for consequential communications, including:

- channel identity,
- owning team,
- normal purpose,
- correction path,
- archival or capture method,
- and whether the channel is primary, mirrored, emergency-only, or support-only.

If a channel is used for consequential public guidance but is not on the registry, that should register as a governance defect.

### 3. Require explicit correction linkage

When a consequential notice is corrected, superseded, withdrawn, or narrowed, the correction should point back to the earlier notice and the earlier notice should point forward to the correcting head where feasible.

Silently editing, deleting, or replacing a notice is too weak once the earlier message may already have shaped behavior.

### 4. Preserve channel-spread truth

A communication record should answer not only what was said, but also where it was pushed:

- which official channels carried it,
- which channels did not,
- whether the push was simultaneous or staggered,
- and whether the communication remained parity-relevant across languages, accessibility modes, and assisted-digital routes.

This is how the archive distinguishes one canonical notice from the actual public communication footprint.

### 5. Separate worksheets from authority-bearing notices

Draft templates, press-prep docs, social copy worksheets, and operator crib sheets may support the communication process. They are not automatically the authority-bearing public notice. The archive should preserve the distinction between:

- draft/prep materials,
- approved outbound notice,
- and later summaries or screenshots of that notice.

### 6. Capture enough structure to resist screenshot folklore

For consequential communications, preserve enough structure to answer:

- notice identifier,
- timestamp,
- channel or channels used,
- current status,
- correction or supersession links,
- and a stable pointer to the canonical preserved content.

The goal is not maximal logging. It is minimal replayable proof strong enough to beat detached screenshots and memory arguments.

### 7. Escalate split-view communication as a real incident signal

If materially different audiences receive materially different guidance across official channels, the archive should treat that as an incident or defect, not as an ordinary editorial mismatch.

Examples include:

- hotline scripts lagging behind the website,
- social updates missing the correction sent to email subscribers,
- a public dashboard showing recovery while the site banner still warns of outage,
- or an accessibility-format notice missing the correction carried by the visual surface.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat official public communications as outside the records boundary.
- Do not let deletions or edits erase the earlier consequential claim.
- Do not treat screenshots as the only durable witness of what was said.
- Do not let unofficial or legacy channels impersonate official current guidance.
- Do not call a correction complete until the relevant channel spread has been checked.

## Failure modes

- **silent edit governance**: an institution changes a consequential communication with no visible correction chain.
- **split-view notice**: different audiences receive different official guidance with no recorded divergence.
- **screenshot governance**: later disputes depend on detached images because the institution did not preserve the actual notice surface.
- **ghost channel authority**: an old or unofficial channel still appears current because no official-channel registry exists.
- **correction sinkhole**: the correction exists somewhere, but not on the channels that carried the original claim.

## Practical tests

A communications-evidence discipline passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is there a registry of official channels for consequential public communications?
2. Can a reviewer reconstruct what was said, where, and when across those channels?
3. Do corrected notices visibly link to the notices they replace or narrow?
4. Can the institution distinguish draft communication materials from authority-bearing outbound notices?
5. Are split-view updates treated as real governance defects rather than editorial noise?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a consequential institution can preserve the formal record but cannot also say **which official channels carried which message, what corrected it, and whether the correction reached the same public surfaces**, then it is still letting **communications drift impersonate accountable notice**.
