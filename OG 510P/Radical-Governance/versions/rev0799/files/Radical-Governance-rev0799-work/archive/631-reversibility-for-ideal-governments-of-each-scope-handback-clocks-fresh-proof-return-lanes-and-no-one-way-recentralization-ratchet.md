# 631 — Reversibility for ideal governments of each scope: handback clocks, fresh proof, return lanes, and no one-way recentralization ratchet

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should treat exceptional upward concentration, distress takeover, emergency command, grant-conditioned control, and other scope shifts as **expiring claims rather than one-way ratchets**: the level that temporarily takes more power should carry a visible handback clock, a fresh-proof burden if it wants to keep the power, a capability-repair plan for the lower tier, a lawful return lane for money/staff/records/discretion, and a public explanation for any permanent reallocation.

## Why this matters

The archive now says three important things more clearly than before. It says **which scope should usually govern which kinds of problems** (`579`, `602`, `603`). It says **what honesty tests and default presumptions should apply before a scope is treated as the right home** (`628`, `629`). And it now says **which legal lane is allowed to move a function across levels and when stronger consent or constitutional depth is required** (`630`).

What it still lacked was the doctrine for the back half of the move: **once power has moved upward, especially under stress, how does the stack stop that move from hardening into permanent recentralization by inertia?**

That gap matters because many constitutions lose their multilevel honesty not through one dramatic abolition but through accumulation. A crisis triggers temporary command from above and the handback never quite happens. A distressed municipality enters special measures and spends years under supervision while the ordinary local constitution quietly atrophies. A grant program turns into the only realistic route for doing a nominally local function. A central digital platform standardizes the workflow so thoroughly that local discretion survives only in speeches. A regional ministry keeps a function because the lower tier is still “not ready,” but nobody funds the repair work that would make readiness possible.

The archive should therefore insist on a separate doctrine of **reversibility**. Good scope design is not only about initial assignment and lawful rescoping. It is also about whether the system can **hand power back down, let temporary concentration expire, and force upper tiers to re-justify retention instead of inheriting it by fatigue**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat exceptional upward control as a lease, not as title

Whenever a higher tier acquires extra authority because of emergency, distress, implementation failure, acute coordination need, or transitional incapacity, the starting presumption should be that it has acquired a **time-bounded lease of control**, not permanent title to the function.

That means the default legal and political question is not “when will the lower tier prove it deserves its powers again?” but rather “what is the exact reason the upper tier is holding them now, and what must happen for the ordinary allocation to resume?”

### 2. Flip the burden of proof after the exceptional trigger passes

A temporary upward move may begin with a lower tier’s failure or with a cross-boundary emergency. But once the trigger event stabilizes, the burden should flip. The higher tier should have to show, in public, why continued retention remains necessary.

The archive should therefore prefer a rule of **fresh proof for continued upper-tier holding**. Continued possession should not rest on the same memo, declaration, or political mood that justified the initial move.

### 3. Every temporary concentration needs a handback clock and return criteria

A visible handback clock should exist whenever powers move upward on a non-permanent basis. That clock does not have to mean a single automatic date, but it should at least specify:

- when the arrangement must be reviewed,
- what conditions count as sufficient for partial or full return,
- what conditions justify temporary renewal,
- who decides,
- and what happens if no affirmative decision is taken.

The archive should reject **silent rollover**. If no one can say when the next handback decision occurs, then the intervention is already sliding toward permanent constitutional change without owning the name.

### 4. Handback should be a capability-repair project, not only a calendar event

Lower-tier incapacity is often real. The answer is not to pretend otherwise. But a higher tier that takes powers temporarily should also acquire a duty to help make handback plausible.

That duty can include:

- repair funding,
- staffing support and training,
- records cleanup,
- system migration help,
- debt or liability triage,
- temporary shared services,
- and narrow technical assistance tied to a published restoration plan.

Otherwise the higher tier gets to say the lower tier is unready while withholding the very resources that would make readiness possible.

### 5. Return powers in typed slices, not only through all-or-nothing restoration

Many functions are bundles. Rulemaking, finance, case intake, approvals, inspection, contract management, appeals routing, and data stewardship may have been pulled upward together even though they do not all need to travel back on the same day.

The archive should therefore allow **phased handback by typed lane**. A municipality or region may recover ordinary intake, local discretionary decisions, and records custody before it recovers specialized technical review, or regain service commissioning before it resumes debt issuance. What matters is that the slice pattern be public and principled rather than improvised.

### 6. Money, staff, systems, and records should move back with the power

Reversibility is fake if the legal competence returns but the operating spine stays above. Any serious handback should therefore say what happens to:

- budget authority and revenue,
- staff and employer status,
- records and case files,
- contracts and procurement vehicles,
- digital systems and access rights,
- liabilities, reserves, and ongoing obligations,
- complaint, appeal, and audit routes,
- and public communication responsibilities.

The archive already rejects function transfer by legal text alone. It should equally reject **handback by headline alone**.

### 7. Distinguish rescue from redesign

Sometimes a temporary intervention reveals that the original allocation really was wrong. A tiny municipality may never have been a viable home for a function. A metropolitan system may be too integrated for purely municipal governance. A region may need direct election because it is already making real territorial bargains.

Where that is true, the polity should say so openly and move from rescue logic to redesign logic. It should use the proper rescoping lane, the correct proof packet, and the required consultation or consent rules.

What it should not do is leave a supposedly temporary intervention in place for years while everyone privately knows it has become the new constitution.

### 8. Grant dependence and platform dependence also need handback doctrine

Recentralization often happens without an explicit takeover. A function stays nominally local or regional, but the upper tier controls it through recurring conditional grants, benchmark approvals, mandatory templates, procurement frameworks, or one canonical case system whose defaults absorb local discretion.

The archive should treat these as **soft holdings of power**. If the center wants to keep them, it should justify them. If they were justified as temporary harmonization, capacity rescue, or transition support, they should also carry review and downgrade rules. Otherwise the polity ends up with local blame attached to centrally scripted action.

### 9. Emergency concentration should decay quickly unless re-ratified in public

Emergency command often needs sharper and faster concentration than ordinary public law should tolerate. But that is exactly why emergency concentration should have the strongest decay discipline.

The archive should therefore prefer:

- short initial terms,
- automatic review,
- written reasons for renewal,
- explicit rights and service protections during the concentration,
- and named handback or redesign decisions once ordinary governance can resume.

No multilevel stack stays honest if emergency centralization becomes the cheapest route for getting durable control.

### 10. Distress supervision should aim at restoration, not tutelage as a way of life

The note on subnational distress already says intervention should be lawful, proportionate, and temporary. Reversibility adds the broader constitutional rule: **special measures should be judged not only by whether they stop collapse, but by whether they restore ordinary self-government on a believable path**.

A supervision regime that keeps renewing because it is administratively convenient, politically useful, or risk-averse has ceased to be rescue. It has become tutelage.

### 11. Periodic scope-pruning should be normal at higher tiers

A healthy stack should periodically ask upper tiers which functions they are still holding only because they once had to. This applies to emergency agencies, central ministries, regional intermediaries, and metropolitan transitional bodies alike.

The archive should therefore treat **competence-pruning review** as normal maintenance. Upper tiers should periodically identify:

- which holdings remain justified,
- which can revert fully,
- which can revert partially,
- which require a fresh redesign route,
- and which support instruments can be simplified or removed.

Without that practice, temporary concentration becomes sediment.

### 12. Residents should be able to see both the ordinary scope and the exceptional scope

People should not have to guess whether a service, permit, emergency action, or local budget line is currently under ordinary local authority, temporary state supervision, shared command, or central override.

The archive should therefore prefer one public status record for scope exceptions that names:

- the ordinary home of the function,
- the current exceptional holder,
- the legal basis,
- the handback clock,
- the next review date,
- the restoration conditions,
- and the review or challenge route.

This keeps reversibility from becoming an internal administrative rumor.

## Reversibility ladder

| Situation | Default stance | What the higher tier must carry | What it must not become |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| emergency concentration | temporary lease of control | trigger, expiry, renewal reasons, handback or redesign decision | permanent constitution by crisis residue |
| subnational distress / special measures | restoration path | support plan, milestones, phased return, review route | tutelage without end |
| transitional central delivery because lower capacity is absent | capacity-building bridge | staffing/help systems plan, restoration budget, typed handback | “not ready yet” as a permanent excuse |
| grant- or platform-shaped control of nominally local functions | reviewable soft holding | simplification or downgrade test, local-discretion map, exit path | local blame with central scripting |
| evidence that ordinary allocation was genuinely wrong | redesign route | fresh proof, lawful rescoping lane, consultation/consent as required | pseudo-temporary intervention masking permanent reassignment |

## Guardrails

- Do not let continued central possession ride forever on the declaration that justified the first intervention.
- Do not demand that lower tiers re-earn ordinary constitutional status through perfect performance.
- Do not return legal title without returning enough money, staff, systems, and records to make the title usable.
- Do not confuse genuine redesign with endless provisional rescue.
- Do not hide central control inside grants, templates, portals, or approval queues while publicly preserving local blame.
- Do not renew special measures or emergency concentration without publishing reasons, milestones, and the next decision point.
- Do not let “capacity” arguments become self-fulfilling by refusing to fund restoration.

## Failure modes

- **one-way recentralization ratchet**: temporary upward control becomes permanent because every renewal is easier than handback.
- **handback by headline**: law says the function returned, but money, systems, staff, or review routes did not.
- **rescue without repair**: a higher tier takes control but never funds the work that would make restoration possible.
- **soft-hold centralization**: grants, templates, or software defaults keep the real discretion above even after formal handback.
- **permanent provisionalism**: everyone behaves as if a temporary arrangement is the real constitution, but nobody admits it and uses the proper redesign route.
- **risk-aversion tutelage**: supervision persists because no minister wants to be blamed for the first failed handback.
- **silent sedimentation**: upper-tier holdings accumulate over years without periodic pruning or explicit re-justification.

## Practical tests

A polity is handling reversibility honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does every temporary upward scope shift have a visible review date or handback clock?
2. After the trigger event stabilizes, does the burden shift to the higher tier to justify continued retention?
3. Is there a published capability-repair plan for the lower tier where incapacity justified the intervention?
4. Can powers return in typed slices rather than only as an all-or-nothing package?
5. Do budget, staff, records, systems, and review routes return with the function?
6. Are grant- and platform-based forms of soft central control also subject to review, downgrade, or exit rules?
7. Can the polity distinguish a genuine permanent redesign from a “temporary” intervention that has simply lasted too long?
8. Are residents and affected authorities able to see the ordinary home, the exceptional holder, the legal basis, and the next review point?
9. Is there a periodic competence-pruning habit at upper tiers?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can explain **why a power moved upward** and **which legal lane allowed the move**, but cannot also say **when the move expires, who must prove continued necessity, how restoration capability is built, and how money/staff/records/discretion return or are lawfully redesigned**, then it still has **scope doctrine without an honest doctrine of reversibility**.
