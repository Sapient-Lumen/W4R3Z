# 488 — Scope-typed governance stack: block, municipality, metro, region, nation, and planet

## One-line thesis

An ideal government is not one uniform sovereign repeated at different sizes, but a scope-typed stack in which each level is optimized for the kinds of problems it can actually see, coordinate, and correct: block for stewardship, municipality for place, metro for networks, region for landscapes and major systems, nation for rights and redistribution, and global bodies for commons, peace, and shared baselines.

## Why this matters

People live in nested geographies. A child crosses a street governed by a municipality, breathes air shaped by a metropolitan economy, drinks watershed-dependent water, relies on national rights and fiscal capacity, and inherits climate risk and pathogen exposure that no city can solve alone. Yet political design often behaves as if one scale should dominate everything.

That creates predictable pathologies. The city is too small for the housing market it is actually part of. The nation is too blunt for the block-by-block texture of public space. The global layer is expected to be a world state when many of its real successes come from narrower functions: coordination, standards, reporting, conflict restraint, or commons management.

The archive therefore needs one compact answer to the user’s question: **what would ideal governments of each scope be?** The answer is not one perfect constitution. It is a competence map.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat the polity as a nested stack of different problem classes

| Scope | Ideal institutional form | Best-fit functions | Should not monopolize |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Micro-local** (building, block, village, neighborhood, ward) | stewardship council, neighborhood assembly, block board, tribal or village institution where legitimate | shared-space stewardship, local norms, micro-safety, participatory budgeting, local care signals, small commons | constitutional rights adjudication, macro redistribution, large infrastructure finance |
| **Municipal** (town, city) | elected council plus professional administration | land use at parcel and street scale, local public realm, sanitation, libraries, permitting, primary civic services, direct service design | labor-market-wide housing balance, regional transit trunks, river-basin management, macro stabilization |
| **Metropolitan / county / functional urban area** | directly elected metro authority or hard law inter-municipal body | transport networks, housing allocation across the labor market, major water/waste systems, emergency coordination, spatial planning across municipal borders, air-quality management | nationwide redistribution, constitutional rights floors, foreign policy |
| **Regional / provincial / state / bioregional** | federal state, province, region, watershed authority, rural-region body | watershed and ecosystem management, major hospitals, grid and water-basin planning, tertiary education systems, regional development, disaster logistics | fine-grained street management, local civic trust work, global treaty setting |
| **National** | constitutional state with legislature, courts, executive, and equal-citizenship guarantees | rights floors, macroeconomic stabilization, major redistribution, citizenship, migration regime, competition policy, nationwide infrastructure, defense, national risk pooling | parcel-level place design, metro network detail, local culture-setting except where rights are at stake |
| **Plurilateral / continental** | treaty union, common market, security compact, basin or grid community | cross-border infrastructure, mobility frameworks, market standards, regional research and industrial policy, shared environmental baselines, treaty courts where agreed | ordinary municipal service delivery, world-state ambitions detached from member consent |
| **Global** | narrow-waist multilateral institutions, treaties, registries, courts, and coordination bodies | climate ceilings, pandemic notification and coordination, peace and security restraint, ocean and space rules, core technical standards, global transparency baselines | ordinary local administration, detailed domestic welfare design, one-size-fits-all implementation |

### 2. Give micro-local institutions stewardship, not feudal sovereignty

The smallest scope is where lived knowledge is thickest. It is good at:

- reading local conditions quickly,
- maintaining shared spaces and micro-commons,
- convening neighbors,
- surfacing needs early,
- and creating legitimacy through proximity.

It is bad at guaranteeing equal floors on its own. The ideal micro-local institution is therefore strong in stewardship and voice, but nested inside wider rights and fiscal frameworks.

### 3. Treat the municipality as the keeper of place

Cities and towns should own the visible, daily, concrete layer of government: streets, permits, schools where applicable, libraries, public realm, neighborhood service design, and the user-facing texture of public administration. This is where public trust is often won or lost.

### 4. Treat the metro as the keeper of systems that outrun city hall

Housing markets, commute sheds, transport trunks, sewerage, logistics, and air pollution do not stop at municipal borders. OECD work has repeatedly shown that fragmented metropolitan governance imposes productivity costs and that metropolitan coordination can reduce the penalty from administrative fragmentation. Metropolitan government should therefore be treated as a normal tier where functional urban systems exist, not as an embarrassing exception.

### 5. Treat regions as the scale for landscapes, basins, and major service platforms

A good region is not just a bigger city. It is the level where watersheds, ecosystems, hospital networks, grid corridors, and rural-urban development strategies can be governed without forcing every problem either downward into local incapacity or upward into national abstraction.

### 6. Treat the nation as the guarantor of equal citizenship and shared risk

The national level is where a polity is usually best able to sustain:

- constitutional rights,
- universal social floors,
- large insurance pools,
- anti-monopoly rules,
- currency and macro stabilization where sovereign,
- redistribution across rich and poor places,
- and common defense.

The ideal nation is strong where solidarity and equal standing require scale, but disciplined enough not to erase lower-level problem-solving.

### 7. Keep continental and plurilateral layers narrow, strong, and problem-specific

Many cross-border functions do not need a full superstate. They need well-scoped treaty machinery: mobility rules, common standards, basin governance, cross-border grids, research coordination, or regional courts for agreed domains. These layers work best when their mandates are precise and their democratic chain is legible.

### 8. Keep the global layer focused on commons, peace, and narrow-waist coordination

Global governance works best when it governs what is truly global:

- climate mitigation ceilings and reporting,
- pandemic notification and coordinated response,
- war restraint and peace-security machinery,
- oceans, polar regions, outer space, and other shared domains,
- and narrow technical coordination where the world needs one interoperable baseline.

The ideal global layer is therefore not a maximal world state. It is a sparse but serious architecture for problems no lower level can solve alone.

### 9. Preserve overlap honestly where geography is not neatly nested

Real systems overlap. A watershed may cut across several provinces. A labor market may span many municipalities. An indigenous governance system may not map cleanly onto municipal or state borders. The ideal archive should therefore prefer **declared overlap** to fake nesting.

## Guardrails

- Do not use municipal boundaries as the planning boundary for metropolitan systems.
- Do not let national uniformity erase legitimate local variation in place-making.
- Do not confuse regional ecological governance with ordinary political gigantism.
- Do not ask global institutions to administer ordinary domestic life.
- Do not hide overlapping authority; name it and govern it.

## Failure modes

- **metro blindness**: cities govern only themselves while the real housing and transport system sprawls beyond them.
- **nation-scale overreach**: the center absorbs place-sensitive questions it cannot see well.
- **local romanticism**: small units are treated as morally sufficient even when they cannot fund or coordinate the common good.
- **global maximalism**: planetary institutions are asked to perform functions better left lower down.
- **border fiction**: governance sticks to inherited lines after the real functional geography has moved.

## Practical tests

A scope-typed stack passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does each major public function sit at the scale where its information and externalities actually live?
2. Is metropolitan governance present where functional urban systems cross city boundaries?
3. Are ecological and major-service systems given a regional or basin-scale home where needed?
4. Does the nation clearly protect equal citizenship, redistribution, and macro risk sharing?
5. Are global institutions kept narrow and serious rather than vague and omnivorous?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a government assigns **street design, labor markets, watersheds, rights floors, and climate stability** to the same scale by default, it is not practicing federalism or subsidiarity; it is practicing **scope blindness**.
