# 579 — Scope allocation by level: intimacy, spillovers, coercion, solidarity, infrastructure, commons, and no government by level confusion

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should allocate functions by a compact **scope-allocation grammar** rather than by inherited prestige or map inertia: keep voice, stewardship, and context-heavy discretion as close to people as possible; shift authority upward when spillovers outrun local borders, when network infrastructures require shared operation, when equal-rights floors and due-process guarantees require stronger uniformity, when risk-pooling and redistribution need a wider base, and when cross-border commons or security problems cannot be solved domestically; and treat continental and global layers as **narrow-waist coordination regimes** for shared problems rather than as imitation nation-states.

## Why this matters

The archive already has a strong note on **ideal constitutional kits by scope** and another on **electoral systems by scope**. What it still lacked was the missing middle question: **how should an ideal polity decide which public powers belong at which scope in the first place?**

That gap matters because governments often inherit level assignments from old bargains, colonial residues, party convenience, or administrative habit. The result is a recurring pattern of level confusion. Neighborhood bodies are asked to solve structural inequality without money. Municipalities are blamed for housing or transport systems that operate at metropolitan scale. Regions are created without real functions. Nations hoard powers that need local adaptation while dumping unfunded mandates downward. Continental and global institutions are then alternately condemned for doing too much or mocked for being too weak, even when the real design error is that nobody decided clearly which cross-border tasks truly require a higher layer.

A more serious constitutional design starts with a small number of allocation tests. The ideal question is not “which level sounds important enough?” It is: **where does this function become most democratically reachable, operationally competent, fiscally supportable, rights-compatible, and systemically honest?**

This note therefore gives the archive a compact grammar for assigning powers across micro-local, municipal, metropolitan, regional, national, continental, and global layers. It is not a formula that erases politics. It is a discipline for making level choices explicit, reviewable, and contestable.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate the constitutional kit question from the competence-allocation question

A good government at any scale needs some combination of forum, executive capacity, finance, oversight, and participation. But that does not answer which functions should live there. The archive should therefore keep two questions separate:

1. what institutional kit a level needs if it exists,
2. and what powers should be assigned to that level at all.

Confusing those questions is one reason constitutions bloat. Designers copy a legislature, executive, and budget template onto a level first, then invent powers to justify the furniture. The archive should reverse that logic. **First assign the function honestly; then size the institutional kit to the function.**

### 2. Use the intimacy test: keep voice, trust, upkeep, and local knowledge close to the people affected

Some functions improve when they stay near lived reality: neighborhood upkeep, local commons, minor public-space norms, community mediation, routine service navigation, local memory, local error surfacing, and the practical noticing of things that have broken or been ignored. These are tasks where tacit knowledge, repeated contact, and social reach matter more than large scale.

The archive should therefore prefer the smallest workable scope for functions that depend on proximity, trust, and context. That usually means micro-local or municipal ownership, with higher levels funding, auditing, or backstopping rather than swallowing the function. The point is not romantic localism. It is to keep problems legible before they become abstracted into a distant case number.

### 3. Use the spillover test: move authority upward when the real system outruns the political boundary

Many failures of government are really boundary failures. Housing markets, commuting patterns, air quality, flood basins, freight corridors, emergency response, hospital catchments, labor markets, and waste systems often span multiple municipalities. Where the functional geography is larger than the existing jurisdiction, local autonomy alone produces fragmentation, veto games, and invisible costs shifted onto neighbors.

The archive should therefore move powers upward when spillovers are large and persistent. Metropolitan or regional levels are usually the right home for functions that require shared urban-system decisions, corridor planning, basin governance, or coordinated infrastructure operation. A city should not be forced to solve a metro problem with municipal tools, and a nation should not treat every metropolitan externality as a reason for central micromanagement.

### 4. Use the coercion-and-rights test: the harder the coercion, the stronger the law-bound safeguards and floors

Some powers are intrusive enough that variation cannot be left mainly to local habit. Arrest powers, detention, criminal charging, serious surveillance, taxation rules, core civil status, elections, nondiscrimination guarantees, and procedural fairness norms all require stronger safeguards than a purely local discretion model can usually provide.

The archive should therefore prefer wider and more law-bound allocation for powers that can seriously constrain liberty, alter legal status, or erode equal citizenship. This does **not** mean every coercive function must be hyper-centralized operationally. It means the constitutional floor, appeal structure, legality controls, and rights guarantees generally need to sit at regional, national, or otherwise stronger law-owning levels. Local execution may remain appropriate, but arbitrary localism is not.

### 5. Use the solidarity test: social insurance, equalization, and shock absorption need a wider pool than daily local politics can support

A central design error in many polities is expecting small tax bases to carry large welfare promises, disaster recovery, or macro shocks on their own. The smaller the unit, the more exposed it is to volatile revenue, local elite capture, and postcode lotteries in basic protection.

The archive should therefore reserve the heaviest solidarity functions for wider fiscal communities. Income redistribution, pensions, unemployment protection, large equalization transfers, disaster backstops, and macroeconomic stabilization usually belong at national or federal scale, sometimes with regional co-financing. Local government can deliver, tailor, and supplement services, but it should not be left alone to insure people against systemic risk.

### 6. Use the infrastructure-and-platform test: shared networks need the scope that matches their topology

Some public systems are not primarily about solidarity or coercion. They are about interoperable operation: transit networks, electricity grids, bulk water, digital identity frameworks, cadastral reference layers, backbone roads, telecommunications coordination, laboratory networks, and other systems whose value depends on continuity across nodes.

The archive should therefore assign infrastructure and platform authority to the scope that matches the network’s actual footprint. Neighborhoods and municipalities can own local nodes and street-level delivery. Metropolitan and regional bodies often need to run integrated networks. Nations usually need to set reference standards, financing frameworks, interconnection rules, and long-horizon resilience obligations. The design mistake is to confuse local access points with network governance, or national standards with everyday operating control.

### 7. Use the commons-and-externalities test: cross-border harms require bounded higher-layer coordination

Climate, infectious-disease alerting, migratory species, shared seas, aviation and shipping interoperability, arms control, and some financial or digital standards cannot be solved by one municipality, one region, or even one nation acting alone. Yet that does not imply a general world domestic government.

The archive should therefore treat continental and global institutions as **problem-specific narrow waists**. Their job is to provide treaty frameworks, common standards, inspection or reporting mechanisms, scientific panels, alerting systems, and bounded compliance machinery where cross-border externalities are real. Their job is not to absorb all ordinary domestic administration. Higher-layer legitimacy grows when mandates stay narrow enough to match the actual problem.

### 8. Use the capability-presumption rule: powers should sit at the closest level that can actually discharge them, but incapacity must trigger visible backstop rather than silent abandonment

The archive should reject both magical subsidiarity and magical centralism. Some functions belong close to people in principle, but only if capacity, funding, and legal support exist. If the lower tier lacks those things, the answer is not to pretend the function is local while leaving failure in place.

A better rule is **closest capable level, with visible backstop and restoration path**. Higher tiers may temporarily assume, co-produce, or heavily support a function, but they should name the reason, the missing capability, the financing fix, and the route back to durable local or regional competence where appropriate. Otherwise decentralization becomes blame-shifting and centralization becomes permanent convenience.

## Scope table

| Allocation test | Default scope tendency | Typical examples | What higher levels still do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Intimacy / local knowledge** | micro-local or municipal | neighborhood upkeep, community mediation, local public-space stewardship, routine service navigation | funding floors, audit, legal remedies, equal-access rules |
| **Functional spillovers** | metropolitan or regional | transit systems, housing-market balancing, flood basins, corridor planning, regional waste systems | statutory powers, equalization, appellate and integrity review |
| **Coercion / rights floors** | regional or national law-owning level, often with local execution | policing rules, detention standards, election integrity, civil status, due-process codes, nondiscrimination | constitutional review, inspection, appeals, training, data standards |
| **Solidarity / insurance** | national or federal | pensions, unemployment protection, major redistribution, disaster equalization, macro stabilization | delivery partnerships downward, targeted supplements, local access points |
| **Shared infrastructure / platforms** | scope matched to network footprint | grids, bulk water, backbone transport, interoperable registries, identity trust frameworks | capital frameworks, interoperability rules, resilience and contingency obligations |
| **Commons / transboundary externalities** | continental or global narrow waist | climate reporting and coordination, disease alerting, oceans, aviation, shipping, some digital and trade standards | domestic implementation, democratic scrutiny through member polities |

### 9. The ideal political shape of each scope follows from the dominant test it passes

Once powers are allocated honestly, the ideal government at each scope becomes easier to state compactly:

- **Micro-local** government should be a voice-and-stewardship layer: small budgets, intense legibility, escalation rights, and local commons care.
- **Municipal** government should be the first full-spectrum everyday state: land use, streets, sanitation, permitting, local policing interfaces, libraries, parks, and practical service delivery.
- **Metropolitan** government should be the shared-systems state: transport, housing balance, functional economic geography, resilience, and land-infrastructure coordination across the real city-region.
- **Regional / provincial / state** government should be the territorial-platform state: hospitals, higher education, basin and corridor systems, serious environmental governance, and federal or plurinational shared rule.
- **National** government should be the rights-and-solidarity state: constitutional guarantees, equal citizenship floors, treasury, redistribution, macro stabilization, and nation-scale regulators.
- **Continental** government should be the bounded cross-border rule-and-coordination layer for mobility, markets, shared infrastructure, rights cooperation, and some regional commons.
- **Global** government should remain a narrow-waist treaty layer for peace, planetary commons, alerting, standards, and inspection where the problem truly outruns states.

### 10. Use floors, frameworks, and review to align levels without erasing discretion

A good multi-level constitution does not choose between total localism and total hierarchy. It usually combines:

- lower-level discretion on context-heavy execution,
- higher-level rights floors and minimum standards,
- formula finance and equalization,
- interoperable data and reporting rails,
- appeal and judicial review,
- and explicit consultation when one level’s choices materially burden another.

The archive should therefore prefer **layered authority** over level absolutism. A nation may set the nondiscrimination rule, a region may run the inspection and appeal machinery, a municipality may deliver the service, and a neighborhood body may notice first that the service is failing. What matters is that the chain is legible and the burden-shifting is governable.

## Guardrails

- Do not assign powers by prestige, symbolism, or map nostalgia alone.
- Do not keep a function local when the real spillovers are metropolitan, regional, or cross-border.
- Do not centralize context-heavy everyday functions merely because the center distrusts variation.
- Do not leave coercive powers highly fragmented without strong rights floors, appeal routes, and legality controls.
- Do not fund solidarity promises from tiny tax bases and then blame local officials for structural insolvency.
- Do not confuse ownership of a local node with authority over the whole network it depends on.
- Do not answer every global coordination problem with either world-state fantasy or nationalist denial.
- Do not call a level “subsidiary” or “decentralized” if it carries responsibility without capacity, revenue, or legal recourse.

## Failure modes

- **government by level confusion**: powers are assigned by habit, prestige, or historical residue rather than by problem fit.
- **postcode solidarity collapse**: basic protection varies wildly because too much redistribution is left to weak local bases.
- **metro orphaning**: real city-region systems exist, but no level has democratic authority to govern them.
- **rights fragmentation**: coercive powers vary so widely that equal citizenship depends on where a person happens to live.
- **network mirage**: local operators exist, but no one governs the shared infrastructure as a system.
- **subsidiarity theatre**: leaders praise local control while withholding the money, staff, or legal room needed to exercise it.
- **central convenience capture**: higher levels keep powers that could be handled closer to people because central retention is politically easier.
- **world-ministry fantasy**: transboundary problems are used to justify institutions with mandates far wider than their democratic or legal basis.

## Practical tests

A scope allocation is constitutionally serious when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. For each major function, is there a visible reason why it sits at this level rather than one level lower or higher?
2. Have intimacy, spillovers, coercion, solidarity, infrastructure topology, and cross-border externalities been considered separately rather than collapsed into one vague subsidiarity slogan?
3. Does the chosen level actually have the money, staff, legal authority, and review machinery needed to perform the function?
4. Where the function remains local or regional, are there still rights floors, appeal routes, and equalization mechanisms strong enough to protect equal citizenship?
5. Where the function moves upward, is the mandate narrow enough that the higher layer is solving a real coordination problem rather than collecting decorative sovereignty?
6. Can the polity explain what discretion remains below, what standards sit above, and how conflicts are resolved when levels disagree?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can list its levels of government but cannot explain **why this function lives here, what test it passed, what level would own it if the spillovers, risks, or solidarity demands were different, and what review path exists when the assignment fails**, then it still has **jurisdictional furniture without a scope-allocation constitution**.
