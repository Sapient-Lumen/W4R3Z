# 570 — Legal-entity, business, and beneficial-ownership registries by scope: community witnessing, municipal entry desks, regional coordination, national public truth, and no government by paper shell

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat organizational identity as constitutional infrastructure rather than clerical residue: communities can help witness real economic and associational life, municipalities or counties should usually provide the reachable entry desk for ordinary business-facing registrations and permits, regional or state layers should coordinate where federal structure or market geography requires it, national or constitutional-state law should own the authoritative registry of legal persons and the rules for unique identifiers, lifecycle events, disclosure, and strike-off, beneficial ownership should be held through a public authority or tightly governed equivalent with rapid lawful access and strong update discipline, and continental or global bodies should provide interoperability rails rather than pretending there can be one sovereign company register for the world.

## Why this matters

The archive now has compact constitutional notes for **civil registration and legal identity**, **land administration and cadastre**, **addresses and administrative geography**, **official statistics**, **procurement**, **anti-corruption**, **markets and company power**, and **digital public infrastructure**. What it still lacked was the parallel institutional layer for **organizations**: how a polity knows that a firm, association, cooperative, charity, partnership, branch, or other legal person exists; which public office makes that existence authoritative; which identifiers persist across systems; how mergers, insolvency, liquidation, suspension, and strike-off are recorded; and how the state can tell who ultimately controls a legal person when corruption, sanctions evasion, procurement abuse, or financial crime risks are at stake.

That gap matters because many governance failures are really failures of lawful organizational identity. A supplier wins contracts under one spelling in procurement, another in tax, and a third in licensing. A company dissolved years ago still appears live in one agency file. A local permit is mistaken for proof of legal personality. A beneficial owner hides behind shell layers while each office holds only a fragment of the picture. A national statistical office cannot align enterprise data because the constitutive register, tax register, and field reality drift apart. The result is not merely administrative untidiness. It is a state that cannot reliably say **who is acting, who controls them, and which obligations follow them across the public system**.

The correct archive stance is therefore **one constitutive truth for legal persons, many linked operational uses**. The firm-facing front door should be reachable, but the public should not have to guess whether the “real” business exists in the chamber-of-commerce file, the municipal permit desk, the tax number list, the procurement vendor table, the banking KYC packet, or a private data broker’s lookup service.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Let the smallest scale surface lived enterprise reality, but do not confuse community witnessing with sovereign incorporation

At the smallest scale, people often know something higher tiers do not: which market traders actually operate, which cooperative really exists, which dormant company is only a paper shell, which local association is active, which business address is fictitious, which manager is the practical point of contact, and which informal enterprise cluster deserves a path into legality rather than harassment. Ideal government should use that local knowledge.

The archive should therefore prefer designs in which neighborhoods, villages, business associations, worker organizations, cooperatives, and community institutions can help surface real operating entities, propose corrections, flag ghost firms, verify local service addresses, and identify where formal records have drifted away from lived economic reality. This is especially important in informal settlements, rural areas, post-conflict recovery, market-town economies, indigenous territories, and places where paper registration historically excluded real producers.

But the archive should reject the idea that every neighborhood, market committee, chamber, or municipality should each maintain its own sovereign corporate truth. Community witnessing should improve reality-testing, not create overlapping constitutive authorities.

### 2. Municipal or county government should usually be the public-facing entry desk for ordinary business interaction, permits, and corrections near where firms actually operate

For many people, “business registration” becomes real not at the constitutional center but at the reachable front desk: local trade permits, occupancy checks, food-service licenses, market-space assignments, signage permissions, inspection scheduling, local tax accounts, and help navigating incorporation or nonprofit filing. That makes municipalities or counties the ordinary human interface for enterprise formalization.

The archive should therefore prefer municipal or county responsibility for business-facing intake and service where proximity matters: guidance for new firms, local permits, local regulatory approvals, address confirmation, local correction routes, and visible help for entrepreneurs, associations, and small producers. A person should not have to climb to the national capital merely to learn which local approvals they need or to correct an obvious public-facing business record error.

But this reachable front door should not silently become the constitutive source of legal personality. A municipal permit, occupancy certificate, or trading licence may authorize activity. It should not be mistaken for the sovereign act that creates a company, determines directors, or settles ownership and control.

### 3. Regional, provincial, or state layers should coordinate shared registry operations where federal structure, legal competence, or market geography require more than one city at a time

Some polities place company law and registry operations at the state, provincial, or regional level rather than the national whole. Others need a regional layer because one functional business area spans many municipalities, because branch registration and insolvency work require pooled capacity, or because local fragmentation would otherwise force firms to repeat identity proofs and updates in every neighboring jurisdiction.

The archive should therefore prefer regional or constitutional-state coordination where law or practical scale demands it: shared registry platforms in federations, common service standards, branch and merger coordination, cross-municipal deduplication, sector licensing with real territorial spillovers, and appeals or supervision where local capacity is too thin or capture risks are too high.

The point is not that every federation must run one unitary registry from the center. It is that whatever level lawfully owns incorporation and organizational status should act as an authoritative public institution, and lower tiers should not force businesses into repetitive, contradictory identity rituals.

### 4. National or constitutional-state law should own the authoritative registry of legal persons, their identifiers, lifecycle events, and basic public facts

A polity needs one authoritative logic for how organizations become governable public facts. Usually that belongs at the national level — or, in federations, at the constitutional state or province that actually owns the relevant law. This level should govern the constitutive register: who may incorporate or register a legal person, what minimum information is required, which unique identifier is assigned, what counts as a merger or dissolution, how directors or authorized representatives are recorded, what must be public, and how strike-off, insolvency, and reinstatement work.

The archive should therefore prefer a public registry architecture that makes basic organizational truth durable and searchable: unique entity identifiers, status history, legal form, registered address, authorized representatives where lawful, branch relationships, filing obligations, major lifecycle events, and machine-readable publication of basic facts. The public should be able to learn whether an entity exists, what sort of entity it is, whether it is live or dissolved, and which identifier allows reliable linkage across the rest of the state.

The point is not maximal centralization of every business-facing step. It is that the public and the state should not have to guess whether the real company exists in a notarial filing, a tax-system extract, a private registry monopoly, or a procurement vendor table.

### 5. Beneficial ownership should sit with or be tightly linked to a public authority that can ensure adequate, accurate, up-to-date information and rapid lawful access

Basic company facts are not enough where corruption, sanctions evasion, organized crime, procurement abuse, extractive opacity, or financial-market risk require the state to know who ultimately owns or controls a legal person. Yet beneficial ownership systems fail when they are built as decorative spreadsheets, fragmented across agencies, or treated as purely private due-diligence artifacts.

The archive should therefore prefer a beneficial-ownership architecture in which information is held by a public authority or body functioning as a beneficial-ownership registry, or through an alternative mechanism that still ensures rapid and efficient lawful access, strong update duties, and real verification logic. Multi-pronged designs are preferable to magical thinking: information from companies themselves should be joined with registry data, competent-authority access, and other public or regulated information sources rather than trusted merely because it was declared once.

This does not mean every ownership detail must be displayed identically to every member of the public in every jurisdiction. It does mean the state should be able to answer, lawfully and quickly, **who owns whom**, and procurement, anti-corruption, tax, and enforcement systems should not have to rediscover ultimate control from scratch each time.

### 6. Statistical business registers, tax systems, procurement systems, and sector regulators should link to the constitutive register, not compete with it

A serious polity needs many organization-related registers. Statistical business registers support economic statistics. Tax administrations maintain taxpayer records. Procurement systems keep supplier tables. Sector regulators license firms in finance, telecoms, health, transport, and other domains. Charity or association registries may track special legal forms. But these should be linked uses of organizational identity, not rival sovereign truths.

The archive should therefore prefer identifier-based linkage with clear custodianship. The constitutive registry should say that a legal person exists, what type it is, and what its core status is. Tax administrations should govern tax obligations. Statistical offices should maintain statistical business registers and frames fit for economic measurement, often using administrative sources. Procurement and sectoral regulators should manage eligibility and compliance within their domains. Good design joins these systems without pretending one operational register should silently replace the others.

### 7. Above the nation-state, prefer interoperability rails and cross-border exchange over fantasies of a world company ministry

Cross-border commerce needs more than domestic company law. Firms operate internationally, branches cross borders, supply chains span jurisdictions, and regulators need ways to match organizations without fragile text guessing. This is where continental and global layers matter.

The archive should therefore prefer international and regional roles built around **interoperability, common identifiers, open access to basic reference data, and lawful register-to-register exchange**. Legal-entity identifier systems, regional interconnection of business registers, common code sets, and standards for machine-readable organizational identity are the right kind of global help. What the archive should reject is the fantasy that one world registry could simply override domestic law, federal competence, public-interest disclosure rules, and constitutional choices about legal forms.

International layers should be narrow waists for cross-border intelligibility. They should not impersonate the sovereign act that creates or dissolves a legal person inside a polity.

## Guardrails

- Do not let every municipality, chamber, notary, or sector portal become its own sovereign source of company truth.
- Do not confuse local permits, tax accounts, or procurement onboarding with the constitutive act that creates legal personality.
- Do not force firms to repeat the same identity update across many public systems when one authoritative register should propagate the change.
- Do not treat beneficial ownership as a decorative self-declaration with no update discipline, no verification logic, and no rapid lawful access.
- Do not collapse statistical, tax, procurement, regulatory, and constitutive registers into one uncontrolled mega-database with unclear custodianship.
- Do not hide basic organizational existence behind private data monopolies, paywalls, or gatekeeper franchises when public trust depends on accessible core facts.
- Do not pretend global interoperability rails can replace domestic company law or constitutional allocation of registry authority.

## Failure modes

- **paper shell government**: a legal person exists on paper but no public institution can reliably say who controls it, whether it is active, or which obligations still attach.
- **permit-as-incorporation**: local trading permission or sectoral licensing is mistaken for proof that a legal entity has been lawfully constituted.
- **identifier fracture**: tax, procurement, banking, statistics, and regulatory systems each invent separate identifiers and rely on fuzzy name matching instead of one stable entity reference.
- **dead-company drift**: dissolved, struck-off, merged, or insolvent entities remain “alive” in some public systems and dead in others.
- **registry capture by gatekeeper**: a chamber, notary, contractor, or private platform becomes the practical sovereign over business existence without equivalent public accountability.
- **ownership fog**: the state can see nominal shareholders or directors but not ultimate ownership or control when risk or law requires it.
- **one-stop-shop theater**: a polished business portal hides fragmented authority beneath it, so the public thinks there is one lawful register when there are really many disconnected ones.
- **world-register fantasy**: international interoperability projects are asked to settle domestic questions of legal form, ownership law, and constitutive authority that only domestic law can settle.

## Practical tests

A constitutional organizational-identity layer is scale-matched and publicly usable when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can an entrepreneur, association, or cooperative get real help through a reachable local or municipal entry desk without that local desk pretending to be the sovereign source of legal personality?
2. Does the polity have one authoritative constitutive registry for legal persons at the level that lawfully owns company or association status?
3. Are unique identifiers strong enough that tax, procurement, statistics, regulatory, and financial-integrity systems can link entities without fragile text matching?
4. Can the state record mergers, branch relationships, dissolution, insolvency, and reinstatement in ways that propagate reliably across public systems?
5. Where beneficial ownership matters, can competent authorities rapidly obtain adequate, accurate, and up-to-date information through a public authority or tightly governed equivalent?
6. Above the nation-state, do interoperability systems improve cross-border intelligibility without pretending to become a world sovereign over company existence?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can tell you that a business has a licence, tax number, bank account, procurement profile, or physical storefront but cannot tell you **which public register makes it a legal person, which identifier persists across systems, what its current status is, and who ultimately controls it when law requires an answer**, then it still has **economic activity without a constitutional organizational-identity layer**.
