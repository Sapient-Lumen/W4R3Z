# 823 — Contracting-boundary-chain templates for cross-boundary scope forms: light-support buying, principal-owned commissioning, chartered concessions, change control, and no heavy power by contract escape hatch

## One-line thesis

When the archive has already established that a cross-boundary problem is real, chosen the honest form, and said who governs, pays, operates, votes, hears disputes, owns the assets, and can unwind the map, it should still require one compact **contracting-boundary chain**: thin connectors may buy only light support and must not become hidden commissioners of core public systems; shared-service backbones may buy pooled administrative tools and support services while political title remains visibly with the principals; bounded functional authorities may procure, franchise, or concession the named system they are chartered to govern, but only through a visible commissioner spine, hard change-control, and full public review; and general-purpose territorial tiers may use contracts as delivery instruments without letting procurement, concessions, PPPs, or managed-service deals become constitutional escape hatches for powers that should remain politically owned.

## Why this matters

`790` gives the archive a short answer key for what ideal governments at each scope should generally be. `810` says how to assemble threshold proof before saying that a metropolitan, pooling, regional-platform, fiscal-floor, or legitimacy-thickness line is really crossed. `812` says which institutional form is honest once that burden is real. `813` says who should sit in the room and which decisions require which legitimacy key. `814` says what money constitution belongs to the form. `815` says where the operator, employer, records home, and continuity spine should live. `819` says how territory and membership should be entered, changed, exited, and unwound. `820` says what vote, quorum, ratification, deadlock, and emergency grammar belongs to the body. `821` says how disputes should actually travel once the body is real. `822` says who owns the assets, carries the liabilities, and takes the residue if the body changes shape.

The archive also already has older procurement, concession, and anti-escape-hatch groundwork in `544`, `563`, `611`, `619`, `685`, and `688`.

What the recent scope stack still lacked was one compact answer to the next question:

**even after form, legitimacy, finance, administration, map, votes, disputes, and assets are typed, when is contracting still an honest delivery tool and when is it becoming the hidden constitution?**

That gap matters because constitutional dishonesty often hides not in the charter or the budget, but in the commercial perimeter.

One failure is **commissioner blur**: a thin connector or shared-service backbone quietly becomes the real governor of a field because it is the body letting, extending, varying, and rescuing consequential contracts even though the archive still describes it as merely co-ordinative or administrative.

Another failure is **procurement innocence theater**: an arrangement is described as if it has no real public power because it lacks broad tax or rulemaking authority, while in practice it decides the service scope, supplier mix, pricing structure, data rights, performance regime, and capital timing through concessions, PPPs, or managed-service agreements.

A third failure is **change-order government**: the initial public decision looks bounded, but amendments, waivers, extensions, and rescue deals later alter the economic balance, scope, and political effect of the arrangement without any honest reapproval, recompetition, or public explanation.

A fourth failure is **contractual subsidiarity laundering**: a body claims that it has not thickened constitutionally because delivery remains outsourced, franchised, or concessioned, even though the public choices hidden inside those agreements are exactly the ones that would have triggered stronger legitimacy, oversight, fiscal, and publication rules if exercised directly.

Official material supports a more disciplined chain. The OECD Recommendation on Public Procurement provides a holistic framework covering the whole procurement cycle and is built around transparency, integrity, stakeholder participation, efficiency, evaluation, risk management, accountability, and integration. The OECD’s 2025 implementation report adds that procurement risk exists over the life of the contract or procured asset, not only at tender stage, and stresses the need for quality data across the whole procurement lifecycle. OECD work on inter-municipal shared services likewise insists that shared-service agreements specify functions, responsibilities, structure, cost sharing, duration, and termination. EU procurement and concession law draw a hard line around substantial modifications, shifts in economic balance, major scope extensions, and termination triggers. World Bank PPP guidance treats contract management, change, expiry, and asset handover as standing public functions rather than after-award housekeeping, and its disclosure framework calls for systematic proactive disclosure across PPP lifecycles. The archive should compress those lessons into one rule: **do not let public power become heavier in contract than it is in constitutional text.**

## Pattern pack

### 1. Use this note after form, money, operator, map, vote, dispute, and asset questions are separated

This note does not decide whether a metropolitan, county-pooling, regional-platform, fiscal-floor, or legitimacy-thickness threshold has been crossed.

Use `810` for that.

This note does not decide whether the honest answer is a thin connector, shared-service backbone, bounded functional authority, or general-purpose territorial tier.

Use `812` for that.

This note does not decide what revenue or reserve chain funds the body.

Use `814` for that.

This note does not decide who operates the machinery, employs the staff, or keeps the records home.

Use `815` for that.

This note does not decide what vote or dispute route belongs to the body.

Use `820` and `821` for that.

This note does not decide who owns strategic assets, carries liabilities, or takes the handback residue.

Use `822` for that.

This note also does not decide whether the body may merely align standards and route cases, may pool permit and inspection support while principals keep legal title, or may hold its own openly bounded licensing, inspection, and sanction powers.

Use `824` for that.

This note applies after those questions are separated and the remaining live issue is:

- who is allowed to buy, commission, franchise, concession, or outsource in the body’s name,
- what kinds of contracts the form may honestly carry,
- what remains a politically owned decision even if delivery is external,
- what amendment, variation, extension, and rescue discipline protects the original public settlement,
- and when contracting stops being a delivery technique and starts functioning as hidden constitutional thickening.

### 2. Distinguish four recurrent contracting-boundary chains before inventing hybrids

The archive should usually begin with four recognizable chains.

| Form | Default contracting-boundary chain | What it may legitimately carry | What it must not hide |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Thin connector** | light-support buying only, usually for meetings, studies, publication, and minor secretariat tools | facilitation, analytics support, shared communications, modest advisory work | commissioning of core service systems, long concessions, or supplier-led policy |
| **Shared-service backbone** | principal-owned commissioning for pooled support services and common administrative infrastructure | shared IT, payroll, case handling, procurement support, specialist back-office services, bounded managed services | covert transfer of political title or system-wide service sovereignty through contract |
| **Bounded functional authority** | visible commissioner spine for one named system, with lawful procurement, franchises, concessions, or PPP-style tools inside that remit | network operation, maintenance, fleet or plant stewardship, ticketing or platform services, bounded concessions, lifecycle contracts | drift into general-purpose government by commercial perimeter |
| **General-purpose territorial tier** | full public procurement constitution with elevated treatment for constitutionally weighty contracts | wide contract portfolio across services and capital works, category strategy, ordinary outsourcing where lawful, selective concessions or PPPs | privatization of core territorial choices or hiding major public power in vendor documents |

The archive should not begin with bespoke delivery poetry until one of these four chains has first been ruled out honestly.

### 3. Thin connectors may buy support, but must not become hidden commissioners

A thin connector is honest only while the body remains mainly connective.

Its contracting-boundary chain should therefore stay extremely light.

That usually means:

- it may buy facilitation, studies, modest software, publication, and meeting support;
- it may co-ordinate a joint market scan or template work for member governments;
- it may help prepare a future joint procurement without yet being the constitutionally decisive buyer;
- it should not let core service contracts, long franchises, concessions, or managed-service monopolies in its own name;
- and it should not use commercial urgency to smuggle binding choices past the member governments that still hold the political title.

The archive should reject the move where a supposedly light forum claims it is “only procuring support” while in fact it is defining the service regime itself.

### 4. Shared-service backbones may commission pooled support, while principals keep visible title to the duty

A shared-service backbone exists because several governments keep the political title to a function while pooling administrative machinery, specialist staff, digital tools, procurement capacity, or support-service delivery.

Its contracting-boundary chain should therefore usually begin from a presumption of **principal-owned public duty with bounded pooled commissioning**.

That commonly means:

- member governments or a clearly principal-owned common vehicle remain visibly accountable for the public duty;
- the backbone may let contracts for common administrative infrastructure, specialist services, processing support, and bounded managed services;
- major service-policy choices, coercive decisions, distributive choices, and constitutional changes do not disappear into the managed-service agreement;
- shared-service agreements and buying authorities are typed in advance rather than improvised by host convenience;
- and duration, variation, exit, records, continuity, and liability hooks are known before pooled buying begins.

The archive should allow real pooled buying here. It should reject the pattern where the backbone quietly becomes the sovereign because all indispensable contracts sit with the host or common operator while the principals retain only ceremonial accountability.

### 5. Bounded functional authorities may use stronger contract tools, but only inside a named public remit

A bounded functional authority becomes the honest answer when one cross-boundary system requires continuous operation, asset stewardship, or a real commissioner for a named field.

Its contracting-boundary chain may therefore be substantially heavier than a backbone’s.

Typical honest ingredients include:

- a visible commissioner or granting-authority spine for the named system;
- procurement, concession, franchise, or PPP-style tools confined to that system rather than to every adjacent ambition;
- explicit docking to the body’s fiscal chain, administrative chain, information chain, oversight chain, and asset-and-liability chain;
- declared performance, safety, continuity, data, and handback obligations;
- and public-law review routes that survive even when the delivery model is commercially complex.

The archive should prefer this chain when one real system needs it. It should be suspicious when a bounded authority begins using commercial instruments to warehouse unrelated policy power beyond its charter.

### 6. General-purpose territorial tiers may contract widely, but cannot outsource constitutional choices

Once the archive concludes that the honest answer is a real territorial tier, it should stop pretending that procurement and concessions are merely technical administration.

At that point the body should usually have:

- a visible procurement constitution covering planning, competition, capacity, risk, integrity, review, and publication;
- a make-buy-franchise-partner test for consequential delivery choices;
- stronger scrutiny for long-tail contracts that affect pricing power, land use, labour structure, system access, or durable capital risk;
- and a clear rule that elections, public law, and budget processes remain the home of core territorial choices even when delivery is external.

A territorial tier may outsource or concession many things. It should not use contract form to privatize the choice of what the territory owes, whom it serves, or what burdens it imposes.

### 7. Treat modification and rescue as the constitutional boundary, not only the award

The archive should not overfocus on the initial procurement ceremony.

The constitutional edge often appears later, when the body extends term, alters economic balance, enlarges scope, swaps contractor, waives performance, or rescues a failing supplier.

Later casework should therefore usually ask:

- was the review or option clause genuinely declared up front,
- did the modification change the economic balance or scope materially,
- did it introduce conditions that would have changed the original competition,
- what reapproval, republication, or recompeting threshold applies,
- and who has authority to approve emergency rescue without normalizing permanent exceptionalism.

This is where EU procurement and concession law are especially useful to the archive: substantial modification is a constitutional alarm bell, not just a commercial housekeeping issue.

### 8. Keep contract-management, data, and oversight chains visibly docked

A body does not have an honest contracting-boundary chain if it can sign but cannot govern the contract life.

Later archive work should therefore usually ask whether the body can show:

- named contract-management responsibility,
- service and risk reporting that belongs in the body’s information chain,
- complaint and challenge routes that belong in its oversight and dispute chains,
- board, committee, or member-government visibility proportionate to the contract’s constitutional weight,
- and publication of material amendments, incidents, and renewal decisions.

If those routes are absent, the supplier relationship is already governing more than the public body is.

### 9. Build expiry, step-in, handback, and replacement routes before contract dependence hardens

Whenever long-lived contracts, franchises, concessions, or PPP-style structures are in view, the archive should ask whether the body has an honest endgame.

That usually means:

- data and records transfer;
- asset-condition and handback criteria;
- software portability and interface continuity where relevant;
- emergency substitute-operation or step-in routes;
- replacement procurement timelines and temporary service plans;
- and explicit docking to the asset-and-liability chain so expiry does not produce orphaned obligations.

The archive should reject the claim that a body is constitutionally light if it cannot realistically retake, replace, or unwind its own contracts.

### 10. Use one short contracting-boundary holding inside applied verdict packets

When `811` is used for a real polity, proposal, or case, the packet should normally be able to add one short holding such as:

> **Contracting-boundary-chain holding:** This backbone may jointly buy shared administrative systems and bounded support services, but service-policy choices, long concessions, and material contract variations require principal-side approval and may not be normalized through host-managed change orders.

or:

> **Contracting-boundary-chain holding:** This authority may grant and manage contracts for its named system, including concessions, but material scope extensions, economic-balance changes, and continuity rescues require published reapproval, review-route clarity, and handback protection.

That keeps applied judgments from stopping at the word **contract**, **franchise**, or **PPP** without saying whether the commercial perimeter is still honest for the form.

## Failure modes

- **contract escape hatch** — a body claims it remains constitutionally light because the heavy public choices are exercised through external agreements rather than through openly owned powers;
- **commissioner blur** — nobody can say who actually speaks for the body in the market, so hosts, consultants, or incumbent suppliers become the effective sovereign;
- **change-order constitution** — the real settlement lives in extensions, amendments, waivers, and rescue deals rather than in the original public decision;
- **vendor-policy drift** — supplier systems, data standards, or commercial defaults effectively determine public policy without any formal acknowledgment;
- **franchise sovereignty without democratic upgrade** — pricing, access, or network design are allocated for years through concessions without the legitimacy, oversight, and publication chain such power deserves;
- **pooled-service outsourcing of accountability** — a backbone buys a managed service and then behaves as if the political principals no longer owe explanation, review, or repair;
- **review blackout** — challenge, complaint, audit, or court routes are unclear once the body acts through contracts rather than direct delivery;
- **expiry panic** — an arrangement pretends to be flexible but reaches the end of term with no handback, retender, substitute-operation, or continuity plan.

## Bottom line

Cross-boundary bodies should not be allowed to hide constitutional thickening inside procurement files, concession deeds, PPP packages, managed-service agreements, or amendment practice. They need a visible contracting-boundary chain that matches the real form: light-support buying for connectors, principal-owned pooled commissioning for backbones, remit-bounded commissioner power for functional authorities, and full procurement constitutions for real territorial tiers.
