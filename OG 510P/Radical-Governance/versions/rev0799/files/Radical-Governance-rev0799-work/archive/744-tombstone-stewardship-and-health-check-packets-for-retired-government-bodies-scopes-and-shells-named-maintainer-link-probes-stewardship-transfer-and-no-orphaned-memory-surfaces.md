# 744 — Tombstone stewardship and health-check packets for retired government bodies, scopes, and shells: named maintainer, link probes, stewardship transfer, and no orphaned memory surfaces

## One-line thesis

Once the archive can create a tombstone (`741`), reroute it when successors drift (`742`), and correct it when the dead-page story is materially wrong (`743`), it should also keep one compact **tombstone stewardship and health-check packet**: one light maintenance object naming who keeps the dead page, redirects, alias map, record-home links, archive-home links, and legacy-question lane reachable and accurate over time, how failures are classified and escalated, and how stewardship itself is rehomed when institutions change again, so memorial objects do not decay into **orphaned memory surfaces**.

## Why this matters

The archive now has most of the constitutional grammar it needs for honest retirement.

It can retire a scope or body without constitutional ghosting (`642`). It can terminate a residual estate without leaving an immortal closeout tail (`740`). It can leave behind one thin public-facing tombstone stating what died, what replaced it, where the records and archives live, and which one bounded legacy-question lane still exists (`741`). It can later reroute that tombstone when the first successor, archive home, record home, or lookup lane changes again (`742`). And it can review and correct material tombstone errors with standing, evidence, dated reasons, and synchronized human-plus-machine repairs rather than helpdesk folklore (`743`).

What it still lacked was the smallest object for the quieter but very practical question: **who keeps the tombstone healthy after the ceremony is over**.

That gap matters because dead institutions often fail not by dramatic constitutional mistake but by slow maintenance drift. A redirect expires after a CMS change. A withdrawn attachment still resolves while its parent page has moved. The named archive home reorganizes its URL structure. The old mailbox still exists but nobody checks it. The legacy-question lane remains nominally listed but now belongs to a team that no longer understands what it is for. The first successor changes again, yet no one updates the alias map or dead-page crosswalk because nobody thinks tombstones have owners. Years later, the polity still technically "preserved" the dead thing, but the public experiences only a broken link, an unanswered inbox, a memorial page with stale branding, and a records trail that can only be decoded by institutional veterans.

The archive therefore needs one compact packet for the **ongoing stewardship** of the tombstone object itself. This packet should be lighter than a live service constitution and much lighter than a residual estate. It should not recreate a full operating institution around the dead thing. It should only keep the memorial object healthy enough to perform its narrow public job: tell the truth about death, route legacy arrivals honestly, preserve discoverability, and remain technically reachable across later administrative drift. Without that packet, the archive still permits **orphaned memory surfaces**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Require one named steward and one named backup steward for every live tombstone

A tombstone packet should never exist as pure ambient residue. It should name:

- one **primary steward** responsible for maintaining the human-facing tombstone and its machine-readable lookup surfaces;
- one **backup steward** or fallback office able to take over if the primary steward disappears, reorganizes, or stops responding;
- and the legal or administrative home from which that stewardship authority flows.

The steward need not be the successor body itself. It may instead be the record custodian, archive custodian, parent ministry, shared web operation, or another bounded maintenance office. What matters is that the archive can answer who is actually supposed to keep the tombstone healthy.

### 2. Keep one stewardship schedule for the exact surfaces the steward owns

A tombstone packet should not assume that “maintaining the dead page” is self-explanatory. The steward should carry one explicit surface schedule that distinguishes at least:

- the **human-facing tombstone page**;
- redirects from old URLs or retired service routes;
- archived or withdrawn attachments that still need visible historical treatment;
- the machine-readable alias or lookup map;
- the published successor, record-home, and archive-home links;
- and the bounded legacy-question lane.

Without a surface schedule, the steward may keep the visible page alive while the attachment redirects, alias table, or archived-route crosswalk quietly fail underneath it.

### 3. Separate steward responsibilities from successor, archive, and question-lane responsibilities

The steward is not automatically the owner of every downstream duty the tombstone points to.

The packet should therefore separate:

- **tombstone stewardship** — maintaining the memorial object and its lookup surfaces;
- **successor responsibility** — handling whatever live function, liability, or route was inherited;
- **record-home responsibility** — preserving authoritative records and records-access obligations;
- **archive-home responsibility** — preserving historic web captures, withdrawn publications, or other memory objects;
- **legacy-question-lane responsibility** — receiving and lawfully routing bounded historical or wrong-door questions.

This prevents the tombstone steward from becoming a shadow authority simply because it maintains the dead page.

### 4. Set a light but explicit health-check rhythm

A tombstone should not require the operating tempo of a live service. But neither should it be reviewed only when someone complains.

The stewardship packet should therefore set one light review rhythm, such as:

- on creation of the tombstone;
- after any successor drift or rerouting act (`742`);
- after any accepted material correction (`743`);
- after a major platform, CMS, domain, or archive migration affecting linked surfaces;
- and one periodic check at a light interval while the tombstone remains publicly discoverable.

The point is not bureaucracy for its own sake. The point is to stop dead-page maintenance from depending entirely on accidental rediscovery.

### 5. Prefer active probes over ceremonial review

A healthy tombstone is not proved by a line in a spreadsheet saying “reviewed”. The packet should require simple active probes, proportionate to the tombstone’s public importance.

At minimum, stewardship should verify:

- that published successor, record-home, and archive-home links resolve;
- that redirects from major retired URLs or service routes still land on the intended object;
- that the legacy-question lane still receives and can route messages;
- that withdrawn or archived attachments remain visibly historical rather than apparently current;
- and that the machine-readable alias map still matches the human-facing page.

Where a tombstone covers a high-volume or rights-bearing legacy route, the archive should prefer live test traffic or monitored forwarding evidence over purely visual inspection.

### 6. Classify tombstone health failures before deciding the response lane

Not every maintenance defect is a constitutional dispute. The stewardship packet should type failures at minimum as:

- **editorial health failure** — typo, broken non-material link, stale formatting, or similar low-stakes maintenance issue;
- **routing health failure** — the public is sent to the wrong successor, wrong record home, wrong archive home, or a dead legacy-question lane;
- **authority-confusion failure** — the dead page, redirect, attachment, or memorial branding now suggests continuing live authority;
- **stewardship-orphan failure** — no responsible maintainer can be identified or the named steward is gone;
- **material historical error** — the tombstone may be substantively wrong and should dock into the challenge-and-correction lane (`743`).

Typed failure classes keep small maintenance work from being over-lawyered while still forcing consequential issues into the right review path.

### 7. Dock material corrections into the correction packet rather than fixing them as “maintenance”

Stewardship and correction are related, but they are not the same thing.

The stewardship packet should say clearly that routine maintenance may fix minor breakage, but if the proposed repair changes the death statement, successor story, archive home, record custody, identifier map, or other materially important field, the steward must open the challenge-and-correction lane (`743`) or the rerouting lane (`742`) rather than silently editing the dead page.

This stops “maintenance” from becoming a euphemism for rewriting history.

### 8. Keep stale surfaces from outliving the steward’s attention

Many tombstone failures arise not on the main page but on secondary surfaces: old attachment URLs, PDFs or HTML attachments that remain indexable, old subdomains, preserved forms, or forgotten route aliases. The stewardship packet should therefore require one stale-surface sweep whenever the tombstone is first created and whenever the web or archive platform materially changes.

That sweep should identify:

- old URLs still receiving traffic;
- attachments or captures that need withdrawal labels, watermarks, redirects, or clearer history signals;
- stale logos, headers, or present-tense language surviving on subpages;
- and legacy aliases still used in search, correspondence, or case references.

The public often encounters the dead thing through residue, not through the canonical tombstone page.

### 9. Require one stewardship-transfer act whenever the steward changes

A tombstone may outlive the first office that maintained it. If the successor ministry reorganizes, the archive function moves, the shared web team changes, or the question lane is rehomed, the archive should not rely on somebody eventually updating the footer.

The packet should require one explicit **stewardship-transfer act** naming:

- the outgoing steward;
- the incoming steward;
- the surfaces being transferred;
- the unresolved maintenance defects, if any;
- and the date from which the new steward is responsible.

If the steward changes without a named transfer act, the tombstone is at immediate risk of becoming an orphaned memory surface even when its text remains correct.

### 10. Keep stewardship intentionally thin so it cannot impersonate live institutional survival

A tombstone steward keeps a memory object healthy; it does not operate the dead institution.

The packet should therefore forbid the steward from using maintenance authority to:

- reopen ordinary intake;
- answer live substantive questions beyond the bounded legacy-question lane;
- rebrand the memorial page as a current service hub;
- or exercise broad successor power merely because it now controls the dead page’s URLs.

The steward may maintain the routing object, preserve discoverability, and keep the archive legible. It may not turn dead-page maintenance into a quiet form of institutional afterlife.

## Guardrails

- Do not let tombstone maintenance exist without a named steward and backup steward.
- Do not collapse stewardship, successor, archive, records, and legacy-question-lane duties into one ambiguous owner.
- Do not treat passive periodic sign-off as enough where active redirects, links, or bounded message lanes can be probed directly.
- Do not let material historical edits travel as ordinary maintenance.
- Do not let attachment residue, stale subdomains, or old aliases escape the stewardship schedule just because the canonical page looks fine.
- Do not let stewardship-transfer happen by disappearance, reorg rumor, or platform migration alone.
- Do not let the steward become a shadow operator for the dead institution.

## Failure modes

- **orphaned memory surfaces**: the tombstone still exists in theory, but no office can actually say who maintains it or fix it when it breaks.
- **healthy main page, broken residue**: the canonical dead page looks correct, but attachment redirects, alias maps, or archived-route links are dead or misleading.
- **maintenance-as-rewrite**: a steward uses “routine upkeep” to alter successor claims, death statements, or custody facts without opening the proper correction lane.
- **steward capture by successor**: the live successor quietly treats tombstone maintenance as permission to absorb the memorial page into its own live branding and general contact flows.
- **silent stewardship drift**: the original steward disappears, but nobody issues a stewardship-transfer act, so failures accumulate until the tombstone becomes folklore.
- **review theater**: a page is marked as reviewed on schedule, but nobody actually tests whether links resolve, mailboxes receive, or attachments still signal historical status.

## Practical tests

A tombstone stewardship and health-check packet is honest when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it name a primary steward and a backup steward?
2. Does it list the exact surfaces the steward maintains, rather than just the main tombstone page?
3. Does it separate stewardship from successor, record-home, archive-home, and legacy-question-lane duties?
4. Does it set a light but explicit review rhythm?
5. Does it require active probes for links, redirects, and bounded contact routes rather than ceremonial sign-off alone?
6. Does it classify failures so minor maintenance, routing breakage, authority confusion, orphan risk, and material error do not all get handled the same way?
7. Does it dock substantive changes into rerouting (`742`) or correction (`743`) rather than letting “maintenance” rewrite history?
8. Does it require an explicit stewardship-transfer act when the maintainer changes?
9. Does it keep stale attachments, aliases, and residue surfaces inside the stewardship field?
10. Does it preserve tombstone health without reviving the dead institution as a live authority?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can leave an honest tombstone (`741`), later reroute it when successors drift (`742`), and correct it when the dead-page story is materially wrong (`743`), it should also be able to show one compact packet for **who maintains that tombstone over time, what surfaces and active probes sit inside its health-check rhythm, how routine upkeep stays distinct from historical correction, and how stewardship itself is rehomed when institutions change again**. Otherwise it still permits **orphaned memory surfaces**.
