# 640 — Post-launch scope observability, variance ledgers, rollback lanes, and no stack change by runtime custom

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should treat materially stack-altering reforms as **actively observable after go-live**, with one linked post-launch variance ledger, short review clocks, named correction and rollback lanes, and explicit closure criteria, so the lived operation of budgets, staff, systems, supervision, and remedies cannot quietly become a different constitutional settlement by runtime custom.

## Why this matters

The archive now says much more clearly **how scope should be assigned** (`579`, `602`-`605`), **when drift should reopen review** (`632`), **where the live stack should be written down** (`633`), **how stack-changing proposals should be described** (`635`), **who should classify them before adoption** (`636`), **how disagreement should be owned** (`637`), **how ambiguity should be blocked from doing constitutional work** (`638`), and **why go-live should depend on one last-mile conformance check** (`639`).

What it still lacked was a doctrine for the period immediately after launch. That gap matters because many of the most important multilevel distortions do not appear in the enacted text and are not fully visible before day one. They appear only once the reform is lived: who really approves cases, who can stop or delay action, whose budget codes become dispositive, which helpdesk citizens must actually use, which territorial units are included by default, whose software permissions override local judgment, and whether review routes remain reachable before facts on the ground harden.

A polity that stops at enactment plus pre-launch attestation is still vulnerable to **runtime custom**. Staff habits, vendor defaults, emergency workarounds, reporting thresholds, supervisory routines, and budget throttles can settle into a durable constitutional practice that no one ever openly approved. The archive should therefore prefer a compact doctrine of **post-launch scope observability**: stack-changing reforms should stay visibly under observation until the polity can honestly say that the live arrangement matches the authorised one, or else the proper correction, rollback, or re-clearance lane has been triggered.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Put every materially stack-altering reform into a short post-launch observation window

A reform that materially changes who governs what should not move directly from **go-live** to **settled constitutional fact**. It should enter one short observation window with a declared start date, monitoring owner, review cadence, and closure test.

The point is not permanent exceptionalism. It is to recognise that many scope effects only become legible in real operation. During this window, the polity should compare the authorised settlement against the arrangement that people, agencies, contractors, and software are actually living.

### 2. Keep one public variance ledger linked to the whole change chain

Every such reform should carry one post-launch variance ledger linked to the packet (`635`), opinion (`636`), override if any (`637`), canonical scope register entry (`633`), jurisprudence digest entry if relevant (`634`), and pre-launch conformance attestation (`639`).

That ledger should not be a generic project log. It should focus on **constitutional variance**: where the live operating bundle differs in any material way from the authorised allocation of competence, finance, supervision, territorial reach, discretion, or review.

Without one linked ledger, institutions will describe each variance as an isolated implementation quirk and the public will never see the cumulative stack effect.

### 3. Compare actual against intended control, not only stated service outputs

Post-launch observability should ask not just whether the reform is functioning, but **who is actually governing after launch**.

The ledger should therefore compare at least:

- who may initiate, approve, refuse, defer, suspend, or override action in practice;
- who controls budget release, transfer conditions, or cash throttles;
- who holds admin rights, workflow permissions, and records authority;
- who sets the operative interpretation when law and guidance diverge;
- which territory the live process actually reaches;
- and whether complaint, reconsideration, appeal, ombuds, or judicial routes remain usable before irreversibility.

A reform may hit its delivery target while still creating a different scope settlement than the one the polity authorised.

### 4. Use mixed signals rather than one heroic dashboard

No single metric can prove that the scope stack is honest after launch. The polity should therefore use a compact mixed evidence set, such as:

- budget and transfer traces;
- staffing and escalation maps;
- contract change logs and vendor admin-right records;
- workflow and permission logs;
- rates of local override, forced template use, or supervisory intervention;
- complaint, appeal, and judicial-filing patterns;
- territorial inclusion or exclusion anomalies;
- and qualitative reports from affected authorities, associations, frontline operators, and service users.

The archive should prefer typed evidence over one reassuring status color. The question is not whether launch looked smooth, but whether the authorised holder of power is the one actually exercising it.

### 5. Classify variance by constitutional significance

Not every post-launch irregularity is a stack problem. The ledger should classify observed variance into at least four buckets:

- **non-material variance** — operational differences that do not change who governs;
- **correctable drift within authority** — live behaviour has slipped but can be repaired without reopening the constitutional lane;
- **material unauthorised rescoping** — lived operation now allocates power differently enough that the approved settlement is no longer an honest description;
- **temporary exception under a named lane** — emergency or transitional conditions are still being relied upon and should remain on the handback clock.

This keeps the doctrine disciplined. The point is not to constitutionalise every outage or staffing issue; it is to notice when lived practice has started doing constitutional work.

### 6. Prefer immediate repair when the authorised settlement is still reachable

Where variance shows drift but the authorised arrangement can still be restored, the first duty should be **repair rather than narrative accommodation**.

That may mean changing permissions, correcting budget coding, rewriting manuals, retraining staff, amending vendor instructions, restoring local discretion, reopening blocked review routes, or withdrawing informal supervisory habits that exceeded the approved lane.

The ledger should state who owes the repair, by when, and how closure will be verified. Without visible repair ownership, runtime custom becomes the easiest path because nobody is clearly responsible for re-aligning the live stack.

### 7. Reopen the constitutional lane when post-launch variance is materially stack-changing

Where lived operation has moved beyond what the authorised packet honestly covers, the polity should not cure the mismatch by simply updating the register after the fact. It should trigger the proper lane again: new packet, renewed opinion, override if needed, or handback procedure.

This is the point where observability docks back into `632`, `635`, `636`, `637`, and `631`. The ledger is not a permission slip for improvisation. It is the trigger surface that tells the polity when post-launch reality has become a fresh constitutional question.

### 8. Keep rollback, kill-switch, and constrained-operation lanes ready before they are needed

A post-launch doctrine is only serious if the polity can respond to bad variance without pretending that all reversals are impossible. The change chain should therefore preserve at least three named response lanes:

- **rollback** — restore the prior lawful arrangement in whole or in part;
- **constrained continuation** — keep the reform live only within a narrower, explicitly bounded footprint while repair or re-clearance occurs;
- **temporary emergency hold** — use a legally named exceptional lane with expiry and handback fields rather than letting operational panic define the constitution.

Without predeclared response lanes, institutions facing a bad launch will often rationalise unauthorised operation as the least disruptive option and thereby turn runtime drift into settled government.

### 9. Give affected authorities and the public a visible route to report scope variance

Post-launch observability should not depend solely on the sponsor, centre, or supervisory authority noticing problems. Affected local or regional authorities, their associations, oversight bodies, workers, and the public should have one visible way to report possible scope variance into the ledger.

That report lane should be separate from ordinary customer support or routine project feedback. It should allow the reporter to say, in effect: **the reform is being lived in a way that changes who governs, who can decide, or who can be challenged**.

This matters because frontline practice often reveals stack drift long before central dashboards do.

### 10. Close the observation window only with an explicit settlement statement

A reform should leave observation status only through one short public closure statement saying either:

- the live arrangement now conforms and the register can show settled live status;
- the live arrangement remains provisional under a named temporary lane;
- or material variance has required rollback, handback, or fresh constitutional clearance.

Closure should not be inferred from silence, elapsed time, or ordinary operational familiarity. The archive should prefer a visible end-state because constitutional custom often hides inside the sentence “this is just how we do it now.”

## Guardrails

- Do not let ordinary delivery metrics substitute for evidence about who actually governs after launch.
- Do not treat post-launch variance as harmless merely because the statute still reads well on paper.
- Do not update the canonical scope register from provisional live to settled live without one explicit closure statement.
- Do not let vendor defaults, platform permissions, or budget controls keep doing constitutional work after they have been identified as drift.
- Do not rely on sponsor-only self-reporting; affected authorities and users should have a route to surface constitutional variance.
- Do not turn the doctrine into indefinite probation for reforms whose live settlement has in fact stabilised honestly.

## Failure modes

- **stack change by runtime custom**: day-to-day habits and workarounds settle into a different scope allocation than the authorised one.
- **dashboard theater**: high-level service metrics look acceptable while budget, permissions, or supervision have quietly shifted control.
- **register laundering**: the public scope map is updated after launch to match drift instead of asking whether drift was lawful.
- **vendor or host residue**: borrowed admin rights, contracts, or transition controls keep real authority in the wrong place after supposed handoff.
- **review collapse after launch**: complaint or appeal routes remain formally present but are too late, too obscure, or too weak to stop irreversibility.
- **temporary exception without exit**: transitional or emergency practices remain live long after their justification has expired.

## Practical tests

A polity is handling post-launch scope observability honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does every materially stack-altering reform enter a short, named post-launch observation window rather than moving directly from go-live to settled fact?
2. Is there one linked public variance ledger focused on constitutional variance rather than generic project noise?
3. Does monitoring compare actual against intended control over decisions, budgets, systems, territorial reach, and remedies rather than only service outputs?
4. Are mixed evidence sources used, including finance, staffing, workflow, complaints, and reports from affected authorities and users?
5. Can the polity distinguish non-material implementation noise from material unauthorised rescoping?
6. When drift is repairable, is there a named owner, deadline, and verification path for restoring the authorised settlement?
7. When lived operation has materially changed the stack, does the polity reopen the proper packet, opinion, override, or handback lane instead of laundering the result into the register?
8. Are rollback or constrained-operation lanes available before crisis pressure makes them politically impossible?
9. Can affected authorities and the public report scope variance through a visible route separate from ordinary support noise?
10. Does the observation window close only through one explicit settlement statement rather than by silence or habit?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a reform can still be described honestly only by pointing to enacted text and launch plans, while the lived control of money, permissions, supervision, territorial reach, or remedies now sits somewhere else, then the polity has not completed the reform honestly. It is governing by **stack change through runtime custom**.
