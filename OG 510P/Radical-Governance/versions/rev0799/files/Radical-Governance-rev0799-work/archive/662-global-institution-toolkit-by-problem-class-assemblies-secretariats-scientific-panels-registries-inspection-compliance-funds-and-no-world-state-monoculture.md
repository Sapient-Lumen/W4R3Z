# 662 — Global institution toolkit by problem class: assemblies, secretariats, scientific panels, registries, inspection, compliance, funds, and no world-state monoculture

## One-line thesis

The ideal global layer should be built from a **small toolkit of treaty-bound institutional forms matched to problem class** rather than from one fantasy model of world government: use assemblies and councils for norm-setting, secretariats and focal-point networks for continuous coordination, scientific panels for shared factual baselines, registries and reporting systems for comparability, inspection or verification where cheating matters, dispute and compliance lanes where interpretation recurs, and dedicated funds where solidarity is required — while refusing both fake world-state domesticity and empty declarations with no operating spine.

## Why this matters

The archive already says, repeatedly and correctly, that the global layer should be **thin, real, and treaty-bound** (`488`, `541`, `565`, `602`, `628`, `660`). That is a valuable constitutional instinct.

But it still leaves one recurring design problem unresolved.

Later notes often need to say that a problem belongs partly at the global layer. Once they say that, a second question immediately appears:

**what kind of global institution is actually appropriate?**

Without a compact answer, design tends to swing between two bad instincts.

The first is **world-state monoculture**: every serious planetary problem is imagined to need a parliament, executive, judiciary, police, and tax state for the whole world.

The second is **norms-without-machinery minimalism**: governments issue a declaration, create a conference, or adopt a treaty heading, but never build the registry, secretariat, reporting loop, scientific assessment body, verification channel, or fund that would make the regime operationally real.

The archive needs a narrower and more reusable rule.

The ideal global layer is not one big government. It is a **toolkit of problem-matched institutional forms**. Different global problems require different mixes of political authorization, expertise, monitoring, implementation support, and dispute settlement.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from the problem class, not from prestige or constitutional analogy

Global design should begin by asking what kind of coordination failure exists.

Is the problem mainly:

- war restraint or collective security,
- emergency notification,
- scientific uncertainty and evidence synthesis,
- technical interoperability,
- free-riding on a shared commons,
- treaty interpretation and dispute,
- or finance for a burden that poorer states cannot carry alone?

Different answers imply different institutions. The archive should therefore resist designing the global layer by domestic constitutional analogy.

### 2. Use assemblies and councils for norm-setting, authorization, and visible political settlement

Some global functions require a place where states formally decide:

- adopt or amend norms,
- authorize collective action,
- set mandates,
- approve budgets,
- elect bureau or committee members,
- or publicly register consent and dissent.

That is the role of assemblies, conferences of parties, councils, or similar political forums.

The archive should treat these bodies as the **authorization layer** of global governance. They are often necessary, but rarely sufficient by themselves.

### 3. Use secretariats and focal-point networks for continuous operations

Many global regimes fail not because the treaty text is absent but because nobody is continuously responsible for operating it.

Secretariats, national focal-point networks, and standing administrative units are what turn episodic diplomacy into ongoing governance. They route notifications, convene parties, manage calendars, hold records, maintain rosters, process reports, support compliance bodies, and preserve institutional memory between political meetings.

Where a regime needs continuous coordination, the archive should prefer a standing secretariat over conference-only governance.

### 4. Use scientific panels and expert assessment bodies where the hard problem is shared factual judgment under uncertainty

Some global questions turn on scientific synthesis, risk assessment, methodology, and comparable evidence rather than only on bargaining.

Climate science, pandemic alerting, chemicals, biodiversity, food systems, aviation safety, ocean health, and other complex transboundary domains often need a body that can assemble evidence credibly and repeatedly.

The archive should therefore use scientific panels, expert committees, and assessment cycles where the problem is not merely “we need a rule” but “we need a shared and reviewable account of reality before rules can bite honestly.”

### 5. Use registries, inventories, and reporting frameworks where comparability is the main bottleneck

A large share of global governance is neither policing nor legislating. It is **making activity comparable across states**.

Registries, inventories, notification systems, and reporting templates matter when the core task is to make national action legible enough for others to compare, challenge, or build on. Climate contributions, shipping and aviation rules, health-event notifications, and treaty participation status all rely on this logic.

The archive should therefore treat registries as first-class institutions, not clerical afterthoughts.

### 6. Use inspection, verification, or peer-review machinery when hidden defection would otherwise dominate

Some global regimes can rely mostly on transparency and self-report. Others cannot.

When the incentive to conceal, underreport, or defect is high, a global regime may need stronger assurance machinery:

- inspection,
- technical review,
- peer-review cycles,
- site visits,
- certification or audit procedures,
- or formally structured monitoring and evaluation.

The archive should not assume every global regime needs inspectors. But when cheating can be both easy and consequential, the regime should not rely on aspiration alone.

### 7. Use dispute-settlement and compliance lanes where interpretation will recur

Many regimes encounter repeated disagreement about:

- what the treaty means,
- whether a state has complied,
- whether a measure is justified,
- or what corrective action should follow.

Where those questions are routine rather than exceptional, the archive should prefer an explicit dispute or compliance lane: a court, tribunal, committee, arbitration path, or other reason-giving body with a defined remit.

That does not require every global regime to imitate a constitutional supreme court. It does require visible interpretation and consequence where ambiguity would otherwise dissolve the regime.

### 8. Use dedicated funds and support facilities where solidarity is part of the regime, not an optional charity afterthought

Some global regimes ask states to do things that poorer or more exposed countries cannot do without help. In those cases the institution set is incomplete unless it includes finance, technical assistance, or implementation support.

Dedicated funds, capacity-support facilities, technology mechanisms, and technical-help windows are therefore not merely developmental add-ons. They are often part of the constitutional honesty of the regime itself.

The archive should reject unfunded universality.

### 9. Use specialized agencies and standards bodies when the real job is high-volume technical interoperability

Shipping, aviation, telecommunications, postal systems, health codes, measurement standards, and similar domains need continuous updating, technical detail, and routinized cross-border compatibility.

The honest answer here is often neither a general political assembly nor a world executive, but a **specialized agency or standards-setting body** operating under a clear mandate, with amendment and review procedures suited to technical rule maintenance.

The archive should treat this as a major global form in its own right.

### 10. Compose regimes from several tools when the problem is mixed

Many real global problems require several tools at once.

A climate regime may need:

- a conference of parties,
- a secretariat,
- a scientific assessment body,
- registries and transparency systems,
- a compliance or facilitative mechanism,
- and finance facilities.

A health-alert regime may need:

- legal duties to notify,
- national focal points,
- a secretariat,
- expert emergency committees,
- and review of core capacities.

The archive should therefore ask not “which single institution is right?” but “which minimum bundle is right for this problem class?”

## Canonical toolkit matrix

| Problem class | Minimum global tools | What not to overbuild | What not to omit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Peace, security, and aggression restraint** | charter or treaty basis, political council or assembly, secretariat support, reporting and monitoring, dispute or judicial lanes where applicable | fake world domestic executive with general police power | visible authorization and review routes |
| **Emergency notification and transboundary alerting** | legal duty to notify, secretariat, national focal-point network, expert emergency or review committees, comparable reporting | permanent maximal bureaucracy for every incident | standing operations and contact architecture |
| **Technical interoperability** | specialized agency or standards body, amendment procedures, secretariat, registries or certification where needed, technical review | broad constitutional language with no operating rule maintenance | durable standards maintenance and version control |
| **Shared commons and free-riding problems** | political forum, scientific panel, reporting registry, transparency or review machinery, compliance lane, finance where burdens differ | world-state fantasy that outruns consent and capability | measurable reporting and consequence for non-performance |
| **Implementation-gap or solidarity-heavy regimes** | dedicated fund or facility, technical assistance, secretariat, review of support and use | universal obligations with no support spine | explicit finance and capacity windows |

## Guardrails

- Do not build the global layer by copying one domestic constitutional template.
- Do not confuse declarations with institutions.
- Do not assume every regime needs inspectors, courts, or large agencies.
- Do not omit registries, secretariats, or finance where the regime plainly depends on them.
- Do not let specialized technical bodies drift into ambient general government.
- Do not ask one global body to solve every problem class at once.

## Failure modes

- **world-state monoculture**: every planetary problem is imagined to require one maximal model of global government.
- **conference minimalism**: states meet, speak, and adopt text, but no operating spine is built.
- **registry neglect**: commitments exist but cannot be compared, updated, or tracked.
- **science by lobbying**: factual baselines are negotiated politically because no credible assessment body exists.
- **unenforced opacity**: the regime depends on self-report even where hidden defection is easy and consequential.
- **solidarity fiction**: poorer states are bound by universal expectations without the finance or technical support needed to comply.
- **specialized-agency creep**: a technical body quietly accumulates broad political authority without explicit reauthorization.

## Practical tests

A global institutional design passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Has the regime identified its actual problem class clearly enough to choose institutions on purpose?
2. Is there a visible political authorization forum where one is needed?
3. Is there a standing operational spine rather than conference-only governance?
4. Are science, reporting, and comparability handled by institutions strong enough for the problem?
5. Where cheating or ambiguity matters, is there a review, compliance, inspection, or dispute lane proportionate to that risk?
6. Where implementation burdens differ sharply, is solidarity built into the regime rather than begged for after adoption?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a proposed global regime can describe **its values, goals, and conference headlines** but cannot show **which assembly authorizes, which secretariat operates, which panel assesses, which registry compares, which lane reviews, and which fund supports**, it still has **multilateral rhetoric without a problem-matched institutional toolkit**.
