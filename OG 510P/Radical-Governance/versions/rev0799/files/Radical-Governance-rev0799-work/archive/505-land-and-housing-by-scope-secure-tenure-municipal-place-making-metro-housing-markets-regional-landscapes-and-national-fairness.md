# 505 — Land and housing by scope: secure tenure, municipal place-making, metro housing markets, regional landscapes, and national fairness

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat housing and land as a scale-matched constitutional domain: micro-local institutions steward shared places and surface need; municipalities handle parcels, streets, permits, and neighborhood upgrading; metropolitan bodies govern the real housing market and fair-share growth; regions protect landscapes, basins, and food land; nations guarantee rights, anti-segregation floors, and housing finance; and global institutions supply rights baselines, data, and crisis support rather than pretending to zone the planet.

## Why this matters

Housing is where territorial governance stops being theoretical. A polity can talk beautifully about subsidiarity and representation while still producing eviction, segregation, speculation, long commutes, exclusionary zoning, and inherited spatial inequality. That is usually a scope failure as much as a market failure.

The smallest scale knows which building is decaying, which corner is unsafe, which landlord or block steward is failing, and where a small commons can be upgraded. But the same scale cannot solve a metropolitan shortage, finance mass social housing, or correct a whole region's segregation pattern. The municipality can shape streets and permits, but often cannot balance the full labor-market housing system on its own. The nation can redistribute and legislate rights, but it cannot sensibly micromanage every parcel.

An ideal government therefore needs a housing constitution by scope. It should secure tenure, keep place-making close to place, move market-wide allocation to the actual city-region, protect rural and ecological landscapes at broader territorial scales, and stop local veto or national abstraction from deciding everything.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from secure tenure and anti-displacement rather than from asset inflation

At every scale, the archive should treat housing first as a condition of safety, dignity, and social membership, not merely as a speculative asset class. That means secure tenure, protection against arbitrary eviction and displacement, accessible basic services, and active anti-homelessness obligations. A polity that talks about growth while tolerating routine tenure insecurity is governing land as collateral rather than habitat.

### 2. Give micro-local institutions stewardship and early-warning roles, not exclusionary sovereignty

Blocks, villages, wards, and neighborhood institutions can surface vacancy, harassment, unsafe buildings, informal settlement needs, and local repair priorities faster than higher tiers. They are useful for stewardship, neighborhood upgrading, block-level maintenance, and resident voice. They should not have the effective sovereign power to exclude newcomers, hoard scarcity, or veto fair-share housing obligations for the wider city.

### 3. Treat municipalities as the keepers of parcels, streets, permitting, and neighborhood quality

The municipal tier is usually the right home for detailed land-use administration: parcel assembly rules, code enforcement, street design, public realm, building control, local public housing management where present, and neighborhood-level upgrading. This is where housing becomes concrete and legible. Municipal government should therefore remain strong in place-making, but not be left alone to solve a city-region shortage created by demand, jobs, and transit patterns that spill far beyond its border.

### 4. Treat the metropolitan scale as the real housing-market and anti-segregation scale

In major urban regions, the real housing market is metropolitan. Commute sheds, school access, job access, and displacement chains cross municipal lines. The archive should therefore prefer metro-level housing targets, fair-share allocation, transit-oriented growth, major infrastructure coordination, and anti-segregation review over fragmented municipal competition. Where the housing market is regional or metropolitan, leaving supply and exclusion entirely to local veto is just scope blindness with a planning vocabulary.

### 5. Give regions responsibility for landscapes, basin constraints, and urban-rural land balances

Regional or state-scale bodies should govern the territorial questions that outrun the metro but remain subnational: watershed constraints, floodplains, forests, agricultural land, peri-urban growth boundaries, extractive pressure, and major corridor planning. A good region protects the long horizon of land use so neither municipal fragmentation nor national impatience liquidates ecological resilience and food land for short-run fiscal convenience.

### 6. Reserve national government for rights floors, redistribution, and system finance

National government is usually the right level for the legal right to adequate housing, anti-discrimination law, tenant and mortgage baseline protections, fiscal equalization, major housing subsidy systems, tax treatment that does not reward speculation, large social-housing finance, homelessness floors, and national data standards. The nation should set the fairness architecture and finance capacity without pretending it can draw every zoning line better than the city.

### 7. Keep the global layer narrow: rights baselines, crisis support, and shared knowledge

Global governance should not try to run ordinary domestic land-use administration. Its role is narrower and still important: articulate housing and land rights baselines, support comparable data and good practice, coordinate on displacement and urban crisis, and connect housing to climate, disaster, and development finance. The ideal global layer helps states and cities avoid catastrophic failure; it does not substitute for territorial government.

### 8. Prefer plural tenure and non-speculative housing forms where they fit

An ideal archive should not assume one property form solves every context. Cooperative housing, community land trusts, public or limited-equity models, rental protections, incremental upgrading, customary and collective tenure, and serviced-land approaches all have a place when rights and clarity are preserved. The important constitutional principle is not uniform tenure form but secure, non-discriminatory, reviewable access to housing and land.

## Guardrails

- Do not let micro-local or municipal veto power block fair-share housing for the wider labor market.
- Do not treat housing only as an investment product while rights, tenure security, and homelessness remain downstream afterthoughts.
- Do not force watershed, floodplain, and agricultural-land questions into parcel-by-parcel local bargaining alone.
- Do not let national subsidy systems entrench local exclusion or metropolitan fragmentation.
- Do not use slum clearance, relocation, or emergency powers to launder forced displacement.

## Failure modes

- **veto localism**: affluent localities freeze out needed housing while the metro absorbs the damage through price spikes and displacement.
- **parcel myopia**: municipalities optimize lot by lot without a city-region growth, transit, or fairness plan.
- **segregation by governance**: zoning, school boundaries, infrastructure, and subsidy systems jointly reproduce territorial inequality.
- **landscape liquidation**: peri-urban and rural land is consumed without basin, food, or resilience discipline.
- **rights without supply or supply without rights**: one side of the housing constitution is narrated while the other is left to fail.

## Practical tests

A scale-matched housing system passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Are tenure security, anti-eviction rules, and anti-homelessness duties explicit rather than assumed?
2. Does the municipality control detailed place-making while the metro governs the actual housing market and fair-share growth?
3. Are regional ecological and agricultural land constraints given a real institutional home?
4. Does national government provide rights floors, anti-segregation law, and serious finance rather than leaving fairness to local tax luck?
5. Is the global layer limited to rights baselines, crisis coordination, and shared learning instead of fantasy world zoning?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **who approves the parcel** but not **which level secures tenure, which level allocates growth across the housing market, which level protects landscapes, and which level pays for fairness**, it still has **a planning code without a housing constitution**.
