# 863 — Applied Schengen Information System alert-database waist: national issuer responsibility, data rights, and no European police by alert

## One-line thesis

The Schengen Information System is best classified as a cross-border alert-database and operational-cooperation waist: it lets authorised national and EU-linked authorities enter, search, and act on alerts for defined persons and objects across Schengen cooperation, but each alert still needs legal category, issuing-authority responsibility, data-quality discipline, SIRENE / supplementary-information routing, access controls, correction / erasure rights, and national or EU legal bases, so the database must not become European police government by alert.

## Why this matters

The archive needed a database-waist case. Port-state-control inspections tested a regional enforcement waist, IHR tested a global alert waist, and FATF tested soft-law hardening. The Schengen Information System adds a different danger: a record in a shared database can move faster than ordinary politics, litigation, or administrative explanation.

An SIS alert may affect border crossing, police checks, return enforcement, missing-person responses, arrest / judicial cooperation, vulnerable-person protection, discreet or specific checks, and objects such as vehicles or documents. The system is powerful because it connects many frontline users. That is also why it must be governed as a waist, not treated as mere IT.

The key governance holding is issuer responsibility. A database entry is not self-legitimating. The public owner of the alert, the category of alert, the data that support it, the action to be taken, the supplementary-information route, and the data-subject rights route must remain reconstructable.

### 4. Alert lifecycle

| Stage | Governance question | Required record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| legal category | what type of alert is permitted? | regulation / national legal basis and alert category |
| issuer | which authority entered the alert? | issuing authority and responsibility record |
| data entry | what data support the alert? | data fields, source, accuracy check, necessity / proportionality where required |
| access | who may search or receive the alert? | competent-authority and role-access list |
| hit | what happened when the alert was found? | hit log, action instruction, supplementary-information route |
| SIRENE / supplement | what extra information moved between authorities? | request / reply record and responsible bureau |
| action | what public act followed? | border, police, judicial, return, protective, or administrative decision file |
| correction | how can inaccurate or unlawful data be corrected or erased? | data-rights route, authority, clock, outcome |
| review | who can supervise or adjudicate? | data protection, court, administrative, or compensation route |
| expiry / renewal | when does the alert lapse, renew, or close? | retention / review clock and deletion proof |

A hit is an event in a legal lifecycle, not a substitute for the lifecycle.

### 5. Data-rights and issuer-responsibility rule

The packet should apply this rule:

```text
The authority that entered the alert remains responsible for the accuracy, currency, lawfulness, correction, update, and deletion of that alert, unless the applicable legal framework assigns a different owner. A different authority that discovers possible inaccuracy must route the contradiction to the issuer rather than silently treating the hit as settled truth.
```

This rule is the database equivalent of the archive’s “no authority by dashboard” principle.

### 7. Form holding

The honest form is **cross-border alert-database and operational-cooperation waist**.

SIS may provide shared alerts, searches, hit handling, supplementary-information exchange, and defined action instructions across authorised authorities. It must not be described as an autonomous police government. The public authority of any action must remain traceable to the alert category, issuer, competent authority, applicable regulation, national or EU legal basis, and review route. The database may accelerate cooperation, but it must not erase responsibility.

### 8. Rival interpretations

| Rival rule | Why it is tempting | Why this packet limits it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| mere database | SIS is an information system | ignores that alerts can trigger coercive or protective action at borders and in policing |
| European police government | the system is large and cross-border | overstates direct central authority and hides national issuer responsibility |
| national sovereignty only | member authorities enter many alerts | underplays shared infrastructure, common categories, agency access, and cross-border action |
| security override | speed is essential | cannot erase data quality, access limits, correction, erasure, court / authority review, or compensation |
| data-protection paperwork | rights exist on paper | insufficient if people cannot identify issuer, route requests, or obtain timely correction |
| hit-as-truth | a database match feels authoritative | database hit must be checked against legal category, identity, action, and currentness |

## Failure modes

- **database sovereignty**: treating the alert record as the real sovereign act.
- **issuer disappearance**: no one can identify who owns correction and deletion.
- **hit-as-law**: frontline action treats a match as sufficient legal basis.
- **redress maze**: affected persons must chase several authorities to discover who can correct the alert.
- **agency-access creep**: EU agency involvement expands practical alert power without clear responsibility.
- **data-quality drift**: expired, inaccurate, duplicate, or poorly matched alerts keep producing effects.
- **secret competent-authority sprawl**: too many users have access without public or supervisory clarity.
- **contractor invisibility**: infrastructure or support contractors shape logs, access, continuity, or security without public-record controls.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Can a reviewer identify the legal category of the alert?
2. Can the affected person or reviewer identify the issuing authority?
3. Is the action taken after a hit legally separate from the existence of the hit?
4. Is there a usable correction and erasure route with a responsible authority and clock?
5. Are supplementary-information exchanges reconstructable?
6. Are access rights limited to authorised users and tasks?
7. Are agency-access roles labelled rather than folded into generic “EU access”?
8. Is there a retention / renewal / deletion record?
9. Can a contradiction from another authority reach the issuer and change the record?
