# 921 — Non-English / Global South official-source governance, language-version drift, translation currentness, and no rule by English summary

## One-line thesis

Non-English and Global South official sources must be governed as live authority, service, language, and currentness objects; an English summary, machine translation, donor deck, benchmark paragraph, or central-government press release is never enough to prove the rule, service route, remedy, or affected-person burden.

## Why this matters

The archive has become much better at U.S., U.K., EU, Canadian, Australian, and other easy-to-fetch English official sources. That strength creates a bias. The highest-risk public-service systems often sit in jurisdictions where the official source is not English, where service pages change more quickly than laws, where municipalities or local offices carry the actual burden, and where international commentary translates the convenient part of a rule while losing notices, exceptions, appeal clocks, proof routes, and assisted channels.

The practical failure is not merely translation error. It is source-state collapse. A Portuguese law, an implementing decree, a ministry informe, a municipal social-assistance office instruction, a gov.br credential level, an AI plan, a pending AI bill, and a data-protection authority consultation are different authority states. A Hindi / regional-language UIDAI page, an English central regulation, a DigiLocker consent FAQ, and a service integration document are also different states. If the archive cites only an English digest, it may preserve the doctrine while losing the rule that actually blocks, pays, identifies, notifies, or excludes a person.

The anti-theater rule is therefore **no rule by English summary**. Summaries can orient readers, but the controlling packet must identify the authoritative-language text, publication status, supersession chain, service consequence, local implementation surface, and translation risk.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Split source language from source authority

Every non-English or Global South official-source case needs at least four states before a citation is trusted:

| State | Question |
| --- | --- |
| authoritative-language state | Which language version is legally or administratively authoritative? |
| translation state | Is the English or machine-translated version official, courtesy, experimental, incomplete, or third-party? |
| publication state | Is the source a law, decree, regulation, ministry guidance, service page, FAQ, open-data surface, press release, consultation, bill, or local instruction? |
| service-consequence state | Can this source change identity proofing, benefits, payment, notice, appeal, records, data sharing, or local-office action? |

A translation can be useful and still be non-authoritative. A service page can be official and still be volatile. A law can be current and still be insufficient because the blocking rule lives in a circular, FAQ, municipal workflow, or platform level.

### 2. Preserve the publication chain

The source packet should keep laws, decrees, administrative acts, service pages, FAQs, consultation pages, PDFs, open-data rows, and local instructions separate. A case cannot flatten them into one “Brazil says” or “India says” surface. The chain should show:

- constitutional / statutory authority where relevant;
- implementing decree or regulation;
- ministry or agency operational instruction;
- service portal and credential route;
- local-office, municipal, state, or implementing-body role;
- currentness clock and supersession risk;
- official-language and translation caveat;
- affected-person route for notice, correction, appeal, assistance, and records.

### 3. Treat benefits and identity as operational evidence, not digital-transformation slogans

Global South digital-government sources often highlight inclusion, scale, mobile access, identity, data integration, interoperability, or AI strategy. Those are not proof of access. For any benefit, subsidy, registry, social-assistance, identity, or document-wallet case, ask:

1. Does a person need a national identifier, digital account, phone, biometric, bank relation, local interview, or document wallet?
2. What happens when the data match says the record is stale, inconsistent, duplicate, ineligible, or incomplete?
3. Who sends notice, in what language and channel, and with what proof of receipt?
4. Where can the person cure the problem if the app, national portal, bank authentication, biometric route, or translation fails?
5. Are local offices resourced to perform the promised assisted route?
6. Is the appeal / review route tied to the source that caused the block?

### 4. Do not import Global North register assumptions

A mature English-language AI inventory is not the default comparator. In many cases the better comparator is a service page that changes weekly, a PDF informe, a legislative status page, a municipal operational bulletin, a data-protection authority consultation, or a portal FAQ. The archive should not demand the wrong artifact. It should demand the artifact that can prove the public consequence.

### 5. Keep machine translation visible

When an official website uses machine translation, or when the archive itself relies on a translation, the source-health record should say so. Keep these fields visible:

- original title and language;
- translated title if used;
- translator or machine-translation notice;
- whether the translation is official, courtesy, experimental, or archive-created;
- last checked date;
- fallback source key for the original-language page;
- claims that depend on the translated interpretation.

### 6. Join source health to claim burden

The claim ledger should be stricter for non-English official sources. A low-level service FAQ can support a service-route fact, but not a legal entitlement. A pending bill can support a legislative-currentness claim, but not an enacted rule. A ministry AI plan can support a policy-direction claim, but not an operational-safeguard claim. A source-health entry should therefore identify what kind of claim the source may safely carry.

### 7. Use local implementation as the cure for abstract inclusion

Digital-government strategies often say services will be simpler, more inclusive, interoperable, data-driven, or AI-enabled. The repair evidence is local and operational: notice comprehension, assisted route uptake, appeal outcome, payment continuity, data correction, offline fallback, disability access, language access, local-office capacity, and records retention.

## Failure modes

- **No rule by English summary**: an English digest substitutes for the authoritative-language law, decree, or service instruction.
- **No benefit by translated summary**: a translated program description is cited as proof that the person can actually apply, update, cure, appeal, or be paid.
- **No inclusion by strategy PDF**: a national digital or AI plan is treated as service-delivery proof.
- **No currentness by official domain**: a gov.br, UIDAI, ministry, or portal page is official but stale, superseded, or not the operative source.
- **No source by press release**: a news item or launch page hides the implementing rule, local workflow, or records route.
- **No remedy by portal FAQ**: an FAQ explains the service but does not create a review right, correction route, or stay of effect.
- **No authority by pending bill**: legislative proposal status is confused with enacted obligation.
- **No multilingual access by machine translation**: a machine-translated interface is treated as equivalent to legally reliable public notice.

## Anti-theater tests

1. What is the authoritative-language source, and is the cited translation legally controlling, official-but-noncontrolling, machine-generated, or archive-created?
2. Is the source a law, decree, regulation, ministry instruction, service page, FAQ, consultation, pending bill, open-data row, or local implementation guide?
3. Has the source been superseded, amended, archived, republished, or moved since the claim was made?
4. Which claim class can the source support: legal authority, service route, implementation evidence, program statistics, policy intent, consultation status, or affected-person remedy?
5. What local or frontline body actually implements the source?
6. Does the source change access to identity, benefits, payment, notice, correction, appeal, records, or data sharing?
7. What happens when a data match, credential level, biometric route, document wallet, or national identifier fails?
8. Are non-digital, assisted, disability, language, and local-office routes documented before exclusion or cancellation?
9. Are publication date, last-checked date, review cadence, and source-health risk flags visible?
10. Can the archive reconstruct the person-facing consequence without relying on an English summary?
