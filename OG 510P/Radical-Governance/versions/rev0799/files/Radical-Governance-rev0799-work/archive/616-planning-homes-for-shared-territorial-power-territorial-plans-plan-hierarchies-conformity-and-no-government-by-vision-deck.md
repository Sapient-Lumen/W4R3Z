# 616 — Planning homes for shared territorial power: territorial plans, plan hierarchies, conformity, and no government by vision deck

## One-line thesis

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, it needs an honest **planning home** as well as democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, participatory, knowledge, and service ones: a territorial planning authority with a real public plan, a declared hierarchy with municipal and state plans, visible conformity or compatibility rules, infrastructure-and-land-use coupling, amendment and review clocks, and no territorial government by advisory strategy deck.

## Why this matters

The archive can now say when shared power has become real government and what kinds of institutional home should follow. It can name the democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, participatory, knowledge, and service constitutions that honest territorial government needs.

What it still lacked was the **planning analogue**.

That absence matters because metropolitan and regional government often becomes real not only when it taxes, hires staff, or runs services, but when it starts making enduring spatial bargains: where growth goes, what infrastructure leads or follows, which landscapes are protected, which corridors get intensified, how housing targets and mobility networks line up, and how one municipality’s land-use choices are prevented from sabotaging the wider territory.

A shared territorial body that makes those bargains through strategy PDFs, informal leader agreements, maps without legal consequence, or one-off project negotiations may already be doing real government. But without a planning home, it still lacks an honest place where the territorial future is stated, argued over, amended, reviewed, and docked into the rest of the constitutional stack.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish real territorial planning from generic strategy

A metro or region should not be treated as having a planning constitution merely because it publishes a vision, growth agenda, transport strategy, or investment brochure. The archive should distinguish **real territorial planning** from generic strategy.

Real territorial planning is where government publicly states how land, infrastructure, settlement, environment, access, and major development priorities are meant to fit together across the territory over time. If the shared tier is making such choices, it needs more than slogan documents.

### 2. Give the territory one visible plan spine

Once shared territorial power is real, the archive should prefer a visible territorial plan spine: one named plan or tightly declared plan family with

- a clear territorial extent,
- a planning horizon,
- a mapped basis,
- stated policy aims,
- links to infrastructure, housing, environment, and economic development,
- and published legal or procedural status.

The point is not one giant document for everything. It is one public answer to the question: **what plan governs the shared territory?**

### 3. Declare the hierarchy among state, regional, metropolitan, and municipal plans

A planning home is dishonest when nobody can say whether municipal plans must conform to the territorial plan, merely have regard to it, remain compatible with it, or are free to ignore it. The archive should therefore prefer a declared planning hierarchy.

That hierarchy does not need to be maximally centralised. But it should say which plans set framework conditions, which plans translate them territorially, which plans remain detailed local instruments, and what happens when they conflict.

### 4. Keep local initiative, but stop local veto from unmaking the territory

An honest planning home should preserve substantial municipal planning work. Municipalities often remain the right place for neighbourhood form, local public realm, site sequencing, and fine-grained permitting choices.

But once the shared territorial tier is real, the archive should reject arrangements in which every component municipality can quietly nullify agreed corridor, housing, logistics, watershed, resilience, or network choices that only make sense at metro or regional scale.

Local initiative should survive inside a declared territorial frame, not as a standing veto over it.

### 5. Dock spatial planning to infrastructure, transport, housing, utilities, and climate adaptation

The planning home should not float as a beautiful land-use layer while the real territorial future is made elsewhere by transport agencies, utility companies, housing funds, flood authorities, or capital-budget rounds. The archive should therefore prefer planning arrangements that visibly dock land-use decisions to the infrastructure and service systems that make them real.

This is especially important for growth corridors, transit-oriented development, water risk, energy networks, green belts, logistics land, major employment zones, and managed retreat or resilience investments.

### 6. Use the real territory, not only inherited lines

A planning home should match the functional territory that is actually being shaped. The archive should therefore prefer territorial plans that declare the real geography they are planning for, even where administrative borders are historically inherited.

Sometimes the right answer is one metropolitan plan. Sometimes it is a regional plan with sub-territorial chapters. Sometimes it is a cross-border planning compact layered over several jurisdictions. But the archive should reject pretending that city limits or county lines automatically define the full spatial problem.

### 7. Give the plan implementation weight, not only narrative force

The archive should prefer a planning home that has visible implementation weight. Depending on the legal system, that may mean conformity tests, compatibility duties, mandatory consideration, linked capital-programme alignment, call-in or objection powers, project sequencing rules, or direct plan-making authority for certain classes of issue.

A plan without downstream effect is not yet an honest territorial planning constitution. It is commentary.

### 8. Put amendment, review, and sunset clocks on the planning stack

Spatial bargains are durable, but they should not become immortal. The archive should therefore prefer planning systems with visible amendment routes, scheduled review points, and update clocks that are separate from improvised crisis rewrites.

This matters because territorial plans can become stale, layered, contradictory, or strategically obsolete. Review discipline keeps the plan spine alive without forcing constant instability.

### 9. Make the planning corpus public, mappable, and challengeable

Ordinary people, developers, municipalities, agencies, and courts should be able to tell what the territorial plan is, what map or text governs, what changed, and where objections or review go. The archive should therefore prefer planning constitutions with public maps, adopted text, change logs, consultation records, and visible challenge lanes.

This is how a shared tier stops governing land and infrastructure by presentation deck, private map room, or consultant narrative.

### 10. Treat cross-border and cross-sector co-ordination as part of the planning home, not as an afterthought

Many spatial problems cross municipal, regional, or even state boundaries. Watersheds, commuting sheds, freight corridors, housing markets, energy systems, and landscape systems do not stop neatly at one line. The archive should therefore treat intergovernmental co-ordination, transboundary planning, and cross-sector integration as part of the planning constitution itself.

A planning home is incomplete if the shared tier can draw an internal territorial future but has no formal way to align that future with adjoining territories, ministries, or major infrastructure operators.

## Planning-home matrix

| Question | Thin or dishonest arrangement | Honest planning home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What plan governs the territory?** | many strategies, no authoritative plan spine | one named territorial plan or declared plan family |
| **How do plans relate across levels?** | unclear coexistence, conflict by surprise | declared hierarchy, conformity, compatibility, or regard rules |
| **How does local planning survive?** | either erased or able to veto everything | strong local initiative inside a visible territorial frame |
| **How is implementation secured?** | advisory maps and slogans only | legal/procedural weight plus capital and permitting hooks |
| **How are sectors aligned?** | transport, housing, utilities, and land use drift apart | spatial planning docks into real investment and service systems |
| **How does the plan stay current?** | ad hoc rewrites or fossilized documents | amendment routes and review clocks are declared |
| **How can the public see and challenge it?** | consultant decks, hidden GIS, fragmented notices | public text, maps, change records, and review lanes |

## Failure modes

- **government by vision deck**: the shared tier makes real territorial bargains through strategies or presentations that do not clearly govern anything.
- **map without force**: a metropolitan or regional map exists, but municipalities and agencies can ignore it without consequence.
- **territorial veto confederacy**: every component government can quietly block any shared spatial settlement, so no honest territorial plan can exist.
- **sectoral override**: transport, housing, water, energy, or economic agencies make the real spatial future outside the plan spine.
- **boundary denial**: the polity plans only inside inherited borders even where the real housing, labour, watershed, or mobility territory is wider.
- **plan sediment**: too many overlapping strategic, statutory, and corridor plans accumulate until nobody can tell which one matters.
- **review starvation**: the territorial plan becomes obsolete because the system has no practical update route.
- **private cartography**: maps, assumptions, or growth scenarios are held mainly in consultants' files, operators' systems, or closed negotiations.
- **implementation orphaning**: the shared tier can describe a territorial future but lacks any route to align budgets, permits, infrastructure timing, or development control with it.

## Practical tests

A shared territorial body has an honest planning home when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can it identify one visible territorial plan or declared plan family that governs the shared area?
2. Does that plan clearly state its geography, horizon, mapped basis, and status?
3. Is the relationship between territorial, municipal, and state plans declared rather than left to improvisation?
4. Can the system explain where local discretion remains and where territorial conformity or compatibility is required?
5. Are transport, housing, utilities, environmental limits, and major infrastructure visibly docked into the plan rather than negotiated elsewhere?
6. Does the planning geography match the real functional or ecological territory being shaped?
7. Does the plan have downstream effect through law, procedure, budgeting, permitting, or project sequencing rather than narrative influence alone?
8. Are amendment, review, and public-challenge routes explicit?
9. Can an ordinary person find the governing text, maps, recent changes, and the next place to contest or propose a change?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a metropolitan or regional tier now makes real long-term bargains about **where growth, infrastructure, protection, housing, access, and resilience should go**, but cannot plainly say **what territorial plan governs, how municipal and state plans relate to it, what weight it has, how it is reviewed, and how people can see and challenge it**, then it still lacks **a planning home for shared territorial power** and is drifting into **government by vision deck**.
