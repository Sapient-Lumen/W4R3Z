# 890 — Ecological legal personhood and rights-of-nature implementation dockets: guardian spines, remedy budgets, monitoring, and no ecological repair by personhood declaration

## One-line thesis

Recognizing a river, lagoon, forest, mountain, basin, wetland, glacier, reef, or other ecosystem as a legal person or rights-bearing subject is not ecological repair until the archive can see the guardian spine, public-owner boundary, affected-community authority, scientific baseline, remedy plan, money, enforcement path, monitoring record, liability boundary, and failure route.

## Why this matters

`864` made plural-order authority visible, but its first applied packet came from Aotearoa New Zealand and a treaty-settlement / conservation setting. That is not enough. Rights-of-nature and ecological legal-personhood cases now appear through constitutional courts, statutes, popular legislative initiatives, administrative decrees, local ordinances, and judicial orders. Some are serious institutional experiments. Some are morally powerful but operationally thin. Some risk confusing symbolic standing with ecological improvement.

The archive therefore needs a docket that can respect legal personhood without being captured by the phrase. A river can become a subject of rights while illegal mining continues. A lagoon can obtain a legal representative while nutrient loads, enforcement fragmentation, land-use incentives, and water-quality indicators remain unresolved. A court can appoint officials as guardians while jurisdiction, liability, funding, and basin-scale authority remain impracticable. A statute can create committees while scientific indicators, public records, and remedy budgets still lag.

The core distinction is this: legal personhood can be a standing device, a representation device, a participation device, a remedy device, a dignity device, a coordination device, or a constitutional-symbolic device. It is not automatically an implementation device.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Honest forms

| Form | Honest label | Primary proof question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| declaratory recognition | the ecosystem is named as a rights-bearing subject | what practical right, remedy, or representation changes today? |
| standing device | persons or guardians may sue or appear for the ecosystem | who may act, in which forum, and with what conflicts / costs? |
| guardian commission | humans represent the ecosystem through public, community, scientific, or mixed bodies | who selects guardians, how are they funded, and how do they decide? |
| biocultural-rights order | ecological harm and community rights are legally coupled | do affected communities have co-decision power or only consultation language? |
| statutory legal person | legislation grants personality and governance organs | what powers, accounts, contracts, records, and public-law duties are created? |
| administrative implementation spine | ministries, local authorities, agencies, committees, and scientists receive duties | who owns each action, date, budget, metric, and failure? |
| judicial negative control | a court order recognizes personhood but is stayed, narrowed, or unworkable | what missing jurisdiction, liability, or implementation element made it fail? |

The archive should classify the form before praising or condemning it.

### 2. Minimum ecological-personhood docket

| Field | Required proof |
| --- | --- |
| subject boundary | exact ecosystem, basin, tributaries, aquifer, catchment, buffer, or living whole covered by the recognition |
| rights catalog | existence, protection, conservation, maintenance, restoration, regeneration, no-pollution, cultural, participation, or procedural rights |
| guardian spine | legal representative, community representative, scientific body, public authority, citizen guardian, and decision rule |
| public-owner boundary | which state duties remain with ministries, municipalities, police, prosecutors, regulators, courts, or park agencies |
| community authority | Indigenous, ethnic, riparian, municipal, fisher, farmer, resident, worker, and user lanes; consent and conflict rules |
| scientific baseline | state of the ecosystem, indicators, monitoring frequency, data publication, and uncertainty / precaution rule |
| remedy plan | decontamination, restoration, illegal-activity suppression, land-use correction, enforcement, compensation, or prevention actions |
| money and capacity | budget, staff, equipment, technical assistance, enforcement capacity, and long-term maintenance funding |
| forum and standing | who can sue, intervene, request information, trigger enforcement, correct records, or demand review |
| liability boundary | who can be liable for harm, who is not made impossibly liable, and what duties personhood does not create |
| record spine | public minutes, plans, indicators, permits, enforcement actions, budgets, lawsuits, compliance reports, and dissent records |
| failure route | escalation, injunction, contempt, audit, parliamentary review, guardian replacement, or downshift if the personhood frame fails |

A beautiful rights catalog without these fields is an ecological aspiration, not a governance form.

### 3. Legal personhood is not the same thing as authority

Personhood can make an ecosystem legible to law. It does not by itself answer who may regulate land use, stop mining, enforce pollution law, fund restoration, prosecute crime, alter concessions, manage agriculture, relocate infrastructure, change water allocations, or compensate communities.

So the archive should always ask:

1. Is the ecosystem now a rights-holder?
2. Is someone authorized to speak for it?
3. Is someone authorized to act for it?
4. Is someone funded to repair it?
5. Is someone legally bound to stop harming it?
6. Is someone empowered to enforce that duty?
7. Can affected communities contest what is done in the ecosystem's name?
8. Can the public see whether conditions improve?

If the answer stops at question 2, the arrangement is thin.

### 4. Biocultural-rights caution

Biocultural rights are powerful because they reject the false split between ecosystem and community. They also create a risk: the river's voice can be used to override the people who live with the river, or community language can be used to hide state inaction.

The docket should therefore separate ecosystem rights, community territorial and cultural rights, public environmental duties, illegal-activity enforcement, scientific monitoring, ordinary users' rights and obligations, and cross-boundary basin governance. Do not let one lane launder another.

### 5. Guardian capture

A guardian can be captured through appointment, funding, expertise, litigation costs, ministerial vetoes, local elite dominance, NGO substitution, donor dependence, or scientific monopoly. The archive should treat guardianship as a public-power waist.

Minimum anti-capture questions:

- Who appoints the guardian?
- Who can remove the guardian?
- Who pays the guardian?
- Who audits the guardian?
- Can affected communities dissent from the guardian?
- Can the guardian sue the state?
- Can the guardian oppose a permit, concession, procurement, or enforcement decision?
- Are guardian conflicts published?
- Are scientific indicators independent?
- Are minutes and compliance records public?

A guardian that cannot act against the main source of harm is a ceremonial face.

### 6. Rights without indicators

An ecosystem right is not administratively intelligible until there is an ecological baseline and an indicator set. The archive should require pre-recognition condition, legal threshold or desired condition, monitoring method, frequency, data owner, public dashboard or report, uncertainty label, causal attribution method, and trigger for enforcement or remedial escalation.

A right to restoration without restoration metrics is a slogan with standing.

### 7. Remedy-budget rule

The archive should not accept personhood as a substitute for repair money. If the harm is mining, nutrients, sewage, deforestation, illegal armed activity, over-extraction, dredging, industrial discharge, landfill runoff, agricultural runoff, invasive species, or climate stress, the docket needs a budget and implementation path.

Minimum remedy-budget fields are remedial action, responsible body, cost estimate, funding source, start date, completion clock, community role, enforcement dependency, public evidence of progress, and consequence for nonperformance.

Without this, the ecosystem has rights but not repair.

### 8. Negative-control rule

Some legal-personhood orders fail because the guardianship assignment is impracticable, the liability frame is too broad, the ecosystem crosses jurisdictions not heard in court, the state officials appointed as guardians cannot practically control the whole basin, or the order does not say how existing authorities, budgets, and enforcement routes change.

Those failures are not arguments against all rights-of-nature work. They are evidence that ecological personhood must be built as an implementation docket, not merely announced.

### 10. Claim-status rule

Each ecological-personhood packet should split at least six claims:

1. **recognition claim** — what legal status was granted;
2. **representation claim** — who can speak or sue for the ecosystem;
3. **implementation claim** — what concrete duties and plans exist;
4. **capacity claim** — whether money, staff, enforcement, and access exist;
5. **ecological-effect claim** — what indicators show improvement or deterioration;
6. **rights-boundary claim** — how ecosystem rights interact with community, property, public-access, livelihood, and state duties.

Do not let recognition claims impersonate ecological-effect claims.

## Anti-theater tests

This docket fails when legal personhood is treated as repair without guardian independence, standing, public records, ecological indicators, remedy budget, enforcement path, community authority, state-duty retention, and failure escalation. It passes when the rights-bearing ecosystem can be represented, monitored, defended, funded, repaired, contested, and audited without turning the guardian into a ceremonial chokepoint.
