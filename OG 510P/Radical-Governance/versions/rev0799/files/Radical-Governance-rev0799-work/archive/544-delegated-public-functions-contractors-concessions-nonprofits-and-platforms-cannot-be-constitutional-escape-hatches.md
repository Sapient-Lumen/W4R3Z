# 544 — Delegated public functions: contractors, concessions, nonprofits, and platforms cannot become constitutional escape hatches

## One-line thesis

An ideal polity may delegate bounded execution to contractors, concessionaires, nonprofit operators, co-operatives, or digital platforms when doing so is genuinely useful, but it should never outsource the state’s continuing duties to set rights-respecting rules, preserve public capacity, keep money and risk legible, maintain records and remedies, guarantee continuity, and retake control when delegation fails; public obligations should follow the public function rather than disappearing into private legal form.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a stronger account of what governments at different scales should be, how those levels should coordinate, and when special-purpose public authorities are justified. What it still needed was one compact note on the institutions that often govern public life **without even being public bodies**.

Much real government now runs through contracts, concessions, grant-funded nonprofit operators, chartered intermediaries, managed-service vendors, social landlords, private utilities, digital platforms, and other hybrid arrangements. Sometimes that is sensible. Often it is camouflage. Hard constitutional questions about price, access, labour standards, coercion, records, due process, data, service continuity, and democratic responsibility are pushed behind procurement language or partnership rhetoric, as if a change in legal wrapper changed the public nature of the task.

That mistake is costly. It produces a state that remains politically answerable for outcomes while becoming administratively less capable, less legible, and less able to intervene. It also invites a familiar trick: keep the public obligations thin, keep the private discretion thick, and call the result innovation.

An ideal government therefore needs a doctrine of **delegated public function**. The right answer is neither absolute in-house dogma nor market romanticism. It is a firebreak doctrine: delegate only bounded execution; keep constitutional duties, fiscal honesty, continuity power, and remedy routes visibly public; and treat every delegated arrangement as something that must remain governable, inspectable, and reversible.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Delegate bounded execution, not the constitutional core

A polity may contract for construction, maintenance, logistics, software, food service, call-centre work, diagnostics, transit operations, social-service delivery, or other bounded tasks. But it should be far more reluctant to delegate the constitutional core: coercive force, detention, final adjudication of rights, compulsory surveillance, binding rule-making, final eligibility decisions without public override, or any function where the state would be unable to explain who is answerable when things go wrong.

The more a function touches coercion, status, liberty, or irreplaceable rights access, the stronger the presumption that public authority itself — not merely a contract manager — must remain visibly in charge.

### 2. Use delegation only after an honest make-buy-partner test

Delegation should not happen because budgets are tight, headcount caps are fashionable, or officials want difficult decisions off the books. Before delegating, the polity should ask a simple question: what public purpose is actually served by this form rather than direct provision, a public authority, an intergovernmental compact, or a simpler regulated market?

The comparison should include not just sticker price but capability loss, vendor lock-in, contingent liabilities, data dependence, labour effects, resilience, exit difficulty, and the political cost of losing direct operating knowledge.

### 3. Keep public duties attached to the function, not the provider label

If the function is public in substance, core public obligations should travel with it. That means rights floors, non-discrimination, records, audit, complaint handling, data protection, accessibility, procurement integrity, transparency where appropriate, and effective review should not evaporate because the person answering the phone, running the clinic, maintaining the software, or operating the network is legally private or nonprofit.

The state may distribute work, but it should not distribute away duty.

### 4. Write hard contracts and charters that cover power, money, data, standards, and exit

Delegation should rest on public instruments strong enough to govern reality, not ceremonial statements of intent. The governing instrument should specify:

- the exact function and service standard;
- who sets policy, who executes, and who can overrule;
- pricing, subsidy, indexation, and tariff rules;
- labour, safety, and accessibility baselines;
- records, audit, and data-governance duties;
- subcontracting limits and disclosure duties;
- continuity, step-in, and handback obligations;
- open standards, interoperability, and transfer requirements where relevant;
- conflict-of-interest rules;
- and the grounds for suspension, termination, takeover, or non-renewal.

If these things are missing, the arrangement is probably not a governed delegation but an improvised surrender.

### 5. Retain in-house capability to commission, monitor, challenge, and resume

A government that delegates everything and retains only invoice approval is not governing; it is sponsoring dependency. The public side should keep enough internal expertise to specify the service, inspect claims, model demand, verify quality, audit cost, challenge performance, retender intelligently, and resume control if necessary.

This matters especially in local government, where outsourcing often erodes the very operational knowledge needed to supervise contractors. Delegation without retained capability is the administrative version of selling your instruments and promising to keep conducting.

### 6. Keep fiscal reality visible, especially long-tail and contingent costs

Concessions and PPPs can hide public obligations inside guarantees, minimum-revenue promises, foreign-exchange protections, handback liabilities, or opaque change orders. Grant-funded nonprofits and platform intermediaries can do something similar through mission drift, emergency bailout expectations, or dependency on single providers.

An ideal polity should therefore disclose the full fiscal shape of delegated arrangements: direct payments, subsidies, contingent liabilities, guarantees, termination costs, rescue risk, asset condition, and the estimated cost of bringing the function back in house or moving it elsewhere.

No delegation should look cheap only because the expensive parts have been moved into the future or into a footnote.

### 7. Publish the authority map so the public can tell who actually decides what

Hybrid governance becomes abusive when ordinary people cannot tell whether a failure belongs to a department, a contractor, a nonprofit operator, a regulator, a municipality, or a platform. Every delegated public function should therefore have a plain-language authority map: who sets policy, who pays, who runs the front line, who holds the data, who hears complaints, who can grant an exception, and who can terminate or replace the operator.

Public confusion should be treated as a governance failure, not as an inevitable side effect of modern service delivery.

### 8. Preserve continuity, step-in power, and public recovery paths

If a delegated operator fails, becomes insolvent, refuses co-operation, suffers a cyber incident, or exits a market, the public should not discover that the state outsourced the only practical way to keep the service alive. Essential functions need continuity rules: asset registers, escrow or handover provisions where justified, interoperable data, transition playbooks, staff continuity planning, emergency operating rights, and the legal capacity for the public side to step in.

A delegation model that works only while the supplier remains benevolent and solvent is not a public-service model. It is a gamble.

### 9. Treat “partnership” language as a warning sign when power is uneven

Not every arrangement called a partnership is reciprocal. Many are ordinary delegations wrapped in flattering language. The archive should therefore be suspicious whenever “partnership” blurs who is principal, who is agent, who bears downside risk, or who can actually say no.

Real co-production with communities, co-operatives, or civic organisations can be valuable. But the more essential the service and the more unequal the bargaining power, the more the polity should write the arrangement as accountable delegation rather than as a feel-good collaboration story.

### 10. Review periodically and be willing to de-delegate

Delegated arrangements should not survive forever on inertia. The polity should periodically test whether the delegated form still serves the public interest, whether the operator has accumulated unacceptable leverage, whether costs remain honest, whether rights protections are working in practice, and whether direct provision, a public authority, a concession redesign, or a different operator would now be better.

The goal is not ideological renationalisation or ideological privatization. It is institutional reversibility. A government should be able to change its mind without first suffering collapse.

## Guardrails

- Do not outsource constitutional duties and then pretend only execution was delegated.
- Do not let private or nonprofit legal form dissolve public-law obligations.
- Do not rely on delegation where the public side lacks the capability to specify, inspect, and resume.
- Do not treat contingent liabilities, guarantees, or bailouts as somebody else’s budget.
- Do not allow platform interfaces or vendor control of data to become de facto veto power over public policy.
- Do not use “partnership” language to hide principal-agent reality.

## Failure modes

- **hollow state**: the government remains blamed for outcomes but lacks the operational knowledge to correct them.
- **contract constitutionalism**: rights, prices, and service access are governed more by procurement clauses than by visible public law.
- **lock-in by design**: data, switching costs, custom infrastructure, or monopoly interfaces make exit politically or technically unrealistic.
- **off-balance-sheet politics**: PPPs, guarantees, rescue expectations, or deferred maintenance make a project look affordable by hiding its real public cost.
- **remedy void**: affected people get bounced between ministry, contractor, regulator, and platform because nobody owns the complaint end to end.
- **philanthropic capture or platform dependency**: nonprofit or platform operators quietly shape public priorities because the state no longer has a comparable delivery channel of its own.

## Practical tests

A delegated public-function architecture passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is the public case for delegating this function stronger than the case for direct provision, a public authority, or another public form?
2. Are constitutional floors, rights duties, records, remedies, and oversight routes explicitly preserved rather than implied?
3. Does the public side retain enough in-house capability to specify, monitor, challenge, and if needed resume the function?
4. Are fiscal commitments, guarantees, contingent liabilities, and exit costs visible enough to prevent fake affordability?
5. Can an ordinary person tell who sets policy, who delivers, who holds the data, and where to go for review or remedy?
6. Is there a real continuity and step-in plan if the operator fails, exits, or refuses co-operation?
7. Is there a scheduled review point at which the polity can redesign, retender, publicise, municipalise, or end the arrangement without institutional panic?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a government can no longer guarantee rights, continuity, remedy, or intelligible authority once a service is contracted, conceded, or platform-mediated, it has not merely delegated delivery. It has **outsourced governability**.
