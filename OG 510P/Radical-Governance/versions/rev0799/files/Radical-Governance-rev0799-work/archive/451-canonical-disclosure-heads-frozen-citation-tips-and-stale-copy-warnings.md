# 451 — Canonical disclosure heads, frozen citation tips, and stale-copy warnings

## One-line thesis

If consequential public-AI disclosures exist in multiple versions, mirrors, excerpts, or derived summaries, the institution should publish one canonical current head, preserve citation-frozen heads for historical reference, and visibly warn when a stale or branched copy is no longer authority-bearing.

## Why this matters

Public-sector AI governance increasingly depends on outward-facing artifacts: transparency records, system cards, impact-summary pages, appeal guidance, peer-review summaries, and change notices. Those artifacts drift. They are copied into PDFs, cached in portals, quoted in decks, mirrored into procurement files, excerpted into notices, and translated into public FAQs. Once that happens, the institution may still have *records* but lose a more important property: a stable answer to which disclosure is the current governing surface and which older surface remains preserved only for historical citation.

Without that distinction, transparency becomes easier to cite but harder to trust. A reviewer may rely on a stale system card. An affected person may be routed by an old appeal path. A regulator may read a mirrored summary that no longer matches the live approval basis. The archive should therefore require explicit disclosure heads: one current authority-bearing head where applicable, one or more frozen citation heads where needed, and clear warnings when a copy is superseded, branched, partial, or no longer safe to treat as current institutional position.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish current authority-bearing heads from citation-frozen heads

For each consequential disclosure family, the institution should be able to answer:

- which artifact is the current authoritative public head,
- which artifacts are frozen historical heads preserved for citation,
- whether both point to the same underlying basis,
- and whether more than one live candidate exists.

This distinction should be explicit rather than inferred from timestamps or guesswork.

### 2. Publish a compact head register for public governance artifact families

Artifact families that commonly need head tracking include:

- system cards,
- transparency-record entries,
- public impact-assessment summaries,
- notice-and-rights pages,
- peer-review or assurance summaries,
- public incident or corrective-action pages,
- and supplier or dependency disclosure pages where the public basis depends on them.

A compact head register should identify the family, the current head, any frozen historical heads, and the reason a head changed.

### 3. Warn visibly on stale, mirrored, or branched copies

A stale or non-canonical copy should not quietly continue looking official. It should visibly indicate where it stands, for example:

- superseded by newer head,
- frozen for citation only,
- excerpt or summary only,
- branch-specific and not the general live disclosure,
- mirror copy maintained for access or continuity,
- or unresolved head ambiguity under active correction.

The archive should prefer visible ambiguity over false authority.

### 4. Keep historical citations stable without confusing them for current authority

Historical review often needs to cite exactly what the institution said at a prior time. That means older heads should remain reachable and stable enough to cite, but should not impersonate the live governing disclosure. A good archive preserves both:

- citation stability for old decisions, reviews, and public claims,
- and a clear route to the current head for present governance.

### 5. Treat material basis change as a head-change trigger

A new canonical head should be considered when the underlying basis changes materially, including:

- approval or release state,
- model, rule, or threshold basis,
- intended use or no-use boundaries,
- appeal route,
- supplier or dependency status,
- incident status or corrective-action posture,
- or public access/exposure boundaries.

This keeps disclosure heads coupled to real governance change rather than cosmetic publishing habits.

### 6. Bind summaries and excerpts back to the head they depend on

A summary page, FAQ excerpt, dashboard tile, or notice snippet should identify the head it derives from. Otherwise a downstream surface can silently continue after the governing disclosure has moved. Derived surfaces should either:

- resolve to the current head,
- name the frozen head they summarize,
- or warn that they are partial and not authority-bearing.

### 7. Preserve unresolved head ambiguity as an explicit governance problem

If two teams publish conflicting “current” cards, or if an approval packet, registry entry, and public notice do not point to the same head, the archive should record that as a live defect. The right answer is not to let users infer authority from whichever PDF looks newest.

## Guardrails

- Do not force reviewers or the public to guess which disclosure is current.
- Do not destroy historical citability in order to create a cleaner present surface.
- Do not let mirrored or excerpted copies silently inherit current authority.
- Do not let material changes leave the old head looking live.
- Do not hide head ambiguity behind informal staff knowledge.

## Failure modes

- **floating authority**: several disclosures look current and nobody can say which one governs.
- **citation trap**: a historically frozen document is mistaken for the live disclosure.
- **stale mirror drift**: copied artifacts remain public after the governing head changed.
- **summary overreach**: an excerpt or FAQ starts standing in for the full authority-bearing record.
- **branch confusion**: local or temporary variants are mistaken for the general live position.

## Practical tests

A canonical-disclosure-head discipline passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does each consequential public artifact family have an identifiable current head?
2. Are historical heads preserved and marked as frozen for citation where needed?
3. Do stale, mirrored, branched, or partial copies warn visibly that they are not the live authority-bearing head?
4. Is head change tied to material basis change rather than only to editorial convenience?
5. Can a reviewer move from any derivative surface to the head it depends on?

## Compression rule for the archive

If nobody can say **which disclosure is current, which is frozen for citation, and whether the answer is unique**, then the transparency surface still lacks a **canonical head**.
