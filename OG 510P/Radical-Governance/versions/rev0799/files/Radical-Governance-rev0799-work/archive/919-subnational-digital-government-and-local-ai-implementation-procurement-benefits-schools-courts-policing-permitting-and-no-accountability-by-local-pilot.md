# 919 — Subnational digital government and local AI implementation, procurement, benefits, schools, courts, policing, permitting, and no accountability by local pilot

## One-line thesis

State, provincial, municipal, school, court, police, and local-service AI must be governed at the implementation seam where procurement, public records, local authority, staff practice, affected-person routes, and service consequences meet; a local pilot, policy template, or inventory row is never enough to prove accountability.

## Why this matters

The archive has grown strong central-government muscles: federal inventories, national guidance, agency source health, procurement, identity gates, benefits, redress, model decisions, public registers, staff copilots, and watchlist / border automation. The missing seam is where most people actually meet government: city call centers, county benefits offices, police and corrections systems, building and permitting workflows, schools, courts, public-health units, emergency management, transportation, housing, and public records desks.

Subnational AI is riskier than central doctrine because it fragments quickly. A state may have a statute; a city may have an algorithmic-tools report; a school system may have nonbinding guidance; a court may adopt a model policy; a police department may buy a vendor service; a county may use a chatbot or meeting summarizer; a procurement office may add AI clauses; and a public-records officer may be left to decide whether prompts, outputs, logs, screenshots, and vendor configuration files are records. Those pieces can all be present and still fail if no one can prove what was actually deployed, by whom, under what authority, on what data, with what public effect, and with what correction route.

The anti-theater rule is therefore **no accountability by local pilot**. Pilot status can be useful, but it must not become a shield against notice, records, procurement controls, risk assessment, staff training, appeal, public metrics, or shutdown receipts.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Split the jurisdictional layer before scoring governance

Local and subnational AI governance needs a layer map before any row, policy, or vendor claim is credited.

| Layer | Live question |
| --- | --- |
| state / province / territory | Is there a statute, executive policy, central inventory, attorney-general rule, CIO standard, education policy, court rule, or procurement condition? |
| county / municipality | Which agency, office, contractor, board, or elected official owns the workflow and public record? |
| school district / local educational agency | Is the AI use instructional, administrative, assessment-facing, discipline-related, safety-related, or student-data-facing? |
| court / tribunal | Is the use judicial, adjudicative, administrative, self-help, evidence-facing, translation-facing, or staff productivity only? |
| policing / emergency / corrections | Is the output a lead, alert, risk score, evidence tool, dispatch recommendation, camera / biometric workflow, or records-management aid? |
| permitting / inspection / licensing | Does the system prioritize, score, route, prefill, recommend, or deny an application or enforcement action? |
| benefits / housing / health / public assistance | Does the system screen, verify, triage, detect fraud, recommend eligibility, route cases, issue notices, or trigger holds? |
| local procurement / platform service | Did AI enter through a cloud suite, SaaS feature, vendor update, chatbot, meeting tool, data platform, or case-management add-on? |

A policy without the layer map often hides the actor that can actually stop, correct, or explain the tool.

### 2. Do not let pilots escape operational controls

A pilot can be lower-risk because it is bounded. It can also be higher-risk because it is less mature. Classify it by consequence, data, authority, and rollback, not by the word “pilot.” A public-service pilot should have:

- pilot owner and authority;
- bounded population, geography, period, data source, staff role, and output role;
- exclusion rules for consequential decisions if the evidence is not ready;
- user notice where feasible;
- public-records and retention plan;
- privacy, civil-rights, accessibility, security, and procurement review;
- incident, complaint, and correction route;
- metrics for benefits and harms;
- exit, scale, pause, or termination criteria;
- data / model / output retention and deletion receipt.

If a pilot touches liberty, benefits, education, housing, health, courts, policing, immigration, licensing, or emergency response, treat “small scale” as an audit trigger, not as a waiver.

### 3. Join inventories to procurement and records

Subnational AI is often discovered through the wrong surface: a press release, contract amendment, staff memo, budget line, policy template, dataset, GitHub repo, open-data row, or vendor marketing claim. The archive should require a procurement-to-inventory-to-records crosswalk:

- procurement file and vendor / platform owner;
- inventory row or no-use certification;
- public-records retention class for prompts, outputs, logs, evaluations, transcripts, datasets, and configuration;
- privacy, security, accessibility, and civil-rights review;
- staff role and training record;
- impacted-service workflow;
- public notice or lawful non-disclosure reason;
- complaint / appeal / correction path;
- decommissioning and residual-data route.

A contract that buys “analytics,” “AI assistant,” “automated triage,” “translation,” “fraud detection,” “case management,” or “predictive” functions should not sit outside the AI inventory just because the inventory team is separate from procurement.

### 4. Treat public records as a governance surface

For local governments, public records are not a downstream transparency afterthought. They are often the only practical way a person, journalist, lawyer, auditor, or councilmember can reconstruct what happened. The records plan should answer:

1. Are prompts, chat histories, meeting transcripts, summaries, generated drafts, vendor outputs, model evaluations, risk assessments, incident reports, and correction logs records?
2. Which retention schedule applies to each artifact?
3. Who has custody when the artifact lives in a vendor platform?
4. What is the export route for audit, disclosure, litigation, and appeal?
5. How are confidential, student, health, law-enforcement, sealed-court, or personnel records segregated?
6. What happens when an AI feature is disabled, a vendor leaves, or a pilot ends?

If the system cannot preserve the record, it cannot safely support a public decision.

### 5. Separate policy adoption from operating evidence

Local governments increasingly adopt AI policies, playbooks, templates, inventories, sandboxes, councils, task forces, and trainings. Those are useful only if they connect to operating evidence:

| Policy artifact | Evidence needed before credit |
| --- | --- |
| AI principles | workflow-specific authority and risk acceptance |
| public inventory | procurement, internal inventory, and records reconciliation |
| model policy | adopted local version, accountable owner, and training / enforcement record |
| sandbox | boundary, test data, barred uses, and exit rule |
| AI task force | decisions, unresolved issues, adoption status, and dissent / public input |
| vendor questionnaire | contract terms, monitoring rights, data exit, audit rights, and incident routing |
| staff guidance | actual usage logs, approved tools, prohibited uses, and supervision |
| public dashboard | row history, correction latency, and affected-service metrics |

The archive should never let “we adopted a policy” substitute for “the policy changed how this tool was used, monitored, corrected, or stopped.”

### 6. Put schools and courts on their own tracks

Schools and courts are not generic administrative offices.

For schools, classify AI by instructional support, student work, grading / assessment, special education, discipline, surveillance / safety, counseling, translation, staff productivity, procurement, and student-data sharing. Nonbinding state guidance is not enough; local educational agencies need policies that resolve parental notice, student rights, accommodations, academic integrity, data privacy, vendor access, equity, and teacher professional judgment.

For courts, split adjudicative use, judicial research, administrative drafting, self-help, transcript / translation tools, scheduling, evidence review, risk tools, and public-facing chatbots. Court AI must protect confidentiality, privacy, bias controls, accuracy, hallucination review, disclosure, judge / staff supervision, record integrity, access to justice, and the difference between internal administration and adjudication.

### 7. Give policing, inspection, and permitting heightened false-positive tests

Local automation can create high-volume small harms: missed housing inspections, permit delays, public-benefit holds, police attention, license denials, school discipline flags, court access barriers, or emergency triage errors. The tests should ask not merely whether the tool is accurate in aggregate, but whether the person can see, contest, and cure the operational consequence.

A lead, match, risk score, priority queue, or chatbot answer should not become public action without a human authority, evidence packet, correction route, and false-positive / false-negative metric.

### 8. Use state law as an implementation clock, not as proof

Subnational statutes and rules can be strong. They also create implementation risk: agencies need forms, definitions, inventories, procurements, training, impact assessments, public statements, appeals, and enforcement. A new state AI law should therefore open a clock:

- what systems are in scope;
- who is a developer, deployer, agency, school, court, contractor, or affected person;
- when inventories, impact assessments, notices, appeals, and public statements are due;
- what older tools must do;
- what exemptions or sector-specific rules apply;
- how enforcement, cure, and private or public complaints work;
- which local governments and public authorities are in or out.

The law is the trigger; implementation evidence is the governance surface.

## Findings table

| Risk | Bad shortcut | Repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| local pilot | pilot label waives controls | bounded authority, records, metrics, rollback, and affected-person route |
| state inventory | no-use or row count treated as truth | nonresponse, excluded branches, procurement, and internal reconciliation |
| city register | transparency row treated as risk control | link row to owner, use state, records, monitoring, complaints, and correction |
| school guidance | nonbinding guidance treated as local policy | local adopted policy, student-data terms, parental notice, and teacher oversight |
| court model policy | generic policy treated as adjudicative repair | adopted rule, confidentiality controls, disclosure, accuracy review, and public record |
| vendor AI feature | SaaS update bypasses inventory | procurement, configuration, data-use, and audit-right crosswalk |
| public records | records plan omitted until dispute | retention/export/classification before deployment |
| policing / permitting lead | lead becomes action | authority, evidence packet, human confirmation, and redress route |

## Failure modes

- **Pilot exceptionalism**: experimental status is used to avoid notice, assessment, records, appeal, and shutdown criteria.
- **Inventory overconfidence**: a state or municipal report is treated as complete despite nonresponse, excluded branches, scope definitions, and vendor-feature blind spots.
- **Procurement invisibility**: AI enters through software suites, professional services, analytics, chatbots, meeting tools, or platform updates without register linkage.
- **Records amnesia**: prompts, outputs, transcripts, logs, and model evaluations disappear before audit or affected-person review.
- **School policy gap**: state guidance exists but local policies do not resolve student data, grading, discipline, accommodations, or parental notice.
- **Court confidentiality drift**: staff productivity or self-help AI leaks confidential, sealed, privileged, or adjudicative material.
- **Local false-positive churn**: triage, risk scores, alerts, inspection queues, or chatbots create small harms too diffuse to trigger formal redress.
- **State-law implementation theater**: impact assessment, appeal, public statement, and risk-management obligations are cited before the implementation machinery exists.
- **Fragmented public accountability**: councils, agencies, courts, districts, and vendors each hold one part of the evidence and no one owns the whole case.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Can the local actor name the exact workflow, public authority, accountable owner, affected population, output role, and lifecycle state?
2. Does the pilot have a start, stop, scale, rollback, incident, complaint, and deletion / retention rule?
3. Can procurement records, vendor features, platform configurations, inventory rows, and public-records schedules be reconciled?
4. Are prompts, outputs, logs, generated transcripts, risk assessments, model evaluations, notices, and correction records retained or exportable?
5. Does the public-facing row or policy link to monitoring, incident, redress, and correction evidence?
6. For schools, are student data, grading, discipline, accommodations, parental notice, academic integrity, and teacher judgment resolved locally?
7. For courts, are confidentiality, privacy, bias, hallucination review, disclosure, adjudicative boundaries, and record integrity resolved by adopted policy?
8. For policing, permitting, inspection, housing, benefits, and public health, can the person contest or cure a consequential output?
9. Does state law or city ordinance have implementation artifacts, deadlines, owners, and enforcement / cure routes rather than only principles?
10. Are benefits and harms reported together, including false positives, false negatives, appeal outcomes, correction latency, and affected-service metrics?
