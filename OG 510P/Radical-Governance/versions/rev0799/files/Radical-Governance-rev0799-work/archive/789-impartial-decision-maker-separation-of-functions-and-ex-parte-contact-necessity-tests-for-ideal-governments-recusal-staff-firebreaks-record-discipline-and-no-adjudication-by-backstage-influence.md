# 789 — Impartial decision-maker, separation-of-functions, and ex parte-contact necessity tests for ideal governments: recusal, staff firebreaks, record discipline, and no adjudication by backstage influence

## One-line thesis

Ideal governments should ensure that **administrative decisions are made by people and processes insulated enough from partisan, investigative, prosecutorial, commercial, and personal bias to be trusted**: require recusal for personal interest, maintain workable separation between case-building and deciding roles, ban or sharply discipline off-record merits communications, and force decisive inputs onto the file rather than allowing adjudication by backstage influence.

## Why this matters

The archive now says that people must be heard (`786`), comprehend the procedure (`787`), receive fair evidence treatment (`785`), and have access to review, interim relief, and execution (`773`, `779`, `777`). What it still lacked as one compact operating rule was the fairness question inside the state itself:

**who is allowed to decide, advise the decider, or communicate about the merits, and under what firebreaks?**

Administrative systems often promise neutrality while organizing themselves for something quite different. The inspector who built the case also drafts the “independent” decision. The enforcement unit privately briefs the decision-maker after the hearing closes. A ministerial office, political adviser, major stakeholder, local notable, or senior manager contacts the decision team off record. The appeal goes to the same official who signed the first act. Or the system says there is no problem because the final signature is formally separate, even though the decisive internal advice never entered the file and cannot be tested.

There is an equal and opposite mistake. Some systems imagine that every administrative decision must mimic a full court hierarchy, which can make routine administration unworkably slow. Not every benefits correction, licensing update, inspection follow-up, or compliance determination needs a wall between every operational function. But where sanctions, contested facts, reputational harm, license loss, professional discipline, or other serious adverse outcomes are on the line, administrative efficiency is not a license for backstage adjudication.

Ideal-government design therefore needs a doctrine of **impartiality architecture**. The point is not to constitutionalize maximum bureaucratic distance in every desk procedure. It is to type the necessary firebreaks: recusal for personal interest; separation between investigative/prosecutorial and deciding roles where fairness requires; recorded staff advice rather than hidden merits steering; disciplined handling of ex parte contacts; clean rules for internal reconsideration and appeal; and an honest record rule so later review can see what actually shaped the outcome.

## Necessity table

| Necessity test | What counts as proof | Honest design consequence | Default answer if proof is weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Need to avoid personal bias and apparent bias** | decision-makers have personal, family, financial, political, or antagonistic ties to the matter | require disclosure, recusal, substitution, and challenge routes | rely on internal honor without visible conflict rules |
| **Need to prevent case-builders from silently becoming judges of their own case** | inspectors, investigators, prosecutors, or enforcement staff shape or decide contested merits after building the case | separate adverse-case construction from decision responsibility where stakes justify it | let the same official or office prosecute and decide |
| **Need to stop backstage merits influence** | parties, interested outsiders, political offices, or internal staff can communicate off record with deciders about the merits | prohibit or tightly regulate ex parte communications and force them onto the record if they occur | tolerate invisible influence so long as no formal order documents it |
| **Need to distinguish lawful procedural management from unlawful merits steering** | status checks, scheduling, translation logistics, or settlement administration can be confused with merits contact | type allowed administrative contacts and keep them separate from substantive advice | either ban all contact or allow disguised merits lobbying |
| **Need to protect internal appeal and second-look honesty** | review is routed to the same person or same committed chain without real independence of judgment | require a different decision-maker or materially different review lane | call rubber-stamping a remedy |
| **Need to preserve usable administration for routine, low-stakes matters** | some matters do not justify full structural separation | scale firebreak intensity to adversity, sanction, complexity, and stigma | over-judicialize every trivial decision or under-protect major ones |
| **Need to make decisive advice reviewable** | internal legal or technical advice effectively determines outcome but never appears in the case record | require disclosed reasoning, summary placement on record, or identifiable advisory channels | let hidden memoranda govern the result |
| **Need to prevent political override of quasi-adjudicative decisions** | ministers, mayors, agency heads, or party actors can informally steer individual outcomes | limit intervention, require public responsibility if lawful override exists, and preserve review | normalize intervention as routine management |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Begin with visible impartiality, not only actual sincerity

A system is not fair merely because officials believe they are fair. It should be designed to avoid both actual bias and the appearance of bias. Personal financial interests, family ties, close friendship, hostility, prior public commitment, or direct involvement in the earlier decision or investigation should trigger recusal or substitution.

### 2. Separate adverse-case construction from adverse-case decision when the stakes justify it

Where the state investigates, inspects, prosecutes, or otherwise builds an adverse case that may end in sanction, deprivation, discipline, license loss, serious compliance burden, or reputational stigma, the person deciding the contested merits should not simply be the same official or immediate case-building chain. The stronger the coercion or controversy, the stronger the separation duty.

### 3. Do not confuse staff support with invisible merits steering

Decision-makers often need clerical, scheduling, legal, or technical support. That is not itself improper. But systems should distinguish logistical or legal support from hidden recommendations on contested merits. If internal advice materially shapes the outcome, later review should be able to tell that it existed, who produced it, and whether the affected person had a chance to answer the decisive points.

### 4. Ban or tightly discipline ex parte merits communications

Communications about the merits with one side, interested outsiders, or undisclosed internal adversaries should generally be prohibited once a contested matter is live. If such contact occurs, the system should force immediate disclosure, place the substance on the record, give the other side a chance to respond, and consider whether recusal, cure, or restart is necessary. Status or scheduling communication should not become a loophole for private persuasion.

### 5. Keep a typed difference between routine administration and quasi-adjudication

Not every office needs courtroom-grade separation. Purely beneficial, low-risk, mass-routine, or clerical matters can often be handled within ordinary line management so long as conflict rules and reasons remain intact. But once a matter becomes adverse, contested, sanction-linked, or reputationally weighty, the administration should move it into a more protected lane.

### 6. Make challenge and recusal routes usable before final decision where possible

People should have a way to raise impartiality concerns before the process is over: challenge a conflicted official, request reassignment, object to undisclosed communications, or ask that a reviewing body decide whether the decision-maker can continue. Waiting until final appeal to mention an obvious bias problem is often too late.

### 7. Keep appeal and reconsideration lanes genuinely second-look

A reconsideration or internal appeal should not normally be routed to the exact same official who made the first contested decision. Even within the same institution, a different officer, panel, or higher authority should conduct the second look where adversity is real. Otherwise the state is just rereading its own certainty.

### 8. Preserve a record rule strong enough to expose hidden determinants

If a decision depends on inspection notes, technical memos, legal advice, stakeholder communications, or internal escalation, the record should reveal enough of that reliance for review to be meaningful. A record that contains only the final order and selected evidence, while the decisive pressure lives elsewhere, is not a fairness record.

### 9. Type lawful override power honestly if it exists

Some systems may lawfully permit ministerial call-in, agency-head review, or hierarchical correction in certain categories. If that power exists, it should be express, bounded, recorded, reasoned, and reviewable. Informal political steering is worse than visible override because it captures the result without owning the responsibility.

### 10. Treat impartiality architecture as part of administrative design, not ethics wallpaper

Recusal, separation, ex parte rules, advisory channels, appeal design, and record discipline belong to one operating system. If the state can describe its notice, hearing, and appeal rights but cannot say who may privately influence the decider, who built the case, who advised the result, and when recusal or reassignment occurs, then the process is still structurally vulnerable even if its forms look fair.

## Guardrails

- Do not let the same official who built the adverse case routinely decide the contested merits in serious matters.
- Do not tolerate private merits communications from parties, political offices, or interested outsiders on the theory that the decider can ignore them.
- Do not hide decisive staff advice off the record and then call the file complete.
- Do not let internal appeal mean the same person, same desk, or same committed chain reissuing itself.
- Do not over-judicialize every clerical or fully beneficial matter; type stronger firebreaks where adversity and consequence actually justify them.
- Do not allow recusal rules to exist only in theory without a usable challenge path and substitute decision-maker.
- Do not treat apparent bias as irrelevant merely because actual corruption is unproven.

## Failure modes

- **adjudication by backstage influence**: the real persuasive work happens off record and outside contestable procedure.
- **prosecutor-judge fusion**: the office that builds the case effectively decides it too.
- **appeal to self**: the second-look lane is structurally incapable of disagreeing with the first.
- **conflict without consequence**: interests are disclosed, or obvious, but no recusal or reassignment follows.
- **status-update laundering**: forbidden merits contact is disguised as scheduling, logistics, or managerial check-in.
- **record hollowing**: the formal file omits the advice, pressure, or communications that actually shaped the decision.
- **political whisper override**: elected or partisan actors influence individual adjudicative outcomes without public ownership.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can tell you **that people get notice, can submit evidence, and may appeal**, but cannot also show **who decides contested adverse matters, how investigators and decision-makers are separated when necessary, what recusal triggers exist, how ex parte contacts are banned or cured, what internal advice enters the record, and why second-look review is not merely appeal to self**, then it still has **administrative procedure without an impartiality architecture**.
