# 745 — Tombstone-consolidation and register-absorption packets for retired government bodies, scopes, and shells: directory merge, alias preservation, correction-chain retention, and no disappearance-by-tidying

## One-line thesis

Once the archive can create a tombstone (`741`), reroute it (`742`), correct it (`743`), and steward it (`744`), it should also keep one compact **tombstone-consolidation and register-absorption packet**: one bounded proof object for folding a standalone dead-page surface into a broader historical register, catalogue, or directory without losing discoverability, correction history, or the plain statement that the old authority is dead, so the polity does not mistake tidier information architecture for permission to erase institutional memory.

## Why this matters

The archive now has most of the retirement grammar it needs.

It can retire a body, scope lane, or closeout shell without constitutional ghosting (`642`, `740`). It can leave one durable tombstone that states what died, what replaced it if anything, where records and archive homes sit, and which one bounded legacy-question lane remains (`741`). It can later reroute that tombstone when successors drift (`742`), correct it when its public story is materially wrong (`743`), and keep it maintained over time with named stewardship and active health checks (`744`).

What it still lacked was the smallest object for the next quiet but common move: **when should a standalone tombstone remain its own page, and when may it be honestly absorbed into a broader historical register or catalogue without becoming disappearance-by-cleanup**.

That gap matters because memorial sprawl is real. Once many bodies, shells, lanes, or retired service routes have died, every dead thing may not need its own fully standalone public page forever. Users may be better served by one historical register, directory, or catalogue that groups related dead authorities, retired schemes, terminated shells, or superseded route families in one structured place. Operations teams may also need a thinner maintenance model than hundreds of quasi-identical standalone tombstones.

But the compression move is risky. A dead authority that once had a clear tombstone can be “merged into the archive” in a way that destroys its findability. A public body can be folded into a list item that drops the original death statement, hides the successor split, or loses the correction chain that once explained why the public story changed. Old aliases, document paths, and case prefixes can disappear because they no longer fit the cleaner collection structure. A grouped directory can quietly become the only remaining trace of several different dead things, even though those dead things do not share the same successor, record home, archive home, or challenge lane. And once the standalone page is gone, later maintainers may no longer know whether the object was intentionally consolidated, accidentally orphaned, or simply deleted in a redesign.

The archive therefore needs one compact packet for **tombstone consolidation**. This packet should not make every memorial page immortal. But it should force the polity to prove that shrinking the number of standalone dead pages preserves the constitutional minimum: the death statement stays plain, the object stays individually discoverable, the route and correction history stays reachable, the legacy lookup lane stays bounded and honest, and the register that absorbs the page does not impersonate either a live authority or a records-management system. Without that packet, the archive still permits **disappearance by tidying**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Require one explicit absorption packet before replacing a standalone tombstone with a register entry or catalogue row

A dead authority should not lose its standalone tombstone merely because a content team wants fewer pages or a platform team prefers one directory.

If the polity wants to fold a standalone tombstone into a broader historical register, it should open one explicit absorption packet saying:

- which standalone tombstone is being absorbed;
- into which register, catalogue, or grouped historical surface it will be absorbed;
- what user need the grouped surface actually serves;
- and what lookup paths, redirects, and history links will remain after the standalone page is retired.

Absorption should be a named act, not an emergent side effect of redesign.

### 2. Distinguish absorption from deletion, withdrawal, and rerouting

A tombstone-consolidation packet should classify the move correctly.

At minimum it should distinguish:

- **presentation absorption** — the standalone page disappears, but the dead object still has an individually addressable entry inside a grouped historical surface;
- **partial absorption** — the main explanatory page is grouped, but a separate redirect, alias page, or correction log remains;
- **index-only addition** — the grouped register is added, but the standalone page remains canonical;
- **mistaken deletion risk** — the proposed move is really loss of lookup rather than honest consolidation.

Without typed classes, maintainers can call almost any removal “consolidation” even when discoverability is plainly degraded.

### 3. Preserve one individually addressable historical identity for every absorbed object

A grouped historical register may be the primary public surface, but each absorbed dead thing should still keep one individually resolvable identity.

That may be:

- a stable anchor or dedicated entry URL within the register;
- a stable machine-readable identifier that resolves to that entry;
- or a redirect from the old standalone path into the exact entry point.

Users should not have to scan a long alphabetical table and guess which line corresponds to the old body they were actually looking for. A dead institution may be grouped with others, but it should not lose its own lookup identity.

### 4. Preserve the canonical death statement and effective date inside the absorbed entry

The grouped register should not downgrade the most important fact to a vague status icon.

Each absorbed entry should still say plainly:

- what ended;
- whether it was dissolved, terminated, superseded, merged, or withdrawn;
- the effective date;
- and whether there is one successor, several successors, or no direct successor.

A cleaner catalogue page is not permission to replace constitutional facts with minimalist metadata.

### 5. Keep successor, record-home, archive-home, and legacy-question routing specific to the absorbed object

Registers and catalogues encourage uniform structure, but different dead objects often route differently.

An absorbed entry should still be able to say, object by object:

- where records live;
- where archived public material lives;
- who answers bounded legacy questions;
- and what successor or split-successor map applies.

The register may supply one common interface, but it should not collapse materially different routing facts into one generic “see department archive” answer.

### 6. Preserve rerouting, correction, and stewardship history rather than flattening to the latest state

A grouped historical surface is not just a latest-state directory. It should preserve enough lineage that later readers can still understand how the dead-page story changed.

An absorbed entry should therefore retain or link to:

- rerouting acts (`742`);
- material correction records (`743`);
- and stewardship-transfer history where relevant (`744`).

Absorption is a compression move, not a license to discard how the polity learned, corrected, or maintained the memorial object over time.

### 7. Keep alias maps, old URLs, and common search names alive through redirects or lookup fields

Consolidation fails most often through lost names.

The absorption packet should therefore prove that the grouped surface still catches:

- prior body names and acronyms;
- former scheme or shell names;
- legacy URL paths where practical;
- major identifier prefixes;
- and search language likely to be used by the public or by historical records.

The archive should not confuse a well-organized register with a well-routed one.

### 8. Forbid grouped registers from becoming ambiguous live front doors or shadow records systems

A historical register is useful precisely because it is thin.

The absorbed surface should not:

- accept new ordinary transactions;
- suggest that the dead authority is still live;
- operate as a substitute records-management system for the underlying records home;
- or blur historical lookup with current service intake.

A grouped memorial directory should improve findability, not become a silent resurrection or a faux database of record.

### 9. Set criteria for when a dead object should remain standalone instead of being absorbed

Not every tombstone should be compressed.

The packet should block absorption where the dead object still has:

- heavy legacy traffic;
- a materially disputed correction history;
- unusually complex successor splits;
- substantial ongoing legacy-question volume;
- or special legal, symbolic, or public-interest weight that justifies one dedicated page.

A grouped register should absorb dead objects when grouping improves clarity, not merely because grouping is aesthetically cleaner.

### 10. Require a re-expansion path if grouping later proves too lossy or confusing

Compression should be reversible.

If an absorbed entry later becomes hard to route, heavily disputed, or publicly confusing, the polity should be able to reopen a standalone tombstone without pretending the grouped phase never happened. The packet should therefore name:

- the trigger for re-expansion;
- the steward who may approve it;
- and the preservation of entry history when a standalone page is restored.

Good consolidation is bounded and reversible, not a one-way ratchet toward invisibility.

## Guardrails

- Do not let “we put it in the archive” stand in for an explicit absorption decision.
- Do not absorb a standalone tombstone into a grouped register unless the dead object remains individually addressable.
- Do not trade away the plain death statement, effective date, successor map, or record-home routing in exchange for a cleaner directory.
- Do not discard rerouting, correction, or stewardship history merely because the public surface is now grouped.
- Do not let alias loss, broken old URLs, or vanished search names masquerade as acceptable tidying.
- Do not use a grouped historical register in place of a live service front door or a records-management system.
- Do not absorb high-dispute or high-volume legacy objects into a generic table just because the standalone page is operationally inconvenient.
- Do not make consolidation irreversible when later evidence shows that grouping confused rather than clarified.

## Failure modes

- **disappearance by tidying**: the polity claims the dead object still exists historically, but its old page is gone and the grouped register no longer makes it individually findable.
- **catalogue flattening**: several materially different dead bodies are grouped under one vague template, obscuring different successors, record homes, or question lanes.
- **latest-state amnesia**: the grouped register keeps only the newest routing facts and discards the rerouting or correction chain that explains how the memorial story evolved.
- **alias collapse**: prior names, acronyms, and legacy paths are lost during consolidation, so only insiders can still locate the absorbed object.
- **historical directory as shadow system**: the grouped register slowly accumulates enough present-tense routing and generic contact language that users mistake it for a live service hub.
- **compression without escape**: a tombstone is absorbed into a grouped register even though later legacy volume or dispute clearly warrants re-expansion into a dedicated page.

## Practical tests

A tombstone-consolidation and register-absorption packet is honest when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is the absorption a named act rather than a side effect of redesign?
2. Does it classify whether the move is presentation absorption, partial absorption, index-only addition, or mistaken deletion risk?
3. Does the absorbed object remain individually addressable inside the grouped historical surface?
4. Does the grouped entry still state plainly what died, when, and what the successor situation is?
5. Does the entry preserve object-specific routing for records, archives, and bounded legacy questions?
6. Are old aliases, major identifiers, and practical legacy paths still caught through redirects or lookup fields?
7. Are rerouting, correction, and stewardship histories preserved or linked rather than flattened away?
8. Does the grouped register remain visibly historical rather than drifting toward live service intake or faux system-of-record status?
9. Does the packet block absorption when traffic, dispute, or successor complexity still justifies a standalone page?
10. Is there a credible re-expansion path if grouping later proves too lossy or confusing?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can create an honest tombstone (`741`), reroute it (`742`), correct it (`743`), and steward it (`744`), it should also be able to show one compact packet for **when a standalone dead-page surface may be absorbed into a broader historical register or catalogue, how individual lookup identity and plain death facts are preserved inside the grouped surface, how alias maps and old paths still resolve, how rerouting and correction history remain reachable, when grouping is not honest enough, and how re-expansion works if compression later proves too lossy**. Otherwise it still permits **disappearance by tidying**.
