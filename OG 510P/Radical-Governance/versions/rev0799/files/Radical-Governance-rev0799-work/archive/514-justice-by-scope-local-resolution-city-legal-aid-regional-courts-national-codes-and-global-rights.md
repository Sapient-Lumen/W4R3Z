# 514 — Justice by scope: local resolution, city legal aid, regional courts, national codes, and global rights

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should govern justice by keeping low-stakes dispute resolution, legal help, and problem sensing close to daily life; sizing trial and appellate institutions to real catchments rather than arbitrary municipal lines; reserving criminal codes, procedural guarantees, judicial independence, prosecution architecture, and equal access floors to the nation; and treating the global layer as a narrow guarantor of rights, cooperation, and last-resort review rather than as a substitute domestic judiciary.

## Why this matters

Justice governance is often mistyped. It is narrated as purely national because constitutions, criminal codes, supreme courts, and judicial appointments usually sit there. But people do not first meet justice at the constitutional summit. They meet it when a landlord threatens eviction, wages go unpaid, a child-support order stalls, domestic violence requires rapid protection, a fine is wrongly issued, or they cannot understand a legal process at all. Those frictions appear close to daily life, and when no one owns them at that level, the formal system becomes both overloaded and socially distant.

The opposite mistake is municipal romanticism: imagining every locality should run a full justice stack. That produces brittle courts, local capture, uneven competence, patronage risk, and postcode justice. Many justice functions need larger catchments to preserve independence, expertise, and even volume: specialized civil and family courts, public defense capacity, prosecution offices, forensic services, detention oversight, appeals, and case-management systems all weaken when they are fragmented into too many tiny islands.

A serious constitutional design therefore needs a scope-typed justice note. An ideal government should place early help, legal capability, and some restorative or administrative resolution close to people; size formal courts and prosecution to real service regions; reserve independence, rights floors, and procedural equality to the nation; and keep the global layer narrow, rights-bearing, and cooperation-oriented.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from people’s justice needs, not from court architecture alone

A justice constitution should begin with where legal problems arise and how people actually navigate them, not only with the organizational chart of superior courts. If a polity can describe its apex judiciary but cannot say where ordinary people obtain legal information, translation, mediation, emergency protection, or affordable counsel, it has a judicial pyramid without a justice system.

### 2. Give micro-local institutions early-resolution and signal roles, not sovereign criminal power

Neighborhood institutions, unions, schools, tenant groups, and community organizations often detect conflict, exploitation, family stress, and administrative mistreatment early. They can help with legal awareness, referral, restorative processes where lawful, and feedback on barriers. They should not operate as miniature sovereign courts, local secret tribunals, or armed punishment centers.

### 3. Treat municipalities as justice access governors

Municipal government is usually the right scale for legal-aid gateways, language access, victim support interfaces, diversion and problem-solving programs, fine and debt review pathways, administrative justice help desks, and safe physical access to justice services. Cities and towns can make justice reachable, legible, and humane. They should not be forced to improvise core judicial independence or carry the whole cost of defense and prosecution alone.

### 4. Size first-instance and specialized courts to real catchments, often regional or metropolitan

Formal justice institutions should follow caseload, travel time, language patterns, detention geography, and specialized expertise. Family courts, labour courts, commercial courts, juvenile justice, forensic services, and prosecution offices often work best at metropolitan or regional scale, where there is enough volume to build competence and enough distance to reduce local capture. The archive should prefer real service regions over inherited municipal pride.

### 5. Reserve procedural equality, criminal law, and judicial independence to the nation

National government is usually the right level for the core legal architecture: constitutional rights, criminal law, procedural guarantees, judicial tenure and discipline frameworks, prosecutorial independence or accountability design, legal-profession rules, national legal-aid floors, prison law, and the equal-protection baseline. Otherwise rights harden into territorial lotteries.

### 6. Separate local help from local domination

A good justice constitution distinguishes between bringing justice closer and letting the powerful capture it. Local presence is good when it improves access, translation, prevention, and trust. It is bad when judges, prosecutors, police, or detention authorities become socially dependent on the same narrow elite networks they are meant to check. The archive should therefore prefer local access with larger-scale independence safeguards.

### 7. Keep the global layer narrow: rights review, extradition limits, evidence cooperation, and legal minimums

Global and supranational institutions should not run ordinary domestic caseloads. Their narrower role is still essential: protect baseline fair-trial and rights norms, coordinate cross-border evidence and anti-impunity cooperation, constrain abusive extradition or return, and provide last-resort review where domestic systems break faith with basic guarantees. That is a justice backstop, not a world court of first resort for daily life.

### 8. Treat delay, opacity, and cost as constitutional failures, not mere management annoyances

Justice denied by delay, untranslated procedure, inaccessible buildings, predatory fees, or unusable digital systems is still denied. The archive should therefore treat timeliness, intelligibility, affordability, and accessibility as part of the constitutional allocation of justice functions rather than as soft customer-service improvements.

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse local presence with local judicial sovereignty.
- Do not fragment courts so much that independence, specialization, or equal treatment collapse.
- Do not nationalize every justice task if the result is distance, delay, and civic illiteracy.
- Do not let prosecution, policing, and detention merge into one unreviewable local bloc.
- Do not use global rights bodies as an excuse to tolerate domestic access failure.

## Failure modes

- **postcode justice**: rights depend on local wealth, travel ability, or municipal discretion.
- **captured locality**: judges, prosecutors, and police are too socially entangled with local power to act independently.
- **remote legality**: the formal system exists but ordinary people cannot reach or understand it.
- **specialization deserts**: tiny jurisdictions cannot sustain competent family, labour, commercial, or youth justice.
- **rights without remedy**: constitutional guarantees exist nationally while first contact remains slow, unaffordable, or unusable.

## Practical tests

A scale-matched justice constitution passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can ordinary people get legal help, translation, referral, and emergency protection close to where they live?
2. Are courts, prosecution, and defense institutions sized to real caseload and travel catchments rather than nostalgic borders?
3. Does national government guarantee procedural equality, legal-aid floors, judicial independence, and rights baselines across the territory?
4. Are local access functions clearly separated from coercive adjudication and criminal power where capture risks are high?
5. Does the global layer act as a narrow rights and cooperation backstop rather than pretending to replace domestic justice?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can describe **its constitution and top courts** but cannot show **where ordinary people get legal help, which level runs independent trial institutions, how equal access is guaranteed across territory, and what outside backstop exists when the system fails**, it still has **legal order without a justice constitution**.
