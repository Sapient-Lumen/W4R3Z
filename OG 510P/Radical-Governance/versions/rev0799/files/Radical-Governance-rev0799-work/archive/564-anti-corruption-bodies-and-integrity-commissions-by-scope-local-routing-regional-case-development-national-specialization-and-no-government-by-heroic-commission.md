# 564 — Anti-corruption bodies and integrity commissions by scope: local routing, regional case development, national specialization, and no government by heroic commission

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should not rely on one heroic anti-corruption commission: keep local complaint intake, disclosure routing, whistleblower handoff, and ordinary integrity advice close enough to be usable; place regional or state-level investigative and inspection capacity where corruption markets, procurement webs, and patronage chains cross municipalities or where tiny jurisdictions are too captured to investigate themselves; reserve national government for the preventive architecture required by law, high-level and cross-regional corruption, asset-declaration verification, beneficial-ownership tracing, international cooperation, and strategy coordination; keep the global layer narrow in standards, peer review, and transnational case support; and never let anti-corruption institutions become either decorative ethics theater or unreviewable prosecutor-states.

## Why this matters

The archive already has stronger notes on public integrity, procurement, audit, prosecution, policing, courts, ombuds, and information rights. It still needs one compact answer to a harder institutional question: **what should anti-corruption bodies themselves look like in a serious multi-level polity?**

That question matters because anti-corruption architecture regularly fails in two opposite ways. Some systems diffuse responsibility until everyone can say integrity matters and nobody is actually answerable for prevention, case development, or escalation. Others build a single spectacular commission, drape it in national virtue, and then ask it to educate, inspect, investigate, prosecute, explain, and symbolize clean government all at once. The first model dissolves into paperwork. The second often becomes either politically selective or institutionally fragile.

Ideal government should instead build a **differentiated integrity stack**. The small and local layers should not be asked to perform elite-case investigation they cannot secure. National government should not swallow every complaint merely because corruption is embarrassing. And cross-border institutions should help with tracing, cooperation, and recovery without pretending they can substitute for domestic integrity systems.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Do not collapse prevention, inspection, investigation, prosecution, and adjudication into one office

Anti-corruption work is not one function. Preventive policy design, conflict management, disclosure verification, internal inspection, criminal investigation, prosecution, adjudication, audit, and public reporting each require different powers, cultures, and safeguards. A sound polity may coordinate these functions tightly, but it should resist concentrating them all in one institution unless the constitutional case is unusually strong and the safeguards unusually hard.

The archive should therefore prefer an integrity architecture in which anti-corruption bodies are specialized enough to matter and separated enough to be checked.

### 2. Keep ordinary integrity routing and advice close to where public power is exercised

Many corruption risks first appear as mundane events: a procurement specification mysteriously narrows, a relative gets hired, a recusal is skipped, gifts accumulate, a permit queue starts moving by favor, a local contractor becomes untouchable, or a frontline worker is told to ignore the rules for a connected person. Those signals need accessible intake, records discipline, ethics advice, conflict checks, and whistleblower routing near the institution where the distortion occurs.

That does not mean every village, ward, district, or municipality needs a full anti-corruption commission. It means the local layer should be able to receive complaints, preserve records, route them to the right authority, protect retaliation targets, and trigger real escalation rather than expecting people to navigate an abstract national maze.

### 3. Move serious case development above the smallest captured jurisdiction

Tiny jurisdictions are often the worst place to house full corruption investigation. Social proximity is high, personnel pools are shallow, record systems are weak, and the targets may sit inside the same elite network that controls jobs, contracts, and police access. The archive should therefore prefer regional, provincial, state, metropolitan, or otherwise supra-local anti-corruption inspection and investigative units for serious case development where local capture risk is high.

This scale is often large enough to support trained investigators, digital forensics, financial analysis, safer case allocation, and separation from the daily patronage web. It is also the level at which corruption markets frequently become visible as systems rather than incidents: repeated public works contractors, procurement collusion across municipalities, licensing chains, health or education supply abuse, or region-wide police and revenue corruption.

### 4. Reserve the national level for the constitutional anti-corruption core

National government is usually the right level for the anti-corruption framework required by constitutional and international obligations: preventive body or bodies, specialized law-enforcement authorities, asset and interest declaration architecture, beneficial-ownership access rules, foreign-bribery coordination, cross-regional intelligence, major public-integrity cases involving senior officeholders, and the legal basis for whistleblower protection, case referrals, and interagency cooperation.

This is also the right layer for a whole-of-government integrity strategy with defined responsibilities, monitoring, and periodic reporting. National government should own the **system architecture**, but not monopolize every allegation or convert anti-corruption into a pretext for routine central intervention in ordinary local administration.

### 5. Give anti-corruption bodies law, tenure, budget, and staffing protection strong enough to survive offended power

An anti-corruption body that can be casually defunded, stripped of staff, or decapitated when it looks in the wrong direction is mostly a warning to its own employees. The archive should therefore prefer a clear legal basis, specified mandate, transparent appointment rules, protected leadership tenure, lawful and narrow removal grounds, budget visibility, control over internal staffing, and safeguards against improper outside instruction.

Independence should not mean absence of accountability. It means the body can act within law without seeking political permission from those it may have to scrutinize.

### 6. Measure preventive bodies by system repair, not only by arrest statistics

Preventive anti-corruption bodies should not be judged solely by headline case counts. Their purpose includes mapping corruption risks, reviewing integrity controls, improving disclosure systems, advising on conflict management, recommending procurement and human-resource fixes, monitoring strategy implementation, and helping governments close the openings through which corruption becomes ordinary.

A state that glorifies arrests while neglecting prevention usually teaches agencies to stage raids instead of repairing institutions. An ideal archive should therefore treat **risk reduction, rule repair, and implementation follow-through** as first-order performance signals.

### 7. Keep criminal anti-corruption investigation forceful but procedurally disciplined

Specialized anti-corruption investigators may need covert powers, financial tracing, digital analysis, and stronger insulation than ordinary administrative reviewers. But they should still operate inside lawful authorization, evidence discipline, disclosure duties, due process, judicial warrants where required, and reviewable case-selection rules.

The archive should reject anti-corruption as permanent exception. A body created to fight corruption should not itself become a standing model of publicity-first accusation, indefinite emergency power, or political theater through selective leaks.

### 8. Build explicit referral lanes among audit, ombuds, procurement review, police, prosecutors, and anti-corruption bodies

A differentiated integrity stack only works when the handoffs are real. Auditors should know when to refer suspected criminality. Procurement review bodies should know when repeated bid-rigging or conflict patterns require integrity escalation. Ombuds institutions should know when maladministration evidence may mask corruption. Police and prosecutors should know when specialized anti-corruption expertise is required. Preventive bodies should be able to push system fixes without pretending to prosecute.

The archive should therefore prefer written referral rules, protected information-sharing authority, and case-tracking discipline over improvised personal relationships between institutions.

### 9. Protect complainants, whistleblowers, and frontline truth-tellers from local retaliation

Corruption complaints often begin with people who have less power than the network they are naming. Local public workers, teachers, nurses, procurement staff, junior police, vendors, detainees, patients, parents, and community members may see corruption long before a national body does. If the first reporting step predictably exposes them to retaliation, silence becomes rational.

Ideal government should therefore provide multiple reporting routes, confidentiality where lawful, anti-retaliation protection, the ability to bypass compromised local hierarchies, and visible notice of what happens after a complaint is received.

### 10. Keep the global layer narrow: peer review, case cooperation, tracing, and recovery support

The international layer matters because corruption regularly outruns one state through shell companies, foreign bribery, procurement intermediaries, property purchases, secrecy jurisdictions, and officials who move assets abroad. Global and regional networks can support mutual legal assistance, direct practitioner contact, asset tracing, recovery, standards exchange, and peer review.

But ideal government should resist outsourcing domestic integrity to international performance. External review can stiffen standards and case cooperation can break bottlenecks, yet no foreign network can supply a country with honest records, protected complainants, competent prosecutors, or a non-captured local administration.

## Guardrails

- Do not turn one anti-corruption body into educator, inspector, investigator, prosecutor, court, and national mascot all at once.
- Do not ask tiny jurisdictions to run elite corruption investigations they cannot secure.
- Do not mistake a national hotline or publicity campaign for a working complaint and protection system.
- Do not measure anti-corruption seriousness only by arrest counts, raid footage, or televised announcements.
- Do not let anti-corruption independence dissolve into secrecy without publication, review, budget honesty, or legal limits.
- Do not use anti-corruption as a constitutional excuse for routine recentralization of all local administration.
- Do not leave referral routes between audit, ombuds, procurement review, police, prosecutors, and preventive bodies informal or personality-dependent.
- Do not let international cooperation rhetoric substitute for domestic investigative, prosecutorial, and court capacity.

## Failure modes

- **government by heroic commission**: one body is asked to embody virtue, absorb every case, and cure structural corruption through spectacle.
- **integrity theater**: strategies, trainings, and hotlines exist, but complaints do not travel, records do not survive, and rules do not change.
- **local capture with no escape route**: serious corruption is left inside the smallest jurisdiction most controlled by the implicated network.
- **prevention without enforcement**: corruption risks are mapped and discussed but never linked to sanctions or forced repair.
- **enforcement without prevention**: elite cases are dramatized while disclosure, procurement, staffing, and conflict systems remain porous.
- **anti-corruption centralism**: national authorities use integrity language to dominate constitutionally local functions regardless of actual case need.
- **uncorrected handoff failure**: audit, procurement, ombuds, police, and prosecution bodies each see fragments of corruption but no institution owns the bridge.

## Practical tests

An anti-corruption constitution passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can people at local level report, preserve, and route corruption concerns without having to begin inside the same hierarchy they are accusing?
2. Are serious investigations placed at a scale large enough to resist capture, support specialist staff, and follow corruption across jurisdictions?
3. Does national government supply a clear legal mandate, protected independence, strategy coordination, and specialist case capacity without absorbing every ordinary integrity task?
4. Are prevention, inspection, investigation, prosecution, audit, and adjudication distinct enough that one integrity institution can still challenge another?
5. Can anti-corruption bodies show not only cases opened, but controls repaired, declarations verified, referrals completed, retaliation prevented, and stolen assets or public losses credibly pursued?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can show **anti-corruption slogans, commissions, and raids** but cannot show **where complaints can safely enter, which scale investigates serious corruption, how specialized bodies survive political anger, and how prevention, audit, prosecution, and adjudication stay connected without collapsing into one office**, it still has **integrity branding without an anti-corruption constitution**.
