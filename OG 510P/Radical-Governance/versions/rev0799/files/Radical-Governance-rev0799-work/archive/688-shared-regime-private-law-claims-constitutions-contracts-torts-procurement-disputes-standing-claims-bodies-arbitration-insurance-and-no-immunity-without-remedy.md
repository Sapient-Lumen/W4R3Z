# 688 — Shared-regime private-law claims constitutions: contracts, torts, procurement disputes, standing claims bodies, arbitration, insurance, and no immunity without remedy

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed review-and-remedy constitution** for private-law disputes that separates contract claims, procurement complaints, property and personal-injury claims, insurance recovery, amicable settlement, standing claims bodies where needed, arbitration clauses, waiver decisions, and payment authority — so the regime can preserve functional immunity from ordinary local courts without sliding into immunity without remedy.

## Why this matters

The archive now has notes on procurement (`685`), administrative justice for staff (`686`), and privileges and immunities for the institution (`687`). That is enough to explain how a regime buys, how it governs its own workforce, and how it defends functional independence. It is still not enough to explain **what happens when private parties, suppliers, landlords, accident victims, or other non-staff actors suffer a claim against the regime itself**.

That gap matters because the very instruments that protect institutional independence can otherwise produce a legitimacy vacuum.

A regime signs contracts. It leases premises. It moves vehicles and equipment. It hosts meetings. It may cause property damage, procurement disputes, or employment-adjacent claims outside the staff-justice lane. If it invokes immunity from domestic jurisdiction but has no visible substitute path, the archive is left endorsing a constitutional shell in which independence is real and remedy is fictional.

Without a typed private-law claims constitution, six familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **immunity without remedy**: the regime blocks domestic courts but does not publish any usable substitute process.

The second is **channel collapse**: contract disputes, tort claims, procurement complaints, and ex gratia or humanitarian claims all enter one generic inbox.

The third is **waiver improvisation**: immunity, arbitration consent, settlement authority, and payment authority are decided ad hoc by whoever happens to own the relationship.

The fourth is **claims amnesia**: the regime settles quietly, repeats the same harms, and keeps no institutional memory of claim type, fault patterns, or recurring vendors and sites.

The fifth is **budget fiction**: a claims right exists in theory but there is no reserve, insurance, indemnity logic, or payment lane to make resolution real.

The sixth is **forum opportunism**: the regime alternates between absolute immunity rhetoric, commercial-contract arbitration, and informal goodwill depending on which is cheapest in the moment.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **shared-regime private-law claims discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Publish one claims map for non-staff disputes

A serious regime should show in one place:

- what counts as a contract dispute,
- what counts as another dispute of a private-law character,
- what procurement complaint route exists,
- where accident, property-damage, or third-party loss claims are filed,
- who can settle amicably,
- which disputes go to arbitration or another standing body,
- and how approved payments are funded.

If outsiders must guess whether they are dealing with procurement, legal, finance, risk, or the secretary-general’s office, the remedy lane is not constitutionally real.

### 2. Separate claim classes before choosing the forum

The regime should distinguish at minimum:

- ordinary contract disputes,
- procurement challenge or protest claims,
- property loss or personal-injury claims,
- vehicle or premises claims,
- small-value administrative claims,
- insured losses,
- and mass or operational claims that may require a standing claims body or specially designed process.

Different claims need different clocks, proof burdens, and settlement tools.

### 3. Use amicable settlement first, but not as a black box

Many private-law disputes should try a structured amicable-settlement lane first.

But the regime should publish filing routes, acknowledgment clocks, information requirements, decision authority, and closure records. “We settle amicably” is not enough if it merely hides unequal bargaining or indefinite delay.

### 4. Put arbitration and adjudication in writing before disputes arise

For contractual relationships, the archive should prefer express dispute clauses naming mediation if any, arbitration if any, governing procedural rules, seat or administering support if relevant, language, and enforcement logic.

The point is not to force every claim into formal arbitration. It is to prevent forum improvisation after the dispute has already become political.

### 5. Reserve standing claims bodies for recurring or field-heavy risk

Where a regime routinely operates in the field, uses vehicles, deploys missions, or creates predictable third-party loss risk, it should consider a standing claims body or predesigned claims commission lane rather than pretending that one-off desk settlement can scale.

Standing bodies are not mandatory for every regime. They are mandatory to think about where operational risk makes them the honest design.

### 6. Separate immunity, waiver, and consent to arbitrate

Waiving immunity from domestic jurisdiction, consenting to arbitration, agreeing on a claims commission, and approving settlement are related but distinct acts.

The regime should therefore type:

- who may waive immunity,
- who may sign an arbitration clause,
- who may create or trigger a standing claims body,
- and who may settle and pay.

This prevents one office from using procedural ambiguity as bargaining leverage.

### 7. Pair claims rights with funding reality

A serious regime should say what pays valid claims: program budget, central reserve, insurance recovery, contingency fund, mission budget, or another named source.

A remedy lane without a payment lane is just a polite form of refusal.

### 8. Keep a claims memory and recurrence register

The regime should track claim types, sites, vendors, recurrent hazards, average resolution time, settlement patterns, and unpaid decisions.

This does not require publishing personal details. It does require preventing legal settlement from becoming institutional amnesia.

### 9. Distinguish lawful confidentiality from invisible claims government

Some claims require confidentiality for privacy, security, or settlement reasons. That should not erase all public legibility.

The archive should prefer aggregate publication of claim categories, volumes, median timing, major recurring causes, arbitration use, and unpaid liabilities. Otherwise immunity disputes remain technically managed but constitutionally invisible.

### 10. Treat private-law remedy as the twin of immunity, not its enemy

The archive should prefer a paired rule:

functional immunity may block ordinary domestic process where necessary for independence, but the regime must then offer an appropriate substitute mode of settlement for contracts and other private-law disputes.

That pairing is how constitutional insulation avoids becoming legal nihilism.

## Working matrix

| Claim class | Honest lane | Why | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| contracts | written dispute clause with amicable stage and arbitration or adjudication route | prevents forum improvisation | absolute-immunity rhetoric after signing |
| procurement disputes | dedicated protest or complaint lane with clocks and record | keeps bidder remedies intelligible | forcing everything into ordinary contract law |
| property or injury claims | named filing route, proof requirements, settlement authority, and payment source | preserves remedy for ordinary third parties | generic inbox government |
| high-volume field claims | predesigned standing claims body or commission where risk justifies it | scales recurring harms honestly | case-by-case theater |
| waiver and consent acts | separate waiver, arbitration-consent, settlement, and payment authorities | preserves constitutional clarity | one-office improvisation |
| claims memory | maintain aggregate claims register and recurrence analysis | turns disputes into governance learning | claims amnesia |

## Guardrails

- Do not invoke immunity against private parties without naming the substitute settlement route.
- Do not collapse procurement protests, contracts, torts, and humanitarian claims into one generic process.
- Do not leave arbitration, waiver, or settlement authority implicit.
- Do not offer a claims route that has no budgetary or insurance back end.
- Do not let amicable settlement become indefinite delay or secrecy by default.
- Do not keep every claim confidential in ways that erase recurrence learning and aggregate accountability.
- Do not sign ordinary commercial contracts while pretending no private-law dispute architecture is needed.

## Failure modes

- **immunity without remedy**: local courts are blocked and nothing usable replaces them.
- **channel collapse**: all non-staff claims are forced into one vague intake lane.
- **waiver improvisation**: procedural and settlement choices depend on who is embarrassed.
- **claims amnesia**: recurring harms leave no durable institutional memory.
- **budget fiction**: valid claims exist without a realistic payment path.
- **forum opportunism**: the regime toggles among immunity, arbitration, and goodwill purely tactically.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it publish one visible map for contracts and other private-law claims by non-staff actors?
2. Does it separate procurement complaints, contract disputes, property-loss claims, and personal-injury claims?
3. Are filing routes, clocks, evidence requirements, and settlement authorities typed in advance?
4. Do standard contracts carry written dispute clauses instead of forum improvisation?
5. Has the regime decided whether a standing claims body is needed for recurring field or operational risk?
6. Are waiver, arbitration consent, settlement approval, and payment authority separated visibly?
7. Is there a named payment source, reserve, insurance, or budget lane for valid claims?
8. Does the regime maintain an aggregate claims memory that can drive prevention and procurement correction?
9. Can it explain what remains confidential and what is still published in aggregate?
10. If it invokes immunity, can it immediately identify the substitute remedy lane?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can **sign contracts, lease property, run vehicles, buy services, or cause third-party loss** but cannot say **what private-law claim classes exist, which substitute forums hear them, who may waive or arbitrate, how valid claims are paid, and how recurrence is tracked**, it still has **immunity architecture without an honest remedy constitution**.
