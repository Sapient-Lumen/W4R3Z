# 730 — Continuity packets for ideal-government scope shifts: staff, budget, records, cases, contracts, assets, cutover, and no ratification by theory alone

## One-line thesis

Once the archive has a default routing table (`726`), an override grammar (`727`), a sideways-authority packet (`728`), and a ratification packet for moving functions across scopes (`729`), it should also keep one compact **continuity packet** for the move itself: no scope shift is constitutionally honest until the polity can show who employs the people, holds the money, owns the contracts, keeps the files, answers the live cases, preserves the review routes, and runs the cutover without dropping rights, services, or accountability.

## Why this matters

The archive can now do most of the high constitutional work around multilevel design.

It can say what the ideal stack is (`725`, `602`, `660`, `661`, `662`). It can say where functions usually belong (`726`, `629`). It can say when to override that default downward, upward, or sideways (`727`). It can say what minimum kit a bounded in-between authority needs (`728`). And it can now say what proof burden and consent ladder should apply when a function is materially moved, pooled, split, or reclassified across scopes (`729`).

That is a strong constitutional spine. But it still leaves one recurring failure point untouched.

A polity may win the argument about **where** a function should live and still fail the transition about **how** the function actually moves.

The law says the region now owns the hospitals, but the payroll, contracts, patient records, appeal routes, and purchasing systems still sit with municipalities. The nation recentralizes a registry, but old local offices still hold the authoritative files. A metropolitan body inherits transport planning, but operators, fare revenues, procurement obligations, works contracts, and active permit files remain scattered across predecessor authorities. A basin authority is created, but nobody has decided who carries the liabilities, who is the employer of record, what happens to ongoing cases, or how residents challenge acts made under the old arrangement while the new one is coming alive.

Those are not mere project-management details. They are where constitutional dishonesty often hides.

The archive already has strong adjacent notes on repair ladders (`605`), delegated tasks (`588`), function transfer as operational cutover (`605`), pending-matter transition rules (`656`), successor accountability (`657`), evidentiary continuity (`658`), evidentiary break presumptions (`659`), and handback or reversibility (`631`). What it still lacked was one shorter reusable object that future notes can cite whenever a scope shift has been **chosen** but not yet made **operationally truthful**.

That missing object is a **continuity packet for scope shifts**.

Its job is simple: prevent a polity from claiming that a function has moved merely because the legal title moved. A real scope shift should not be treated as complete until the operating constitution moves with it.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate the constitutional decision from the operational cutover

A scope shift usually has at least two moments:

- the act that authorises or ratifies the new scope home, and
- the act or sequence that makes the new home operational.

Those moments may coincide, but they should not be assumed to. The continuity packet should therefore publish, at minimum:

- the ratification date,
- the cutover date or phased cutover schedule,
- the temporary holding arrangement if the dates differ,
- and the conditions that must be satisfied before the new holder becomes fully live.

This blocks a common fiction: **legal reassignment presented as if it already proved operational readiness**.

### 2. Name every continuity object that must survive the move

A sponsor should not speak only in terms of "the function" moving. The packet should list the concrete continuity objects that travel with, or remain attached to, the function.

At minimum, the archive should expect a statement on:

- staff and employer status,
- budget authority and treasury lanes,
- receivables, liabilities, and reserves,
- active cases and queues,
- contracts, grants, and procurement pipelines,
- records, archives, and systems of record,
- assets, facilities, and equipment,
- digital access, credentials, and operator permissions,
- review and appeal routes,
- public notice and front-door continuity,
- and legacy accountability for acts already taken.

If a sponsor cannot name those objects, it is usually proposing a map change rather than a live transfer.

### 3. Preserve the public front door while the backstage changes

A scope shift is dishonest if residents, workers, firms, or counterparties must guess where to file, whom to contact, or which notice is still authoritative during transition.

The continuity packet should therefore state:

- the canonical front door during transition,
- whether the old front door continues to receive and route submissions,
- how misfiled submissions are cured,
- how deadlines are protected during ambiguity,
- and when public-facing surfaces actually change.

The archive should prefer front-door continuity longer than back-office continuity. Backstage complexity is the state's problem, not the governed person's burden.

### 4. Move live cases, queues, and deadlines without procedural restart

The archive's pending-matter doctrine (`656`) already explains how to avoid midstream grab. The continuity packet should compress that into one operational rule: a lawful transfer should preserve procedural posture unless the law clearly requires otherwise.

The receiving body should therefore inherit, where applicable:

- filing date,
- queue position,
- completeness status,
- evidence already lodged,
- service and notice history,
- hearing or meeting dates already set,
- and the currently attached review lane.

No individual should lose place, rights, or lawful expectation merely because the state redesigned the stack.

### 5. Transfer people honestly: employer, terms, representation, training, and reserved capability

A scope shift that moves work but leaves public capability undefined is usually only a paper reform.

The continuity packet should therefore say:

- who becomes employer of record,
- what happens to terms, seniority, pensions, and bargaining relationships,
- what staff consultation or representation duties apply,
- which critical roles must transfer with the work,
- what training or credential refresh is needed before cutover,
- and which residual capability remains with the former holder for audit, oversight, handback, or principal-lane reasons.

The archive should reject both false extremes: pretending people do not matter because powers moved in law, and pretending full staff transfer is always required even where the old body still keeps review, audit, or guarantor functions.

### 6. Transfer money as a governed lane, not an afterthought

A function is not really rehomed until the fiscal lane is visible.

The continuity packet should therefore state:

- the operating budget that follows the function,
- the treatment of earmarks, grants, fees, and own-source revenues,
- the reserve and cash-management rule during transition,
- which liabilities stay, move, or are shared,
- who pays for legacy obligations,
- and what happens to capital projects already in flight.

Otherwise the polity can move the duty without moving the means, or move the visible budget while laundering the liabilities elsewhere.

### 7. Keep records, evidence, and system-of-record custody explicit

The archive's evidentiary notes (`658`, `659`) already warn against proof laundering by migration. The continuity packet should turn that warning into a practical checklist.

It should say:

- where the authoritative record lives before and after cutover,
- what provenance or chain-of-custody attestation is required,
- whether the old holder keeps a copy, reference stub, or read-only access,
- how retention schedules survive the move,
- how inspection, disclosure, and discovery duties remain reachable,
- and how the public can still locate the file after the transfer.

A scope shift that loses the record, blurs custody, or breaks lookup is not an administrative inconvenience. It is a constitutional defect.

### 8. Transfer contracts, grants, vendors, and assets with step-in clarity

Many scope shifts fail not because the law is vague, but because the operational substrate is locked inside leases, concessions, software, equipment, maintenance arrangements, grant agreements, or outsourced service chains.

The continuity packet should therefore name:

- which contracts novate, assign, stay, or terminate,
- which change-control approvals are needed,
- who holds step-in power during failure,
- who inherits vendor-management and performance duties,
- what assets and facilities move with the function,
- and which dependencies remain temporarily hosted by the former holder.

This keeps public duty from disappearing into concession patchwork or vendor-admin residue.

### 9. Preserve review, remedy, and accountability across the boundary

The new holder of a function is not automatically the right reviewer of old acts. The packet should therefore distinguish among:

- prospective decisions made after cutover,
- pending review of earlier decisions,
- complaints about predecessor conduct,
- liabilities or faults arising before transfer,
- and oversight or audit work that still reaches the former holder.

The archive should insist that people know whether they are challenging a new decision, an old decision, or the transfer itself. Otherwise review becomes jurisdiction hunt.

### 10. Use a named cutover commander, freeze window, fallback path, and rollback test

A multilevel reform should not rely on ambient coordination. The continuity packet should publish an operational command spine for transition.

At minimum it should name:

- the accountable cutover lead,
- the shared fact base or cutover ledger,
- any change freeze before go-live,
- the rehearsal or readiness test required,
- the fallback path if the new holder is not ready,
- and the rollback or partial-rollback rule if the transfer breaks critical service or rights.

This keeps constitutional change from being treated as a ceremonial date plus crossed fingers.

### 11. Publish legacy-accountability rules so rescoping cannot launder blame

A successor may inherit a function without inheriting moral or legal responsibility for every predecessor act. But the packet should state the split openly.

It should therefore say:

- who answers for past unlawful acts,
- who responds to information requests about predecessor conduct,
- who appears in court or tribunal for legacy matters,
- who closes old audits or investigations,
- and what successor duties exist to preserve cooperation and records.

No scope shift should let offices say the old body no longer exists and the new body is not responsible either.

### 12. Keep continuity time-bounded and reviewable

Not every dependency should move on day one. Transitional hosting, dual running, staff secondment, or shared systems may be lawful for a bounded period.

But the packet should state:

- what remains transitional,
- when that bridge expires,
- what evidence proves the bridge can end,
- and what handback or redesign path opens if the move stalls.

This prevents the archive's familiar failure mode of **permanent temporariness**: the function is said to have moved, yet the old holder remains the real operating state forever.

## Continuity matrix

| Continuity object | Minimum question | Anti-failure rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **People** | who employs, trains, and retains the capability? | no function transfer by borrowed payroll ambiguity |
| **Money** | who budgets, pays, collects, and carries liabilities? | no duty transfer without fiscal lane visibility |
| **Cases** | who owns the live queue and preserves clocks? | no procedural restart by map change |
| **Records** | where is the authoritative file and who attests continuity? | no proof laundering by migration |
| **Contracts and assets** | what novates, transfers, stays hosted, or terminates? | no public duty trapped inside vendor or estate residue |
| **Review and accountability** | who hears challenges and answers for legacy acts? | no blame laundering by successor fog |
| **Public front door** | where do people go during transition? | no rescoping by routing confusion |
| **Cutover control** | who commands transition and what happens if readiness fails? | no go-live by calendar alone |

## Guardrails

- Do not ratify a scope shift as complete before the continuity packet is credible.
- Do not assume that legal title, fiscal title, record custody, and employer status move automatically together.
- Do not make the governed person solve internal routing ambiguity.
- Do not restart queues, deadlines, or cases merely because the state reorganized.
- Do not move duties without moving the means, the people, or the files.
- Do not let the new holder review predecessor acts without explicit fairness and jurisdiction rules.
- Do not use transitional hosting as camouflage for a transfer that never truly happened.
- Do not let rescoping erase legacy accountability, audit trails, or disclosure duties.

## Failure modes

- **ratification by theory alone**: the constitutional argument is won, but nothing operational moved.
- **paper transfer**: statute or treaty changes, while payroll, systems, and contracts remain elsewhere.
- **front-door fracture**: public filing and notice routes change before the backstage is ready.
- **queue amnesia**: active matters lose dates, posture, or evidentiary continuity during handoff.
- **liability dumping**: the receiving body gets the duty while the sending body sheds the cost or blame.
- **record orphaning**: nobody can clearly identify the authoritative file after migration.
- **temporary forever**: transitional hosting becomes the real constitution through inertia.
- **successor fog**: old body, new body, and host body all deny responsibility for review or repair.

## Practical tests

A scope-shift continuity regime is honest when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does the packet distinguish ratification from operational cutover?
2. Has it named every material continuity object rather than referring only to "the function" in the abstract?
3. Can the public still find one authoritative front door during transition?
4. Are filing dates, queue positions, and review routes preserved for live matters?
5. Are employer, capability, consultation, and training arrangements explicit for staff?
6. Are budget authority, liabilities, and capital commitments visibly assigned?
7. Is record custody, provenance, retention, and lookup continuity explicit?
8. Are contracts, grants, vendors, facilities, and step-in powers accounted for?
9. Are legacy accountability and predecessor review duties still reachable after the move?
10. Is there a named cutover lead, a readiness check, and a fallback or rollback path if the move fails?
11. Are any temporary bridges time-bounded, reviewable, and tied to a real exit?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can explain **why a function should move** and even **who validly ratified the move**, but cannot also say **how people, money, records, cases, contracts, assets, remedies, and cutover control survive the transition**, then the scope shift is still incomplete. It is only **a constitutional decision without an honest continuity packet**.
