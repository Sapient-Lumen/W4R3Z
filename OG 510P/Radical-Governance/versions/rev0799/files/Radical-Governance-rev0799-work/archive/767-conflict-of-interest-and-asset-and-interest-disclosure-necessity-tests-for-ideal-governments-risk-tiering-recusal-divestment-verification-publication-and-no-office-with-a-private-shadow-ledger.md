# 767 — Conflict-of-interest and asset-and-interest-disclosure necessity tests for ideal governments: risk tiering, recusal, divestment, verification, publication, and no office with a private shadow ledger

## One-line thesis

Ideal governments should treat conflicts of interest and asset-and-interest disclosure as **a live decision-integrity system rather than a yearly paperwork ritual**: officials in risk-bearing roles should disclose relevant interests and assets under tiered rules, update them when circumstances change, recuse from affected decisions, divest or use stronger separation tools where conflicts are not manageable, and face real verification and sanction, so office is not shadowed by a private ledger the public never sees.

## Why this matters

The archive already says a great deal about **public integrity by scope** (`499`), **civil service** (`496`), **anti-corruption bodies** (`564`), **public appointments** (`760`), **political finance** (`764`), and **access to information** (`765`). What it still lacked as one compact operating rule was a sharper answer to another recurring constitutional question:

**how should an ideal polity identify, manage, and prove the handling of private interests before they distort public decisions?**

Many integrity systems fail in a familiar sequence. They require declarations, but only annually. They collect assets, but not outside roles, debts, gifts, beneficial interests, close family links, or major sources of income. They publish forms, but do not verify them. They notice a conflict, but have no typed route for recusal, blind trust, divestment, reassignment, waiver with reasons, or removal from a decision chain. They create ethics advice, but no one knows whether recommendations were followed. They sanction false filing on paper, but never actually do so.

Ideal-government design therefore needs a doctrine of **conflict-and-disclosure necessity**. The live design question is not whether public officeholders should be trusted in the abstract. It is whether the polity can see, test, and manage private interests before those interests silently rewrite the public decision path.

## Necessity table

| Necessity test | What counts as proof | Honest design consequence | Default answer if proof is weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Need to detect decision-shaping private interests** | officials control procurement, licensing, regulation, taxation, appointments, adjudication, subsidies, land use, or enforcement | conflict-of-interest rules with mandatory disclosure for risk-bearing roles | generic ethics language only |
| **Need to separate manageable from unmanageable conflicts** | some private interests can be screened off while others make neutral judgment unrealistic | typed remedies: disclosure, recusal, reassignment, divestment, blind trust, waiver with reasons, or office incompatibility | case-by-case improvisation |
| **Need to distinguish high-risk and low-risk offices** | not every role justifies the same reporting burden or publication level | tiered disclosure coverage and form depth by role and power | one giant form for everyone or no form at all |
| **Need to know whether disclosure is true rather than decorative** | undeclared interests, shell ownership, or family-linked benefit are plausible risks | verification powers, database access, risk scoring, and audit trails | filing without checking |
| **Need for public confidence and external scrutiny** | some interests should be publicly visible to deter corruption and allow challenge | publication rules, redaction logic, and accessible registers proportionate to risk | sealed disclosures with no outside visibility |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from the risk map of decisions, not from moral aspiration alone

Conflict systems should be designed around where public decisions create private advantage. High-risk zones often include procurement, land use, concessions, licences, enforcement, inspections, grants, tax rulings, regulator decisions, judicial or quasi-judicial functions, and appointments. The design problem is operational: where can an undisclosed private stake distort judgment or appear to do so?

### 2. Define interests broadly enough to catch the real conflict field

Assets matter, but so do liabilities, gifts, outside employment, board seats, beneficial ownership, partnerships, trusts, close-family interests where relevant, major income sources, professional affiliations, and future-employment negotiations. A system that asks only about declared property and listed shareholdings will miss much of the real capture surface.

### 3. Use tiered coverage rather than one disclosure burden for all offices

Heads of government, ministers, legislators, senior civil servants, regulators, judges, prosecutors, procurement officers, local development decision-makers, and heads of state-owned enterprises usually justify stronger disclosure than low-risk administrative roles. Tiering should affect both what must be declared and what is public.

### 4. Require event-triggered updating in addition to periodic filing

Annual declarations are not enough where conflicts can arise overnight through a new contract, outside position, inheritance, debt restructuring, family business involvement, or job negotiation. Ideal systems should require updates at appointment, on material change, and at exit where post-public-employment monitoring depends on the disclosure trail.

### 5. Build a real decision-management ladder: disclose, recuse, reassign, or separate

Disclosure is only the first step. Once a conflict is identified, the system should specify what happens next: recusal from the file, reassignment, removal from meetings, exclusion from procurement panels, separation from licensing chains, or where necessary divestment, blind-trust mechanisms, resignation from an outside role, or outright incompatibility with office.

### 6. Keep waivers exceptional, reasoned, and reviewable

Sometimes expertise is scarce or a conflict is unavoidable in a small jurisdiction. In those cases a waiver or mitigation path may be honest, but only if reasons are written, the mitigation is stated, the decision-maker is named, and the route remains reviewable. Otherwise “waiver” becomes a synonym for indulgence.

### 7. Verify declarations using external data, not only honesty prompts

Verification should be risk-based and proportionate, but real. Ideal systems should let the responsible authority cross-check tax, company, land, procurement, beneficial-ownership, and other lawfully available databases; flag anomalous patterns; and maintain an audit trail showing what was reviewed and what action followed.

### 8. Publish enough to let the public see whether office is shadowed by private interests

Not every field must be public in full; security, privacy, and safety concerns sometimes justify redaction. But secrecy should be the exception. For high public offices especially, the public should be able to see enough to understand outside interests, major holdings or categories, recusal triggers, and whether the system is actively managed rather than sealed from scrutiny.

### 9. Link disclosure rules to advice, whistleblowing, and enforcement lanes

Officials need guidance, but complainants and colleagues also need somewhere to escalate concerns. Ethics advisers, integrity agencies, audit bodies, commissioners, or courts should be able to receive concerns, compare them with filed disclosures, and trigger follow-up without routing everything back through the same chain of patronage.

### 10. Treat persistent non-management as a constitutional failure, not a paperwork defect

False filing, non-filing, hidden beneficial ownership, ignored recusal duties, and repeated failure to resolve known conflicts should attract a real escalation ladder: correction, publication of breach, administrative sanction, removal from file or office where warranted, civil consequence, or criminal referral in serious cases.

## Guardrails

- Do not equate annual filing with actual conflict management.
- Do not define interests so narrowly that debts, beneficial ownership, outside roles, or family-linked benefit escape the system.
- Do not demand the same disclosure form and publication level from every office regardless of risk.
- Do not collect declarations without verification powers or database access.
- Do not treat recusals, waivers, and divestment choices as informal etiquette rather than documented public-law actions.

## Failure modes

- **office with a private shadow ledger**: major interests exist but remain invisible to the public and often to oversight bodies.
- **checkbox ethics**: declarations are filed on schedule but never verified, compared, or acted on.
- **paper recusal**: an official declares a conflict but still steers the file through informal channels.
- **family-front evasion**: benefits move through spouses, relatives, trusts, or shell entities while the formal filer looks clean.
- **verification vacuum**: the system publishes forms and guidance but cannot test truthfulness or resolve conflicts in time.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can tell you **who holds office** but cannot also show **what relevant interests and assets risk-bearing officials must disclose, when those disclosures update, how conflicts are actually managed or cured, what becomes public, who verifies the filings, and what sanctions follow concealment or non-management**, then it still has **public office without a constitutional rule against the private shadow ledger**.
