# 605 — Repair ladders for multilevel government: lane fixes, connectors, kit completion, cooperation, territorial upgrades, map surgery, and no reform by single instrument

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should repair scope misfit through an ordered ladder: diagnose the exact pathology first, then clarify lanes, add missing connectors, complete the minimum institutional kit at the live tier, use intermunicipal or intergovernmental cooperation where scale outruns unit size, create or strengthen narrow special-purpose machinery only where a real functional geography demands it, upgrade to an honest metropolitan or regional tier when shared politics and multi-sector system governance are unavoidable, and reach for mergers, splits, or boundary surgery only after lighter repairs have been tested, consulted, and continuity-proofed.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a scope-allocation note, a constitutional-kit note, a design-order note, a diagnostic note for overbuild and underbuild, and separate notes on intermunicipal co-operation, territorial reorganisation, and special-purpose authorities. What it still lacked was the **repair doctrine that sits between diagnosis and surgery**.

That gap matters because multilevel reform is often trapped by one-instrument thinking. Some governments answer every pathology with merger. Others answer every capacity problem with a new authority, every metropolitan problem with a mayoral club, every fiscal problem with a grant, every local failure with recentralisation, and every coordination problem with a memorandum. The result is not repair but institutional drift.

The archive therefore needs one compact note that treats reform as an **ordered institutional menu**. Not every misfit should be answered by boundary change, and not every fragmented field should become a special district. Honest repair should start with the smallest intervention that can make the stack intelligible, capable, and democratically anchored again.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Diagnose the exact misfit before choosing the instrument

Repair should start by naming the pathology precisely: overbuild, underbuild, duplicate chains, orphan function, paper metro, paper region, false localism, false centralism, missing connector, or broken transition between levels. The archive should reject reforms that begin with a tool before the misfit is described.

A polity that cannot say what is wrong should not yet be choosing merger, abolition, agency creation, or function transfer.

### 2. First try a **lane fix** before a territorial fix

Many multilevel failures are not failures of map size but failures of role clarity. Several tiers say they are responsible for “housing,” “transport,” “water,” or “development,” but nobody can name who sets rules, who plans, who pays, who operates, who inspects, and who hears complaints.

The first repair should therefore often be a **lane fix**: split the field into rule-setting, finance, planning, delivery, data, enforcement, and remedy lanes; assign a visible lead for each lane; and publish the accountability map. If that solves the confusion, map surgery was never the real answer.

### 3. Then add the missing **connectors** before inventing a new tier

A stack can fail because levels are assigned reasonably but cannot coordinate, pool, equalize, or exchange authoritative information. The next repair step should therefore test whether the problem is really one of missing connectors:

- intergovernmental councils or compact machinery,
- equalization or formula finance,
- interoperable records and indicators,
- pooled procurement or shared technical support,
- review and intervention routes,
- and handback rules after rescue or emergency concentration.

If the assignment is sound but the connectors are missing, creating a new authority may only add another actor to a broken field.

### 4. Complete the **minimum institutional kit** at the level that already owns the work

Sometimes the level is correct but underbuilt. A municipality may be the right home for a function yet lack staff, legal powers, budgeting room, records discipline, or review routes. A region may be the right platform but lack planning capability, formula finance, or an assembly with enough visibility to legitimate its choices.

The archive should therefore prefer **kit completion** before reassignment where the live tier already matches the function and geography. Repair may require professional staffing, stable revenue, stronger records, better procurement, an ombuds lane, or a clearer executive-legislative spine rather than a constitutional reshuffle.

### 5. Use **intermunicipal or intergovernmental co-operation** where the problem is scale, not vocation

When municipalities are too small to deliver a service, plan an investment, or sustain technical staff on their own, the next honest repair is often co-operation rather than forced merger. Shared service centers, joint offices, pooled procurement, service consortia, common platforms, and contractual partnerships can enlarge capability without erasing local civic legibility.

The key test is whether the function remains fundamentally local but needs pooled capacity, or whether it has become a genuinely shared territorial system that requires a stronger political home. Co-operation is best when the problem is **capacity or transaction cost**, not when the field has become a multi-sector metropolitan constitution in disguise.

### 6. Use **special-purpose authorities** only where a narrow functional geography is undeniably real

A watershed, airport zone, trunk utility, flood basin, port complex, or regional transit spine may justify a bounded authority when the geography of the system is not the geography of ordinary territorial government. In such cases the repair may be a narrowly chartered body with visible finance, open books, review routes, and a remit that can be explained in one sentence.

But the archive should treat this as a precise instrument, not a general repair reflex. If a field needs broad strategic trade-offs across land use, housing, transport, taxation, resilience, and democratic priorities, then a narrow special-purpose body may be too weakly legitimated for the real work it is being asked to carry.

### 7. Upgrade to an honest **metropolitan or regional tier** when politics has become shared, not merely technical

Once a territory is governed through several consortia, agencies, grants, inter-mayoral bargains, and workaround platforms, the question is no longer only operational. It becomes constitutional. A real metropolitan labour market, housing market, transit network, flood system, or regional service platform needs a government that can make visible trade-offs and be judged for them.

The archive should therefore treat **territorial upgrade** as the repair for repeated shadow governance. When multi-sector bargaining is already happening above the municipality, the honest solution may be to create or strengthen a metropolitan or regional tier with a public forum, executive spine, fiscal base, and review routes proportionate to the work.

### 8. Reach for **territorial reorganisation** only after alternatives are tested and communities consulted

Mergers, splits, annexations, boundary changes, incorporations, and status changes remain real tools. But they should be late-ladder tools. The archive should expect a visible alternatives test asking whether lane fixes, connectors, kit completion, co-operation, or territorial upgrade without abolition could solve the problem more cleanly.

Where territorial surgery is still justified, the reform should state why the lighter options failed or were insufficient, consult affected communities early, preserve records, staff, contracts, debt, and service continuity, and keep legal review alive before the change becomes irreversible.

### 9. Treat **function transfer** as operational cutover, not legal incantation

Even when the right repair is to move a function, law alone does not move a capability. Honest transfer requires staff, files, contracts, assets, liabilities, workflows, facilities, digital access, budget lines, notice to affected people, and a named cutover date with fallback procedures.

The archive should therefore reject “reassignment by statute alone”. A transfer that leaves the receiving tier without the operational pack is not decentralisation or recentralisation. It is administrative fiction.

### 10. Build **review clocks and handback paths** into every repair

No repair should be immortal on arrival. Cooperative arrangements should be reviewed for effectiveness and democratic clarity. Special-purpose authorities should face narrowing, conversion, or dissolution review. Distress takeovers and central interventions should have handback clocks. New metropolitan or regional tiers should be revisited after an initial bedding-in period.

The archive should prefer reforms that say what success would look like, what indicators will be used, when the review occurs, and what further adjustment paths remain open.

## Repair ladder matrix

| Repair instrument | Best used when | Main danger if overused |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Lane fix** | field is blurred but geography is basically right | cosmetic clarity that leaves capability gaps untouched |
| **Connector repair** | levels are sensibly assigned but coordination/equalization/interoperability is weak | adding process without restoring visible lead responsibility |
| **Kit completion** | the right tier exists but is underbuilt | pouring resources into a level that still lacks the right scope |
| **Co-operation / shared services** | function remains local but scale economies or staff scarcity are real | shadow government by consortium |
| **Special-purpose authority** | one narrow functional geography needs ring-fenced governance | quango sprawl and weak democratic anchoring |
| **Metropolitan / regional upgrade** | multi-sector shared politics already exists above local level | creating a paper tier without money or legitimacy |
| **Territorial surgery** | lighter fixes cannot align geography, capability, and accountability | map surgery by managerial fiat |
| **Function transfer** | another tier is plainly the honest home for the function | reassignment by legal text without operational cutover |

## Guardrails

- Do not choose the reform instrument before naming the pathology.
- Do not answer every fragmentation problem with amalgamation.
- Do not answer every capacity problem with a new authority.
- Do not use co-operation to hide the need for a real metropolitan or regional tier.
- Do not use special-purpose bodies to smuggle in multi-sector territorial power without democratic upgrade.
- Do not perform territorial surgery without an alternatives test, consultation, and continuity planning.
- Do not treat legal function transfer as proof that practical capability moved.
- Do not launch a repair without a review date, an evaluation packet, and a visible next-step rule if the repair underperforms.

## Failure modes

- **reform by single instrument**: one tool becomes the answer to every pathology.
- **merger maximalism**: governments use boundary change where role clarity or co-operation would have worked.
- **consortium drift**: intermunicipal co-operation quietly becomes undeclared metropolitan government.
- **quango substitution**: special-purpose authorities absorb fields that really need general-purpose democratic judgment.
- **paper upgrade**: a metro or region is created without fiscal base, staffing, or review routes.
- **cutover fiction**: a function is “moved” but the staff, records, and contracts do not move with it.
- **repair without sunset**: reforms accumulate, but no one checks whether the chosen instrument remains the least-distorting one.

## Practical tests

A multilevel repair program is serious when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Has the polity named the exact pathology before selecting an instrument?
2. Were lane fixes and connector fixes tested before creating or abolishing a tier?
3. Has the existing level been checked for minimum-kit completion before powers were moved away?
4. If co-operation is proposed, is the function still fundamentally local rather than an undeclared shared polity?
5. If a special-purpose authority is proposed, is the geography narrow and the charter visibly bounded?
6. If a metropolitan or regional upgrade is proposed, is there already shared politics and not merely shared engineering?
7. If territorial surgery is proposed, were lighter alternatives tested and affected communities consulted?
8. If functions are transferred, does the cutover packet move staff, records, contracts, money, and review routes as well as legal responsibility?
9. Does the reform publish a review clock and a next honest adjustment path?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a multilevel reform can tell you **which instrument it wants to use** but cannot tell you **what pathology it diagnosed, why lighter repairs were insufficient, what operational pack must move, and when the chosen repair will be reviewed**, then it still has **a taste for reform without a repair ladder for multilevel government**.
