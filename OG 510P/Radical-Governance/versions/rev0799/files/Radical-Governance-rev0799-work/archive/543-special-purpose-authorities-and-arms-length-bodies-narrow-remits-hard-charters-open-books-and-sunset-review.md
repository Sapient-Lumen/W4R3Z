# 543 — Special-purpose authorities and arm’s-length bodies: narrow remits, hard charters, open books, and sunset review

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should use special-purpose authorities, public corporations, and arm’s-length bodies only where a bounded cross-municipal or regional function — such as transit, utilities, watershed management, market regulation, or other impartial technical tasks — genuinely requires them; give them narrow remits, hard-law charters, published financing, public accountability, and periodic review; and never let them become shadow governments, patronage shelters, or constitutional escape hatches.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a strong theory of scope, a stronger account of intergovernmental machinery, and a clearer answer to what governments at different scales should be. What it still needed was one compact note on the institutions that often govern the most important systems **without being full general-purpose governments**.

That missing layer matters because many real polities do not govern metropolitan transit, ports, water basins, hospitals, development finance, utilities, housing delivery, or market regulation directly through a municipality, region, or ministry. They govern through transport authorities, utility boards, basin bodies, public corporations, development agencies, regulators, and other arm’s-length entities. Sometimes that is appropriate. Often it is not. Too many systems create these bodies to avoid territorial conflict, to hide debt, to soften labour rules, to bypass city councils, or to promise “professionalism” while quietly removing democratic friction.

The result is a familiar pathology: the polity looks constitutionally neat on paper, but much of the real state lives in a half-lit zone of authorities and agencies that can tax, borrow, procure, regulate, or operate essential systems without clear democratic anchoring, visible review, or honest periodic justification.

An ideal government therefore needs a doctrine for special-purpose bodies. The right answer is neither abolitionist romanticism nor agency sprawl. It is **bounded institutional pluralism**: create such bodies when a function truly demands ring-fencing, expertise, continuity, or functional geography beyond one municipality; keep them visibly subordinate to the constitutional order; and force them to justify their existence over time.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Create a special-purpose body only after comparing it against ordinary government forms

A polity should not create an authority, agency, or public corporation merely because the task is hard, politically awkward, or fashionable. Before creating one, it should ask whether the function could be delivered instead by a ministry, a municipality, a regional government, a joint office, a compact, a regulated private concession, or a simpler intergovernmental mechanism.

The creation test should therefore be explicit: what problem does this form solve that an ordinary department, municipality, or intergovernmental arrangement cannot solve without unacceptable loss of impartiality, continuity, expertise, or cross-boundary coherence?

### 2. Let form follow function, but never let function become a pretext for sovereignty leakage

Some functions do justify distance from ordinary day-to-day political control. Independent regulators, certain adjudicative bodies, some central infrastructure operators, and some joint metropolitan or basin authorities need professional continuity and credible insulation from arbitrary ministerial or municipal interference. But arm’s length is a tool, not a permission slip.

The correct design logic is therefore: **the more a function requires impartiality or cross-boundary technical coherence, the more carefully autonomy should be specified — and the more deliberately accountability should be built alongside it**. A body that must be insulated from improper interference should still remain inside law, budget discipline, audit, records rules, and review.

### 3. Give every body a hard-law charter that names remit, geography, powers, money, and review

An ideal special-purpose authority should not live on vague enabling language or ceremonial mission statements. Its charter should say, in hard public law or equivalent binding constitutional machinery:

- what function it performs;
- which territory, network, catchment, or market it covers;
- what powers it does and does not hold;
- what revenue sources, transfer formulas, borrowing powers, and reserve rules apply;
- who appoints or elects its governors;
- what transparency, audit, procurement, and public-record duties bind it;
- and how it is reviewed, merged, re-scoped, or terminated.

If a polity cannot write this compactly, it probably does not yet know why the body exists.

### 4. Keep the remit narrow and resist authority creep

A special-purpose body should do one bounded class of things well. It should not accumulate a floating mandate just because it is administratively convenient. Transit authorities should not quietly become land empires without visible public rules. Basin bodies should not mutate into general regional governments through project accretion. Public corporations should not become debt warehouses for off-budget politics. Development agencies should not become patronage portfolios disguised as local growth.

The central guardrail is simple: when a body starts to look like a general-purpose government, it should either be constitutionalised as such or slimmed back to a special purpose. The archive should resist hybrid forms that keep the reach of government while shedding the obligations of government.

### 5. Match the governance board to the body’s real powers

Board design should be proportionate to what the body can actually do. The stronger the powers — especially powers to tax, borrow heavily, set binding regional plans, compel compliance, or regulate essential services — the stronger the legitimacy and review architecture should be.

That usually means:

- **member-government representation** where the body exists to co-ordinate clearly shared cross-municipal services;
- **independent expertise** where impartial regulation or fiduciary stewardship matters;
- **direct democratic anchoring or explicit legislative control** where the body’s powers are broad enough to shape daily life at metropolitan or regional scale;
- and **clear exclusion of conflicted appointees** where the body allocates contracts, licences, or regulated advantages.

A board should never be designed so opaquely that no one can explain whose interests are being represented or who can remove whom for failure.

### 6. Keep finance visible and stop debt, subsidy, and procurement from becoming hiding places

Special-purpose bodies often become attractive because they can hide politically costly decisions. Debt can be parked there, labour obligations can be diluted there, procurement can be softened there, and transfers can be routed there without ordinary budget debate.

An ideal polity should therefore keep these bodies inside strong public-finance discipline. Their revenues, subsidies, contingent liabilities, guarantees, debt, capital plans, procurement rules, and operating deficits should be public enough that neither ministers nor local executives can use them as fiscal side doors. When the body is a public corporation or other enterprise-like form, its ownership rationale and public-purpose obligations should be disclosed and periodically reviewed rather than treated as permanently self-justifying.

### 7. Require public accountability at least proportionate to the public role

Professionalism is not a substitute for accountability. A public body that delivers an essential service, holds monopoly assets, or operates at metropolitan or regional scale should be at least as accountable to the public as its functional importance warrants.

That means open meetings or published outcomes where appropriate, published plans and performance targets, audit, legislative hearing routes, complaint and remedy channels, conflict-of-interest rules, accessible records, and honest explanations of what the body can and cannot decide. It also means that member governments or ownership ministries should not claim the body is “independent” when what they really mean is that no one wants to answer for it.

### 8. Use periodic review, merger, or sunset as ordinary governance, not as scandal response

Most authorities should not be immortal by default. The polity should periodically ask whether the body still fits the real functional geography, whether its remit has drifted, whether another form would now work better, and whether it should be merged, constitutionalised, re-scoped, or retired.

This does not mean creating instability. Essential operators need continuity. But continuity should come from good law and good transition planning, not from the superstition that once a public authority exists it must exist forever.

### 9. Keep arm’s-length bodies below constitutional floors they cannot waive

No special-purpose form should be allowed to escape fundamental public-law obligations. Civil-rights duties, labour baselines, audit, procurement integrity, public-records obligations, anti-corruption controls, judicial review, and remedy channels should follow the public function even when delivery is routed through an authority, corporation, or agency.

The body may be functionally special, but it is not constitutionally outside the polity.

## Guardrails

- Do not create a new authority when a ministry, municipality, region, or compact could do the job with less opacity.
- Do not confuse arm’s-length status with unaccountable autonomy.
- Do not let enterprise-like legal form dissolve public-law duties.
- Do not allow special-purpose bodies to accumulate general-purpose powers without constitutional upgrading.
- Do not let debt, subsidy, or procurement opacity become the hidden reason a body exists.

## Failure modes

- **agency sprawl**: every difficult function gets a new body, and the constitutional map becomes unreadable.
- **shadow government**: the most important decisions migrate into authorities that are too powerful to be “technical” and too insulated to be fully democratic.
- **governance vacuum**: bodies enjoy autonomy without clear steering, review, or performance accountability.
- **off-budget sovereignty**: debt, guarantees, and subsidy obligations accumulate outside ordinary fiscal scrutiny.
- **patronage shelter**: supposedly professional bodies become havens for appointments, rent distribution, or politically selective contracting.
- **authority creep**: a body created for one bounded task slowly becomes a de facto regional or metropolitan government without ever asking the public for that mandate.

## Practical tests

A special-purpose body architecture passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is there a clear public case for this body instead of an ordinary ministry, municipality, region, or compact?
2. Are remit, geography, powers, money, and review written into a hard charter rather than inferred from habit?
3. Is the board or leadership design proportionate to the body’s real powers and conflicts?
4. Are budgets, debt, subsidies, procurement, and liabilities visible enough to prevent off-book government?
5. Do ordinary public-law obligations — audit, records, integrity, rights, remedies, and judicial review — still apply?
6. Is there a scheduled review path for merger, redesign, re-scope, or sunset rather than institutional immortality by default?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can name **who governs a system day to day** only by pointing to a maze of authorities, agencies, boards, and corporations whose charters, finances, and review routes are hard to explain, it does not yet have **professional public administration**. It has **shadow constitutionalism by entity proliferation**.
