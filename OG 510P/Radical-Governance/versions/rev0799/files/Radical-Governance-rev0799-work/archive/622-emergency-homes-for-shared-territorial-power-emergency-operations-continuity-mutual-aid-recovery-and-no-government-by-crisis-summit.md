# 622 — Emergency homes for shared territorial power: emergency operations, continuity, mutual aid, recovery, and no government by crisis summit

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, it needs an honest **emergency home** as well as democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, participatory, knowledge, service, planning, capital, regulatory, contract, workforce, and digital-operating ones: a named territorial emergency authority or crisis cell; a real all-hazards risk picture for the territory; predefined escalation and mutual-aid rules with municipalities, operators, and the state; continuity of government and continuity of essential services; exercised communication and warning routes; explicit recovery and handback doctrine; and bounded extraordinary powers — so the territory stops being governed through improvised mayoral summits, operator improvisation, and temporary command arrangements that silently become permanent constitutional furniture.

## One-line thesis

Real metropolitan or regional government needs its own **emergency constitution**: a named territorial emergency authority, an all-hazards risk picture, predefined escalation and mutual-aid rules, continuity of government and essential services, exercised communication and warning routes, recovery and handback discipline, and bounded extraordinary powers — not government by crisis summit.

## Why this matters

The archive now says when shared territorial power becomes real government, what democratic form it should take, and what fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, participatory, knowledge, service, planning, capital, regulatory, contract, workforce, and digital-operating homes it needs.

What it still lacked was the **crisis and continuity analogue**.

That absence matters because many metropolitan and regional bodies become most real during emergencies. Floods, wildfire smoke, grid failures, transport shutdowns, drought, industrial accidents, cyber incidents, and extreme weather do not care much about municipal lines, and they rarely respect the comforting assumption that ordinary committee routines can simply stretch to meet exceptional conditions. When a shared tier already carries real territorial responsibilities for transport, water, land use, health capacity, environmental management, or infrastructure, the question in crisis is not whether it exists; the question is whether it has an honest emergency constitution of its own.

Without that constitution, crisis rule defaults to improvised summitry among mayors, ministries, operators, and police or civil-protection actors. Responsibilities blur, municipalities wait for signals that do not come, operators make de facto public choices without a territorial mandate, and temporary command arrangements linger after the emergency because nobody designed a visible handback path.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine for the **emergency home of shared territorial government**: when a real metropolitan or regional tier must stop improvising through borrowed crisis machinery and become able to prepare, co-ordinate, communicate, continue, recover, and hand back lawfully in its own name.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish ordinary co-ordination from an emergency home

Many shared bodies can survive with light co-ordination in ordinary times. That is not enough once the territory must handle genuinely shared hazards. A metro or region needs an emergency home when crisis response depends on cross-boundary prioritisation, common warning, mutual aid, emergency logistics, operator co-ordination, continuity choices, or recovery sequencing that no single municipality can honestly carry alone.

If the real territorial bargains appear only during emergencies, then the emergency lane is already one of the places where the government truly exists.

### 2. Start from a territorial all-hazards risk picture, not from generic emergency boilerplate

A shared tier should know the real hazard profile of its territory: flood plains, wildfire interface, heat risk, industrial and transport hazards, lifeline dependencies, cyber and communications fragility, vulnerable populations, and likely cascading failures across networks and services.

That does not mean every crisis gets a giant bespoke plan. It means the territory has one serious all-hazards picture and then adds hazard-specific annexes where they are genuinely needed. Emergency doctrine should be territorial and risk-based, not a shelf of generic documents copied from member governments.

### 3. Give the shared tier a named emergency authority or crisis cell

Once territorial crisis co-ordination is real, the archive should prefer one visible answer to the question: **who convenes, who decides within the lawful lane, and who holds the common operating picture?** The answer may be a statutory emergency authority, a metropolitan or regional crisis-management office, or a legally grounded crisis cell within the shared tier.

What matters is that the role is declared before the crisis. A territory should not discover mid-event that emergency convening depends on whichever mayor volunteered a room, whichever ministry official picked up the phone, or whichever operator happened to own the dashboard.

### 4. Predeclare escalation, mutual-aid, and support thresholds across the stack

A real shared tier should not wait for panic or television pressure before deciding when to ask for help, when to pool resources, or when to hand specific functions upward or outward. The archive should prefer visible triggers for escalation: cross-boundary spillover, resource exhaustion, infrastructure interdependence, operator failure, public-health scale, communications breakdown, or rights risk beyond local capacity.

The same doctrine should define mutual aid downward and sideways: how municipalities support each other, how the shared tier allocates scarce common assets, how operators participate, and how the state enters when the territory’s own depth is exhausted.

### 5. Combine continuity of government with continuity of essential services

Emergency design should cover both **continuity of government** and **continuity of territorial services**. The first asks whether the shared tier can still convene legally, issue decisions, keep records, communicate, and authorise spending. The second asks whether transport, water, waste, sheltering, emergency procurement, inspection priorities, and other essential services can continue or be restored in a declared order.

A territorial emergency home is incomplete if it can hold briefings but cannot keep services running, or if it can keep buses and pumps moving but cannot lawfully decide, record, review, and communicate what it is doing.

### 6. Dock emergency action to warning, public communication, and inclusion duties

Shared territorial crisis rule becomes dishonest when the public has to piece together warnings from scattered municipal posts, operator feeds, or rumor. The archive should prefer one visible territorial communication doctrine: who issues alerts, how warnings are harmonised across member governments, how false or stale information is corrected, and how inclusive communication works across different channels, languages, disabilities, and digital access conditions.

Emergency communication is not a press add-on. It is part of the emergency constitution because it determines whether residents can locate authority, understand instructions, and challenge obvious mistakes in time.

### 7. Exercise the system and train leaders, not just frontline responders

A shared emergency home should be exercised in forms proportionate to the territory’s actual risks: seminars, workshops, table-tops, drills, functional exercises, and where justified full-scale rehearsals. The archive should prefer exercise programmes that include leaders, operators, and counterpart governments, because many failures appear not in the field but in convening, escalation, communication, and cross-institution judgment.

A plan that has never been exercised at the territorial level is mostly theory.

### 8. Include operators and infrastructure dependencies inside the constitutional picture

Metropolitan and regional emergencies often turn on infrastructure systems that are publicly owned, privately run, nationally regulated, or split across all three. The archive should therefore prefer emergency structures that can see and manage critical interdependencies, share information securely, request action from operators through declared routes, and keep continuity choices from becoming private bargaining behind closed doors.

If essential-service continuity depends on operator action, then the operator relationship belongs inside the emergency constitution, not beside it.

### 9. Treat recovery and "build back better" as part of the same constitutional lane

The emergency home should not vanish when the sirens stop. A real shared tier needs one visible recovery lane: damage and needs assessment, prioritisation for reopening and reconstruction, re-entry rules, temporary service substitutions, capital rephasing, and a connection from immediate response to longer-run resilience, land-use, and infrastructure decisions.

Otherwise the territory gets a sharp response phase followed by a fog of recovery deals, with rebuilding priorities quietly captured by incumbents, grant availability, or the loudest jurisdictions.

### 10. Keep extraordinary powers lawful, temporary, reviewable, and handed back

The archive should prefer crisis powers that are legally grounded, necessary, proportionate, transparent enough to be followed, and tied to clear renewal, review, and handback rules. Emergency clauses may concentrate action temporarily, but they should not become a back door to permanent territorial recentralisation, indefinite emergency procurement, or durable suspension of ordinary scrutiny.

If the shared tier cannot explain when emergency powers end and how ordinary governance resumes, then its emergency constitution is not yet honest.

## Emergency-home matrix

| Question | Thin or dishonest arrangement | Honest emergency home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Who convenes territorial crisis action?** | rotating mayoral summit or ministry improvisation | named territorial emergency authority or crisis cell |
| **What risks govern preparation?** | generic plans copied from elsewhere | one territorial all-hazards risk picture plus real annexes |
| **When does the stack escalate?** | panic, media pressure, or personal access | declared triggers for escalation, support, and mutual aid |
| **Can government itself continue?** | no lawful meeting, records, spend, or decision continuity | continuity of government with legal, records, and finance fallback |
| **Can essential services continue?** | operator improvisation and opaque prioritisation | declared service-continuity and restoration priorities |
| **How is the public warned and informed?** | scattered posts, rumors, and inconsistent instructions | harmonised warning and multi-channel communication doctrine |
| **How does the territory learn?** | post-crisis blame without structured review | exercised plans, after-action review, and visible improvement loop |
| **How does crisis rule end?** | emergency residue and unclear handback | time-bounded powers, review, recovery lane, and explicit return to ordinary governance |

## Failure modes

- **government by crisis summit**: real territorial emergency rule happens through improvised meetings among mayors, ministries, and operators with no declared constitutional lane.
- **paper emergency plan**: the territory has binders but no usable operating picture, no exercised thresholds, and no leader training.
- **service continuity blindness**: the shared tier can coordinate statements but cannot prioritise or maintain essential territorial services.
- **operator shadow command**: infrastructure operators make de facto public choices without a visible public authorisation and review chain.
- **warning patchwork**: residents receive conflicting or inaccessible instructions depending on municipality, platform, or operator.
- **recovery capture**: rebuilding and reopening decisions drift into grant opportunism, incumbent lobbying, or quiet bilateral deals.
- **exception without handback**: extraordinary powers or emergency procurement become the new normal because nobody designed a visible return path.

## Practical tests

A shared territorial emergency constitution passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can the territory name the lawful emergency authority or crisis cell that convenes common action, and is that answer valid before the crisis starts?
2. Does it maintain one serious all-hazards risk picture for the territory, with hazard-specific detail only where justified?
3. Are escalation, mutual-aid, and support thresholds visible enough that cross-boundary crises do not depend on panic or personal access?
4. Can the shared tier continue both its own decision/records/finance functions and the continuity of essential services in a declared priority order?
5. Does it have harmonised warning and communication routes that work across municipalities, operators, and non-digital channels?
6. Are operators and critical infrastructure dependencies inside the information-sharing and decision picture rather than outside it?
7. Are exercises, after-action review, recovery doctrine, and handback rules real enough that crisis governance does not become permanent constitutional residue?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a metropolitan or regional body can show **a resilience strategy, a few operator contacts, and emergency press releases** but cannot show **a named territorial emergency authority, an all-hazards risk picture, predeclared escalation and mutual-aid rules, continuity of government and essential services, harmonised warning routes, exercised plans, recovery doctrine, and handback discipline**, then it still lacks **an honest emergency home for shared territorial power**.
