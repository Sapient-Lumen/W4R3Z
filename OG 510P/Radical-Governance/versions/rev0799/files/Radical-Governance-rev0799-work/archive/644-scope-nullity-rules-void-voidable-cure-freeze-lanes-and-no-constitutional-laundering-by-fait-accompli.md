# 644 — Scope-nullity rules: void, voidable, cure, freeze lanes, and no constitutional laundering by fait accompli

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should distinguish **void**, **voidable**, and **curable** defects in materially stack-altering acts, and should require one public **scope-nullity ruling** or equivalent settlement object when a reform skipped the required scope lane: illegality should not ripen into constitutional fact merely because budgets spent, portals launched, or officials kept operating.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a much stronger doctrine for the full visible life cycle of stack-bearing authority. It says **where functions usually fit** (`579`, `602`, `603`), **how burden of proof, lawful rescoping, and reversibility should work** (`628`-`631`), **when the stack must be reopened and how it should be recorded** (`632`-`635`), **how scope-changing proposals should be classified, overridden, interpreted, and checked before launch** (`636`-`639`), and **how live arrangements should be observed, settled, retired, and reactivated** (`640`-`643`).

What it still lacked was one explicit doctrine for a recurrent constitutional failure mode: **what happens when a stack-changing move was never validly cleared at all**.

That failure is common in real systems. A ministry begins exercising a metropolitan transport veto even though the grant circular never passed the consent lane the archive would require. A local function is quietly centralized by software permissions, staffing instructions, or emergency guidance without the packet, opinion, or override that would have made the move legible. A territorial body keeps acting after the legal basis for its competence was struck down or never properly commenced. A negative scope opinion is ignored and the system goes live anyway. Months later, officials argue that unwinding is impossible because contracts are signed, staff have transferred, and the public now relies on the arrangement.

The archive already says that implementation drift should not be legalized after the fact (`639`, `640`), that retirement should kill live authority and residue together (`642`), and that dormant powers should not revive by convenience (`643`). But it still needed one note for the harder question: **when a stack-bearing arrangement is unlawful, what exactly follows?**

Without that doctrine, the archive risks two opposite errors. One error is **constitutional laundering by fait accompli**: an unlawful arrangement is tolerated because reversing it seems disruptive, so practice quietly becomes title. The opposite error is **remedial maximalism**: every defect, however technical or curable, is treated as requiring total institutional collapse even when narrower correction would restore legality without unnecessary collateral damage. An honest archive needs a rule for both.

The right answer is not that every defect voids everything, nor that illegality can always be regularised later. The right answer is that materially stack-altering defects should be **typed**, **publicly classified**, and **paired with the least autonomy-distorting remedy that actually restores legality**. Some moves are null from the start. Some are contestable until corrected or quashed. Some may be cured prospectively but not retrospectively. Some require an operational freeze while rights, money, records, and continuity are protected. The polity should say which is which.

That is the role of a compact doctrine of **scope-nullity rules**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish ordinary implementation error from true scope defect

Not every administrative mistake is a scope-nullity problem. The doctrine is triggered when the defect bears on the **constitutional location, holder, boundary, consent route, supervisory power, or durability** of public authority.

The question is not merely whether procedure was imperfect. The question is whether the defect goes to one of the stack-bearing conditions the archive has already made visible:

- the correct legal lane (`630`, `635`-`638`);
- the required consultation or consent route (`590`, `595`, `630`);
- the integrity of self-government or supervision boundaries (`583`, `584`, `593`);
- the conformance between authorized and implemented authority (`639`, `640`);
- or the lawful continuation, retirement, or reactivation of authority (`641`-`643`).

If the defect sits at that level, it should not be treated as routine housekeeping. It is a constitutional defect in the live stack.

### 2. Require one public scope-nullity ruling or equivalent settlement object

When a materially stack-altering defect is credibly raised, the polity should produce one visible object that classifies the defect and the immediate consequence: a **scope-nullity ruling**, or an equivalent court, legislative, audit, supervisory, or intergovernmental act if the system allocates the role elsewhere.

That object should state:

- what measure, workflow, or arrangement is challenged;
- what stack-bearing defect is alleged;
- whether the defect makes the act **void**, **voidable**, **curable**, or **non-material**;
- whether operations must freeze, narrow, continue temporarily, or hand back;
- what happens to acts already taken;
- what cure, if any, is legally available;
- what rights, review, and challenge routes remain open;
- and how the canonical scope register changes while the defect is being resolved.

Without that object, everybody starts improvising their own theory of validity.

### 3. Keep void, voidable, curable, and harmless defects distinct

The archive should not let every defect collapse into one vague category of “irregularity”. At minimum it should distinguish four outcomes.

- **Void**: the arrangement lacked a prerequisite so central that it never honestly entered live authority at all. Examples may include absence of required legal basis, missing constituent or territorial consent where constitutionally required, or assumption of powers that the acting body could not possibly hold in the chosen lane.
- **Voidable**: the arrangement is legally contestable and should remain vulnerable to suspension, quashing, or narrowing until the reviewing body decides. Examples may include serious but not automatically null defects in consultation, reasons, or classification.
- **Curable prospectively**: the defect can be corrected for the future through a valid lane, but the later cure does not pretend the earlier unlawful exercise was lawful all along.
- **Harmless or non-material**: the defect did not materially change the stack, self-government position, rights routes, or supervisory relation, and therefore does not warrant stack-level invalidation.

This is the note’s central anti-theater move. The polity should not pretend that all defects are equal, but it should also not let the word “curable” become an all-purpose laundering device.

### 4. Tie defect severity to the missed safeguard, not to political inconvenience

The classification should turn on **what safeguard was missed**, not on how expensive correction would be.

A defect should weigh more heavily where it involves:

- missing constitutional or statutory authority;
- bypass of required consultation or consent;
- silent narrowing of protected local or regional discretion;
- unlawful prior approval or supervision in an own-competence field;
- mismatch between the adopted measure and the implemented operational holder;
- or blocked access to judicial, administrative, or democratic challenge.

What should not determine validity is the familiar argument that reversal would now be awkward because budgets are committed, staff transferred, vendors contracted, or portals already live. Those facts matter for remedial design, but not for whether the underlying move was lawful.

### 5. Use freeze lanes when continued operation would compound illegality

Once a serious stack-bearing defect is identified, the polity should be able to place the arrangement into a **freeze lane** rather than choosing only between total continuation and total collapse.

A freeze lane may include:

- no further geographic expansion;
- no new coercive or irreversible decisions;
- no new staff transfer or procurement deepenings;
- no reconfiguration of digital permissions beyond safety and continuity needs;
- preservation of records, logs, and financial trails;
- and provisional routing to a lawful interim holder where rights or essential services require continuity.

This matters because some unlawful arrangements should stop growing before the final ruling, even when an immediate hard stop would cause avoidable harm.

### 6. Later cure should normally work forward, not backward

The archive should reject a common laundering move: **post hoc cure that claims the invalid arrangement was always valid**.

If the proper packet, consultation, consent, opinion, override, or enactment lane was skipped, a later lawful act may authorise the arrangement from that later date onward. It should not usually erase the fact that earlier operation lacked proper authority.

That distinction matters for:

- accountability of decision-makers;
- rights to challenge or remedy for those affected during the unlawful period;
- treatment of penalties, fees, approvals, or refusals made without proper authority;
- and the integrity of the scope register, which should show the period of contested or invalid operation honestly.

Prospective cure is often compatible with continuity. Retroactive laundering is not.

### 7. Preserve rights and continuity without turning necessity into title

When a defect is serious, continuity questions become real. People may depend on benefits, permits, transit service, emergency routing, water operations, or public notices issued through the defective arrangement.

The archive should therefore separate **continuity protection** from **validity judgment**. A polity may need temporary safe-harbour rules for reliance interests, emergency continuity, or replacement routing. But those accommodations should be named as emergency or transitional protections, not described as proof that the unlawful holder really had the power all along.

This protects both ordinary people and constitutional honesty. The state should not force the public to bear all the cost of its own unlawful design. But neither should it convert necessity into title.

### 8. Dock nullity rulings into the live stack memory

A scope-nullity ruling should not remain a free-floating legal opinion. It should update the same memory objects the archive has been building:

- the **canonical scope register** (`633`);
- the **scope-jurisprudence digest** (`634`);
- the **scope-diff packet** if a cure or redesign is pursued (`635`);
- any relevant **scope opinion** or **override** lineage (`636`, `637`);
- the **clear-statement** and **conformance** surfaces where ambiguity or deployment drift contributed (`638`, `639`);
- and the **observability**, **settlement**, **retirement**, or **reactivation** lanes where downstream status changes must now occur (`640`-`643`).

Otherwise the archive will know that the arrangement was unlawful in one corner while operators elsewhere continue to treat it as live.

### 9. Make clear which defects demand handback, redesign, or retirement rather than cure

Some defects are not honestly curable because the whole arrangement was wrongly typed from the start. In those cases the polity should say so and move to a different lane:

- **handback** where emergency upward concentration outlived its basis (`631`);
- **redesign** where the real problem is that the system needs a different constitutional home (`605`, `627`);
- **retirement** where the arrangement should end rather than be salvaged (`642`);
- **reactivation with fresh clearance** only if a lawfully dormant arrangement is the right answer after all (`643`).

The archive should not let “cure” become a sentimental way of avoiding the admission that a whole design was mistaken.

### 10. Publish the period of invalidity, contestation, or provisional continuity visibly

The live stack should have visible status terms for arrangements under nullity stress. At minimum the register should distinguish:

- **valid live**;
- **contested live**;
- **frozen pending ruling**;
- **void**;
- **voidable under review**;
- **prospectively cured**;
- **retired after invalidity**;
- and **reissued through lawful redesign**.

A future reader should be able to see whether an arrangement was always live, temporarily tolerated for continuity, later cured prospectively, or struck out and replaced. Constitutional memory should not depend on oral history.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat political inconvenience, sunk cost, or operational dependence as proof that an unlawful stack change became lawful.
- Do not treat every procedural defect as automatically void if the missed safeguard was not stack-bearing.
- Do not let later lawful enactment retroactively launder earlier unlawful exercise unless the constitutional order expressly and lawfully provides for that exceptional effect.
- Do not allow freeze lanes or continuity measures to become indefinite substitute title.
- Do not keep nullity findings in litigation files only; they should update the public stack memory.

## Failure modes

- **constitutional laundering by fait accompli**: an unlawful arrangement is left standing because unwinding is said to be too difficult after operational rollout.
- **retroactive cure theater**: a later enactment is narrated as proving the old unlawful arrangement had always been valid.
- **remedial maximalism**: every defect is treated as fatal even when a narrower remedy would restore legality without avoidable collateral harm.
- **freeze without clock**: interim containment becomes a long-term substitute for deciding validity.
- **nullity amnesia**: a court, audit, or supervisory finding exists, but the live register, public guidance, and operational systems continue as if nothing happened.

## Anti-theater tests

1. When a materially stack-bearing defect is raised, is there one visible ruling or settlement object that classifies the defect and consequence?
2. Does the polity distinguish void, voidable, prospectively curable, and non-material defects instead of calling everything an irregularity?
3. Is defect severity tied to the missed safeguard rather than to political embarrassment or unwind cost?
4. Can the system freeze growth and irreversible exercise without pretending the arrangement is valid?
5. If cure is possible, does it work forward rather than rewriting the legality of the past?
6. Are continuity protections named separately from claims about valid authority?
7. Do nullity findings update the register, jurisprudence digest, and linked scope artifacts?
8. When the design was wrong in kind, is the polity forced toward handback, redesign, or retirement instead of sentimental “cure”?
9. Can a future reader tell the exact period of contestation, invalidity, temporary continuity, and lawful replacement?
10. Did the public and affected authorities keep real review and remedy routes while the defect was being handled?

## Closing image

A constitutional stack should not be washable by momentum. When a scope-changing measure skipped the lane it needed, the polity should be able to say: **this defect was fatal, or this one was contestable, or this one can be cured for the future; this is what freezes now; this is how continuity is protected without laundering the past; and this is how the live stack record changes**. Anything weaker is just **constitutional laundering by fait accompli**.
