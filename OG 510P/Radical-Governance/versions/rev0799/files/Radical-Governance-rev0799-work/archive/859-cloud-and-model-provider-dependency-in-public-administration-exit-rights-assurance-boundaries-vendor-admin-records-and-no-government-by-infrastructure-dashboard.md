# 859 — Cloud and model-provider dependency in public administration: exit rights, assurance boundaries, vendor-admin records, and no government by infrastructure dashboard

## One-line thesis

When public administration depends on cloud platforms, AI models, identity providers, or managed software, the archive should treat the supplier layer as a public-governance surface requiring shared-responsibility maps, vendor-admin logs, portability, model/change notices, fallback channels, procurement contestability, and exit proof before it becomes invisible government by infrastructure dashboard.

## Why this matters

Public authority increasingly runs on private infrastructure. That does not make private suppliers the government, but it does mean that supplier architecture can decide what the government can know, prove, explain, migrate, repair, or stop.

A cloud console may control logs. An identity provider may control access. A model provider may control output behavior, safety filters, tool access, and update cadence. A managed case platform may control notice, queues, evidence, and deletion. A procurement vehicle may decide which vendors become practically available. None of these layers need formal police power to shape public outcomes.

The archive therefore needs a supplier-dependency lens. It should neither panic about all outsourcing nor normalize platform dependence as a mere technical implementation choice. The correct question is whether public power remains reconstructable, contestable, portable, and legally owned after the supplier becomes infrastructure.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Map the supplier authority surface

| Supplier surface | Governance question | Required record |
| --- | --- | --- |
| cloud hosting | where do data, logs, keys, backups, and regions live? | shared-responsibility map and data-location record |
| identity and access | who can create, suspend, escalate, or revoke users and roles? | privileged-access log and emergency-access policy |
| model service | who controls model version, safety settings, refusals, tools, and updates? | model / system card, version log, change notice, fallback path |
| case platform | who controls workflow states, queues, notices, and evidence? | process map, state model, exportable case packet |
| analytics / scoring | who controls variables, thresholds, retraining, and monitoring? | score file, monitoring record, override and appeal log |
| procurement vehicle | which suppliers can enter and on what terms? | entry criteria, competition record, conflict disclosure, off-ramp |
| integration partner | who can change interfaces or data transformations? | interface-control document and test record |
| support desk | who sees sensitive facts and can change operational state? | support-access log and confidentiality route |

A supplier is governance-relevant when its configuration can change public outcomes or evidence.

### 2. Separate assurance from delegation

Security authorization, risk assessment, certification, or marketplace eligibility is not the same as lawful delegation of public power.

| Artifact | What it can show | What it cannot show by itself |
| --- | --- | --- |
| cloud authorization | baseline security and risk controls | legality of the public decision using the service |
| AI risk framework | risk categories and mitigation practices | authority to decide benefits, penalties, or rights |
| procurement award | contractual eligibility | democratic legitimacy or service justice |
| vendor system card | declared behavior and limits | actual operational effects in public context |
| impact assessment | anticipated risk and mitigation | live performance, bias, or appeal sufficiency |
| incident report | known failure and remediation | absence of hidden failures |

The archive should cite assurance artifacts as evidence, not as substitutes for public-law authority.

### 3. Require public continuity proof

Before supplier-dependent administration becomes consequential, preserve a continuity packet.

| Continuity element | Minimum test |
| --- | --- |
| export | can the agency export cases, logs, reasons, notices, and data in usable form? |
| migration | can another provider or public system operate the service without rights loss? |
| fallback | can humans or alternate systems keep essential service running? |
| keys and credentials | can the public authority recover control after vendor failure or dispute? |
| records | can audits, discovery, public-records requests, and appeals reconstruct past action? |
| model rollback | can the agency pause or roll back a model or feature after harm? |
| termination | can contract end without destroying service continuity or evidence? |

A public service that cannot survive supplier exit is not fully publicly governed.

### 4. Make vendor-admin action reviewable

Vendor administrators can sometimes do what ordinary public officials cannot: inspect logs, modify environments, restore backups, change configurations, reset credentials, patch models, alter workflows, or access sensitive operational data.

The minimum rule is:

| Vendor action class | Required control |
| --- | --- |
| routine maintenance | role-limited access and periodic review |
| privileged emergency access | named authorization, time box, session record, after-action review |
| configuration change | change ticket, test evidence, approval, rollback path |
| data access | purpose, data class, minimization, retention, and confidentiality record |
| model update | version notice, impact triage, testing, and user-facing effect label |
| incident response | incident clock, correction plan, affected-party route, public record class |

Without vendor-admin records, the public record is incomplete.

### 5. Add model-provider dependency gates

For AI systems and model services used in public administration, require:

| Gate | Question |
| --- | --- |
| use-case authority | is this model allowed for this public function? |
| source hierarchy | what sources may the model rely on or cite? |
| abstention rule | when must the model refuse, defer, or route to a human? |
| version pin | which model and configuration produced the output? |
| output record | what was shown to the user or official? |
| human responsibility | who owns the decision after model assistance? |
| contest route | how can the affected person challenge the output or decision? |
| supplier change notice | what model, policy, tool, or safety change requires retesting? |

A model provider should not become a hidden administrative-law layer.

### 6. Treat anti-lock-in law as a governance source

Portability and switching rules matter because exit shapes legitimacy. Cloud-switching rules, procurement competition, open standards, data portability, and reusable public-sector solutions are not just market policies. They are safeguards against infrastructure capture.

The archive should therefore track:

- switching charges and exit fees;
- exportable data and digital assets;
- interoperability standards;
- contractual termination rights;
- proprietary interfaces;
- support for multi-cloud or fallback operation;
- public records after migration;
- whether the user or agency can continue a case after supplier exit.

### 7. Use a supplier-dependency verdict

Every consequential supplier-dependent system should receive one of these verdicts:

| Verdict | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| acceptable support tool | supplier does not control consequential public outcomes |
| governed infrastructure dependency | supplier layer matters but is mapped, logged, portable, and reviewable |
| capture-prone dependency | records, exit, correction, or model-change controls are incomplete |
| unlawful or unsafe delegation risk | supplier effectively decides or blocks public rights without authority and remedy |
| emergency-only bridge | temporary dependency allowed under sunset and continuity conditions |

This creates a design path between blanket rejection and careless outsourcing.

## Failure modes

- **dashboard government**: treating the supplier console as the real administrative state.
- **authorization laundering**: using security authorization or certification as proof of legal legitimacy.
- **AI procurement fog**: acquiring model capability without use-case authority, contest route, or version record.
- **vendor-admin invisibility**: letting supplier staff change public environments without public-record-grade logs.
- **exit impossibility**: promising contractual termination while data, logs, keys, workflows, or trained staff make migration impossible.
- **model-update surprise**: allowing provider-side changes to alter public behavior without retesting or notice.
- **support-desk leakage**: exposing sensitive case facts through vendor support without minimization and audit.
- **marketplace monoculture**: letting a procurement marketplace become the practical limit of public imagination.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Can the agency export a complete appeal-ready case packet after supplier exit?
2. Can a reviewer identify which supplier, model, region, version, configuration, and policy controlled the action?
3. Are vendor privileged sessions named, time-boxed, logged, and reviewed?
4. Does security authorization remain distinct from legal authority to decide or advise?
5. Can the system continue essential service during provider outage, termination, or dispute?
6. Can affected persons challenge supplier-shaped errors through a public route rather than a private help desk only?
7. Does procurement preserve competition, portability, and public records rather than only low price or fast deployment?
8. Can the public authority pause, roll back, or replace model functionality after harm?
