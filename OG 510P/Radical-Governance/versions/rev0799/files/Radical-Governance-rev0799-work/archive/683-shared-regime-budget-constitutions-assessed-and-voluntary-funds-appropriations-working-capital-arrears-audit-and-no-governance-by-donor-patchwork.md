# 683 — Shared-regime budget constitutions: assessed and voluntary funds, appropriations, working capital, arrears, audit, and no governance by donor patchwork

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed budget constitution** that separates adopted appropriations, assessed contributions, voluntary or earmarked funds, working-capital and reserve instruments, arrears consequences, internal and external audit, and public follow-through — so the regime is not governed by donor patchwork, cash panic, or audit afterlife.

## Why this matters

The archive now has notes on participation, representation, decision rules, officers and records, integrity, interpretation, sessions, and language (`671`–`682`). That is enough to explain **who sits in the regime, how it meets, what it publishes, and how it reasons**. It is still not enough to explain **how the regime pays for itself and how money discipline constrains power**.

That gap matters because many shared regimes do not fail first at high constitutional theory. They fail at the money layer.

A budget is adopted, but the real institution runs on earmarked side funds. Assessed contributions exist on paper, but working practice depends on voluntary bridges, emergency borrowing, or deferred payments. Arrears accumulate, yet no one can tell what legal or political consequences follow. Audit exists, but recommendations do not re-enter decision-making. The public is told the regime has one programme of work while insiders know there are actually several constitutions of money: the formal budget, the donor map, the cash crisis plan, and the quiet emergency exception lane.

Without a typed financial constitution, six familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **donor patchwork governance**: the official budget says one thing while real capacity follows whichever activities attracted earmarked money.

The second is **pseudo-budget constitutionalism**: the governing body votes on an appropriations document that does not honestly map execution reality.

The third is **cash panic government**: liquidity instruments, working-capital advances, or internal borrowing silently become the real operating constitution.

The fourth is **arrears amnesia**: states or members fall behind, yet the regime has no visible discipline about consequences, waivers, or recovery ordering.

The fifth is **cost-recovery fiction**: voluntary or trust-funded work consumes core institutional capacity without paying its real administrative or control costs.

The sixth is **audit afterlife**: internal and external audit exist, but they do not visibly feed back into corrections, budget choices, or governance reform.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **shared-regime budget discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Publish one visible budget spine

A serious regime should show, in one place:

- the adopted programme budget or appropriations,
- the amount to be financed by assessed contributions,
- the amount expected from voluntary or other revenue,
- any working-capital or reserve instruments,
- the authority to spend,
- and the reporting and audit cycle.

If outsiders must reconstruct the regime’s money constitution from scattered resolutions, donor dashboards, and year-end statements, the regime is already being governed partly off-book.

### 2. Separate assessed finance from voluntary or earmarked finance

Assessed contributions and voluntary contributions do not play the same constitutional role.

Assessed finance expresses the common baseline the members have collectively decided to sustain. Voluntary or earmarked finance may expand activity, accelerate priorities, or fund bounded trust purposes. It should not silently replace the common baseline while the regime pretends its core capacity is still collectively owned.

The archive should therefore prefer a rule that every budget surface states clearly:

- what is core and collectively financed,
- what is voluntary,
- what is earmarked or trust-based,
- and which activities are at risk if voluntary streams disappear.

### 3. Tie authority to an adopted appropriations lane

The regime should say who adopts the budget, who proposes it, who may reallocate within it, and what thresholds require return to the governing body.

This is the difference between a budget constitution and a funding atmosphere. Supplementary reallocations, reserve use, contingency releases, or donor-funded expansions should travel through named lanes rather than executive improvisation.

### 4. Type working-capital, reserves, and internal borrowing separately

Liquidity support is not free constitutional ambiguity.

Working-capital funds, reserve accounts, internal borrowing, bridge financing, and contingency instruments should each have:

- a legal basis,
- a permitted purpose,
- a ceiling,
- a replenishment rule,
- and a public reporting lane.

The archive should prefer a rule that short-term liquidity tools solve timing problems without quietly rewriting the regime’s long-term finance model.

### 5. Publish one arrears constitution

If assessed contributions matter, arrears matter too.

A serious regime should say:

- when an amount becomes overdue,
- when it becomes in arrears,
- what rights or consequences may be affected,
- how waivers or exceptions work,
- and in what order incoming payments are applied.

Arrears discipline does not require cruelty. It does require visible rules rather than selective embarrassment.

### 6. Make voluntary funds carry their real administrative cost

Voluntary funds, extra-budgetary awards, and trust-style instruments should not consume core staff time, systems, legal work, integrity control, or reporting infrastructure without paying their share of those costs.

The archive should therefore prefer full-cost or transparently justified partial-cost recovery rules, plus clear statements of any exceptions. Otherwise donors buy programme surface while the common budget quietly absorbs the institutional load.

### 7. Keep one treasury and accounting logic even when money comes from many sources

Multiple funding sources do not justify multiple realities.

A serious regime should keep one chart of accounts, one audited financial architecture, and one intelligible classification system linking programme, fund source, expenditure, and balance-sheet effect. Special funds may exist. They should still land in a common accounting and reporting spine.

### 8. Pair internal oversight with genuinely independent external audit

Budget discipline is not complete when the accounts close.

The regime should publish:

- internal oversight or internal audit responsibilities,
- the mandate and independence rule for the external auditor,
- audit-report publication routes,
- management-response duties,
- and follow-up tracking.

Audit is part of the living finance constitution, not a ceremonial appendix to it.

### 9. Expose donor concentration and earmark dependence

If a regime is materially dependent on a small number of donors, on heavily earmarked finance, or on unstable project funding for core functions, that fact should be public.

The archive should therefore prefer routine disclosure of:

- concentration risk,
- earmarking intensity,
- unfunded core obligations,
- and functions temporarily carried by exceptional finance.

This keeps “strategic flexibility” from becoming a euphemism for constitutional dependence.

### 10. Force money repair back into governance repair

When a regime faces chronic arrears, reserve depletion, donor capture risk, or repeat audit findings, the answer should not be only another emergency bridge.

The regime should have a named lane that asks whether the finance model itself needs reform: higher assessed finance, narrower mandate, stronger cost recovery, reserve rebuilding, contribution-rule revision, or clearer budget authority. Otherwise the regime will live in permanent fiscal triage.

## Working matrix

| Budget object | Honest rule | Why | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| adopted programme budget | identify total appropriation, funding mix, and spending authority | keeps the formal budget real | pseudo-budget constitutionalism |
| assessed contributions | define due dates, arrears status, and consequence or waiver lanes | preserves common baseline ownership | arrears amnesia |
| voluntary or earmarked funds | disclose scope, earmarks, and dependency | prevents donor patchwork | treating earmarked finance as neutral core capacity |
| working-capital / reserve instruments | give each purpose, ceiling, replenishment, and reporting rules | keeps liquidity tools bounded | permanent government by bridge financing |
| cost recovery | charge real support and control costs to voluntary work unless a stated exception applies | prevents silent subsidy of earmarks | donor programmes riding free on the core budget |
| internal and external audit | publish independence, reporting, and follow-up duties | closes the accountability loop | audit afterlife |

## Guardrails

- Do not let the official budget hide how much of the regime depends on voluntary or earmarked money.
- Do not let working-capital or borrowing tools become a substitute for finance reform.
- Do not treat arrears as purely political embarrassment with no legal type discipline.
- Do not let donors finance core institutional functions without visible dependency disclosure.
- Do not treat full-cost recovery as optional rhetoric if shared resources are actually being consumed.
- Do not publish audits without a visible management-response and follow-up lane.

## Failure modes

- **governance by donor patchwork**: the regime’s real agenda follows earmarks rather than the adopted common budget.
- **pseudo-budget constitutionalism**: the governing body approves a budget that does not honestly describe execution reality.
- **cash panic government**: short-term liquidity tools become the silent operating constitution.
- **arrears amnesia**: unpaid contributions accumulate without typed consequence or recovery rules.
- **cost-recovery fiction**: voluntary activity consumes common institutional capacity without paying for it.
- **audit afterlife**: auditors write, bodies receive, and nothing structural changes.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it publish one visible budget spine linking appropriations, funding sources, authority to spend, and reporting?
2. Are assessed and voluntary funds visibly separated rather than merged into one bland revenue line?
3. Are reserve, working-capital, and internal-borrowing instruments separately typed with purpose and ceiling?
4. Is there a public arrears rule naming overdue status, waiver lanes, and consequences?
5. Do voluntary or earmarked funds carry their real support and control costs under a stated rule?
6. Do all material funding streams land in one accounting and reporting architecture?
7. Are internal and external audit roles published, and is external audit independence explicit?
8. Are management responses and implementation follow-up for audit findings visible?
9. Does the regime disclose donor concentration and earmark dependence where they are material?
10. When fiscal stress repeats, does the regime have a reform lane rather than only another emergency bridge?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can **collect money, spend money, borrow time through reserves, and publish accounts** but cannot say **what counts as common finance, what counts as voluntary dependence, when arrears bite, how liquidity tools are bounded, and how audit findings change future governance**, it still has **financial activity without an honest budget constitution**.
