# 884 — Transition receipts and decommissioning dockets: rollback, replacement, migration, sunset, and no governance repair by disappearance

## One-line thesis

A public tool, authority, platform, pilot, emergency measure, supplier arrangement, database, or coordination body is not repaired merely because it is paused, renamed, absorbed, replaced, made optional, or ended; the archive needs a transition receipt proving terminal status, successor function, affected-user notice, record preservation, data closeout, procurement / contract closeout, defect learning, and relaunch gates before disappearance becomes governance.

## Why this matters

The archive now has strong launch and operating tests: cross-boundary form choice, waist capture, claim ledgers, defeat tests, supplier exit, handback, symbolic-authority mismatch, capacity floors, model-mediated decisions, public generative guidance, and staff-facing AI shadow action. But the most common institutional repair move is neither clean launch nor clean abolition. It is transition.

A defective public system may be converted into a modernization platform. A chatbot beta may vanish into a maintenance page. A joint body may be absorbed into a new department. A receiver may enter waiver status. A data platform may be renewed under a new contract. An emergency app may stop collecting public-health data but keep border-processing functions. A pilot may become a product. A product may become infrastructure. A failed body may be rebranded as a new office. A supplier may be changed while data, logs, and operating assumptions remain.

That is where governance records often disappear. The political announcement says the problem is solved. The operational reality says functions, people, data, vendors, records, risks, and costs moved somewhere else. This note creates a transition-receipt docket so the archive can distinguish genuine closure from successor amnesia.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Transition forms

| Form | Honest label | Receipt question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| rollback | capability removed and prior route restored | what changed back, and who was affected during the bad period? |
| withdrawal | interface or body ended without direct replacement | where do users go, what records remain, and what lessons were published? |
| conversion | emergency / pilot tool becomes ordinary service infrastructure | what authority, data, contract, and benefit claims were reapproved for ordinary use? |
| absorption | function moves into another department, body, or platform | who receives duties, records, contracts, claims, and complaints? |
| migration | data, users, or workflows move to a successor system | what was transferred, validated, deleted, and preserved for audit? |
| waiver / dormancy | oversight or control pauses but can reactivate | what powers remain live, what clock controls release, and what reactivation trigger exists? |
| reprocurement | supplier or contract changes while function persists | what exit proof, portability, and residual dependency were tested? |
| sunset | legal authority or program terminates | what closure record, final accounts, appeal tail, and rights tail remain? |
| relaunch | repaired tool returns | what incident ledger, validation, and launch gate changed? |

Transition is a governance event, not a communications event.

### 2. Minimum transition receipt

A transition receipt should answer at least ten questions:

| Field | Required proof |
| --- | --- |
| terminal event | date, decision-maker, legal / administrative status, and whether the old function is ended, optional, dormant, or converted |
| successor map | what function moved where, what vanished, what stayed, and who now owns it |
| affected users | who used, relied on, was excluded by, or was harmed by the old system, and how they were notified or remedied |
| records | what logs, decisions, prompts, versions, contracts, meeting minutes, defect reports, and correspondence were preserved |
| data | what personal, operational, financial, or model data was migrated, deleted, retained, corrected, or repurposed |
| contracts | what suppliers, licences, task authorizations, subcontracts, service levels, warranties, and exit obligations closed or continued |
| defects | what failures, incidents, audit findings, complaints, and near misses were recorded and assigned to repairs |
| finances | final cost, residual cost, successor budget, cost allocation, sunk-cost explanation, and benefit-claim separation |
| public route | where users, staff, auditors, courts, legislators, and complaint bodies go after the transition |
| relaunch gate | what evidence is required before the same function returns, expands, or is embedded elsewhere |

A receipt can say that some records are unavailable. It cannot silently pretend that missing records do not matter.

### 3. Anti-theater transition labels

These labels are not enough by themselves:

- **beta ended**;
- **no longer mandatory**;
- **modernized**;
- **absorbed into a new department**;
- **waiver granted**;
- **system retired**;
- **contract expired**;
- **new supplier selected**;
- **pilot completed**;
- **lessons learned**;
- **dashboard updated**;
- **all recommendations accepted**.

Each may be true. None is a transition receipt. The archive should ask what happened to duties, records, users, money, data, contractors, remedies, and successor gates.

### 4. Receipt ladder by risk

| Risk grade | Examples | Receipt floor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| low-risk interface | content page, style aid, search improvement | redirect, owner, source record, feedback closure |
| public guidance | chatbot, help wizard, eligibility explainer | bad-answer ledger, correction clock, user notice rationale, successor source gate |
| staff workflow | drafter, parser, queue aid, vulnerability flag | output / field preservation, staff-training update, queue-effect review, model-change gate |
| operational platform | data platform, border app, service portal, health or welfare system | data migration / deletion, supplier closeout, benefit evidence, fallback and continuity proof |
| legal-effect system | debt, benefit, licence, enforcement, immigration, policing | notice, review, stays, remediation, appeal tail, individual record preservation |
| emergency authority | public-health, security, fiscal, disaster, or crisis powers | legal terminal event, ordinary-law reauthorization, emergency-cost closeout, civil-liberties and affected-user receipt |

The higher the risk, the less credible a terse closure notice becomes.

### 5. Successor-amnesia channels

- **function laundering**: the old function survives inside a new platform while the old controversy is declared closed.
- **data laundering**: emergency data, logs, or profiles become ordinary-service inputs without renewed authority.
- **contract laundering**: task authorizations, support relationships, or vendor influence survive a nominal contract end.
- **cost laundering**: successor benefits are used to blur emergency-period cost and procurement failures.
- **incident laundering**: defects become anecdotes rather than a reusable failure taxonomy.
- **rights-tail evaporation**: people affected by the old system lose notice, review, refund, correction, or complaint routes.
- **audit-tail evaporation**: records necessary for auditors and courts disappear before accountability finishes.
- **pilot-to-platform drift**: a pilot ends by becoming infrastructure without a public launch gate.
- **decommission theater**: the interface disappears but records, duties, risk, or supplier dependencies remain unmanaged.

### 7. Claim-status rule

A transition claim should be split into at least four claims:

1. **terminal claim** — what ended, paused, converted, or moved;
2. **successor claim** — what replaced it and who owns the function now;
3. **repair claim** — what defects were fixed, accepted, or left unresolved;
4. **tail claim** — what rights, audit, contract, data, and cost records survive after transition.

Do not let a positive successor claim erase a negative repair claim. A platform can become useful after an emergency and still have an unrepaired emergency-period procurement, data, or affected-user failure.

### 8. Decommissioning as design evidence

Bad decommissioning is a design failure. It teaches that the original system lacked:

- a sunset clause;
- a data-retention and deletion map;
- a contract exit and portability plan;
- a version / testing ledger;
- a public correction and incident route;
- a successor approval gate;
- an individual rights tail;
- a final cost and benefit separation;
- a record-preservation hold for audit and litigation.

Future launch dockets should require those elements at the start. The best transition receipt is partly written before launch.

### 9. Holding

The archive should treat transition as a first-class governance event. The core rule is: **no governance repair by disappearance**. A tool, body, platform, pilot, supplier, database, or emergency measure may be ended, converted, or absorbed, but the archive should not score that as repair until a transition receipt proves what happened to authority, users, data, records, contracts, defects, costs, and successor gates.

## Anti-theater tests

This note fails whenever officials say “it ended,” “it is no longer mandatory,” “we accepted the recommendations,” “the beta closed,” “the function moved,” or “a new department owns it” without a public receipt. The repair record must separate terminal status, successor ownership, defect learning, affected-user tail, contract and data closeout, and relaunch conditions.
