# 604 — Diagnosing scope misfit: overbuild, underbuild, duplicate chains, orphan functions, and no government by institutional furniture

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should **periodically diagnose scope misfit** rather than merely inherit its jurisdictional stack: detect overbuilt levels, underbuilt levels, duplicate chains, orphan functions, paper metros or paper regions, false localism, and false centralism by testing whether each public problem has a real governing vocation, a visible lead for each lane of work, a minimum complete institutional kit, matched money and capability, and lawful connectors and review paths.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a stronger answer to three adjacent questions: **which functions belong at which scope** (`579`), **what an ideal government at each scope should look like** (`602`), and **in what order a serious polity should design the stack** (`603`). What it still lacked was a compact way to inspect an actually existing system and say what is wrong with it **without collapsing into slogan**.

That gap matters because many governments are neither simply centralised nor decentralised. They are misbuilt in more specific ways. Some are **overbuilt**: a level has chambers, titles, and ceremony without a distinct governing vocation. Some are **underbuilt**: a level is assigned heavy duties without the records, staff, money, or review routes needed to discharge them honestly. Some are **duplicated**: several levels all claim to govern the same field, but nobody can name who leads which lane. Some are **orphaned**: a real public problem sits between jurisdictions and is handled only by bargains, emergency fixes, or special-purpose workarounds. Some are **paper metros** or **paper regions**: the geography is real, but the democratic and administrative body is missing or hollow. Others are **false localism** or **false centralism**: the map says one thing, but the operational reality says another.

Without a diagnostic discipline, constitutional debate becomes prestige war. Every side points to a favorite scale, calls it either empowerment or efficiency, and ignores the narrower question: **what exactly is misfit, and what is the smallest honest repair?**

This note gives the archive a compact diagnostic grammar for answering that question.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Diagnose from public problems and lived service paths, not from constitutional pride

The first diagnostic question is not whether a tier exists in law, nor whether a political class is attached to it. It is whether a real public problem has a real governing home. Start with concrete paths: who notices the problem, who plans, who funds, who regulates, who delivers, who answers when things fail, and who can be challenged.

A multilevel system is misfit when those paths cannot be traced without insider knowledge. This is true even if the constitutional diagram looks elegant.

### 2. Distinguish **overbuild** from **underbuild**

Two opposite failures are often confused.

**Overbuild** means a level has more institutional furniture than its vocation warrants. It may have a chamber, executive titles, symbolic autonomy, or elaborate procedure without enough distinct work to justify them. Decorative provinces, ceremonial counties, and bodies that mainly relay grants or resolutions are common examples.

**Underbuild** means a level has more responsibility than its institutional kit can honestly carry. It may be elected but lack ordinance power, records discipline, technical staff, fiscal room, borrowing lanes, or effective review and remedy. Many municipalities are underbuilt in exactly this way: politically visible enough to absorb blame, administratively thin enough to fail.

The archive should treat these as different pathologies. Overbuild calls for consolidation of role, lane clarity, or narrowing of the institution. Underbuild calls for more kit, money, capability, or reassignment of tasks.

### 3. Detect **duplicate chains** and **overlap fog** by splitting fields into lanes

Shared responsibility is not itself a defect. The defect appears when multiple levels claim the same field without anyone naming the distinct lanes: rule-setting, finance, planning, delivery, data, inspection, appeals, and residual risk.

A system is misfit when several tiers can each plausibly say “transport,” “housing,” “health,” or “development,” but no one can specify who leads which lane. That is how citizens get a singular problem and a plural answer.

The corrective is not always to assign the whole field to one level. Often the corrective is to **type the field** more clearly: municipal intake and local service navigation, metropolitan planning and network operations, regional coordination or specialist support, national standards and solidarity finance, treaty-bound cross-border coordination where needed.

### 4. Find **orphan functions** and **shadow governments**

Some functions are not duplicated; they are homeless. Metropolitan land use, basin governance, labor-market matching, regional innovation systems, major asset maintenance, and cross-border emergency planning often fall into this category. Everyone touches them, but nobody owns them.

Where orphan functions persist, shadow governments appear: informal mayoral clubs, ministry steering groups, special-purpose authorities, party channels, procurement consortia, or emergency command habits doing work that the constitutional stack has never admitted openly.

The archive should treat this as a diagnostic warning. Shadow governance may sometimes be a necessary bridge, but if it becomes durable it usually signals that the formal stack is missing a real governing home.

### 5. Detect **paper metros**, **paper regions**, **false localism**, and **false centralism**

A **paper metro** exists when a metropolitan labor market, transport system, housing market, or utility geography is plainly real, but the metropolitan institution is too weak, too indirect, or too optional to govern it. The result is bargaining among municipalities, ministries, and operators where public authority should have been made visible.

A **paper region** exists when a province, state, department, or region has a formal shell but cannot name a distinct territorial-platform role, lacks meaningful own resources, or has too little shared-rule weight to matter.

**False localism** exists when a municipality is praised as self-governing while actually serving as an underfunded outlet for delegated or centrally scripted tasks.

**False centralism** exists when the center keeps legal responsibility for uniform rights or national policy but lacks lawful local presence, operational feedback, or credible delivery lanes, causing real power to drift into contractors, party brokers, or local informalism.

These are not ideological insults. They are specific diagnostic states.

### 6. Test **mandate, money, and capability** together

A scope is misfit whenever one of the three legs is missing.

- If the mandate exists without money, the system is practicing fiscal theater.
- If money exists without capability, the system is buying failure more expensively.
- If capability exists without lawful mandate or discretion, the system is often deconcentrated administration in costume.

The archive should therefore diagnose each major function through a three-leg test: **who is legally responsible, who can pay, and who can actually perform?** If those answers point to different tiers without an explicit connector, the design is unstable.

### 7. Check whether the stack has enough **connectors** to stay coherent

Even a well-assigned stack can fail through missing connectors. The diagnostic question is whether the system has enough vertical and horizontal machinery to keep assigned powers from collapsing into fragmentation.

At minimum, a serious multilevel stack should reveal:

- coordination routes where investment, regulation, and rights span levels,
- horizontal co-operation where small jurisdictions need pooled capacity,
- equalization where basic local self-government would otherwise collapse into tax-base luck,
- interoperable records and indicators so accountability survives handoffs,
- and review, intervention, and handback lanes for breakdowns.

If a system has assignments but not connectors, it is only a partial constitution.

### 8. Prefer the **smallest honest repair** before redrawing the whole map

Not every misfit requires abolition, merger, or constitutional overhaul. The repair sequence should usually run from smaller to larger interventions:

1. clarify the lane,
2. assign a visible lead,
3. add the missing connector,
4. add the missing kit,
5. repair finance or capability,
6. create or strengthen the real scope that the function needs,
7. and only then consider map surgery or wholesale transfer of powers.

This matters because governments often reach too quickly for mergers or recentralization when the real problem is orphaned lanes, missing equalization, or absent metropolitan coordination.

### 9. Make the **scope-audit packet** public, compact, and repeatable

A polity should be able to publish a short diagnostic packet for any major field or tier. The packet should name:

- the public problem,
- the geography that matters,
- the levels currently involved,
- the lanes of work,
- the current lead for each lane,
- the money/capability/review status,
- the identified pathology,
- and the proposed repair with a review date.

A good archive should prefer this kind of repeatable packet over broad, moralizing claims that one level of government is simply “too big” or “too small.”

## Quick diagnostic matrix

| Pathology | Core symptom | Most likely honest repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Overbuild** | institution has ceremony without a distinct vocation | narrow the role, merge lanes, or remove redundant furniture |
| **Underbuild** | duties outrun legal, fiscal, or administrative kit | add kit, money, staff, review, or reassign tasks |
| **Duplicate chain** | several levels claim the same field without typed lanes | split lanes and assign a visible lead for each |
| **Orphan function** | real problem sits between jurisdictions with no accountable home | create or strengthen the missing scope or connector |
| **Paper metro / paper region** | functional geography is real but the institution is hollow | give the scale real powers, finance, and legitimacy |
| **False localism / false centralism** | legal story and operational reality diverge | align mandate, capability, and democratic accountability |

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse institutional age or prestige with proof of vocation.
- Do not assume that overlap is solved simply by saying “coordination” without naming leads and lanes.
- Do not treat shadow government as normal when it is actually compensating for constitutional absence.
- Do not answer underbuild only with blame, or overbuild only with symbolism.
- Do not redraw boundaries before checking whether connector repair would solve the problem more honestly.
- Do not diagnose local failure without testing whether the national legal and fiscal environment made the failure predictable.

## Failure modes

- **furniture worship**: a tier is defended because its titles, buildings, and rituals already exist.
- **jurisdictional moralism**: centralism and localism are argued as virtues in the abstract rather than tested against actual functions.
- **field blur**: a sector name substitutes for a typed assignment of rule, money, delivery, and remedy lanes.
- **shadow constitutionalism**: real governing work migrates into agreements, grant conditions, agencies, or political clubs that the public cannot easily see or contest.
- **repair maximalism**: policymakers jump straight to merger or abolition when a narrower institutional repair would have worked.
- **diagnosis without review**: one-off reform language is published, but no packet, indicator, or return check exists to test whether the misfit was actually fixed.

## Practical tests

A scope diagnosis is serious when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can the polity name the distinct vocation of each relevant level in the field being reviewed?
2. For each lane of work, is there one visible lead and one visible review or remedy path?
3. Does the geography of the governing unit match the geography of the actual problem closely enough to govern honestly?
4. Is the empowered level neither overbuilt nor underbuilt relative to its vocation?
5. Are mandate, money, and capability aligned, rather than distributed across the stack by accident?
6. Are there explicit connectors for coordination, equalization, and interoperability where fragmentation would otherwise be predictable?
7. Can the polity identify any shadow or workaround institution that is carrying work the formal stack has never admitted openly?
8. Has the proposed repair been sized to the smallest intervention that would make the system honest again?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a government debate can say only that one level should have **more** or **less** power, but cannot say whether the real pathology is **overbuild, underbuild, duplicate chains, orphan functions, paper scale, false localism, false centralism, or missing connectors**, then it still has **a rhetoric of reform without a diagnostic for scope misfit**.
