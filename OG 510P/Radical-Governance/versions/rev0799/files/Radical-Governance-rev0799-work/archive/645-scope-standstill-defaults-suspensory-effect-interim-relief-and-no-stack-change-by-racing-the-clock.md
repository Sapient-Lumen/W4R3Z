# 645 — Scope-standstill defaults: suspensory effect, interim relief, and no stack change by racing the clock

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should give timely, credible challenges to materially stack-altering acts a visible **scope-standstill** or equally fast **interim-relief lane**, so the live constitutional stack cannot be rewritten simply because deployment moved faster than review.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a much stronger doctrine for the visible life cycle of stack-bearing authority. It says **where functions usually fit** (`579`, `602`, `603`), **how burden of proof, lawful rescoping, and reversibility should work** (`628`-`631`), **when the stack must be reopened and how it should be recorded** (`632`-`635`), **how scope-changing proposals should be classified, overridden, interpreted, and checked before launch** (`636`-`639`), **how live arrangements should be observed, settled, retired, and reactivated** (`640`-`643`), and **how unlawful stack changes should be typed and cured without constitutional laundering** (`644`).

What it still lacked was one explicit doctrine for a recurring review failure: **what protects the stack while a serious scope challenge is still unresolved**.

Without that doctrine, even a strong legality system can lose in practice. A ministry centralises a municipal function through software permissions and budget routing, then argues that courts should not interfere because the new workflow is already live. A metropolitan body begins exercising new vetoes before the required consent route is tested. A negative scope opinion is challenged, but the sponsor implements anyway and invites reviewers to unwind the arrangement only after staff have moved, contracts have deepened, and public dependence has grown. A local or regional authority has formal standing, but no realistic way to stop irreversible change before judgment arrives.

That failure mode is not the same as the one in `644`. Nullity and cure tell the polity what to do **once validity is classified**. This note addresses the earlier and often decisive question: **what happens in the meantime**.

If the system has no visible interim discipline, review arrives after the constitutional fact has already been manufactured. The public is then told that unwinding would be too disruptive, the courts are accused of threatening continuity, and the challenger is blamed for seeking remedy too late even though no timely suspensory lane existed. The stack changes not by law, but by speed.

The archive should reject that. A credible multilevel constitution needs a compact doctrine of **scope-standstill defaults**: when a timely, non-frivolous challenge targets a materially stack-altering act, the polity should either hold the change in place before go-live or provide a fast interim lane that can preserve the meaningful status quo before the dispute becomes irreversible.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat pending review as a constitutional stage, not as empty waiting time

A stack-bearing challenge should create its own visible status in the live constitutional record. The period between filing and final disposition is not procedural dead air. It is a real constitutional stage in which irreversible effects can accumulate.

The archive should therefore treat pending review as a governed state with its own rules for:

- what may continue;
- what must pause;
- what evidence and records must be preserved;
- what rights and challenge routes remain open;
- and which actor carries the burden of justifying deviation from the ordinary hold.

If none of that is explicit, speed will decide the case before judgment does.

### 2. Use a default standstill where the challenged move has not yet gone live

When a materially stack-altering act is challenged **before** operational cutover, the preferred default should usually be simple: **do not let the challenged change go live until the interim lane speaks**.

That does not require final merits judgment first. It requires one rapid checkpoint that decides whether the challenge is serious enough, the potential injury structural enough, and the threatened implementation irreversible enough that the default hold should remain in place.

This is the cleanest version of the doctrine because it avoids the false drama of later untangling. Where the live stack is still not yet flipped, the polity should prefer **pause before cutover** over **repair after momentum**.

### 3. If the change is already live, use targeted interim relief rather than all-or-nothing theater

Not every case can be handled by a total stop. Some changes will already be partly live when the challenge crystallizes. Others concern essential services where a hard stop would impose avoidable public harm.

The answer should not be to let everything continue untouched. The answer is a visible lane of **targeted interim relief** that can preserve meaningful review without pretending the arrangement is settled.

That interim relief may include:

- freezing geographic expansion;
- blocking further staff, asset, or record transfer;
- suspending new coercive or irreversible decisions by the disputed holder;
- preserving the prior appeal or complaint route alongside the new one;
- maintaining old and new systems in parallel where necessary for lawful continuity;
- and requiring one interim operator to act subject to explicit non-prejudice language.

The point is to prevent review from becoming ceremonial while still protecting continuity where continuity genuinely matters.

### 4. Put the burden on the sponsor of the challenged change to justify urgency

Once a timely and credible challenge is filed, the burden should not rest entirely on the challenger to prove catastrophe while the sponsor enjoys the benefit of momentum.

The sponsor of the stack-changing act should have to show, in public and quickly:

- why immediate continuation is necessary;
- why narrower interim measures would not suffice;
- what irreparable public harm would follow from a pause;
- what continuity safeguards exist for affected people;
- and how the arrangement can remain reviewable if some operations continue.

This is especially important where the sponsor controlled the timing, the cutover plan, or the legal lane selection in the first place.

### 5. Emergency exceptions should be narrow, temporary, and non-prejudicial

A genuine emergency may justify interim continuation of some challenged authority. But emergency should not become the back door through which a disputed scope change secures practical victory before review.

Where emergency continuation is allowed, the archive should prefer:

- short clocks;
- a written emergency justification;
- limitation to safety, continuity, or non-deferrable acts;
- preservation of logs, reasons, and reversibility evidence;
- explicit statement that interim continuation does **not** resolve validity;
- and automatic return to the ordinary review lane once the emergency claim cools.

Emergency operation may justify a temporary bridge. It should not quietly manufacture title.

### 6. Preserve the meaningful status quo, not merely the appearance of order

Interim protection should aim at the **meaningful constitutional status quo**, not just the latest administrative configuration.

That means asking what actually needs preserving:

- the prior lawful holder of the function;
- the prior review or appeal route;
- the prior territorial discretion that the challenger says was narrowed;
- the prior fiscal or supervisory relation;
- or the public’s ability to know which tier is truly answerable.

A system can look orderly while the real constitutional position is already being rewritten underneath. The archive should therefore prefer interim orders that preserve the stack’s reviewability and reversibility, not merely managerial calm.

### 7. Separate continuity arrangements from claims about rightful title

A common failure mode is to let the interim operator, fallback host, or continuity bridge become evidence that the disputed holder was the rightful constitutional home all along.

The archive should reject that move. Interim arrangements should carry explicit **without-prejudice** status. They may keep services flowing, rights reachable, and data safe, but they should not be narrated as proving the merits.

This matters especially where a higher tier temporarily takes over a lower-tier function, where a host municipality carries a disputed metropolitan function, or where a ministry continues running a service during nullity review. Continuity answers **who must act now**. It does not answer **who lawfully owns the competence**.

### 8. Dock the standstill state into the same live stack memory as everything else

A challenge with suspensory effect should update the same objects the archive has already built:

- the **canonical scope register** (`633`);
- the **scope-jurisprudence digest** (`634`);
- the relevant **scope-diff packet**, **opinion**, and **override** lineage (`635`-`637`);
- the **conformance** and **observability** surfaces if implementation timing is part of the dispute (`639`, `640`);
- and any later **nullity**, **settlement**, **retirement**, or **reactivation** object (`641`-`644`).

At minimum the register should be able to say:

- **challenged pre-live — held pending interim review**;
- **live but partially stayed**;
- **continuity bridge active without prejudice**;
- **emergency continuation under short clock**;
- or **interim relief refused with reasons**.

Otherwise the public sees a live system while the legal record knows it is under constitutional restraint.

### 9. Keep the interim lane fast enough to matter

A standstill rule that produces six months of silence is not really a standstill rule. It simply changes the form of the fait accompli.

The archive should therefore prefer:

- short filing-to-hearing clocks for stack-bearing interim requests;
- reasoned decisions in public;
- visible anti-starvation escalation if the reviewing body stalls;
- and automatic reconsideration if the interim state outlives its original assumptions.

Speed matters here because the whole point of the note is to stop constitutional reality from being built faster than legality can speak.

### 10. Make room for partial stay, severability, and phased protection

Not every scope dispute requires freezing every surrounding function. A well-designed interim lane should be able to preserve the reviewable core while allowing separable, lawful, or purely housekeeping components to continue.

That may mean:

- staying the coercive part but not the informational part;
- staying territorial expansion but not continuity of incumbent service;
- staying new enforcement powers but not existing assistance functions;
- or staying transfer of title while permitting temporary operating support.

The archive should reject both extremes: **blanket continuation because full stop seems hard**, and **performative total freeze when narrower protection would do**.

## Guardrails

- Do not let a materially stack-altering change outrun review merely because implementation plans were made early and objections were not.
- Do not confuse temporary continuity arrangements with judgment on the merits.
- Do not make emergency continuation open-ended or self-renewing.
- Do not treat denial of interim relief as proof that the challenged change is lawful on the merits.
- Do not keep interim restraint off-book; the live stack memory should show when a change is stayed, narrowed, or running under protest.

## Failure modes

- **stack change by racing the clock**: the sponsor moves faster than review and then treats sunk cost as constitutional argument.
- **standstill without speed**: the interim lane exists on paper but takes so long that the same fait accompli forms underneath it.
- **continuity as title**: a temporary bridge or caretaker arrangement is later narrated as proof of rightful constitutional ownership.
- **emergency as solvent**: safety claims dissolve every ordinary protection and never cool back into reviewable normality.
- **invisible interim state**: operators know a change is under challenge, but the public register, channels, and service surfaces still present it as ordinary settled government.

## Anti-theater tests

1. When a timely, credible challenge targets a materially stack-altering act, is there a visible standstill or fast interim-relief lane that can still matter before irreversibility sets in?
2. If the challenged change has not yet gone live, does the default favor pause before cutover rather than heroic untangling afterward?
3. If some live operation must continue, are the continued parts narrowly defined, reasoned, and explicitly without prejudice to the merits?
4. Is the sponsor of the change forced to justify urgency and irreparable harm, rather than benefiting automatically from momentum?
5. Can the interim lane preserve the meaningful constitutional status quo rather than just the newest operational configuration?
6. Are emergency exceptions short-clocked, logged, and prevented from deciding the merits by habit?
7. Do standstill states update the canonical register and linked scope artifacts so the public can see that the stack is under review?
8. Is the interim lane fast enough that “review pending” does not simply mean “change already entrenched”?
9. Can relief be partial and severable where that protects review better than either total continuation or total stoppage?
10. After the interim phase ends, can a future reader see exactly what was paused, what continued temporarily, and why?

## Closing image

A constitutional stack should not be won on speed alone. When a serious scope dispute is filed, the polity should be able to say: **this change is paused before cutover**, or **this part may continue temporarily for continuity**, or **this emergency bridge is short, logged, and without prejudice**, and **this is the public record that shows it**. Anything weaker invites **stack change by racing the clock**.
