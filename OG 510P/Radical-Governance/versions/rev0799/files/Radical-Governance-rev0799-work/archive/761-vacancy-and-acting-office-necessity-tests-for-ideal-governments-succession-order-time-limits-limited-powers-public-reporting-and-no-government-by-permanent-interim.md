# 761 — Vacancy and acting-office necessity tests for ideal governments: succession order, time limits, limited powers, public reporting, and no government by permanent interim

## One-line thesis

Ideal governments should treat vacancies, succession, holdover service, and acting appointments as **continuity bridges rather than alternate constitutions of rule**: they should publish who succeeds automatically, who may serve temporarily, what powers are narrowed during interim service, how quickly the permanent appointment lane must reopen, and why courts, regulators, watchdogs, ministers, and public-service leadership should never be governed indefinitely by serial temporary placeholders.

## Why this matters

The archive already says a great deal about **executive continuity** (`493`), **civil service and workforce continuity** (`496`, `620`, `715`), **head-of-state succession and incapacity** (`754`), **government formation and caretaker limits** (`752`), **constitutional review** (`756`), **independent institutions** (`759`), **emergency powers** (`757`), and **scope-transition continuity packets** (`730`-`738`).

What it still lacked was one compact doctrine for a quieter but chronic constitutional problem:

**when an office falls vacant, who may bridge continuity, for how long, with what powers, and under what pressure to complete the real appointment?**

That gap matters because many polities do not openly seize institutions. They leave them vacant, install “acting” leaders, roll forward expired terms, or use temporary assignments to bypass merit, confirmation, or independence protections. A state can therefore preserve the form of lawful appointments while governing key offices through serial provisionality.

Ideal-government design therefore needs a doctrine of **continuity without vacancy capture**. The question is not whether temporary service is ever allowed. It is whether temporary service remains visibly subordinate to the permanent constitutional lane.

## Necessity table

| Necessity test | What counts as proof | Honest design consequence | Default answer if proof is weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Need for immediate continuity of core functions** | the office cannot sit empty without interrupting legal authority, public safety, finance, adjudication, or service continuity | automatic succession order or tightly bounded acting service | vacancy left to improvisation |
| **Need for temporary bridge while lawful selection completes** | the permanent appointment route cannot be completed before operations would stall | short acting term linked to a mandatory permanent-appointment clock | repeated acting renewals that substitute for real selection |
| **Need for incapacity or absence management** | the holder is alive or lawfully in office but temporarily unable to act | deputizing or incapacity rules distinct from removal or vacancy | informal assumption of powers by proximity or force of habit |
| **Need for quorum or panel continuity** | a collegial court, board, or commission risks becoming non-operational through ordinary vacancy | limited holdover, substitute, or replenishment rules that preserve institutional balance | ad hoc appointments that change the body's constitutional composition |
| **Need for emergency continuity** | war, disaster, or communications collapse interrupts ordinary succession or confirmation logistics | emergency continuity bridge with immediate review and fresh-validation clocks | unlimited temporary command by whoever can act first |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish succession, acting service, holdover, and replacement as different legal states

The archive should not let all vacancy language collapse into one vague idea of “someone covering the role”. Ideal governments should distinguish:

- **succession**, where the law pre-names who takes over automatically;
- **acting service**, where someone temporarily exercises the office pending proper appointment;
- **holdover service**, where the prior holder remains temporarily after term expiry to prevent a legal void;
- **replacement**, where the permanent appointment or election route completes.

Those states solve different problems and carry different legitimacy. If they are blurred together, temporary service will quietly harden into a substitute constitution.

### 2. Pre-name continuity routes for apex offices rather than improvising under stress

Constitutions should not wait for crisis to decide who signs, commands, certifies, or convenes when an apex office falls vacant or the holder is incapacitated. Heads of state, heads of government, ministers with irreplaceable statutory powers, court presidencies where necessary, treasury or budget sign-off roles, and similarly critical offices need declared succession or deputizing rules.

The archive should therefore prefer ex ante continuity maps over emergency improvisation. A polity that decides succession only after a vacancy has already become political has waited too long.

### 3. Time-limit acting service and tie it to a live permanent-appointment clock

Temporary service is honest only if it remains temporary in law and in practice. The archive should therefore require visible maximum durations, one clear renewal rule if any renewal is ever lawful, and automatic pressure to reopen or complete the permanent selection process.

An acting official who can serve indefinitely, or whose term can be repeatedly refreshed by inaction, is not a bridge. That office has already acquired a shadow appointments lane.

### 4. Keep acting officials inside the ordinary eligibility and conflict rules

Governments often use acting service to bypass the qualifications, merit competitions, conflict screens, or confirmation routes that would bind a permanent appointment. Ideal governments should therefore insist that temporary appointees still satisfy the core legal requirements of the office class unless the constitution creates a very narrow emergency exception.

The archive should reject continuity rules that become a back door for appointing people who could not lawfully hold the office if the polity were being honest.

### 5. Narrow interim powers where irreversible decisions would pre-empt the permanent officeholder

Some continuity holders must be able to keep the institution moving. But they do not always need the full political, staffing, or constitutional powers of a fully appointed officeholder.

The archive should therefore prefer an explicit distinction between

- **ordinary continuity powers** needed to keep lawfully ongoing work moving, and
- **high-consequence powers** such as major policy redesign, permanent appointments, irreversible contracts, structural reorganization, large discretionary grants, or constitutional confrontation, which may need additional authorization or should await the permanent officeholder.

### 6. Protect courts and referee institutions especially strongly against provisional capture

Temporary placeholders are most dangerous where the office is supposed to resist government rather than serve it. Courts, prosecution leadership where independent, election bodies, audit institutions, ombuds, and similar referee institutions should not be filled casually with serial acting leaders or ad hoc members chosen for convenience.

The archive should therefore place a high burden of proof on temporary occupancy in referee institutions and should prefer continuity rules that preserve composition, neutrality, and challengeability rather than letting executive convenience decide who wears the robe or chair.

### 7. Use holdovers to preserve legality, not to reward deliberate appointment failure

Sometimes the cleanest way to avoid a legal gap is to let the previous officeholder remain in place for a short period until a successor is lawfully appointed. That can be honest. But the archive should be wary of holdover systems that let appointing actors benefit from delay or let incumbents remain indefinitely because replacement is politically hard.

Holdover service should therefore be explicit, temporary, challengeable, and paired with pressure on the nominating or confirming actors to complete the replacement.

### 8. Publish vacancy, acting, and expiry data as constitutional information

Vacancy capture flourishes in opacity. Ideal governments should therefore publish who is serving in acting capacity, on what legal basis, from what date, under what expiry rule, with what narrowed powers if any, and where the permanent appointment process stands.

The archive should treat this as constitutional information, not internal HR trivia. The public cannot contest what it cannot see.

### 9. Keep continuity bridges separate from emergency-power myths

A vacancy or incapacity does not automatically justify emergency government. Most continuity problems are ordinary constitutional design questions and should be solved through succession, acting, deputizing, or quorum rules.

The archive should therefore reject the move from “an office is vacant” to “someone now has extraordinary crisis power.” Vacancy rules preserve continuity; they do not suspend the constitution.

### 10. Treat repeated provisionality as a design failure that triggers repair

If the same office or office class is repeatedly filled by acting leaders, serial renewals, or chronic holdovers, the problem is no longer episodic. It signals that the appointment route, confirmation design, qualification rules, pay structure, political incentives, or body composition needs reform.

The archive should therefore treat repeated provisionality as a repair trigger rather than as an acceptable background condition of government.

## Guardrails

- Do not let temporary service become an alternate route around merit, confirmation, or independence protections.
- Do not leave apex-office succession to improvisation.
- Do not let acting appointments run indefinitely or renew automatically without friction.
- Do not give interim officeholders unlimited authority to make irreversible staffing, fiscal, contractual, or constitutional moves unless the law explicitly justifies it.
- Do not hide vacancy and acting status from the public.
- Do not confuse ordinary continuity design with emergency-power design.

## Failure modes

- **permanent interim**: an office is governed through serial acting renewals rather than lawful replacement.
- **vacancy capture**: strategic delay becomes a method of institutional control.
- **eligibility bypass**: temporary status is used to install someone who could not survive the real appointment lane.
- **acting sweep**: a government fills multiple referee or senior public-service roles through provisional assignments.
- **holdover dependency**: expired mandates continue indefinitely because replacement has become politically inconvenient.
- **interim irreversibility**: temporary officeholders make long-term appointments, contracts, or structural changes that pre-empt successors.
- **crisis laundering**: a routine vacancy is narrated as justification for exceptional powers.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **who is currently “acting”** but cannot also say **why the vacancy exists, what legal state applies, what powers are narrowed, when the temporary authority expires, how the permanent appointment clock is being forced forward, and how the public can contest abuse**, then it still has **continuity language without a continuity constitution**.
