# 671 — Shared-regime representation ladders: credentials, chief delegates, alternates, observers, speaking rights, committee access, and no state voice by self-appointment

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed representation ladder** that separates party or member status from delegate credentials, chief delegates from alternates and advisers, participants from observers, and speaking rights from voting rights — with accrediting authority, challenge lane, substitution rules, public roster, and functional-independence protections — so no one can conjure state or member voice by badge, donor access, press credential, or self-appointment.

## Why this matters

The archive now says more clearly how a shared regime should choose depth (`663`), allocate burdens (`664`), land commitments (`665`), monitor compliance (`666`), differentiate commitments (`667`), change over time (`668`), and govern entry and exit (`669`–`670`).

But one constitutional object is still missing:

**who exactly is allowed to act for a participant once the participant is inside the regime?**

Without that object, institutional language turns fuzzy very fast.

A permanent mission may imply it can bind the state on every question because it is physically present in the host city. A ministry may send experts and describe them as the "delegation" even though no proper credentials were issued. A conference may let observers speak so freely that outsiders start sounding like members. A chair may treat an adviser, consultant, or host-state liaison as if they carry the same standing as a duly accredited chief delegate. NGOs, intergovernmental organizations, and regional coordinators may all be in the room, but their rights bundle is rarely identical.

Those distinctions are not ceremonial. They determine who may speak, submit, vote, object, trigger agenda rights, serve on a bureau, or accept compromises on behalf of the participant. They also determine whether representation is independent enough to do the job, or captured by host-state logistics, donor dependence, or secretariat improvisation.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **representation discipline inside shared regimes**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate participant status from representation status

Being a party, member, observer, or applicant does not automatically tell the public **who may speak or act** for that entity at a given session.

The archive should therefore keep separate:

- regime status of the entity,
- accredited delegation for the session or period,
- and the rights attached to each role inside that delegation.

A state may be fully bound and still fail to credential a lawful voting delegate for a particular sitting. An observer may be present without any claim to vote. Representation status is its own constitutional layer.

### 2. Name the accrediting authority and acceptable form of credentials

A regime should not leave it to vibes, badges, or hotel-side assurances to determine who represents whom.

The archive should require each regime to state:

- which authority may issue credentials,
- what form counts,
- where and when credentials must be lodged,
- and what provisional status applies before final acceptance.

Where credentials may be issued by heads of state, foreign ministers, health ministers, treaty focal authorities, or the competent authority of a regional organization, the rule should say so. If a note verbale, signed letter, or deposited instrument is acceptable, that should also be explicit.

### 3. Distinguish chief delegate, delegate, alternate, adviser, and technical expert

A serious regime should not call every person in the room a "delegate".

The archive should keep separate at least:

1. chief delegate or head of delegation,
2. delegate with speaking and voting authority,
3. alternate who may replace or stand in for a delegate,
4. adviser who may speak only with permission,
5. and expert or technical support personnel who inform the delegation but do not act in its name unless expressly authorized.

This is the simplest way to stop role drift.

### 4. Keep a credentials review lane and challenge lane

Credentials review is not anti-participation. It is what keeps representation legible.

The archive should therefore prefer:

- a credentials committee or other named review body,
- provisional seating pending review where appropriate,
- an objection lane when another participant contests credentials,
- and a visible decision on acceptance.

But challenge should not become a weapon for silent disenfranchisement. Until the challenge is resolved, the regime should say what provisional rights do or do not apply.

### 5. Give each representation status an explicit rights bundle

The public should be able to tell, from one visible roster, whether a person or delegation may:

- attend plenary,
- attend committees,
- speak,
- submit proposals,
- co-sponsor text,
- raise procedural points,
- vote,
- serve as officer,
- or only observe.

The archive should reject any regime where these rights have to be reconstructed from custom, WhatsApp messages, or chair discretion.

### 6. Keep observer, intergovernmental, and nongovernmental participation explicit and non-voting unless the text says otherwise

Observers, associated organizations, intergovernmental bodies, and NGOs can contribute real value. They should not be confused with parties or members.

The archive should therefore insist that regimes say:

- which observer classes exist,
- how observer status is granted or reviewed,
- what meetings each class may attend,
- where they may speak in the speaking order,
- what documents they can access,
- and that they do not vote unless the constituting text expressly provides otherwise.

Observer access is a participation design choice. It is not disguised membership.

### 7. Separate bureau, committee, and officer rights from ordinary floor rights

Representation inside a regime is usually layered.

A chief delegate might vote in plenary but not automatically sit on a bureau. A regional coordinator might route information without exercising the full rights of the participant. A committee chair may control the order of speakers but still remain bound by the committee's rulebook. A substitute may sit in one body but not another.

The archive should therefore ask each regime to type:

- plenary rights,
- committee rights,
- bureau rights,
- subsidiary-body rights,
- and substitute or proxy rules.

Otherwise power flows through committee architecture that remains publicly invisible.

### 8. Preserve functional independence, privileges, and conflict discipline

Representatives need enough legal and practical protection to exercise their functions independently. But those protections are functional, not personal.

The archive should therefore prefer a simple rule:

- privileges and immunities exist to protect the participant's independent representation,
- conflicts of interest, donor capture, and secretariat dependence should still be disclosed and constrained,
- and regimes should never let convenience support (travel grants, secondments, hosts, or consultants) blur who actually speaks for the participant.

A state voice or member voice should not be rentable.

### 9. Publish one public delegation and rights register

Representation claims should be checkable without calling the chair's office.

A serious regime should maintain a public or otherwise appropriately visible register showing, at minimum:

- participant name,
- delegation head,
- accredited delegates,
- alternates where relevant,
- observer class where relevant,
- and the rights bundle attached to the role.

The register need not expose unnecessary personal data. But it should expose enough to answer who may speak, vote, submit, and stand in.

### 10. Give substitution, remote participation, and emergency continuity their own rule

Shared regimes now operate across travel shocks, sanctions, disasters, pandemics, and technical failures.

The archive should therefore require a rule for:

- how an alternate becomes the acting delegate,
- how remote attendance counts or does not count,
- how quorum interacts with virtual presence,
- and what evidence establishes that the participant was actually represented.

Without this, emergency adaptations quietly rewrite representation law.

## Working matrix

| Representation status | Usual honest rights bundle | Typical credential basis | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chief delegate / head of delegation | attend, speak, vote, designate substitutes, sometimes nominate or negotiate on behalf of the participant | formal credentials from the competent authority | assuming hotel presence or permanent mission location alone proves authority |
| Delegate | attend, speak, vote in the bodies covered by the rules | formal credentials naming the person or delegation | treating all listed attendees as interchangeable voting delegates |
| Alternate | attend and replace a delegate where the rules permit | credentials plus substitution rule | invisible seat-swapping with no record |
| Adviser / expert | attend and, with permission, speak on particular points | delegation list, usually under chief delegate control | adviser speech treated as if it were a binding vote or objection |
| Observer state or organization | attend defined meetings, access documents, make statements when allowed, no vote unless expressly provided | observer invitation or approved observer status | calling observers "members" or giving them implied voting power |
| NGO or civil-society observer | attend and speak within observer rules, no vote | observer accreditation under explicit criteria | opaque access privileges negotiated privately with the chair or secretariat |
| Bureau or officer role | agenda-shaping or procedural powers fixed by rules, not a free-floating mandate | election/appointment under rules plus underlying representation status | chair control without a visible mandate or limits |

## Guardrails

- Do not let member status collapse into "anyone from that country in the room can act."
- Do not let observers acquire voting authority by custom, deference, or chair drift.
- Do not let secretariat convenience substitute for credentials discipline.
- Do not hide committee and bureau rights inside informal practice.
- Do not treat functional immunities as personal privilege or as immunity from conflict rules.
- Do not let emergency remote procedures quietly rewrite who counts as represented.

## Failure modes

- **self-appointment voice**: a person claims to speak for the participant because no one openly challenged them.
- **badge constitutionalism**: conference access badges are treated as proof of legal authority.
- **observer inflation**: observers receive practical rights that mimic membership without the legal path of entry.
- **committee dark power**: real bargaining and text control happen in bodies whose membership and rights bundles are unreadable.
- **substitution fog**: alternates or advisers begin acting as voting delegates with no visible trigger.
- **rent-a-delegation**: logistical sponsors, donors, or host institutions effectively determine representation capacity.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can the public tell who validly represents each participant for this session or period?
2. Does the regime say who may issue credentials and what form they take?
3. Are chief delegates, delegates, alternates, advisers, and observers visibly distinguished?
4. Is there a named credentials review and challenge lane?
5. Can a reader tell exactly who may speak, submit, and vote in each body?
6. Are observer and NGO rights explicit and non-voting unless the text says otherwise?
7. Are substitution and remote-participation rules visible rather than improvised?
8. Are independence protections and conflict constraints both acknowledged?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can say **who the participants are** but cannot say **which natural persons are actually authorized to act for them, with what rights, through what credentials, under what challenge and substitution rules**, it still has **membership language without an honest representation constitution**.
