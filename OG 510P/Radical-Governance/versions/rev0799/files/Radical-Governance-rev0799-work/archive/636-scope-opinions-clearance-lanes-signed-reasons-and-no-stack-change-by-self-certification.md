# 636 — Scope opinions, clearance lanes, signed reasons, and no stack change by self-certification

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should require any materially stack-altering proposal to receive one public **scope opinion** from a named review lane that classifies whether the measure leaves the scope stack untouched, changes it lawfully through an ordinary lane, requires stronger consultation or consent, or is not adoption-ready at all, so the constitutional map cannot be rewritten by sponsor self-certification.

## Why this matters

The archive now says much more than it used to about the multilevel stack. It says **which scope usually fits which class of problem** (`579`, `602`, `603`). It says **how to diagnose misfit and choose repairs in sequence** (`604`, `605`). It says **when a co-operative layer has become real government and needs constitutional upgrade** (`606`-`627`). And the most recent notes now say **how scope claims should be tested, presumed, moved, handed back, reopened, written down, remembered, and publicly diffed** (`628`-`635`).

What the archive still lacked was the simplest pre-adoption discipline in the chain: **who publicly says what kind of stack change a proposal actually is?**

That gap matters because materially stack-altering proposals rarely arrive with honest labels attached. A ministry says a bill is “implementation detail” when it quietly narrows municipal discretion. A budget office calls a condition “temporary program management” when it really creates central command over a local lane. A platform team says a new workflow is only technical standardization even though it moves operational control upward. A metropolitan compact is sold as co-ordination while it acquires taxing, planning, or service-setting powers that amount to government. An emergency instrument describes itself as continuation of support long after it has become de facto hierarchy.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine for **scope opinions**. A multilevel constitution is not kept honest only by having a good theory of scope, a good register, and good post hoc case memory. It also needs one explicit moment where a named public lane says: **this proposal does or does not change the stack, this is the legal depth of that change, these are the missing preconditions if any, and this is the exact status under which adoption may proceed**.

Without that discipline, the stack can still drift through a familiar trick. Everyone agrees some packet or analysis should exist, but the sponsoring institution remains the only body allowed to characterize what its own proposal is doing. That is not review. It is **constitutional self-certification**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Require one scope opinion whenever a proposal could materially alter the live scope register

A scope opinion should be required whenever a proposal, taken seriously rather than rhetorically, could materially alter:

- the legal home of a function or lane,
- the real operational holder of power,
- the territorial footprint of responsibility,
- the finance path that determines real discretion,
- the override or supervision lane,
- the review or redress route,
- or the temporary-versus-permanent status of an exception.

This doctrine docks into `633` and `635`. The canonical scope register tells the polity what the live stack is. The scope-diff packet shows the before-and-after claim. The scope opinion is the public classification step that says whether the claimed change is being described honestly and routed through the correct lane.

### 2. Keep the opinion separate from the proposal sponsor’s self-description

The sponsoring ministry, mayor, authority, or coalition may draft the proposal and may draft the packet. It should not be the only institution entitled to say what kind of constitutional move the proposal represents.

The archive should therefore prefer a review lane that is *institutionally distinct* from the sponsor’s immediate political interest. Depending on the legal system, that could be:

- a parliamentary committee,
- a constitutional or legal-affairs office,
- an intergovernmental consultation committee with publication duties,
- a regulatory-scrutiny or better-regulation body,
- a council of state or equivalent advisory body,
- or another named public review lane with visible mandate and publication discipline.

The archive does **not** require one universal institutional design. It requires one universal honesty rule: materially stack-changing proposals should not proceed on sponsor characterization alone.

### 3. Give the opinion a small set of standard dispositions

A scope opinion should not dissolve into open-ended prose. It should return one of a short set of standardized dispositions, such as:

- **no material scope effect** — the proposal does not materially change the live stack;
- **within existing scope** — the proposal changes policy content inside an already lawful allocation;
- **ordinary rescoping** — the proposal materially changes the stack but can lawfully proceed through the named ordinary legislative lane;
- **protected-self-government impact** — the proposal affects protected local or regional self-government and requires consultation, stronger justification, or legal modification before proceeding;
- **consent or constitutional lane required** — the proposal reaches protected constituent bargains, boundary status, entrenched competences, or equivalent matters and cannot honestly proceed by ordinary route alone;
- **temporary exception only** — the proposal is tolerable only under explicit emergency, pilot, distress, or handback conditions;
- **not adoption-ready** — the packet, legal basis, consultation, evidence, or handback design is too weak for honest adoption.

The point is not rigid taxonomy for its own sake. The point is to stop institutions from hiding a major constitutional move inside the vague label “reform.”

### 4. Make the opinion answer four questions directly

A useful scope opinion should answer, in compact form, four direct questions:

1. **Is the proposal really stack-altering or not?**
2. **If it is, what is the lawful lane and depth of change?**
3. **What preconditions are missing, incomplete, or contested?**
4. **What exact register, jurisprudence, review-clock, or handback updates follow if it proceeds?**

If the opinion cannot answer these in public, then the polity is still relying on reconstruction from speeches, committee exchanges, or later litigation.

### 5. Link the opinion to the diff packet, the canonical register, and the jurisprudence digest

A scope opinion should never float free as a memo in another silo. It should dock visibly into the archive’s recent operating objects:

- it should attach to the **scope-diff packet** (`635`),
- it should say whether the **canonical scope register** (`633`) changes and exactly where,
- and it should note whether the **scope-jurisprudence digest** (`634`) is being implemented, distinguished, narrowed, tested, or ignored.

This gives the polity one public chain: **proposal → packet → opinion → adoption or return → register update → later jurisprudence or review if challenged**.

Without that chain, the archive would still have good doctrines but a weak operating memory.

### 6. Publish both concurrence and disagreement, not only a single clearance stamp

Some systems will use a committee, some an advisory council, some an intergovernmental body, and some a legal office. In any of those forms, the archive should prefer publication rules that preserve not only the final disposition but also:

- the main reasons,
- the contested classification issues,
- whether affected authorities disagreed,
- whether the sponsor revised the proposal after opinion,
- and whether the opinion was unanimous, qualified, or divided.

The aim is not theatrical dissent. It is to prevent the false appearance that all scope questions were easy and uncontested when they were actually live constitutional arguments.

### 7. Make negative or conditional opinions have real procedural consequences

A scope opinion should not be decorative. If it finds that the wrong lane is being used, that consultation is missing, that self-government is being impaired without lawful route, or that a temporary exception lacks a handback design, then something consequential should follow.

Depending on the system, that may mean:

- mandatory revision before second reading,
- formal statement of override reasons by the executive or legislature,
- referral to a stronger constitutional lane,
- suspension of adoption until consultation is completed,
- automatic sunset if the proposal is nevertheless pushed through as an emergency,
- or fast-track standing for judicial challenge.

The archive does not require every opinion to be veto-like. It does require that a failed opinion change the process in a visible way.

### 8. Treat software, grants, guidance, and budget devices as opinion-triggering instruments when they really rescope power

A polity should not reserve scope opinions only for constitution bills and obvious transfer statutes. Many of the most important stack changes happen through softer instruments:

- conditional grants,
- budget riders,
- platform defaults,
- mandatory templates,
- performance frameworks,
- procurement standards,
- and emergency continuation orders.

The archive should therefore require supplementary or full scope opinions whenever these devices materially change who actually decides, who can refuse, who bears risk, or who gets blamed.

Otherwise the review lane will become a constitutional formality that polices only the obvious texts while real government moves elsewhere.

### 9. Distinguish scope opinion from routine tutelage over ordinary local acts

This doctrine should not become a universal pre-clearance machine. The archive already rejects routine a priori tutelage over ordinary local by-laws and decisions (`592`). Scope opinions are for **material questions about the stack itself**, not for making every municipal or regional act wait for central permission.

That distinction matters. A healthy multilevel constitution should allow ordinary local self-government to operate inside its lawful sphere without habitual upper-tier countersignature. Scope opinions exist to classify **changes to the sphere**, not to supervise every act within it.

### 10. Keep opinions short, searchable, and comparable across time

A good scope-opinion system should be concise enough to use repeatedly and structured enough to compare. A minimal opinion packet should usually include:

- proposal identifier,
- sponsor,
- affected function and lane,
- linked diff packet,
- disposition,
- legal route,
- consultation and consent status,
- jurisprudence status,
- temporary or permanent status,
- required next actions,
- and publication date.

If opinions become long bespoke essays, their comparability dies. If they become one-word stamps, their honesty dies.

## Guardrails

- Do not let the sponsoring body be the only institution that classifies whether its proposal changes the stack.
- Do not treat silence, non-response, or elapsed time as affirmative scope clearance.
- Do not reserve serious review only for formal transfer statutes while grants, budgets, and software do the real rescoping.
- Do not turn scope opinions into routine tutelage over ordinary local government acts.
- Do not publish only the clearance outcome while hiding the reasons, disagreements, or missing preconditions.
- Do not allow a negative or conditional opinion to leave no procedural trace.

## Failure modes

- **stack change by self-certification**: the sponsor labels its own proposal non-constitutional and no one else gets a formal classification role.
- **clearance theater**: an opinion exists, but it is formulaic, unpublished, or unable to affect timing, route, or revision.
- **soft-instrument escape**: the review lane watches bills while grants, software, and budget devices do the real rescoping.
- **false ordinary-law treatment**: a proposal that touches protected self-government or constituent bargains is routed as routine administrative detail.
- **silent disagreement**: affected authorities object, but the public record only preserves the sponsor’s final summary.
- **tutelage creep**: a doctrine designed for stack-level honesty is turned into habitual prior approval for ordinary local acts.

## Practical tests

A polity is using scope opinions honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is there a clear trigger for when a proposal needs a scope opinion?
2. Is the review lane institutionally distinct from the sponsoring body?
3. Does the opinion use a small standardized set of dispositions rather than vague prose alone?
4. Does it say whether the proposal is stack-altering, which legal lane applies, and what preconditions remain?
5. Is the opinion linked to the canonical scope register, the scope-diff packet, and relevant jurisprudence entries?
6. Can the opinion be triggered by grants, budgets, platforms, and emergency continuation instruments as well as by statutes?
7. Do negative or conditional opinions change the process in a visible way?
8. Are reasons, disagreements, and revisions publicly visible?
9. Is the doctrine bounded so that it does not become routine tutelage over ordinary local self-government?

## Compression rule for the archive

If the only institution allowed to say whether a proposal changes the multilevel stack is the institution proposing the change, then the polity does not yet govern scope honestly. It governs by **self-certifying rescoping with later argument about what happened**.
