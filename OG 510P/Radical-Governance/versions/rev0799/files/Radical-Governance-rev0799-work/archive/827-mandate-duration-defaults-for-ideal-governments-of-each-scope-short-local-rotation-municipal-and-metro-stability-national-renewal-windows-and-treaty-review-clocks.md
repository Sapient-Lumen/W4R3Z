# 827 — Mandate-duration defaults for ideal governments of each scope: short local rotation, municipal and metro stability, national renewal windows, and treaty review clocks

## One-line thesis

The archive should answer the question **how long should governing mandates last at each scope?** with scale-matched defaults rather than one calendar idol: short and recallable mandates for micro-local stewardship, medium terms for municipalities and metropolitan governments that need enough runway to budget and deliver, regional and national renewal windows that are usually long enough to govern but short enough to correct, and plurilateral or global bodies that live on fixed review and reauthorization clocks rather than ambient permanence.

## Why this matters

The archive already has a stronger answer to **what each scope is for** (`790`), **which regime form usually fits it** (`825`), **from where authority should draw its mandate** (`826`), **how governments are formed and removed** (`752`), **how vacancies and acting office should be handled** (`761`), and **how the stack should be maintained over time** (`793`). What it still lacked was one compact rule for the missing time question:

**once a polity knows what this tier is, who authorizes it, and how it enters and exits office, how long should that mandate last before it must return for renewal?**

That missing layer matters because constitutional calendars quietly decide political character. Terms that are too short can turn real government into permanent campaigning, annual bargaining panic, or timid avoidance of difficult capital and housing decisions. Terms that are too long can thicken into local bossism, insulated presidential drift, or treaty secretariats that outlive the clarity of their mandate. Copying one term length downward and upward across the whole stack is therefore as misleading as copying one regime form across every level. A neighborhood steward, a city council, a metropolitan transport authority, a regional cabinet, a national parliament, and a treaty secretariat do not need the same democratic clock.

The archive should therefore add one doctrine of **mandate-duration fit**. Duration should track the intimacy of the office, the reversibility of its mistakes, the length of the investment and coalition horizon it must carry, and the cost of governing under constant electoral interruption.

## Scope table

| Scope | Default duration instinct | Why this fit is usually best | What it must not become |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Micro-local** | short terms, rotation, or 1–2 year stewardship windows with recall | keeps tiny authority intimate, reversible, and low-drama | entrenched neighborhood mini-sovereignty |
| **Municipal** | medium terms, usually around one ordinary budget-and-delivery cycle | gives councils and administrations enough time to budget, staff, contract, and be judged on visible results | permanent campaign city hall |
| **Metropolitan** | medium-to-full terms, usually long enough for joint capital and land-use sequencing | lets metro bodies make transport, housing, resilience, and utility decisions that outrun one annual cycle | summit churn with no implementation runway |
| **Regional / provincial / state** | full governing windows, often 4 years and sometimes 5 where coalition and federal bargaining require it | supports platform governance, coalition repair, and major territorial planning without over-insulating the tier | decorative long-term incumbency |
| **National** | legislative renewal usually within 4–5 years; longer requires special caution | balances governability with democratic correction and limits constitutional amnesia | overlong apex insulation or chronic snap-election leverage |
| **Continental / plurilateral / global** | fixed office terms plus explicit review conferences, reauthorization clocks, or treaty-cycle checks | matches delegated authority to periodic political reinspection rather than pretending mandate lasts forever | ambient permanence by procedural neglect |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from action horizon and correction cost, not from one sacred number

The archive should not ask first whether a term is “short” or “long” in the abstract. It should ask:

- how quickly this tier’s mistakes can be corrected;
- how much continuity its work requires;
- how much coalition-building or capital sequencing it must sustain;
- and how dangerous it would be if incumbents could sit without meaningful renewal.

A good constitutional calendar is not a beauty preference. It is a fit rule between the office and the work.

### 2. Keep micro-local mandates short, recallable, and rotation-friendly

At block, village, ward, neighborhood, or similarly intimate scale, the archive should prefer short mandate windows.

These bodies usually exist for voice, upkeep, witnessing, tiny discretionary priorities, and rapid escalation. Their comparative advantage is closeness, not long insulated authority. That means terms should usually be brief, rotation should remain normal where practical, and recall or simple replacement should stay lawful and legible.

The archive should therefore distrust long, prestige-seeking local terms for very small bodies. If a steward cell needs five or six years of insulation before residents can reconsider it, the body is already trying to govern above its honest weight.

### 3. Give municipalities enough runway to budget, staff, and deliver before judgment

Municipal government is different. A city or town must pass budgets, oversee professional administration, let contracts mature, manage capital maintenance, and show visible improvement in streets, sanitation, licensing, parks, safety coordination, and other ordinary services.

The archive should therefore usually prefer municipal terms that are not ultra-short. A municipality governed on a constant election rhythm struggles to separate genuine evaluation from campaign fog. Residents should be able to judge a council or mayoral team on enacted budgets, service continuity, and real delivery rather than on endless launch announcements.

This does not require one universal municipal number. It does require a default presumption against annualized churn and against constitutional calendars so short that administration cannot stabilize before the next contest begins.

### 4. Metropolitan mandates need enough time for joint capital and land-use discipline

Metropolitan government often handles decisions whose consequences arrive slowly but bind a whole urban public: transit trunks, housing obligations, flood systems, wastewater, freight corridors, utility sequencing, resilience works, and strategic land use.

The archive should therefore usually give metropolitan bodies at least the same or slightly more stability than municipalities, not less. A metro body whose mandate expires before major projects, funding packages, or land-use bargains can even be tested will govern through symbolism, emergency extensions, or informal mayoral summits instead of through public responsibility.

Metro legitimacy should be periodic and visible, but it should not be so frantic that every cross-boundary project becomes hostage to immediate campaign turnover.

### 5. Regional and national legislatures should usually renew within one ordinary governing window

For regional and national tiers, the archive should generally treat **four years as the central default intuition** and **five years as the outer ordinary democratic band** for legislative renewal, unless a stronger positive case exists.

That band is not magic. It is a discipline. It is usually long enough for coalition formation, budgeting, platform management, and legislative learning, while still short enough to force democratic correction before incumbency hardens into constitutional habit. A legislature that turns over much faster may never escape campaign mode. One that runs much longer begins to ask citizens for patience beyond what ordinary representative government should demand.

Where directly elected presidents or governors exist, the archive should be even more cautious about long terms if the office carries concentrated power. Longer executive insulation can be especially dangerous when paired with weak removal routes, strong decree powers, or fragmented legislatures.

### 6. Distinguish term length from term limits

The archive should also refuse one recurring confusion: **how long one term lasts** is not the same question as **how many times a person may hold the office**.

For assemblies, councillors, and many locally elected representatives, the archive should usually be cautious about hard term-limit enthusiasm. Elections, party competition, recall where lawful, incompatibility rules, transparency, pay discipline, and lawful removal often do more honest democratic work than a universal ban on experienced re-election.

The stronger case for strict term-limit thinking usually arises with highly personalized, one-person executive offices that concentrate symbolic and operational power. The archive should therefore be much readier to question repeated executive tenure than to assume that every representative body needs the same anti-incumbency rule.

### 7. Do not let dissolution or caretaker periods silently rewrite the term

A constitutional term is not only its scheduled endpoint. It is also the law governing early dissolution, interregnum, and caretaker behavior.

The archive should therefore keep duration design tied to `752` and `761`:

- dissolution should remain bounded rather than a standing intimidation device;
- caretaker periods should preserve continuity without becoming stealth incumbency;
- acting office should be temporary, visible, and narrowing rather than a quiet extension lane;
- and emergency exceptions should not become routine methods for stretching or suspending ordinary renewal.

A polity with nominally short terms can still drift into entrenchment if dissolution, emergency, or acting arrangements quietly let the same actors govern without fresh authorization.

### 8. Above the state, prefer review clocks and reauthorization cycles over ambient permanence

Plurilateral and global bodies often do not renew legitimacy mainly through one simple mass election. Their authority is mixed, delegated, and treaty-shaped. The archive should therefore ask not only **who appointed the body**, but **what predictable cycle forces political reinspection of its mandate**.

That usually means some combination of:

- fixed terms for secretariat leadership or commissioners;
- conference-of-parties, charter-review, or treaty-review meetings on known intervals;
- periodic reporting and peer-review cycles;
- explicit reappointment, reauthorization, amendment, or withdrawal windows;
- and publication duties showing when the next meaningful review is due.

The archive should distrust upper-tier institutions that cannot say when their powers, leadership, or operating settlement next face real renewal pressure. Delegated authority without review clocks easily becomes permanence by inattention.

## Guardrails

- Do not copy one term length across every scale.
- Do not give micro-local offices long insulated mandates merely to make them feel important.
- Do not force municipalities and metros into such short cycles that capital discipline and administrative accountability collapse into campaign theater.
- Do not let national legislatures or directly elected apex offices drift beyond ordinary democratic renewal bands without a named positive case.
- Do not confuse term limits with term length.
- Do not let treaty or global bodies operate on ambient permanence with no visible review clock.

## Failure modes

- **calendar monoculture** — one constitutional number is copied across the whole stack whether or not the work fits.
- **permanent campaign government** — terms are so short that budgeting, staffing, and delivery never escape election mode.
- **slow-entrenchment insulation** — offices are held long enough that ordinary renewal begins to feel exceptional.
- **executive overhang** — a powerful directly elected office receives a longer or thicker mandate than the rest of the system can safely absorb.
- **interregnum extensionism** — dissolution, acting status, or emergency periods quietly lengthen real tenure beyond the nominal term.
- **treaty permanence fog** — upper-tier bodies continue indefinitely because nobody can identify the next review, reauthorization, or amendment window.

## Practical tests

A mandate-duration design is probably close to right when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is the term long enough for this tier to complete one honest governing cycle rather than only a publicity cycle?
2. Is it short enough that ordinary democratic correction arrives before incumbency hardens into presumed ownership?
3. Do removal, dissolution, acting-office, and caretaker rules preserve rather than quietly rewrite the intended duration?
4. Are one-person executives treated with greater caution than plural representative bodies where concentrated power is at stake?
5. For delegated plurilateral or global bodies, can the constitution name the next real review, reauthorization, or renewal clock?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a proposed constitution can say **who authorizes a government** but cannot also say **how long that authorization lasts, why that duration fits the scope, and what lawful clock forces fresh renewal instead of drift**, then it still has **mandate theory without a mandate time constitution**.
