# 702 — Unbundling mixed-function claims before scope choice: lane split first, packet second, rivalry third, and no one-tier answer to a composite field

## One-line thesis

Before the archive decides which scope should ideally govern a field, it should first test whether the field is really a **composite bundle of different lanes with different scale logics**; if so, it should split the field into lane-sized claims, build packets for those lanes, and run rivalry only on the pieces that remain genuinely contested, rather than forcing one total jurisdictional answer onto a mixed-function question.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a tighter ladder for scope design. `699` gives the reusable scope spine. `700` gives the minimum design packet. `701` gives an adjudication matrix for competing packets. But one compact discipline was still missing: **how to tell whether the thing being compared is wrongly bundled in the first place**.

That gap matters because many disputes about the “ideal government” for a function are not really disputes about one function. They are disputes about a **field bundle** that contains several distinct lanes with different constitutional jobs. A polity asks whether housing should be local, metropolitan, regional, or national. But the bundle usually includes land-use control, infrastructure coordination, finance, equalization, subsidy rules, tenant protection, homelessness response, inspection, data, and appeals. Transport is treated as one field, even though street design, corridor planning, fare policy, network integration, safety regulation, capital finance, and incident command often point to different scales. Health is narrated as one jurisdictional question while primary care access, disease surveillance, hospital networks, insurance pooling, professional licensing, pharmaceutical purchasing, and public-health emergency powers plainly do not all travel together.

When those bundles stay fused, the archive is pushed toward false choices.

- One camp gives a total local answer because some lanes are place-sensitive.
- Another gives a total national answer because some lanes concern equal rights or solidarity.
- A third chooses the metropolitan or regional answer because network spillovers are real.

Each camp sees part of the field clearly and the whole field badly.

The archive therefore needs one compact rule: **decompose first**. Scope adjudication should not begin until the archive knows whether it is looking at a real unit of government choice or at a bag of differently-scaled tasks held together by common vocabulary.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat many “fields” as candidate bundles, not as self-validating units

The archive should begin from suspicion toward large sector labels. “Housing”, “transport”, “education”, “public health”, “water”, “economic development”, and “climate” may be useful political headings, but they are often too coarse for constitutional assignment.

A field should therefore earn treatment as one scope-choice object. Until then, the archive should assume it may be a **candidate bundle** made of several lanes.

### 2. Ask the bundle test before building a packet

Before creating a packet under `700`, the archive should ask whether the candidate field contains materially different answers to any of the following:

- intimacy or place-sensitivity,
- spillover geography,
- solidarity or risk-pooling duty,
- coercive-risk profile,
- infrastructure topology,
- rights-floor uniformity,
- professional-density requirement,
- capital-finance scale,
- or treaty/cross-border exposure.

If the answers differ sharply inside the field, the field is probably composite and should be split before packet design proceeds.

### 3. Split by constitutional lane, not by bureaucratic habit

The archive should not decompose a field merely by copying ministry boxes. It should split by **constitutional lane**: the kind of public job being done.

Typical lanes include:

- rights floor or common legal effect,
- strategic planning,
- capital finance and risk-pooling,
- ordinary delivery or place management,
- technical inspection or specialist review,
- data, records, and official indicators,
- complaints, appeals, and remedy,
- and emergency command or fallback intervention.

This keeps decomposition anchored in governing work rather than org-chart folklore.

### 4. Stop splitting when a lane has one dominant scale logic

The point of decomposition is not infinite atomization. It is to reach the level where a lane has one dominant constitutional logic.

A good split stops when a lane can be described in one sentence and that sentence points mainly to one scope packet or one small rivalry set. If further splitting no longer changes the likely scope choice, the archive should stop.

### 5. Build lane-sized packets, not one omnibus packet

Once a bundle has been split, the archive should build a compact packet for each consequential lane rather than one omnibus packet for the whole field.

That packet can stay short because `700` already gives the minimum fields: archetype, vocation, boundary logic, minimum complete kit, fiscal and capability proof, exception ladder, and review or upgrade triggers.

This allows the archive to say, for example:

- tenant-protection floors fit the national guarantor lane,
- homelessness intake and ordinary place response fit the municipal lane,
- housing-market observatories and labour-market planning fit the metropolitan or regional lane,
- and subsidy equalization may fit the national or provincial solidarity lane.

That is usually more truthful than asking whether “housing” as such is local or national.

### 6. Run rivalry only where a real rivalry remains

After decomposition, some lanes will no longer be genuinely contested. Their scale logic will be obvious enough that no meaningful packet rivalry remains. Other lanes will still support two or more plausible packets.

Only those remaining lanes should go into the `701` adjudication matrix.

This reduces argument inflation. The archive should not stage a grand rivalry where decomposition has already settled most of the field.

### 7. Prefer typed stacks over single-tier vanity answers

A composite field will often yield a **typed stack** rather than a single owner. That is not a failure of design. It is often the honest result.

The archive should therefore prefer a lane-typed stack when:

- place-sensitive delivery and common rights floors both matter,
- network spillovers are real but not total,
- specialist review needs pooled capacity,
- capital finance exceeds local bases,
- or emergency command must temporarily scale upward without erasing ordinary local competence.

A single-tier answer should win only when the field is genuinely unitary or when one level can honestly carry all consequential lanes without hiding costs elsewhere.

### 8. Mark residual glue functions openly

Some bundle-level work remains after lane-splitting: general policy framing, interlane sequencing, monitoring of field outcomes, and visible public communication about who does what.

The archive should mark these as **glue functions** rather than pretending they prove one level owns the whole field. Glue work may live at one scale while constituent lanes remain distributed.

### 9. Use decomposition to expose false localism and false centralism alike

Bundle-splitting is a discipline against both directions of error.

It exposes **false localism** when a localist claim quietly relies on higher-tier equalization, regulation, or pooled specialist capacity it never names. It exposes **false centralism** when a centralist claim quietly depends on municipal intake, local discretion, or metro-scale system knowledge it narrates as mere implementation residue.

Decomposition forces each side to name the borrowed lanes it was previously hiding.

### 10. Record the decomposition map as part of the archive object

If a later note decomposes a field, it should leave behind one compact map that says:

- the original bundle name,
- the lanes identified,
- the lane descriptions,
- whether each lane is settled or contested,
- the candidate packets where rivalry remains,
- and the resulting stack or bracketed rivalries.

That map should travel with the note so later authors do not rebundle the field by accident.

## Bundle-test prompts

A field probably needs decomposition when several of the following are true at once:

1. the people-facing front door is local but the money model is not;
2. the delivery geography is municipal or neighbourhood-scale but the risk pool or rights floor is national or provincial;
3. the everyday service map differs from the infrastructure map;
4. the planning horizon differs sharply from the emergency-command horizon;
5. specialist review requires pooled capacity that ordinary intake does not;
6. common data or inspection standards are needed across many local operators;
7. one part of the field carries coercive or constitutional stakes that the rest does not;
8. rival proposals keep talking past one another because they are each defending different subfunctions.

## Compact decomposition object

| Field | Lane | Typical scale logic | Packet status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Housing** | tenant-protection floor | guarantor / rights-floor | usually settled at national or equivalent floor-setting level |
| **Housing** | homelessness intake and place response | ordinary place | usually municipal packet |
| **Housing** | commuter-belt land and housing-supply co-ordination | shared urban system | often metro or regional rivalry |
| **Housing** | subsidy equalization and macro-fiscal support | solidarity / risk-pooling | usually national or provincial packet |
| **Transport** | local street design and curb management | ordinary place | usually municipal packet |
| **Transport** | network integration, fares, and corridor planning | shared system | often metropolitan packet |
| **Transport** | rail capital finance and national safety standards | territorial platform or guarantor lane | often regional or national packet |

The archive should treat this compact object as the normal length unless the field is unusually hard.

## Guardrails

- Do not force decomposition where a field is already genuinely unitary.
- Do not let ministry boundaries decide the lane split.
- Do not keep splitting once the dominant scale logic is already clear.
- Do not treat glue functions as proof that one level owns every constituent lane.
- Do not run packet rivalry on a bundle that has not yet passed the bundle test.
- Do not narrate a typed stack as conceptual weakness when it is simply the honest answer.

## Failure modes

- **one-tier answer to a composite field** — the archive chooses one winner for a field whose lanes plainly belong at different scales.
- **bureaucratic decomposition** — the field is split by inherited departments rather than by constitutional job.
- **infinite slicing** — decomposition continues past the point of changing the scope logic, creating analytic clutter instead of clarity.
- **glue inflation** — coordination or communication work is mistaken for ownership of the whole field.
- **hidden borrowed lanes** — a packet wins rhetorically because the supportive lanes it depends on were never named.
- **rebundling by habit** — later notes quietly collapse the lane map back into the old umbrella sector label.

## Practical tests

A note is using decomposition honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Has it tested whether the supposed field is a real unit of scope choice or a composite bundle?
2. Are the lanes split by constitutional job rather than by administrative convenience?
3. Has splitting stopped once each lane has a dominant scale logic?
4. Are packets built lane by lane rather than only for the omnibus field label?
5. Is the `701` adjudication matrix used only for the lanes where rivalry genuinely remains?
6. Can an outsider see the resulting typed stack, not just the original sector headline?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a later note argues about **which scope should govern a field** but has not first asked **whether the field is a composite bundle whose lanes point to different scale logics**, then it still has **scope adjudication without prior decomposition**, and its winner is likely answering the wrong question.
