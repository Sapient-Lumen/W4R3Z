# 755 — Constitutional referendum necessity tests for ideal governments: amendment classes, double majorities, question clarity, balanced information, and no plebiscitary shortcuts

## One-line thesis

A constitutional referendum should not be treated as a universal cleansing ritual; ideal governments should use it when a constitution genuinely requires **direct constituent ratification** or when a change is foundational enough to justify a people-level decision, and even then only with clear lawful questions, balanced information, explicit downstream effects, and double-legitimacy rules where territorial bargains are at stake.

## Why this matters

The archive already has notes on **constitutional change by scope** (`498`), **political competition** (`500`), **electoral systems** (`566`), **local participation rights** (`597`), **rescoping authority** (`630`), the **ideal-government stack** (`725`, `749`), **government formation** (`752`), and **upper-chamber necessity** (`753`).

What it still lacked was one compact doctrine for a narrower but repeatedly recurring constitutional question:

**when should foundational questions go to referendum at all, and what kind of referendum is honest once the answer is yes?**

That gap matters because referendums are often invoked as a moral shortcut. Reformers appeal to “letting the people decide” even when the question is unclear, the legal effect is vague, the issue is ordinary legislation rather than constitutional refoundation, or the vote is being used to bypass representative institutions, minority protections, territorial bargains, or implementation discipline.

Ideal-government design therefore needs a doctrine of **constitutional referendum necessity**. A referendum should survive because it performs a real ratification or constituent-consent function, not because plebiscitary drama feels more democratic than the slower work of lawful constitutional change.

## Necessity table

| Necessity test | What counts as proof | Honest referendum design consequence | Default answer if proof is weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Founding or refounding moment** | adoption of a new constitution or similarly foundational settlement | ratification referendum with full draft, campaign rules, and explicit entry-into-force path | legislative or constituent-assembly enactment alone |
| **Entrenched constitutional amendment** | the constitution itself requires popular approval for the class of amendment proposed | referendum after the parliamentary or constituent threshold is met | heightened parliamentary procedure without plebiscitary overlay |
| **Territorial or shared-rule bargain** | the proposal alters constituent powers, boundaries, autonomy, or federal balance | double-majority or parallel territorial consent architecture | ordinary nationwide majority vote alone |
| **Local or regional status settlement** | incorporation, merger, split, or autonomy change legally requires direct affected-population consent | bounded subnational referendum tied to a lawful transition plan | consultative exercise with no pretence of self-executing constitutional effect |
| **Ordinary policy frustration** | sponsors merely want to bypass parliament, courts, or coalition bargaining | no constitutional referendum | ordinary legislative, budgetary, or review routes |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish amendment classes before deciding on a referendum

The first question is not whether public voting sounds attractive. It is what kind of constitutional change is actually being proposed.

A technical adjustment, institutional repair, federal rebalance, total rewrite, autonomy settlement, or founding moment may each justify different ratification pathways. The archive should therefore sort amendments by **depth and kind** before assuming that one referendum rule fits them all.

### 2. Reserve referendums for genuinely constituent or specially entrenched decisions

A referendum makes its strongest sense when the change is constituent in character: adoption of a new constitution, approval of a major re-founding settlement, or ratification of an amendment class that the constitution itself entrenches behind direct popular approval.

The archive should resist the idea that every significant disagreement deserves a referendum. Some questions are constitutional; many are simply political.

### 3. Where territorial bargains are at stake, add double legitimacy rather than only national aggregation

If a proposal changes the powers, borders, or constitutional standing of constituent units, one aggregate majority of the whole country is often not enough.

Ideal-government design should then consider **double-majority** or parallel-consent rules: popular approval overall plus approval by affected units, by a majority of units, by a territorial chamber, or through another form of shared-rule consent that matches the bargain being altered.

### 4. Do not use referendums to bypass ordinary parliamentary or governmental responsibility

A constitutional referendum is not a clean substitute for ordinary legislating, coalition-building, confidence-testing, budgeting, or executive accountability.

The archive should therefore resist referendum pathways that are really attempts to avoid parliament, neutralize courts, launder unlawful executive ambitions, or collapse policy disappointment into one polarizing yes-or-no event.

### 5. Demand question clarity, unity, and lawful subject matter before the vote exists

Every referendum worthy of the archive should say clearly:

- what legal text or principle is being voted on,
- whether the question is specifically worded or generally worded,
- what constitutional level it affects,
- whether the parts of the question genuinely belong together,
- and whether the subject matter is even lawful for referendum treatment.

The electorate should not be asked to ratify a bundle whose pieces have no intrinsic connection, or to vote on a question whose constitutional effect cannot be stated plainly in advance.

### 6. Require independent pre-scrutiny and balanced public information

A referendum should not go forward on the sponsor’s wording alone.

There should be a named body or lane that can review clarity, legality, campaign fairness, and the availability of balanced official information. Voters should receive enough neutral explanation to understand both the proposed change and the principal case against it.

### 7. State the legal effect and downstream procedure before voting day

The archive should distinguish among:

- **binding votes on a final text**;
- **binding votes on a principle that still requires drafting**;
- **consultative votes** whose political but not legal force must be explained;
- and **locally bounded consent votes** tied to explicit transition plans.

A referendum is constitutionally unsafe if voters cannot tell whether “yes” enacts a text, instructs a drafting phase, authorizes negotiations, or merely signals a preference.

### 8. Be skeptical of turnout quorums and casual supermajority overlays

Turnout quorums and approval thresholds often reward abstention tactics, minority blockage, or post-vote legitimacy fights.

The archive should therefore be cautious about routine quorum design. If special thresholds are used, they should be reserved for truly fundamental constitutional significance and justified openly rather than added as a vague extra safeguard.

### 9. Pair referendum routes with deliberation rather than plebiscitary ambush

Even where a referendum is justified, the vote should usually come after a visible period of structured deliberation: parliamentary debate, constituent-assembly work, citizens’ assemblies, expert review, territorial negotiation, or public response ledgers.

The aim is not to smother popular decision under elite gatekeeping. It is to ensure the electorate is voting on something intelligible, lawful, and implementation-ready rather than on a slogan.

### 10. Keep subnational referendums bounded unless the constitution itself makes them constitutive

Local and regional referendums can play an honest role in incorporation, merger, split, charter, or autonomy questions. But absent a lawful constitutional route, they should not be narrated as self-executing secession, unilateral competence grab, or instant treaty change.

The archive should therefore treat subnational popular votes as powerful **inputs inside a legal constitutional lane**, not magic exits from it.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat referendums as a default answer to political frustration.
- Do not submit unclear, misleading, or compound questions to the electorate.
- Do not hide whether the vote is binding, consultative, text-based, or principle-based.
- Do not let one undifferentiated national majority rewrite a territorial bargain that constitutionally belongs to more than one demos.
- Do not use referendums to undercut rights floors, judicial protection, or ordinary government-responsibility mechanisms by rhetorical shortcut.

## Failure modes

- **plebiscitary shortcut**: a sponsor uses referendum rhetoric to bypass the lawful constitutional lane.
- **question fog**: voters are asked to approve a text or principle whose effect is obscure, compound, or internally mismatched.
- **false finality**: a consultative or principle vote is narrated as if it were self-executing law.
- **territorial overwriting**: one aggregate majority rewrites a federal or autonomy bargain without the consent architecture it requires.
- **abstention gaming**: quorum design rewards boycott and leaves the result politically unstable.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can explain **that it held or plans a constitutional referendum** but cannot also say **which necessity test the vote passes, what class of change is at issue, whether the result is binding, how territorial consent is handled, and how question clarity and balanced information were secured**, then it still has **plebiscitary theatre without a referendum constitution**.
