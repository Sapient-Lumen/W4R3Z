# 814 — Fiscal-chain templates for cross-boundary scope forms: dues and service payments, assigned revenues, equalization keys, capital lanes, and no heavy power by shadow treasury

## One-line thesis

Once the archive has said **which cross-boundary form is honest** and **what legitimacy chain belongs to it**, it should also be able to say **what fiscal chain belongs to that form**: thin connectors should live on modest dues and project-preparation money with no disguised distributive budget, shared-service backbones should run on principal-owned service payments and declared cost-sharing plus reserve rules, bounded functional authorities should use assigned revenues, user charges, or rule-bound transfers matched to their narrow charter with explicit capital discipline, and general-purpose territorial tiers should have a public budget constitution with own-source or rule-bound shared revenues, equalization, and visible borrowing limits rather than living by discretionary grants, annual pleas, or off-book vehicles.

## Why this matters

The archive can now do four things that matter greatly for ideal governments by scope.

`790` says what the default stack is. `810` says how to assemble threshold claims before the archive says that a metro, county-pooling, regional-platform, fiscal-floor, or legitimacy-thickness line is really crossed. `812` says what institutional form is honest once the need is already real. `813` says what legitimacy chain and decision gates belong to that form. `822` says who actually owns the strategic assets, carries the debt, books the reserves, and takes the handback once that form becomes durable enough to have a real balance sheet.

What the archive still lacked was one smaller but recurring judgment:

**once the form and legitimacy chain are chosen, how should the money actually move?**

That gap matters because many live governance systems do not fail first at the level of territorial map or chamber design. They fail at the level of fiscal honesty.

A metropolitan forum remains labelled “co-ordination” even though it is already allocating real burdens through side contributions and quiet ministry transfers. A shared-service body is called purely administrative even though the host municipality is effectively underwriting the whole arrangement and deciding upgrade priorities through treasury control. A narrow authority gets a serious charter, but then lives on unstable rescue grants and hidden guarantees that make its real sovereignty impossible to read. Or a territorial tier is given elections and statute, but still depends on discretionary programmes so completely that its budget politics remain elsewhere.

The result is a specific constitutional error: **the form and legitimacy chain may be nominally right, but the fiscal chain is mismatched to the gravity of the power being exercised.**

Older archive notes already contain pieces of the answer. `567`, `581`, `608`, `664`, `683`, and `714` all help. But the archive did not yet have one short bridge note that turns those scattered insights into a reusable template for the current ideal-governments-by-scope run.

Official materials strongly support such a bridge. The European Charter of Local Self-Government ties local responsibilities to adequate, commensurate, diversified, and partly own financial resources, and treats borrowing mainly as a capital tool rather than an operating crutch. OECD work on inter-municipal shared services shows that pooled provision works better when legal, governance, fiscal, reporting, and cost-sharing arrangements are specified rather than improvised. OECD work on effective public investment across levels of government stresses co-ordination at the relevant geographic scale and capacities matched to that scale. OECD fiscal-decentralisation and granular municipal-finance work shows why autonomy and dependence should be judged through typed revenue and expenditure structures rather than by rhetoric alone. World Bank metropolitan-governance and municipal-finance work likewise distinguishes between contributions, transfers, own-source revenues, and debt tools instead of treating every shared body as if one funding model were enough. The archive should compress those lessons into one rule: **do not let the fiscal chain lag behind the real power chain.**

## Pattern pack

### 1. Use this note after threshold, form, and legitimacy work, not instead of it

This note does not decide whether a cross-boundary threshold is crossed.

Use `810` for that.

This note does not decide whether the honest institutional answer is a connector, backbone, bounded authority, or full tier.

Use `812` for that.

This note does not decide who occupies the chamber, board, or ratification chain.

Use `813` for that.

This note applies only after the archive can already say what kind of form is honest and what legitimacy chain belongs to it, but still needs to say:

- what the ordinary operating base is,
- whether the body may use dues, service payments, assigned revenues, charges, shared taxes, or transfers,
- where equalization or burden-sharing sits,
- where contingency and capital lanes sit,
- and which money moves require a second key rather than executive habit.

### 2. Separate four fiscal chains before inventing hybrids

The archive should usually begin with four recognizable fiscal chains that pair with the four form choices in `812`.

| Form | Default fiscal chain | What may honestly fund it | What does **not** count as enough by itself |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Thin connector** | modest dues, subscriptions, project-preparation support, or narrow secretariat budget | meetings, shared data, strategy work, joint studies, voluntary pilots | shadow burden-sharing budget, hidden levy, or undeclared redistributive transfers |
| **Shared-service backbone** | principal-owned service payments, declared contributions, cost-sharing formula, reserve rule | pooled staff, procurement, IT, casework, specialist support, continuity capacity | host-city underwriting, vague “solidarity” side-payments, or tax-like powers by service contract |
| **Bounded functional authority** | assigned revenues, user charges, rule-bound transfers, and explicit capital lane matched to the narrow remit | network operations, asset stewardship, lifecycle maintenance, lawful debt for long-lived assets | annual rescue bargaining, opaque guarantees, or broad territorial redistribution hiding inside a single-purpose body |
| **General-purpose elected tier** | own-source or rule-bound shared revenues, equalization, public budget authority, visible capital discipline | multi-sector territorial budgeting, land-use tradeoffs, distributive allocations, major pooled investment | decorative chamber with money still controlled by discretionary programmes or off-book vehicles |

The archive should not start with bespoke financial compromise until one of these four fiscal chains has first been ruled out honestly.

### 3. Thin connectors should stay on small money and visibly non-sovereign finance

A thin connector is honest when its budget is plainly limited to:

- secretariat support,
- shared data and planning,
- convening,
- voluntary project preparation,
- small joint studies,
- or other clearly connective work.

The archive should resist three temptations here:

- letting project-preparation funds become a shadow capital allocator,
- letting ministry grants quietly choose winners and losers among member governments,
- or letting dues become a disguised burden-sharing system without the form or legitimacy needed to own that politics.

A thin connector therefore does **not** need a full territorial treasury. But it does need a hard ban on pretending that a small co-ordination budget can carry distributive or coercive work without constitutional upgrade.

### 4. Shared-service backbones should use principal-owned service finance, not hidden sovereignty transfer

A shared-service backbone exists because several governments keep the political title to a function while pooling administrative machinery, specialist staff, procurement, digital systems, case handling, or continuity capacity.

Its fiscal chain should therefore begin from a simple premise:

**the member governments remain the principals unless the archive has said otherwise.**

That means the backbone should normally have:

- a declared cost-sharing formula or service-pricing rule,
- a visible host-compensation rule if one member carries treasury or employer-of-record burdens,
- reserve and contingency rules for demand spikes, staff shortages, outages, or legal claims,
- explicit treatment of asset ownership and replacement,
- and a clear statement of which financial changes need member ratification rather than managerial discretion.

Ordinary operating finance may sit with the pooled executive. But the archive should require member-government ratification or pre-authorisation when the backbone would otherwise drift into substantive cross-subsidy, portfolio expansion, large liability exposure, or politically consequential prioritisation among places.

### 5. Bounded functional authorities need a named revenue base and bounded capital lane before they need general territorial finance

A bounded functional authority is the honest answer when one cross-boundary system needs continuous stewardship, asset management, system balancing, or operations.

Its fiscal chain should therefore be heavier than a backbone’s, but still recognisably tied to one system.

Typical honest ingredients include:

- assigned tax shares or rule-bound transfers,
- user charges or tariffs where the function fits them,
- explicit operating subsidy for public-service obligations that charges cannot cover,
- renewal and maintenance reserves,
- and capital borrowing or long-horizon finance bounded to the authority’s charter.

The archive should resist three failures here:

- **farebox fantasy**, where a system with public-service obligations is financed as though demand revenue alone should carry it;
- **rescue-budget governance**, where the authority survives mainly through episodic emergency bargaining;
- **debt shrubbery**, where guarantees, leases, or affiliated vehicles hide the authority’s real fiscal weight.

A bounded authority does **not** yet need the whole fiscal constitution of a general-purpose tier. But it does need a stable revenue base and explicit capital discipline proportionate to the burden it already carries.

### 6. General-purpose territorial tiers should normally have a public budget constitution, not a decorative budget surface

Once the archive has concluded that the honest answer is a general-purpose territorial tier — metropolitan, county-style, regional, or otherwise — it should usually stop searching for convenience finance.

At that point the fiscal title should normally sit in:

- own-source taxes or charges and/or rule-bound shared revenues,
- a visible equalization or burden-sharing rule where territorial disparities matter,
- a public budget and treasury process attached to the directly accountable political home,
- capital and debt authority bounded by law,
- and one outwardly intelligible budget spine.

Discretionary programmes, competitions, or ministry-by-ministry grants may still supplement this. But the archive should treat them as typed supplements, not the body’s everyday oxygen.

The key rule is simple: **once the politics is truly territorial, the money constitution of that territory should normally be visible inside the territory’s own accountable budget home.**

### 7. Distinguish four money lanes even when some of them are small or nil

Whatever the form, the archive should usually ask four separate questions:

- what funds ordinary operations,
- what corrects asymmetry or unfair burden,
- what absorbs shocks and contingencies,
- and what lawfully funds long-lived capital or liabilities.

In a thin connector, the equalization and capital lanes may genuinely be nil.

In a shared-service backbone, equalization may take the limited form of a cost-sharing correction rather than a full territorial solidarity formula.

In a bounded authority, the capital lane may be substantial while equalization remains narrow.

In a general-purpose tier, all four lanes are usually real and politically salient.

The archive should still name them explicitly. Silence is how operating gaps get disguised as capital, contingencies become permanent constitutions, and solidarity occurs through opaque side bargains.

### 8. Use second-key fiscal gates for high-consequence money moves below the full-tier threshold

`813` already says when a board or executive should not act alone.

This note gives the money version of that rule.

Even below the full-tier threshold, the archive should usually require a second fiscal key for decisions such as:

- compulsory levies or levy-like member contributions,
- major tariff restructuring with broad distributive effect,
- borrowing or guarantee exposure above a declared threshold,
- asset disposal,
- capital-programme enlargement,
- reserve depletion beyond ordinary operating flex,
- or charter expansion that changes the fiscal burden map.

A narrow authority or backbone can remain narrow only if its heaviest money decisions do not quietly become ordinary internal management.

### 9. Publish fiscal-upgrade triggers when lighter forms start carrying heavier politics

A fiscal chain is not permanent merely because it was once appropriate.

Later archive work should say what would force an upgrade, such as:

- a connector becoming the real allocator of burdens,
- a backbone accumulating persistent cross-subsidy fights,
- a bounded authority widening from one system into multi-sector territorial bargaining,
- or a general-purpose body still depending mainly on discretionary grants long after it claims broad democratic ownership.

When those triggers appear, the archive should stop saying only that the form has matured. It should also say that the **fiscal chain** has matured and now requires thicker own revenues, equalization, treasury authority, or capital discipline.

### 10. Name downshift tests as well

The archive should also guard against permanent fiscal over-thickening.

If a crisis authority has narrowed back to one technical system, if extraordinary subsidy has expired, if a bridge budget no longer needs a territorial treasury, or if shared administration has returned to straightforward service support, then a heavier fiscal chain may no longer be necessary.

Own-source revenue, equalization machinery, or debt authority should not survive merely because they once solved a harder problem.

### 11. Require one short fiscal-chain holding in applied verdicts

When `811` is used for a live case, the archive should normally be able to add one short holding such as:

- **honest current form is a shared-service backbone; member municipalities retain fiscal title and all cost-sharing changes above the reserve rule require member ratification**;
- **honest current form is a bounded transit authority; operating base is assigned revenue plus fare and rule-bound transfer, while borrowing, guarantee exposure, and major tariff redesign require a second fiscal key**;
- **honest current form is now a metropolitan tier; annual member pleas and discretionary grants are no longer adequate and the territory should own a visible budget constitution with equalization and capital discipline**.

That keeps applied judgments from stopping at the word “authority” or “metro” without saying how the money actually flows. Where the unresolved issue is not money but who actually runs the machinery, keeps the files, or carries continuity, the packet should also carry a short administrative-chain holding under `815`. Where the unresolved issue is not the revenue chain itself but who owns the durable assets, bears contingent obligations, and takes the handback on exit or dissolution, the packet should also carry a short asset-and-liability-chain holding under `822`.

## Guardrails

- Do not let project-preparation budgets or connector dues become a shadow distributive constitution.
- Do not let a host government become the undeclared treasury sovereign of a shared-service backbone.
- Do not let a bounded authority rely on annual rescue bargaining while pretending to have stable fiscal title.
- Do not let user charges or fares masquerade as a complete funding model where public-service obligations plainly exceed them.
- Do not let guarantees, leases, or affiliates hide the real fiscal burden of a public authority.
- Do not leave a general-purpose territorial tier dependent on programme competitions or ministry favor for its ordinary life.
- Do not widen fiscal power without widening the legitimacy chain and second-key design that should accompany it.
- Do not keep heavy fiscal machinery alive after the underlying territorial or functional burden has narrowed again.

## Failure modes

- **dues theater**: a connector claims small-budget innocence while really allocating burdens through side contributions and selective grants.
- **host treasury capture**: one member government underwrites the backbone and quietly controls priorities through cash-flow dominance.
- **farebox fantasy**: a public-service authority is judged as though user revenue alone should fund social and territorial obligations.
- **rescue-budget sovereignty**: an authority’s real constitution lives in repeated emergency bailouts rather than in its chartered revenue base.
- **off-book exposure**: guarantees, affiliates, or special vehicles carry public liabilities that the apparent sovereign denies.
- **grant-oxygen dependency**: a territorial tier exists in law but still breathes mainly through discretionary higher-tier programmes.
- **equalization denial**: structural territorial asymmetry is real, but the constitution refuses to name any burden-sharing rule.
- **fiscal overhang after downshift**: temporary heavy finance survives after the need for it has materially narrowed.


Commercial form should not be used to launder fiscal weight. Where the unresolved issue is whether procurement, concessions, franchises, or PPP-style structures are hiding constitutional thickness off-budget or through long-tail commitments, use `823` alongside this note rather than stretching the fiscal chain into a full contracting-boundary judgment.
