# 806 — Evidence registers and provenance chains for scope assessments: exhibit IDs, source typing, redaction ledgers, finding links, and no verdict by orphaned exhibit

## One-line thesis

The archive should pair its assessment-mission, sampling, tracer-route, finding-register, and close-out notes with one compact **evidence-register grammar**: **every serious scope review should keep a stable exhibit register that says what each evidence object is, where it came from, what version or date it belongs to, what was redacted or withheld, which finding it supports or contradicts, and how later reviewers can reuse it, so constitutional judgments are not built from orphaned exhibits, floating screenshots, or unverifiable quotations**.

## Why this matters

The archive can already say **what the ideal scope stack is** (`790`), **how to benchmark it** (`791`), **how to stress it** (`792`), **when to revisit it** (`793`), **how to move toward it** (`794`), **what proof must stay public** (`795`), **how persistent failure can justify intervention** (`796`), **who may challenge those judgments** (`797`), **which peer class counts before scoring begins** (`798`), **what must happen to temporary constitutional objects** (`799`), **how a serious mission should run** (`800`), **how its findings should be stated** (`801`), **how those findings become owned repair work** (`802`), **how claimed repair is later verified** (`803`), **how one real path through the stack can be walked** (`804`), and **how the sample entering the review should be chosen honestly** (`805`).

What the archive still lacked was one compact rule for a quieter but decisive question:

**how does the evidence itself stay stable enough to survive from mission, to finding, to response, to appeal, to close-out, without turning into citation fog?**

That gap matters because review quality often fails at the evidence-room layer rather than at the scoring layer. One mission keeps a folder of screenshots and cannot later say which policy version was in force at the time. Another quotes an interview note but loses the contradictory document it was meant to answer. Another publishes a proof pack whose public claims cannot be tied back to any stable internal exhibit spine. Another lets the host reissue a spreadsheet and silently replace the numbers on which a finding rested. Official practice points toward the missing discipline: GAO’s Yellow Book frames government audits around sufficient, appropriate evidence and audit documentation, and GAO’s data-reliability guidance says evidence quality questions do not disappear just because the object is computer-processed data. WHO’s JEE process requires a self-evaluation report plus referenced supplemental documentation, then an external team reviews the dossier, conducts site visits, and produces a report that the host reviews before publication. OECD’s DAC peer review methodology likewise uses an explicit analytical framework, self-assessment, documentation from the reviewed member, country and headquarters exchanges, and up-to-date statistical reporting. OECD’s Recommendation on Open Government, meanwhile, treats transparency, integrity, accountability, and stakeholder participation as a governance culture that reaches across levels of government rather than a publication afterthought. ## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate the evidence register from the proof pack and the finding register

`795` says **what minimum outward proof should be published**.

`801` says **how the resulting judgment should be stated**.

This note says **how the underlying evidence objects remain identifiable, typed, and reusable across that whole chain**.

The archive should not let those collapse into one another. A proof pack can stay public-safe and compact. A finding register can stay verdict-oriented. The evidence register is the quieter internal spine that lets both remain honest.

### 2. Give every relied-on evidence object a stable exhibit ID

Every serious mission should keep a compact exhibit register in which each relied-on object gets a stable ID, such as:

- `EX-001` for a statute or decree,
- `EX-014` for a transfer ledger extract,
- `EX-032` for a field observation note,
- `EX-057` for a tracer-route packet,
- or `EX-071` for a stakeholder submission.

The exact naming scheme matters less than the discipline. A finding should later be able to point to stable exhibits rather than to memory, inbox archaeology, or “the spreadsheet the ministry sent us that week.”

### 3. Type the source, not just the file

A serious register should record what kind of object each exhibit is, for example:

- normative text,
- administrative record,
- budget or transfer record,
- observed practice note,
- testimonial or interview note,
- computed metric,
- map or territorial artifact,
- complaint or case extract,
- system screenshot,
- or public claim made by the reviewed body.

That typing matters because different evidence objects carry different risks of drift, incompleteness, and overclaim.

### 4. Record provenance and lawful acquisition, not only possession

The archive should not treat “we have the file” as the end of the matter.

For each material exhibit, the register should usually preserve:

- who supplied or generated it,
- how it was obtained,
- when it was obtained,
- what legal or procedural basis permitted access,
- and whether the object is original, copied, exported, summarized, translated, or observer-generated.

This is the difference between a living evidence chain and a pile of attachments.

### 5. Freeze version, date, and temporal applicability

Governance evidence is often time-sensitive rather than timeless.

The register should say not only which document was used, but also:

- the version or issue date,
- the effective or operative period if relevant,
- whether later versions exist,
- and whether the exhibit was current at the time of the reviewed event, route, or finding.

Without that, a review quietly substitutes today’s policy for yesterday’s practice.

### 6. Distinguish raw, derived, and editorial artifacts

A serious evidence room should separate at least three layers:

- **raw evidence** — the original record, observation, or supplied object,
- **derived evidence** — extracted tables, summaries, translated passages, coded route logs, or calculated metrics,
- **editorial artifacts** — draft prose, interpretive notes, working hypotheses, or presentation surfaces.

Later findings should know which layer they are actually standing on.

### 7. Bind each finding to a minimum support set and a contradiction set

`801` already says findings should carry confidence and contradiction residue.

This note adds the evidence rule: each material finding should usually point to:

- a **minimum support set** of exhibits on which it materially rests,
- and, where relevant, a **contradiction set** of exhibits or claims that pull the other way.

That keeps contradiction from disappearing backstage and stops the register from becoming a warehouse with no verdict linkage.

### 8. Keep a redaction and withholding ledger instead of pretending the file is whole

Some evidence cannot be disclosed in full because of privacy, security, whistleblower safety, legal privilege, or other lawful constraints.

The archive should not respond by making the evidence object vanish. Instead it should preserve a short ledger saying:

- that an exhibit exists,
- what kind of object it is,
- why it is partially withheld or summarized,
- what substitute description or excerpt exists,
- and whether the withholding affects what the review can honestly claim.

That rule keeps confidentiality from laundering blind spots into certainty.

### 9. Preserve the transformation chain for numbers, excerpts, screenshots, and translations

A striking governance failure is often not missing evidence but **untraceable transformation**.

If a mission converts a ledger into a chart, a statute into an excerpt, an interview into coded themes, a route walk into a stopwatch table, or a foreign-language record into translated prose, the register should keep one short transformation note saying:

- what source exhibit was transformed,
- by whom,
- how,
- and where the transformed version now appears.

Otherwise later actors cannot tell whether they are reading the record, an extract, or an editorial invention.

### 10. Do not let evidence replacement happen silently

If an exhibit is corrected, superseded, reissued, or withdrawn, the earlier version should remain legible in the register.

The archive should prefer:

- visible supersession,
- dated replacement reasons,
- and explicit notes about whether the change affects any existing finding, response item, or closure decision.

A clean evidence register should therefore make silent substitution harder than honest amendment.

### 11. Reuse the same exhibit IDs across finding, response, appeal, and close-out

The same evidence spine should travel through the later stages.

A repair ledger under `802` should be able to say which exhibits originally grounded the finding. A contest under `797` should be able to point to the same numbered object rather than restaging the whole evidence hunt. A close-out review under `803` should be able to add new exhibits while still pointing back to the original support set and contradiction set.

That continuity is what lets the archive distinguish genuine repair from narrative reset.

### 12. Publish a public-safe evidence map, not the whole room

The archive does not need to dump every exhibit into public view.

But `795` should often be able to publish a compact outward evidence map showing, at minimum:

- the exhibit classes used,
- the main relied-on public documents,
- the existence of withheld or confidential classes,
- the dates or versions that mattered,
- and the evidence IDs attached to each public-facing finding.

That gives outsiders something stronger than “trust us” without exploding the archive.

### 13. Keep the register tight enough to live inside this archive

The user’s archival constraint matters here.

The archive should prefer:

- short exhibit descriptions,
- stable citations rather than large attachments,
- quote discipline over long excerpt dumps,
- and redacted or summarized stubs instead of retaining bulky scratch artifacts once they are no longer necessary.

This note is therefore not a license to hoard evidence. It is a rule for keeping a small archive honest.

## Minimum fields for an evidence register

| Field | What it tells later actors | Minimum form | Typical failure if missing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Exhibit ID** | stable handle for reuse | short unique code | findings cannot be tied back to objects |
| **Source type** | what kind of evidence this is | norm / record / observation / testimony / metric / route packet / map | different evidentiary risks get blurred together |
| **Provenance** | who produced or supplied it and how it arrived | one short line | possession is confused with trustworthiness |
| **Version / date** | which temporal state the object belongs to | issue date or capture date | current material overwrites the reviewed period |
| **Access / handling status** | public, restricted, redacted, summarized, withheld | one short status | confidentiality silently turns into certainty |
| **Support link** | which findings, routes, or repairs use it | one or more finding IDs | evidence room never connects to verdicts |
| **Contradiction / supersession note** | what pulls against it or replaced it | optional short note | silent substitution or unresolved conflict |

## Minimal evidence-register grammar

| Exhibit class | Typical object | What the register must preserve | What it prevents | Next use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Normative text** | statute, decree, municipal order | citation, version date, effective period | today’s rule laundering yesterday’s event | benchmark and legality findings under `791` / `801` |
| **Administrative record** | transfer extract, staffing table, case log | source office, extraction date, relevant fields, any redactions | spreadsheet mystique and uncheckable numbers | repair ownership under `802` |
| **Observed practice note** | site visit or route-walk observation | observer, date, scope, linked route or site | ceremonial visit becoming free-floating anecdote | mission and tracer proof under `800` / `804` |
| **Testimonial note** | stakeholder interview or hearing submission | actor type, date, confidentiality status, contradiction links | quotation theater and unattributed consensus claims | challenge and contradiction handling under `797` / `801` |
| **Closure proof** | retest packet or field revisit result | original exhibit links, new evidence IDs, residual-risk note | closure by self-certification | close-out review under `803` |

## Use rule for the archive

When later notes need to say not just **how a mission ran** or **what it found**, but **how the underlying evidence objects stay identifiable, typed, lawful, and reusable across the whole review chain**, the archive should usually do these things in order:

1. **use `800` to declare the mission, review lanes, and one evidence room**,
2. **use `805` to say which objects, sites, routes, files, or stakeholders entered the frame and what claims that sample can support**,
3. **use this note to assign exhibit IDs, provenance, version state, handling status, and support links to the relied-on evidence objects**,
4. **use `804` where one of those exhibits is a tracer-route packet or route-derived observation set**,
5. **use `807` when some of those exhibits concern reviewer disclosures, restriction decisions, or composition objections that must stay tied to the mission-integrity record rather than to private inboxes**,
6. **use `801` to state findings in language that matches the actual support set and contradiction set**,
7. **use `808` when anchor exhibits, precedent cases, or departure notes need stable IDs so calibration does not depend on oral memory**,
8. **use `802` to carry those same evidence IDs into the host’s response ledger**,
9. **use `797` if a party contests the finding and needs to challenge a specific support object or withholding decision**,
10. **use `803` to add later close-out exhibits without losing the original evidence spine**,
11. **use `795` to publish the compact public-safe evidence map rather than dumping the whole room**.

The archive should not let constitutional judgments float free from a typed and reusable evidence spine.

## Guardrails

- Do not let screenshots, excerpts, or translated passages appear without a source exhibit.
- Do not replace an exhibit silently when a newer export or corrected file arrives.
- Do not collapse normative, testimonial, observed, and computed evidence into one undifferentiated list.
- Do not let confidentiality make a relied-on exhibit invisible without at least a handling stub.
- Do not quote or retain large bodies of material when a short citation or stub will do.
- Do not let a public proof pack imply more underlying evidence than the register can actually name.

## Failure modes

- **orphaned exhibit review** — conclusions survive, but nobody can say which objects they rest on.
- **spreadsheet mystique** — numbers are treated as self-authenticating because they arrived in a table.
- **version laundering** — later policy text quietly replaces the rule in force at the time reviewed.
- **withholding black hole** — confidential evidence exists, but its absence is hidden rather than typed.
- **transformation blur** — excerpts, charts, coded notes, and translations drift too far from source objects.
- **narrative reset** — the response or close-out phase restages the evidence instead of reusing the same spine.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Does every material finding point to stable exhibit IDs rather than to free-text memory?
2. Can a later reader tell whether an exhibit is normative, testimonial, observed, administrative, or derived?
3. Is the version or date of each key exhibit visible enough to stop temporal laundering?
4. Are contradictory or superseded objects preserved rather than quietly dropped?
5. Are withholdings and redactions logged in a way that shows what remains unprovable?
6. Can the same evidence spine travel from mission to finding to response to appeal to close-out?
7. Does the public proof pack reveal at least the shape of the evidence map without exposing protected material?
