# 537 — Outer space and orbital commons by scope: local hosting, regional spaceports, national authorization, and global sustainability guardrails

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat outer space as a global commons of use rather than a frontier of silent enclosure: local communities should hold real say over launch sites, tracking stations, astronomy impacts, land use, emergency planning, and public benefit where space infrastructure touches the ground; regional institutions should coordinate spaceport corridors, environmental review, disaster interfaces, and research ecosystems where activities spill across jurisdictions; nations should authorize and continuously supervise national activities in space, register objects, govern liability, spectrum and orbital use discipline, public research, procurement, and security constraints, and prevent private launch markets from becoming lightly regulated sovereignty proxies; and the global layer should remain narrow but real in non-appropriation, peaceful use, registration, debris mitigation, conjunction information-sharing, and long-term sustainability rather than letting orbit become an unmanaged congestion market.

## Why this matters

Space governance is often distorted by distance. Because orbit looks remote, terrestrial politics are smuggled out of sight. Launch sites displace communities; satellite constellations reshape astronomy, connectivity, and spectrum use; and debris risks accumulate globally while authorization remains national and competition increasingly commercial. The result is a familiar failure: local burdens, national licensing, and global externalities with no clearly defended commons logic.

The archive already has notes on digital public infrastructure, climate, defense, markets, public knowledge, and extractives. What it still needs is one compact note on the **space constitution** that ties local hosting, national authorization, and orbital sustainability together. Space is not only a prestige sector. It is also a governance problem about shared environments, peaceful use, liability, transparency, and whether one actor’s shortcut can damage everyone’s access.

An ideal government therefore allocates space governance by scope. It keeps terrestrial hosting and visible impact close to communities, scales innovation and launch ecosystems regionally where that matters, reserves authorization and supervision to the state, and uses the global layer only where non-appropriation, registration, debris, and peaceful coexistence clearly outrun one country.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Begin from peaceful use and shared access, not frontier mythology

A good polity should not treat orbit as empty land waiting to be claimed by whichever state or firm moves first. The starting point should be peaceful use, non-appropriation, equitable access, and the preservation of the space environment for future users.

### 2. Keep hosting impacts and dark-sky realities close to communities

Launch sites, test ranges, tracking stations, data centres, and telescope impacts are terrestrial and often local. Communities should have standing in siting, environmental review, emergency planning, light and noise protection, cultural and Indigenous consent where relevant, and the distribution of public benefit from hosted infrastructure.

### 3. Coordinate launch ecosystems and research corridors regionally where they actually spill over

Spaceports, recovery zones, logistics corridors, university and industrial clusters, wildfire or coastal risk, and emergency services often span more than one municipality. Regional institutions should therefore coordinate the terrestrial footprint of space activity rather than letting one locality absorb costs for a wider ecosystem.

### 4. Reserve authorization, supervision, liability, and registration to the nation

Nations should own the licensing and continuing supervision of national activities in outer space, object registration, liability architecture, procurement discipline, export and security constraints, public research priorities, and the baseline debris and safety rules imposed on domestic operators. Those duties cannot be outsourced to local boosterism or industry self-governance.

### 5. Distinguish public benefit from private orbital enclosure

Commercial launch, remote sensing, navigation, broadband, and Earth-observation services can create real public value. But no good polity should let first-mover scale, spectrum control, or constellation size quietly become a private claim on shared orbital environments.

### 6. Treat debris, conjunction risk, and end-of-life disposal as constitutional obligations, not aftermarket cleanup

Space governance that treats debris mitigation and disposal as voluntary etiquette is already misgoverning the commons. Design life, conjunction assessment, re-entry risk, disposal, and information-sharing should be built into authorization from the start.

### 7. Protect scientific, civil, and developmental uses from pure power politics

Space systems support weather, disaster warning, navigation, communications, and science. Governance should keep those civil and developmental functions visible and protected rather than letting military competition or prestige narratives consume every policy choice.

### 8. Keep the global layer narrow but real: treaties, registration, sustainability guidelines, and traffic transparency

Global space governance should not become a single planetary operator. Its legitimate role is narrower: preserve the treaty floor, registration and notification discipline, shared sustainability guidelines, debris and conjunction transparency, and cooperative norms strong enough to stop orbit from becoming a tragedy of cumulative obstruction.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat local hosting communities as irrelevant because the payload reaches orbit.
- Do not let regional competition to attract launch or data infrastructure outrun environmental and consent discipline.
- Do not confuse national licensing with a permission slip to externalize debris and re-entry risk globally.
- Do not let commercial scale quietly rewrite the non-appropriation principle in practice.
- Do not let security narratives erase the civil and scientific uses that justify shared space governance.

## Failure modes

- **frontier exceptionalism**: ordinary public-law disciplines are suspended because space is framed as inherently extraordinary.
- **orbital enclosure by scale**: first movers accumulate de facto control over shared environments through sheer constellation volume.
- **ground-truth denial**: local land, noise, astronomy, and emergency harms are dismissed because the mission is nationally prestigious.
- **debris procrastination**: end-of-life obligations are deferred until congestion becomes everyone’s problem.
- **sovereignty laundering**: governments use private operators to do what they would hesitate to claim directly as state power.

## Practical tests

A scale-matched space constitution passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Do host communities have real standing in terrestrial siting, environmental review, and benefit-sharing for space infrastructure?
2. Are regional spillovers from launch, logistics, research, and emergency planning governed where they actually occur?
3. Are national authorization, continuing supervision, registration, and liability architecture clearly owned by the state?
4. Are debris, disposal, and conjunction obligations built in before launch rather than treated as voluntary aftercare?
5. Does the global layer stay narrow but real in non-appropriation, peaceful use, registration, and long-term sustainability guardrails?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can describe **its launch market, satellite industry, or national space strategy** but cannot show **who can challenge terrestrial hosting harm, where regional spillovers are governed, which level owns authorization and liability, and how orbit is protected from cumulative debris and enclosure**, it still has **space policy without a space constitution**.
