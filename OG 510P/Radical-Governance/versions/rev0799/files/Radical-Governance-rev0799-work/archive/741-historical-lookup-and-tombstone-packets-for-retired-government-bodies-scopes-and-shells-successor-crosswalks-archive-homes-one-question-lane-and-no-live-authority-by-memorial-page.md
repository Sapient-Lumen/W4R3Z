# 741 — Historical lookup and tombstone packets for retired government bodies, scopes, and shells: successor crosswalks, archive homes, one question lane, and no live authority by memorial page

## One-line thesis

Once the archive can retire a scope (`642`) or terminate a residual estate (`740`), it should also keep one compact **historical lookup and tombstone packet**: one durable public-facing dead-page object that says the authority is no longer live, names any successor, points to the record and archive home, keeps one legacy-question lane, and refuses to let memorial pages, redirects, or archived surfaces impersonate present-day governmental power.

## Why this matters

The archive already has most of the constitutional retirement grammar it needs.

It can now say how a stack-bearing arrangement becomes settled (`641`), how it retires (`642`), how it might later be reactivated only by a fresh act (`643`), how public records should outlive disappearing institutions (`571`), how official notice and service routes should remain explicit rather than presumed (`575`), how public service front doors should stay canonical and assisted rather than scattered (`577`), and how a post-custody residual shell must eventually reach real termination (`740`).

What it still lacked was the smallest public-facing object that survives *after* that retirement or termination is honest: **what should people actually find when they go looking for the dead thing**.

That gap matters because the public does not experience closure through a statute book alone. People experience it through URLs, phone numbers, letterheads, search results, archived documents, former portals, old case references, supplier escalation paths, and human memory inside institutions. If those surfaces are allowed to drift without one compact doctrine, the same old failure modes recur.

A dead body vanishes too cleanly, leaving nobody able to find a legacy record or where an unanswered complaint went. Or the dead body remains too alive: old branding, old domains, old forms, or old PDFs stay available with just enough implied freshness that the public treats them as still authoritative. Or the successor exists, but the only surviving public trace is a generic redirect that conceals the fact of constitutional change. Or a historic record is preserved, but there is no durable lane for the person who arrives years later with a case number, grant reference, permit ID, pension question, or disclosure request that still points to the old institution.

The archive therefore needs one compact packet for the honest public remnant of a dead authority: **a historical lookup and tombstone packet**. This packet should be thinner than a residual estate and much thinner than a successor authority. It should not reopen intake, quietly preserve live power, or act as a shadow service channel. It should only do four things well: state that the old thing is dead, identify what replaced it if anything, tell people where the durable records and archive home now sit, and keep one bounded route for legacy questions that still need lawful routing. Without that packet, the archive still permits **live authority by memorial page**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Require one explicit tombstone object whenever a public authority, scope lane, or closeout shell ceases to be live but remains historically discoverable

A retirement certificate or termination certificate proves the constitutional end-state. The tombstone packet is the public-facing lookup object that persists after that end-state.

It should be opened whenever a person is still likely to search by the old body, old route, old programme, old case prefix, old URL, or old document set. If a dead institution remains discoverable in search, references, records, or social memory, it should also leave one canonical tombstone rather than a mixture of silent disappearance, stale copies, and ad hoc forwarding.

### 2. State the death condition in plain language before anything else

The first job of the tombstone packet is not courtesy. It is constitutional honesty.

The packet should say plainly:

- that the body, scope lane, shell, or programme is **retired**, **dissolved**, **terminated**, **merged**, **superseded**, or **withdrawn**;
- the effective date of that status;
- the legal or administrative basis for the change;
- and whether there is a successor, partial successor set, or no direct successor at all.

Users should never have to infer death from a broken transaction flow, an archived attachment, or a silently redirected domain.

### 3. Distinguish four public-facing classes of dead thing

A tombstone packet should classify what ended, because different deaths require different lookup logic.

At minimum it should distinguish:

- **body tombstone** — a public body, office, board, or authority has ceased to exist;
- **scope-lane tombstone** — a competence, approval lane, jurisdictional route, or special-status channel has ended even if the parent institutions continue;
- **service-route tombstone** — a former portal, mailbox, call line, or transactional front door is dead and replaced;
- **closeout-shell tombstone** — a residual estate or interim shell is terminated and now survives only as historical memory.

Without these classes, the public sees one vague “archived” label while materially different constitutional facts hide underneath.

### 4. Keep one successor crosswalk, but do not let redirect convenience conceal institutional change

If anything replaced the dead thing, the tombstone packet should provide one visible crosswalk explaining where its functions, records, unresolved questions, or public routes went.

That crosswalk should distinguish:

- direct successor;
- split successor set;
- archive-only successor for records;
- and question-routing successor for legacy inquiries.

A redirect may still be useful, but the redirect should not impersonate continuity. A user should be able to tell that a constitutional holder changed rather than simply believing the same institution moved house.

### 5. Name the durable record and archive homes separately from the question lane

The place where historical records live is not always the place that answers future questions. The tombstone packet should therefore separate:

- the **record home** for authoritative files and transferred records;
- the **archive home** for preserved public-facing historic material such as web captures, gazettes, or withdrawn publications;
- and the **legacy-question lane** that handles ongoing routing, access, or challenge questions.

This matters because archives preserve memory, while departments or successors may answer present-day access or remedial questions. A dead body’s historical page should not collapse those functions into one ambiguous contact box.

### 6. Keep exactly one legacy-question lane and bound what it can do

A tombstone packet should retain one narrow question lane for people who still arrive with old references, legacy identifiers, or unresolved historical needs.

That lane may route:

- records-access and disclosure questions;
- historical verification requests;
- residual complaint or challenge redirection;
- or successor lookup for live obligations that moved elsewhere.

But it should not reopen ordinary transactions, accept new applications, restart general service delivery, or invite the public to continue using the dead institution as a live front door. The packet should say what the legacy lane **does not** do, not only what it does.

### 7. Treat archived websites, withdrawn pages, and preserved attachments as evidence surfaces, not live service surfaces

Web archives and withdrawn publications are often essential. But the archive should force one explicit distinction between:

- preserved historical content for memory or evidence;
- explanatory tombstone text about why the thing ended;
- and live pages where current action is actually supposed to happen.

Archived pages, watermarked attachments, and withdrawn documents should remain reachable when appropriate, yet visibly marked so they cannot masquerade as current policy, current intake, or current authority. Preservation should not become accidental reactivation.

### 8. Preserve old identifiers, names, and search paths through a lookup map rather than through hidden folklore

People return years later using what they actually have: old case numbers, permit IDs, scheme names, office names, defunct URLs, scanned letters, or legacy acronyms. The tombstone packet should therefore keep one lookup map covering the identifiers and names by which the dead thing is still likely to be found.

That lookup map should support:

- alias names and prior branding;
- legacy URLs or document paths where practical;
- historical case or programme prefixes;
- and one explanation of how those identifiers relate to current record homes or successor routes.

The public should not need an internal veteran to decode institutional archaeology.

### 9. Block memorial drift into shadow authority

The dead page should not become a miniature undead institution. The tombstone packet should therefore block:

- active forms or transactional widgets;
- live promises written in the present tense unless clearly marked as historical quotations;
- fresh “contact us for help” language that suggests broad current service;
- stale logos or headers that imply continuing public status;
- and ambiguous links that send users into successors without explaining the state change.

A memorial page should be informative, not operational.

### 10. Set a review discipline for the tombstone itself without turning it into an immortal service line

A tombstone packet should not be frozen and forgotten. It should have light review duties:

- check that successor links still work;
- confirm archive-home links remain reachable;
- confirm the legacy-question lane still exists or has itself lawfully moved;
- and confirm the page still accurately states the dead thing’s status.

But this review should not be a license to keep the dead institution notionally alive forever. The packet is a maintenance-light routing and memory object, not a standing service.

## Guardrails

- Do not let silence, 404s, or generic redirects stand in for a public tombstone.
- Do not let a preserved website or withdrawn attachment imply that the underlying authority remains live.
- Do not hide constitutional change behind convenience redirects that erase successor distinctions.
- Do not collapse archive custody, record custody, and question-routing into one vague contact point.
- Do not allow the legacy-question lane to reopen ordinary intake or fresh service delivery.
- Do not destroy alias names, old identifiers, or search clues just to make the dead thing look cleaner.
- Do not let the tombstone itself become a permanent shadow institution with live branding and undefined duties.

## Failure modes

- **live authority by memorial page**: the dead institution’s page still looks authoritative enough that people keep treating it as current government.
- **historical disappearance**: the institution dies so completely that nobody can later find records, successors, or the lawful route for legacy questions.
- **redirect laundering**: a successor absorbs traffic without ever explaining that legal authority changed.
- **archive confusion**: preserved web captures or withdrawn documents look current because they lack strong death labeling.
- **identifier amnesia**: the public arrives with old names or numbers, but no lookup map survives.
- **legacy-lane creep**: the narrow post-death question lane silently becomes a live substitute service desk.
- **split-successor fog**: several successors exist, but the dead thing leaves no one crosswalk explaining who took what.

## Practical tests

A tombstone packet is honest when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it plainly say the old thing is no longer live, and from what date?
2. Does it classify what kind of dead thing this is: body, scope lane, service route, or closeout shell?
3. Does it name the legal or administrative basis of the ending clearly enough for later readers?
4. Does it provide a visible successor crosswalk rather than a silent redirect alone?
5. Does it separate record home, archive home, and legacy-question lane?
6. Is the legacy-question lane bounded so it cannot reopen ordinary service?
7. Are archived pages and withdrawn documents visibly historical rather than apparently current?
8. Does one lookup map preserve legacy names, aliases, URLs, or identifiers?
9. Does the page avoid live branding, fresh intake, or broad-service language that would recreate authority by implication?
10. Is there a light review rhythm to keep the tombstone accurate without pretending it is a live institution?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **this authority, scope lane, service route, or closeout shell is dead**, it should also be able to show one compact public-facing packet proving **how that death is stated, what replaced it if anything, where records and archives now live, which one bounded lane answers legacy questions, how old names and identifiers still resolve, and why the memorial surface cannot be mistaken for current authority**. Otherwise it still permits **live authority by memorial page**.
