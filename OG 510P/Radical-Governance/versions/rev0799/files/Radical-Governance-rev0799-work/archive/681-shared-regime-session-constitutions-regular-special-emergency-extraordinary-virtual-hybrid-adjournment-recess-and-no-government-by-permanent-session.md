# 681 — Shared-regime session constitutions: regular, special, emergency, extraordinary, virtual, hybrid, adjournment, recess, and no government by permanent session

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed session constitution in their rules of procedure** that separates regular, special, emergency, resumed, adjourned, recessed, virtual, and hybrid sittings — so convening power, urgency, quorum, agenda compression, and temporary meeting modalities do not quietly become a shadow amendment power.

## Why this matters

The archive now has notes on representation (`671`), decision rules (`672`), officers and records (`673`, `674`), initiative and subsidiary bodies (`675`, `676`), external participation and integrity (`677`, `678`), and interpretation and jurisprudence memory (`679`, `680`).

That is enough to explain **who may act**, **how they decide**, and **how doctrine is remembered**. It is still not enough to explain **when the regime is actually in session and under what procedural shape**.

Every serious shared regime eventually encounters pressure to bend its session architecture.

A regular sitting is too slow, so actors improvise a “special consultation” that behaves like a lawmaking session. An emergency is declared, but nobody can tell who may trigger it or what compressed rights follow. A body adjourns and resumes later, but the public record makes it look like one uninterrupted meeting. A virtual or hybrid meeting is treated as if it were simply the same as physical session, even though the regime has silently altered quorum, speaking order, floor access, or document timing.

Without a typed session constitution, six familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **permanent-session drift**: the regime acts as if urgency is always available and therefore every decision can travel through compressed process.

The second is **convening capture**: whoever controls the summons, suspension, or resumption schedule gains hidden constitutional leverage over outcomes.

The third is **emergency laundering**: special or emergency forms are used to do ordinary business that should have travelled through the regular session lane.

The fourth is **modality confusion**: virtual or hybrid presence is treated as costless equivalence even when floor rights, quorum, language support, or access conditions have changed.

The fifth is **calendar opacity**: observers cannot tell whether a body is recessed, adjourned, closed, resumed, or still technically live.

The sixth is **agenda compression by timing**: short notice or session redesign becomes a way to evade slower rights-bearing lanes.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **shared-regime session discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Publish one session map in the rules of procedure

A serious regime should say in one place:

- what counts as a regular session,
- who may call a special or extraordinary session,
- what makes an emergency session an emergency,
- who may suspend, adjourn, recess, or resume,
- whether virtual or hybrid sessions are allowed,
- and what procedural consequences follow from each session type.

If actors must infer session type from press releases, chair announcements, or travel calendars, the regime is already partly governed by convening discretion.

### 2. Separate regular, special, and emergency lanes

A regular session should be the default lane for ordinary business.

A special or extraordinary session should require a named trigger and a narrower rationale.

An emergency session should require an even clearer trigger, a fast convening rule, and visible limits on what may be compressed.

Not every urgent political preference is a constitutional emergency.

### 3. Type suspension, adjournment, recess, and resumption separately

The archive should prefer a rule that distinguishes:

- **recess** as a short pause inside a still-living session,
- **adjournment** as a formal halt pending a later resumption or the next session,
- **suspension** as a temporary procedural stop,
- and **closure** as the end of the session.

These states matter because rights, agenda carryover, and documentary continuity often differ across them.

### 4. Do not let convening power become hidden substantive power

Who may call a session, delay it, or refuse to place a matter on the calendar is already exercising constitutional power.

The regime should therefore state:

- who may request a session,
- who verifies the request,
- who issues the summons,
- what minimum notice applies,
- and what happens if the ordinary convener refuses or delays.

### 5. Give virtual and hybrid meetings a typed temporary lane

Virtual or hybrid sitting may be lawful and useful. It should not be treated as a metaphysical non-event.

The archive should therefore prefer a rule that says explicitly:

- whether remote presence counts for quorum,
- which participants may speak remotely,
- how interpretation, authentication, and vote security work,
- what record is created,
- and whether the modality is standing, exceptional, or sunsetted.

A changed meeting modality is a changed governance condition even when the legal question appears identical.

### 6. Limit emergency compression to what the emergency actually needs

Urgency may justify shorter notice, tighter agendas, or narrower speaking windows. It should not justify constitutional shapelessness.

A serious regime should say which rights remain intact in emergencies, such as:

- quorum,
- credentials checks,
- minimum document availability,
- vote traceability where voting occurs,
- and later publication of the official record.

### 7. Publish one session status spine for the public

The regime should keep one visible calendar or register showing:

- session type,
- opening and closing status,
- recesses and resumptions,
- agenda version,
- modality,
- and the official documentary lane.

This is the minimum anti-folklore layer for procedural time.

### 8. Tie agenda carryover to typed session states

When a session is resumed or adjourned, the regime should say what carries forward automatically, what must be re-tabled, and what expires.

Otherwise actors will manipulate session labels to revive dead business or kill live business without saying so.

### 9. Keep exceptional procedures visibly exceptional

If a regime uses special emergency procedures or modality-specific procedures, they should have:

- an adoption basis,
- a scope,
- a duration,
- and a reversion rule.

Temporary procedure that never visibly expires is often stealth amendment.

### 10. Preserve calendrical honesty

The archive should prefer a rule that public-facing materials never imply that a body is continuously in ordinary session when it is actually:

- between sessions,
- in a resumed emergency session,
- operating under special procedures,
- or acting through a smaller body under delegated authority.

Calendrical honesty is part of constitutional honesty.

## Working matrix

| Session object | Honest rule | Why | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| regular session | default lane for ordinary business with normal notice and agenda practice | protects baseline deliberation | treating every question as emergency business |
| special or extraordinary session | require named trigger, summons authority, and narrower rationale | keeps exceptions constitutional | special-session inflation |
| emergency session | fast call plus explicit compression limits and surviving guarantees | prevents emergency laundering | urgency as blank cheque |
| recess / adjournment / resumption | publish distinct state changes and carryover effects | preserves calendrical and legal continuity | making time status depend on insider memory |
| virtual or hybrid modality | state quorum, speaking, voting, interpretation, and record rules explicitly | avoids modality confusion | pretending remote procedure changes nothing |
| session calendar and register | show live status, modality, agenda version, and documentary lane | protects public legibility | governance by travel rumor or chair email |

## Guardrails

- Do not let special or emergency sessions become the unexamined default.
- Do not let convening power hide inside one chair, bureau, or host government without a fallback lane.
- Do not collapse recess, adjournment, suspension, closure, and resumption into one vague pause.
- Do not treat virtual or hybrid sitting as identical to in-person procedure unless the regime has said why and how.
- Do not let emergency compression erase quorum, records, or minimum notice guarantees unless the legal order says so plainly.
- Do not leave the public to infer whether a body is live, recessed, resumed, or closed.

## Failure modes

- **government by permanent session**: urgency becomes the ambient constitutional climate.
- **convening capture**: actors shape outcomes by controlling when and whether the body meets.
- **emergency laundering**: ordinary lawmaking travels through crisis procedure.
- **modality confusion**: remote attendance changes power without visible acknowledgement.
- **calendar opacity**: outsiders cannot reconstruct when business was live or under what procedure.
- **agenda compression by timing**: short notice becomes a substitute for substantive authority.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it publish one rules-of-procedure map for regular, special, emergency, and resumed sessions?
2. Are summons authority, request thresholds, and fallback convening paths explicit?
3. Are recess, suspension, adjournment, closure, and resumption visibly distinct states?
4. If virtual or hybrid meetings are allowed, are quorum, floor rights, interpretation, voting, and record rules stated explicitly?
5. Do emergency procedures identify what may be compressed and what may not?
6. Is there one public session calendar or register showing live status, modality, and official agenda version?
7. Are carryover and expiry rules visible when business crosses session boundaries?
8. Do temporary special procedures have a scope, duration, and reversion rule?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can **meet, pause, resume, or switch modality** but cannot say **what kind of session it is, who called it, what procedural consequences follow, what still guarantees fairness, and when the exceptional lane ends**, it still has **institutional motion without an honest session constitution**.
