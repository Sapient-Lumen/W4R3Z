# 793 — Maintenance calendars for ideal governments of each scope: benchmark refresh, stress-drill cadence, trigger overrides, public registries, and no constitutional care by sporadic panic

## One-line thesis

The archive should pair its ideal-government answer key, benchmark cards, stress cards, and scope-audit triggers with one compact **maintenance-calendar rule** for each scope — fixed benchmark refreshes, typed drill cadence, trigger-based overrides, publication and follow-up duties, and explicit closure or rescoping lanes — so multilevel constitutions are maintained as routine public infrastructure rather than rediscovered only during reform waves or crisis embarrassment.

## Why this matters

`790` now gives the archive a compact answer key for what ideal governments at each scope should generally be. `791` gives a short benchmark card for judging whether a real constitution or proposal matches that stack in ordinary time. `792` gives a stress-test and drill-card format for asking whether that arrangement remains honest under adverse conditions. `632` gives a public registry of drift triggers for when the scope question must be reopened ahead of schedule. `771` already says serious evaluation needs planning, publication, and follow-up rather than impressionistic learning theater.

What the archive still lacked was the maintenance rhythm that ties those tools together.

Without such a rhythm, benchmarking and stress-testing tend to become ceremonial bursts. A government runs one reform review, produces one elegant scorecard, survives one ugly shock, and then slides back into constitutional amnesia. The problem is rarely total absence of standards. It is lack of a visible **calendar of care** that says what gets checked annually, what rides election or capital cycles, what must be drilled more often than it is redesigned, what immediately overrides the normal clock, and what public record closes the loop after each check.

The archive therefore needs one compact **maintenance-calendar note**. Not a giant compliance framework. Not a new bureaucracy of permanent review. One typed rule for how an honest multilevel polity keeps its constitutional stack legible over time. Later notes should now also treat `800` as the place to go once the missing issue is no longer **when the review happens** but **how that review is actually carried out credibly on the ground**. They should now also treat `802` as the place to go once the missing issue is not the calendar itself but **how each finding is turned into owned repair work, closure proof, and overdue escalation between review cycles**. They should now also treat `803` as the place to go once the missing issue is not the cadence of review but **how later close-out checks, monitoring tails, and reopen triggers verify that claimed repairs still hold between cycles**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate standing clocks from trigger overrides

A healthy polity should maintain both:

- **standing clocks** for routine benchmark refresh, drill cadence, and periodic deep review, and
- **trigger overrides** for shocks, drift events, or democratic-warning signs serious enough to reopen the question ahead of schedule.

This distinction matters because a polity that uses only fixed calendars becomes slow and self-satisfied, while a polity that uses only ad hoc triggers becomes governed by crisis attention.

### 2. Give every scope both a benchmark rhythm and a drill rhythm

Every scope should have two rhythms, not one.

The **benchmark rhythm** asks whether the arrangement still fits its vocation, kit, fiscal congruence, and scrutiny burden in ordinary time under `791`.

The **drill rhythm** asks whether the arrangement still holds constitutional shape under adverse conditions under `792`.

These are related but not interchangeable. Benchmarks without drills create calm-weather vanity. Drills without benchmarks create permanent emergency optics.

### 3. Let the rhythm follow the power, the hazard, and the replacement cost

The archive should reject one universal review interval for all institutions.

A micro-local steward body with tiny funds does not need the same maintenance rhythm as a metropolitan transport authority, a provincial hospital platform, a national equalization system, or a global alerting regime. The honest rule is to make the cadence depend on:

- how much power or money the institution carries,
- how severe the hazard or service discontinuity would be if it failed,
- how quickly drift can accumulate without public notice,
- and how expensive or disruptive replacement would be if maintenance is delayed.

The result should be **typed calendars**, not one administrative superstition about annualism or quinquennialism.

### 4. Tie deep review to real constitutional and fiscal cycles

Routine light-touch checks can be annual or seasonal. Deep review should usually ride an existing cycle that already matters: multi-year capital planning, electoral turnover, census or boundary review, treaty review conference, or other constitutional moment when redesign is actually feasible.

That keeps maintenance from becoming a floating essay exercise detached from budgeting, representation, or legal change.

### 5. Keep the normal clock short enough to detect drift, but long enough to allow learning

Over-frequent redesign is a maintenance failure too.

A polity should not reopen every territorial or institutional settlement every few months. But neither should it wait a decade to admit that the functional city has outgrown a summit club, that a region has become decorative, or that a supposedly local function is now centrally scripted by grants and platforms.

The archive should therefore prefer short **monitoring and drill clocks**, paired with longer **deep benchmark and redesign clocks**.

### 6. Every scheduled review should end in one of four outputs

A maintenance calendar is only honest if each scheduled review closes into one visible lane. Each benchmark refresh or drill cycle should end with one of four outputs:

- **maintain** — the arrangement still fits and no major repair is due,
- **repair** — connectors, kit, reserves, or scrutiny need completion within the current scope,
- **support-with-handback** — temporary support is justified but must run under `631`,
- **reopen scope** — the evidence is strong enough to activate `632` and formal rescoping review.

This prevents recurring assessment from degrading into decorative narrative.

### 7. Publish the calendar, the result, and the follow-up owner

The maintenance rhythm should be public.

The polity should publish:

- what is due for benchmark refresh,
- what is due for drills,
- which trigger overrides are open,
- the result of each completed review,
- the owner of each follow-up task,
- and the next review point.

The archive should reject review culture where only insiders know which constitutional questions are live, deferred, or quietly buried.

### 8. Distinguish benchmark failure, drill failure, and trigger activation

The archive should not collapse all maintenance signals into one bucket.

- A **benchmark failure** says the ordinary-time fit is weak.
- A **drill failure** says the arrangement may be underprepared or mis-scoped under stress.
- A **trigger activation** says some drift or event has become serious enough to jump the normal queue.

Those signals may overlap, but they are not the same. The calendar should preserve that distinction so the wrong repair is not chosen for the wrong warning.

### 9. Make after-action closure part of the maintenance clock

A drill or real-world shock should not be treated as complete when the adrenaline subsides.

The calendar should require a visible after-action closure step naming:

- what happened,
- what was learned,
- what changed immediately,
- what still needs repair,
- whether temporary concentration must hand back,
- and whether the event generated a formal rescoping signal.

Otherwise the archive gets the appearance of learning without institutional closure.

### 10. Preserve room for asymmetry without losing comparability

Different territories may need different drill frequencies or deeper review cycles because they are remote, sparse, metropolitan, archipelagic, fiscally fragile, border-exposed, or hazard-dense.

That is legitimate. But the archive should still insist on one comparable grammar: benchmark clock, drill clock, trigger override rule, publication duty, and closure lane. Asymmetry may change the frequency, not the need to declare the rhythm.

### 11. Do not let the review calendar become a substitute for political judgment

A published maintenance calendar is an instrument of discipline, not a machine that decides the constitution automatically.

It should sharpen political judgment, force evidence onto the record, and prevent neglect. It should not pretend that a missed indicator or a predetermined date can by itself settle whether a function needs repair, handback, or redesign.

## Maintenance-calendar grammar

| Field | What the calendar should say | Typical outputs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Archetype claimed** | which scope the arrangement says it is | micro-local / municipal / metropolitan / regional / national / continental / global |
| **Benchmark refresh clock** | how often ordinary-time fit is rechecked under `791` | annual light review / biannual / every electoral or capital cycle |
| **Drill clock** | how often a reusable stress or continuity drill is run under `792` | seasonal / annual / biennial / hazard-season specific |
| **Deep review clock** | when a fuller redesign-capable review should occur | every 4-6 years / treaty-review cycle / census-linked / electoral-cycle linked |
| **Trigger override** | what events or warning signs jump the queue under `632` | drift signal / major shock / repeated emergency / fiscal cliff / legal finding |
| **Publication duty** | what must be publicly logged after each check | result, follow-up owner, deadline, next review point |
| **Closure lane** | what type of decision each review must yield | maintain / repair / support-with-handback / reopen scope |

## Scope cadence table

| Scope | Benchmark refresh | Drill cadence | Deep review | Typical trigger override |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Micro-local** | annual steward and records check | seasonal or hazard-season tabletop where relevant | every 3-4 years or when boundaries/representation change | repeated escalation failure, capture by patronage, or persistent inability to route local problems upward |
| **Municipal** | annual ordinary-time benchmark with budget and service review | at least annual continuity drill for essential front-door services; more often for hazard-exposed cities | every electoral cycle or major capital-plan cycle | repeated distress support, service collapse, hidden grant-control drift, or metropolitan spillover overload |
| **Metropolitan** | annual shared-system benchmark | annual or hazard-linked drill for trunk systems, basin risk, mobility, and data handoff | every 4-5 years or when metro powers materially deepen | host-city capture, repeated summit improvisation, or major mismatch between metro power and metro scrutiny |
| **Regional / provincial / state** | annual platform and fiscal-congruence benchmark | annual or biennial drill for hospitals, logistics, grids, basins, or corridor continuity | every electoral cycle or constitutional review cycle | persistent national rescue, decorative-region signals, or platform failure revealing wrong scale |
| **National** | annual guarantees, equalization, and institutional-capacity benchmark | annual continuity and constitutional-restraint drill; additional hazard-specific exercises as needed | every 4-6 years or major constitutional cycle | repeated emergency renewal, equalization stress, constitutional bottlenecks, or chronic upward capture of ordinary place functions |
| **Continental / plurilateral** | annual competence and compliance benchmark | periodic joint exercises tied to corridor, mobility, health, or security function | treaty review conference or other declared multi-year review point | implementation breakdown, repeated dispute non-resolution, or panic-driven competence creep |
| **Global** | annual reporting and mandate-fit benchmark | periodic multilateral simulation or review linked to the regime’s narrow-waist function | treaty-review cycle or other declared multi-year conference rhythm | alerting failure, reporting collapse, repeated implementation blame fog, or pressure to widen beyond treaty vocation |

## Use rule for the archive

When later notes ask not only **what is the ideal government here?** but **how should this arrangement be maintained over time?**, the archive should usually do seven things in order:

1. **use `790` for the short answer-key stack**,
2. **use `791` for ordinary-time fit**,
3. **use `792` for shock-weather honesty**,
4. **use `632` when drift or warning signs jump the normal queue**,
5. **use this note to state the standing review and drill rhythm**,
6. **use `794` when the issue is no longer ordinary maintenance but a phased transition toward a different or repaired scope arrangement**,
7. **use `796` when repeated missed maintenance, failed recovery, or overdue corrective work has crossed into enforced repair, special measures, or formal intervention rather than ordinary upkeep**,
7. **add only the domain-specific cadence departure that actually matters**.

The archive should not keep reinventing constitutional maintenance from scratch in each domain note.

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse maintenance rhythm with permanent redesign.
- Do not rely on triggers alone and call that a maintenance system.
- Do not run benchmarks so rarely that drift becomes folklore.
- Do not run drills without visible after-action closure and handback checks.
- Do not let publication stop at scores while hiding ownership and deadlines.
- Do not let the calendar become an automatic constitution-changing machine.
- Do not let asymmetry excuse undeclared review rhythms.

## Failure modes

- **constitutional care by sporadic panic** — benchmarking and redesign happen only after scandal or breakdown.
- **calendar without overrides** — obvious drift persists because no trigger can jump the queue.
- **override without calendar** — every issue becomes a reform emergency and no routine maintenance culture forms.
- **review without closure** — assessments are completed but never resolved into maintain, repair, support-with-handback, or reopen-scope lanes.
- **publication theater** — scores appear publicly but ownership, deadlines, and next review points remain invisible.
- **drill amnesia** — shocks generate lessons, but no public closure or rescoping signal follows.
- **one-rhythm superstition** — every scope is forced into the same review interval regardless of power, hazard, or replacement cost.

## Practical tests

A polity is maintaining its ideal governments honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does every scope or major institutional home have both a benchmark clock and a drill clock?
2. Are deep redesign-capable reviews tied to real constitutional, electoral, fiscal, or treaty cycles rather than floating essays?
3. Can serious triggers reopen the question ahead of schedule under a visible override rule?
4. Does each completed review end in maintain, repair, support-with-handback, or reopen-scope?
5. Are the calendar, results, follow-up owners, deadlines, and next review points public?
6. Do after-action reviews from real shocks feed directly into the same maintenance registry rather than a separate folklore channel?
7. Can the polity distinguish benchmark weakness, drill weakness, and trigger activation without collapsing them into one vague concern?
8. Are asymmetries in cadence explained by real hazard, power, or morphology rather than by neglect?

## Compression rule for the archive

If the archive wants to say not only that a government-by-scope design is good, but that it is **being kept good**, it should usually be able to do so with one short maintenance calendar naming the benchmark refresh clock, drill cadence, deep review clock, trigger override rule, publication duty, and closure lane. If it cannot, the maintenance claim is still too atmospheric to count as a stable archive judgment.
