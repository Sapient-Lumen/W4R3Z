# 618 — Regulatory homes for shared territorial power: licensing, permits, standards, inspection, enforcement, and no government by operator rulebook

## One-line thesis

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, it needs an honest **regulatory home** as well as democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, participatory, knowledge, service, planning, and capital ones: a visible territorial rulebook, named licensing and permitting lanes where risk demands them, a clear separation among rule-maker, regulator, operator, and reviewer, proportionate inspection and enforcement, and no government by operator rulebook.

## Why this matters

The archive can now say when shared territorial power has become real government and what kinds of institutional home should follow. It can name the democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, participatory, knowledge, service, planning, and capital constitutions that honest territorial government needs.

What it still lacked was the **regulatory analogue**.

That absence matters because many metropolitan and regional governments become real not only when they tax, plan, or invest, but when they begin deciding who may operate across the territory, what service, safety, environmental, market-entry, interoperability, or consumer-protection conditions apply, what permits are required, how compliance will be checked, what happens when operators fail, and who can waive, suspend, or enforce rules.

A shared tier that makes those choices through host-city departments, ministry leftovers, concession contracts, operator clubs, or memorandum networks may already be doing real government. But without a regulatory home, it still lacks an honest place where territorial rules are stated, permissions are issued, compliance is checked, and enforcement can be challenged in public.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish a regulatory constitution from planning or service rhetoric

A metro or region should not be treated as having a regulatory constitution merely because it publishes a strategy, signs service agreements, or coordinates operators. The archive should distinguish **real territorial regulation** from planning language and service-management rhetoric.

Real territorial regulation is where a shared tier sets binding conditions for entry, operation, quality, interoperability, safety, pricing, data-sharing, environmental performance, curb or network use, or other conduct across a territory. If that work is real, the territory needs more than contract residue and informal coordination.

### 2. Give the territory one visible rulebook for shared powers

Once shared territorial power is real, the archive should prefer one visible rulebook or tightly declared family of rulebooks for the shared territory, with

- the territorial scope stated,
- the classes of actor covered,
- the kinds of permission or registration required,
- the standards or performance obligations imposed,
- the monitoring and reporting duties attached,
- and the sanctions or corrective paths available.

The point is not maximal codification. It is one public answer to the question: **what regulatory conditions govern this shared territory?**

### 3. Separate rule-maker, regulator, operator, and reviewer

A regulatory home is dishonest when the same territorial body writes the rules, operates the service, inspects itself, and adjudicates its own disputes without visible separation. The archive should therefore prefer a declared split among

- the body that makes or approves rules,
- the body that grants, denies, varies, or revokes permissions,
- the public or private operators that must comply,
- and the complaint, appeal, or review lane.

The split need not always mean entirely separate institutions, but the functions should be identifiable enough that regulation does not collapse into operator self-rule.

### 4. Use licensing and permitting where risk is real, not as ambient bureaucracy

A shared territorial tier should not use licences, permits, authorisations, concessions, registrations, or certifications as decorative obstacles. The archive should prefer permission systems only where risks are high enough, hard enough to reverse, or network-wide enough that ex post correction would arrive too late.

Where permissions are justified, the territory should make the threshold, criteria, evidence requirements, timing, and renewal or expiry rules explicit. This keeps territorial regulation from degenerating into improvised discretion.

### 5. Keep permissioning risk-based, proportionate, and joined-up

An honest regulatory home should not force applicants or operators through a maze of duplicative territorial and municipal permissions where one joined-up lane could do the job. The archive should therefore prefer risk-based and proportionate permitting with clear information, bounded evidence requirements, coordinated interfaces, and visible time clocks.

This matters especially in transport, environmental control, network infrastructure, land use, concessions, shared mobility, platform work at territorial scale, and utility settings where overlapping permissions can become a shadow constitution of delay and private access.

### 6. Give the territory an inspection and compliance spine of its own

A metro or region does not have a full regulatory home if it can announce standards but cannot inspect, receive mandatory reports, audit compliance, or issue corrective directions. The archive should prefer one visible compliance spine that says

- what data or reports regulated actors must provide,
- what inspections or audits may occur,
- how the territory prioritises attention by risk,
- and what happens when non-compliance is found.

Otherwise the real constitution of the territory becomes whatever operators volunteer.

### 7. Make enforcement ladders visible before failure arrives

A regulatory home becomes murky when the only visible choices are total passivity or catastrophic revocation after a scandal. The archive should therefore prefer a public enforcement ladder: warning, corrective plan, conditional continuation, administrative penalty, temporary suspension, receiver or substitute operation where lawful, licence loss, and explicit handback or continuity duties where the governed service is essential.

This is how a shared tier stops governing by threat theatre and starts governing through predictable corrective stages.

### 8. Dock regulation to the planning, service, and capital constitutions

The regulatory home should not float separately from the planning home, service home, or capital home. The archive should prefer arrangements where territorial rules visibly dock to land use, network plans, service obligations, infrastructure constraints, and medium-term investment logic.

This means the shared tier should be able to show how its permits, standards, or operator obligations serve the territorial plan, protect service continuity, and avoid regulatory bargains that quietly defeat the investment and planning spine.

### 9. Publish waivers, exemptions, and special deals as constitutional events

A regulatory constitution is dishonest when the written rulebook is strict but real territorial government occurs through private exemptions, host-city indulgence, ministry letters, or bespoke operator deals. The archive should therefore prefer visible publication of waivers, exemptions, pilot derogations, compatibility determinations, and equivalent exceptions.

Otherwise the true regulatory order of the territory lives in side letters and access relationships rather than in the public rulebook.

### 10. Keep territorial regulation challengeable and learn from it

A regulatory home remains incomplete if operators and residents can see only the final rule or sanction. The archive should prefer visible reasons, complaint channels, review routes, appeal where law provides it, ex post evaluation of regulatory effects, and periodic pruning of obsolete or duplicative conditions.

This is how a territory stops governing by frozen accretion and starts governing regulation as a revisable constitutional responsibility.

## Regulatory-home matrix

| Question | Thin or dishonest arrangement | Honest regulatory home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What rules govern the shared territory?** | contract residue, ministry leftovers, and operator custom | one visible territorial rulebook or tightly declared family of rulebooks |
| **Who does what?** | rule-making, operation, inspection, and review blur together | rule-maker, regulator, operator, and reviewer are identifiable |
| **When are permissions used?** | ambient bureaucracy or informal discretion | licensing or permitting is tied to real risk and explicit criteria |
| **How do applicants move through the system?** | duplicative multi-door maze | proportionate, coordinated, and time-bounded permission lanes |
| **How is compliance checked?** | voluntary operator disclosure | declared reporting, inspection, audit, and risk-prioritisation rules |
| **How is enforcement staged?** | silent tolerance or sudden crackdown | public ladder from correction through suspension or revocation |
| **How do rules relate to the territorial project?** | regulation drifts away from plans and services | rules dock visibly to planning, service, and capital commitments |
| **How are exceptions handled?** | side letters and bespoke indulgence | waivers, exemptions, and derogations are published and reasoned |
| **How is drift corrected?** | old rules pile up without learning | review, appeals, ex post evaluation, and pruning are visible |

## Failure modes

- **government by operator rulebook**: the real territorial constitution is set by incumbent operators, platform terms, concession habit, or host-government working custom.
- **permission-maze territorialism**: the shared tier governs mainly by overlapping approvals, opaque timing, and navigation burdens.
- **self-regulating operator state**: the same body writes rules, runs services, inspects conduct, and judges disputes.
- **waiver constitution**: the written rulebook is secondary to unpublished exceptions and access relationships.
- **inspection theatre**: standards exist on paper but there is no credible reporting, audit, or inspection spine.
- **cliff-edge enforcement**: the system oscillates between tolerated non-compliance and spectacular crisis intervention because no corrective ladder exists.
- **plan-rule split**: the territorial plan promises one future while the actual permit and enforcement regime quietly produces another.
- **frozen accretion**: obsolete or duplicative rules remain because the territory can add conditions but not prune them.

## Practical tests

A shared territorial regulatory constitution passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is there one visible territorial rulebook, or one tightly declared family of rulebooks, for the shared power in question?
2. Can the territory state clearly who makes rules, who grants or varies permissions, who operates, and who hears complaints or review?
3. Are licences, permits, registrations, or concessions justified by explicit risk and governed by declared criteria, evidence rules, timing, and renewal or expiry paths?
4. Is there a visible compliance spine with reporting, inspection, audit, and risk-based prioritisation rather than voluntary operator disclosure alone?
5. Does the public rulebook disclose waivers, exemptions, pilot derogations, and other special deals as constitutional events rather than private residue?
6. Are enforcement ladders, reasons, review routes, and periodic pruning visible enough that territorial regulation can be corrected without scandal government?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a metropolitan or regional body can describe **its plans, services, and flagship projects** but cannot show **one visible rulebook, declared permission lanes, a separation among rule-maker, regulator, operator, and reviewer, a real inspection and enforcement spine, and a public record of exceptions and challenge**, then it still lacks **an honest regulatory home for shared territorial power**.
