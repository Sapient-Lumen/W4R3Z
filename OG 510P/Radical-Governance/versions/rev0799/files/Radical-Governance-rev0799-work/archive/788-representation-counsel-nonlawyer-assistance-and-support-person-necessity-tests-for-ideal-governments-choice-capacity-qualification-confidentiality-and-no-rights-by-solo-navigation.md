# 788 — Representation, counsel, nonlawyer assistance, and support-person necessity tests for ideal governments: choice, capacity, qualification, confidentiality, and no rights by solo navigation

## One-line thesis

Ideal governments should ensure that **people facing administrative power can choose not to face it alone**: preserve the right to be accompanied, represented, advised, or otherwise assisted; distinguish full legal representation from limited nonlawyer help and ordinary support-person presence; verify authority without turning authorization into obstruction; and type stronger assistance duties where stakes, complexity, disability, language barriers, or vulnerability make solo navigation an unfair fiction.

## Why this matters

The archive now says that people must be heard before materially adverse decisions (`786`), that procedure must be understandable in language and format (`787`), and that evidentiary burden, file access, and administrative appeal must be fair and usable (`773`, `785`). What it still lacked as one compact operating rule was the person-side answer to a recurring administrative question:

**when must the state accept that a person needs another human being beside them in order for procedure to be real?**

Administrative systems routinely assume an autonomous, literate, calm, digitally fluent adult with time, stable housing, ordinary paperwork, and no fear of authority. Real people are often none of those things. They may need a lawyer because the case is sanction-heavy, reputation-damaging, or precedent-setting. They may need a union representative, accredited adviser, social worker, disability advocate, parent, trusted relative, interpreter-co-ordinator, or community navigator because the legal issue is embedded in illness, migration, care, school exclusion, benefits loss, licensing, child protection, or professional discipline. They may only need a support person who can help them stay oriented, take notes, and stop the process from becoming intimidation by room design.

Many states answer this badly in both directions. One error is **solo-navigation romanticism**: the administration treats representation as suspicious, burdensome, or unnecessary because the matter is “just administrative,” even where the state’s file, expertise, and time discipline plainly dominate the person. The opposite error is **credential monopoly drift**: only expensive counsel can speak, simple authorisations are over-policed, and helpful nonlawyer assistance is excluded even in systems where lawyers are scarce or unaffordable.

Ideal-government design therefore needs a doctrine of **assistance necessity**. The point is not that every permit desk or school form requires counsel. It is to force the state to answer several questions openly: when a person may choose a representative or assistant; when nonlawyer support is legitimate; what authority must be shown; how confidentiality, conflict, and competence are protected; when the administration must provide or help secure assistance; what rights belong to the person even when represented; and how the system avoids treating “you could have brought a lawyer” as an excuse for unreadable, coercive, or over-technical procedure.

## Necessity table

| Necessity test | What counts as proof | Honest design consequence | Default answer if proof is weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Need to let people avoid facing power alone** | materially adverse procedure can proceed against unrepresented people who cannot realistically navigate it | guarantee accompaniment, advice, representation, or support-person presence unless specific disruption is shown | treat solitude as the neutral baseline |
| **Need to distinguish full representation from lighter forms of assistance** | some people need a lawyer, others need an accredited nonlawyer, navigator, union rep, or trusted support person | type assistance lanes by stakes, task, and competence required | either lawyer-only gatekeeping or ungoverned informal helpers |
| **Need to preserve the person’s agency even when represented** | representatives can overshadow, settle, waive, or concede against the person’s real understanding or wishes | keep key rights with the affected person unless explicit authority says otherwise | treat the representative as owning the person’s case |
| **Need to verify authority without weaponizing paperwork** | administrations use rigid power-of-attorney rituals to delay or exclude legitimate helpers | require proportionate authorization methods and cure defects quickly | turn authority checks into representation denial |
| **Need to protect confidentiality and conflicts** | helpers may access sensitive files, welfare records, trade secrets, or family information | define duties of confidentiality, conflict checks, and revocation for representatives and accredited assistants | rely on trust alone or over-ban assistance entirely |
| **Need to make assistance real where lawyers are scarce or unaffordable** | rights depend on expensive counsel in high-volume systems with weak legal-aid coverage | permit qualified nonlawyer assistance, accredited advisers, and supported self-representation | let formal rights collapse into professional scarcity |
| **Need to protect vulnerable persons from nominal but unusable choice** | children, persons with disabilities, detained people, migrants, low-literacy users, and others face coercive or confusing procedure | trigger stronger duties to inform, facilitate, or provide assistance | say “representation was allowed” even though access was fictional |
| **Need to prevent support persons becoming covert witness or influence channels** | companions can answer for the person, coach testimony inappropriately, or pressure participation | define when support is silent, when it may confer, and when it may address the authority | leave support roles undefined until conflict erupts |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from a right not to face consequential procedure alone

Where an administrative process may materially affect rights, interests, status, livelihood, benefits, housing, child or family arrangements, immigration position, professional standing, or significant compliance exposure, the person should ordinarily be allowed to be accompanied, advised, represented, or otherwise assisted. The starting presumption should be that administrative power does not become fair merely because it is not labelled criminal or judicial.

### 2. Distinguish four different roles that states too often blur

Ideal systems should separate:

- **counsel or full legal representative**, who may make legal submissions and, where authorized, act on the person’s behalf;
- **qualified nonlawyer representative or accredited adviser**, who may handle defined categories of cases or tasks;
- **ordinary assistant or navigator**, who helps with forms, evidence organization, scheduling, or communication but does not necessarily conduct the whole case;
- **support person**, whose role is primarily stabilizing, interpretive, communicative, or welfare-protective rather than adversarial.

Once these roles are typed, the system can decide sensibly who may speak, who may access files, who may sign, who may settle, and who may simply attend.

### 3. Keep the person’s core procedural rights non-transferable unless authority is explicit

Even where a representative is appointed, certain rights should remain visibly attached to the affected person unless the person expressly authorizes otherwise: to be informed of the proposed adverse action, to receive final decisions, to request accommodation, to change or dismiss representatives, to attend hearings where attendance is lawful, and to receive explanations in comprehensible form. Representation should not quietly erase personhood.

### 4. Verify authority proportionately and cure defects fast

Authorities may legitimately need to know whether someone is authorized to access a file, speak for another, receive notice, or waive rights. But proof of authority should be proportionate to the act. A simple written authorization, portal delegation, signed attendance form, or identity-confirmed oral confirmation should often suffice. Systems should cure authority defects quickly rather than using them as a soft exclusion device.

### 5. Permit qualified nonlawyer assistance where it expands real access without sacrificing fairness

Many systems cannot deliver fairness through lawyer-only models. Ideal governments should therefore allow accredited advisers, union representatives, clinic advocates, regulated immigration advisers, benefits navigators, or other qualified nonlawyer assistants where the case type, volume, and risk profile justify it. Competence and accountability matter, but monopoly by cost is not a fairness model.

### 6. Trigger stronger assistance duties where stakes or vulnerability make solo procedure unrealistic

Some matters require more than a passive right to bring help. Where deprivation is severe, detention or institutionalization limits autonomy, language or disability barriers are known, the person is a child, the case is unusually technical, or there is acute asymmetry of power, the administration should actively inform the person of representation options and, where law provides, route them toward legal aid, accredited advisers, or other assistance.

### 7. Keep confidentiality and conflict discipline visible

Representatives and assistants who can see files, receive notices, or speak for the person should be subject to clear confidentiality duties. The system should also have a way to manage conflicts: one helper representing adverse interests, employer pressure on worker representatives, repeated capture of vulnerable users by for-profit intermediaries, or “assistance” that is actually exploitative control.

### 8. Define the support-person lane rather than improvising it at the door

Some people do not need formal representation but do need a person beside them. A support person may help the affected person remain calm, understand the process, take notes, organize documents, or request breaks. Ideal systems should make this lane visible and say when the support person may speak, consult privately, or simply remain present. Undefined support roles too often become arbitrary permission by staff mood.

### 9. Preserve direct communication with the person where dignity or comprehension requires it

Even when a representative handles most of the file, the authority should not default to total communication blackout with the affected person in matters where comprehension, consent, or welfare is central. Representation can streamline procedure, but it should not become a reason never to ensure that the person actually understands the state action that binds them.

### 10. Design assistance as part of the procedure constitution, not as off-book benevolence

Representation, nonlawyer help, support persons, legal aid, accommodation, and file access all belong to one fairness architecture. If the system can describe hearing clocks and evidence rules but cannot say who may help the person, how that help is authorized, what it may do, and what happens when assistance is unaffordable or unavailable, it still has procedure without an assistance constitution.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat administrative procedure as presumptively fair just because the person could, in theory, have hired a lawyer.
- Do not make lawyer-only representation the hidden price of usable rights where simpler or accredited assistance would suffice.
- Do not weaponize powers of attorney, notarization, or identity paperwork to exclude trusted helpers from urgent or routine matters.
- Do not let representatives extinguish the person’s own right to notice, explanation, participation, or accommodation unless authority is explicit.
- Do not ban support persons in the name of order when the real effect is intimidation or procedural disorientation.
- Do not allow unqualified assistance to become covert exploitation of vulnerable people through fee extraction, misinformation, or conflict.
- Do not assume that allowing help is enough; in some cases the state must also help people find it.

## Failure modes

- **rights by solo navigation**: the system is formally open but practically usable only by insiders or the well-resourced.
- **credential monopoly**: only expensive professionals can meaningfully participate, even in routine or high-volume matters.
- **assistant invisibility**: nonlawyer help exists in practice but has no lawful status, quality standards, or access rules.
- **authority paperwork trap**: representation is blocked by over-formalized proof demands no ordinary person can meet quickly.
- **representation eclipse**: the representative becomes the case owner and the person disappears from comprehension and consent.
- **support-person arbitrariness**: whether a companion may attend depends on staff discretion, office culture, or intimidation value.
- **capture by helper**: the supposed assistant has undisclosed conflicts, exploitative fees, or pressure leverage over the person.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can tell you **that people may appeal, be heard, and access the file**, but cannot also show **who may accompany, advise, represent, or support them; when nonlawyer assistance is lawful; how authority and confidentiality are managed; what happens when vulnerability or complexity makes solo navigation unrealistic; and which rights remain visibly attached to the person even when represented**, then it still has **administrative procedure without an assistance constitution**.
