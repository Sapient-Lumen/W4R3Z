# 828 — Mandate-exit defaults for ideal governments of each scope: local recall, municipal dismissal, confidence loss, bounded dissolution, and treaty lapse

## One-line thesis

The archive should answer the question **how should governing mandates end when they fail, expire early, or lose their public basis?** with scope-matched exit defaults rather than one romantic removal myth: simple recall or replacement for tiny steward bodies, council-centered dismissal and tightly bounded mayoral removal at municipal scale, explicit confidence-loss and successor grammar for metropolitan and regional governments with real power, bounded parliamentary exit and dissolution rules for most national governments, and fixed reauthorization, for-cause removal, or treaty-lapse rules for plurilateral and global bodies that must never drift into ambient permanence.

## Why this matters

The archive already has a stronger answer to **what each scope is for** (`790`), **which regime form usually fits it** (`825`), **from where authority should draw its mandate** (`826`), **how long that mandate should usually last** (`827`), **how governments are formed and removed in full constitutional detail** (`752`), **how vacancies and acting office should be contained** (`761`), and **how caretaker restraint should work between verdict and handover** (`763`). What it still lacked was one compact answer-key note for the narrower exit question:

**once a mandate is live, what is the ordinary way it should end when confidence breaks, legality collapses, or the polity needs a successor before the natural term runs out?**

That missing layer matters because constitutions often fail less by choosing the wrong election than by leaving the death of authority underdesigned. Tiny local bodies become miniature presidencies because nobody wrote a simple replacement rule. Cities elect mayors or appoint managers without deciding whether the decisive removal lane is political, legal, or plebiscitary. Metropolitan bodies accumulate real power but remain governed through permanent bargaining among incumbents because no public confidence-loss rule exists. National constitutions announce no-confidence, dissolution, impeachment, and caretaker powers, but let them overlap so badly that each crisis becomes a fight over who may narrate the constitution. Plurilateral and global bodies speak the language of co-ordination while quietly surviving on extension, rollover, and procedural forgetfulness.

The archive should therefore add one doctrine of **mandate-exit fit**. Exit routes should track the intimacy of the office, the kind of authority being exercised, the cost of delay, the danger of personal rule, and the need to preserve continuity without letting continuity become a shadow constitution.

## Scope table

| Scope | Default exit instinct | Why this fit is usually best | What it must not become |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Micro-local** | simple recall, assembly replacement, or rotation end | keeps tiny steward authority intimate, reversible, and non-theatrical | entrenched neighborhood mini-sovereignty |
| **Municipal** | council dismissal of manager; tightly bounded recall or impeachment only for directly elected executive office where law allows | protects democratic direction while keeping service continuity visible | mayoral or committee drift with no clean stopping rule |
| **Metropolitan** | explicit loss-of-confidence, removal, or term-end grammar with stated successor and caretaker lanes | prevents real metro power from surviving on permanent summit bargaining | functional government without a public exit constitution |
| **Regional / provincial / state** | assembly-confidence exit as default; bounded dissolution; direct executive removal only where the constitution positively justifies it | preserves governability while keeping democratic correction legible | decorative federalism masking insulated regional strongmen |
| **National** | no-confidence or successor-first exit in parliamentary default systems; impeachment for legal wrongdoing; dissolution only as a bounded deadlock breaker | prevents both executive entrenchment and snap-election blackmail | dual-command collision repaired by improvisation |
| **Continental / plurilateral / global** | fixed expiry, reauthorization lapse, member recall where seats are delegated, and for-cause removal for officeholders under treaty rules | matches delegated authority to explicit review and handback | treaty permanence by procedural neglect |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate expiry, political removal, legal removal, and continuity bridge as different constitutional states

The archive should not let every exit question collapse into the lazy phrase **“removal from office.”** Ideal governments should distinguish at least four different things:

- **ordinary expiry** at the natural end of term;
- **political exit** because confidence or support has been withdrawn;
- **legal removal** because law-breaking, incapacity, or disqualification has been established;
- and **continuity bridge** rules that keep services lawful while a successor is chosen.

Those states solve different problems and should not be smuggled into one another. A constitution that treats them as interchangeable will eventually use legality to disguise politics, politics to bypass legality, or acting office to postpone both.

### 2. Keep micro-local exits simple, local, and non-judicialized

At neighborhood, village, block, or ward scale, the archive should usually prefer simple exit routes.

These bodies exist primarily for voice, witnessing, upkeep, tiny discretionary priorities, and fast escalation. Their authority should therefore be easy to reverse. Assembly replacement, simple-majority recall where lawful, rotation end, or clear resignation-and-replacement rules are usually more honest than elaborate impeachment theater.

The archive should therefore distrust any micro-local design that requires a quasi-national drama to replace a tiny steward body. If a neighborhood chair can survive only by invoking thick legality, judicialized procedure, or symbolic personal mandate, the office is already pretending to be a larger sovereign than it is.

### 3. At municipal scale, distinguish executive removal from representative free mandate

Municipal exit rules need more care. A city must protect democratic direction, continuous service delivery, and representative integrity at the same time.

The archive should therefore draw a bright line:

- where the municipality is **council-manager** in substance, the decisive political exit lane should usually be **council dismissal of the manager** plus clear continuity through clerk, deputy, or interim administrator;
- where the municipality has a **directly elected executive mayor**, the archive should usually reserve mid-term removal for tightly bounded **recall or impeachment / judicial-removal lanes** rather than for casual committee intrigue;
- and for **ordinary councillors or assembly members**, the archive should stay wary of converting the free representative mandate into an imperative, constantly revocable instruction sheet.

This matters because local democracy can be damaged from both directions: by over-insulated mayors who behave like mini-presidents, and by hyper-plebiscitary recall cultures that make ordinary governing impossible.

### 4. Metropolitan and regional governments with real power need explicit confidence-loss grammar

Once a metropolitan or regional body can tax, plan, bind land use, allocate housing obligations, sequence transport and utilities, or otherwise exercise real shared territorial power, the archive should insist that it possess a public exit constitution.

That means the archive should be able to say:

- who can withdraw confidence;
- whether replacement must be simultaneous or may follow after a short interval;
- whether dissolution exists and under what bounds;
- who holds caretaker authority meanwhile;
- and how strategic systems continue during the interregnum.

Without that grammar, real metro or regional power will survive on intergovernmental bargaining among incumbents rather than on a public rule of replacement. The body will then have constitutional weight without constitutional honesty.

### 5. National default: use confidence exit first, not executive cliff edges

Because `825` already treats confidence government as the archive’s ordinary national default, this note should compress the corresponding exit rule.

For most large plural national polities, the archive should prefer:

- ordinary **loss of confidence** as the political exit lane for governments;
- **successor-first** or constructive mechanisms where fragmentation makes negative majorities easy to assemble;
- **impeachment or equivalent legal-removal procedures** for grave legal wrongdoing rather than as routine policy disagreement tools;
- and **dissolution** only as a bounded deadlock breaker, not as a standing prime-ministerial threat.

The point is not to make governments fragile. It is to prevent the opposite danger: executives who can treat parliament’s formal confidence power as hollow because dissolution threats, caretaker abuse, or acting-office drift let them remain in command anyway.

### 6. Treat dissolution as a snap brake, not a private weapon

The archive should be especially careful about dissolution. Dissolution is sometimes necessary. It can break government-formation deadlock, return a conflict to the electorate, or clear a genuinely blocked chamber-executive relation. But an unlimited dissolution power distorts the whole confidence chain.

The archive should therefore prefer dissolution rules with visible bounds:

- stated triggers or decision paths;
- time barriers near fresh elections and near the natural end of term;
- limits on who may request, authorize, or refuse dissolution;
- and caretaker restraints strong enough to stop incumbents from turning an election call into a mandate-stretching power grab.

A constitution that says parliament can remove a government but also lets that government routinely threaten instant dissolution has not really solved the exit question. It has merely made removal politically expensive.

### 7. Fixed-term personal executives need narrower, more legalized exit routes than confidence systems

Where a constitution deliberately adopts a directly elected president, governor, or mayor with fixed term, the archive should not fake a parliamentary exit constitution. The political and legal lanes must be distinguished more sharply.

That usually means:

- no routine ordinary-majority legislative dismissal for policy disagreement;
- no easy plebiscitary recall unless the polity can show a strong positive case and high thresholds;
- and legal-removal routes tied to law-breaking, incapacity, or constitutional breach rather than to generalized dislike.

The archive should also be wary of personal executives who pair fixed terms with weak succession rules or overly elastic emergency claims. The combination of hard-to-remove authority and vague crisis power is one of the cleanest roads to entrenchment.

### 8. Continental, plurilateral, and global bodies should die by mandate lapse if political owners do not renew them

Upper-tier delegated institutions need the clearest anti-drift rule of all.

Where authority is delegated through treaty, charter, compact, or member-state mandate rather than through a unitary demos, the archive should generally prefer:

- **fixed office terms** for officeholders;
- **member recall** only where a seat is openly delegated rather than personally representative;
- **for-cause removal** for judges, commissioners, and secretariat heads where independence matters;
- and, above all, **explicit reauthorization, review-conference, or mandate-lapse rules** so functions do not live forever by force of administrative habit.

A narrow-waist institution that cannot explain how its authority ends will almost always widen by sentiment, security panic, or bureaucratic self-preservation.

### 9. Tie exit design tightly to acting-office and caretaker restraint

An exit rule is only honest if the bridge after exit is honest. The archive should therefore keep this note tied to `761` and `763`.

Whenever a constitution specifies who can fall, it should also specify:

- who acts;
- for how long;
- under what narrowed powers;
- which appointments, contracts, or structural changes are frozen;
- and what public reporting proves the bridge has not become a shadow government.

Many constitutions do not fail because they lack a removal clause. They fail because the acting and caretaker lane after removal quietly becomes the real government.

### 10. Publish exit state as constitutional information, not elite process trivia

The public should be able to see whether a government is in full mandate, caretaker status, acting status, under a pending confidence vote, under impeachment process, or approaching treaty lapse or reauthorization.

The archive should therefore treat exit-state information as a public constitutional surface. Otherwise insiders can use opacity to convert temporary authority into normal authority, or pending loss of mandate into a fog of “business as usual.”

### 11. Treat repeated emergency exits, chronic recall attempts, or serial extensions as design failure

If a polity repeatedly experiences:

- constant recall campaigns,
- serial dissolutions,
- chronic caretaker governments,
- frequent acting-office rollovers,
- or endless treaty or mission extensions without serious review,

then the problem is no longer merely political bad luck. The exit constitution itself is misfit. The archive should treat repeated exit-path stress as a repair trigger calling for institutional redesign, not as ordinary background drama.

## Guardrails

- Do not let tiny steward bodies acquire removal drama designed for sovereign states.
- Do not let municipalities blur representative free mandate and executive removal into one permanent plebiscite.
- Do not let metropolitan or regional bodies hold real shared power without a visible confidence-loss and continuity grammar.
- Do not let dissolution become a routine intimidation device.
- Do not let impeachment absorb ordinary politics or recall absorb ordinary disappointment.
- Do not let acting or caretaker status become a substitute constitution.
- Do not let treaty or compact institutions survive indefinitely by extension, rollover, or forgotten review.

## Failure modes

- **mini-presidential localism** — tiny offices become symbolically over-insulated and hard to replace.
- **municipal imperative-mandate drift** — councillors are treated as permanently recallable delegates while executive power remains underdesigned.
- **metro summit perpetuity** — real metropolitan authority survives on bargaining among incumbents because no public exit rule exists.
- **regional strongman borrowing** — a regional tier copies presidential insulation without a compelling federal or territorial reason.
- **snap-election blackmail** — executives neutralize no-confidence through broad dissolution threats.
- **legal-politics blur** — impeachment or judicial removal becomes a disguised confidence vote.
- **permanent caretakerism** — removal exists on paper but continuity bridges quietly become the real government.
- **treaty immortality** — upper-tier institutions persist because nobody wrote a credible lapse, renewal, or handback rule.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **who currently governs** but cannot also say **how that authority normally ends, who may end it, what kind of exit this is (expiry, political loss, legal removal, or continuity bridge), what caretaker or acting limits follow, and what happens if renewal never comes**, then it still has **authority talk without an exit constitution**.
