# 870 — Symbolic authority and mandate-capacity mismatch dockets: formal bodies, real levers, transition receipts, and no accountability by mission statement

## One-line thesis

A body becomes symbolically overloaded when it is publicly blamed, praised, funded, or reorganized as if it owns an outcome, while the legal powers, money, land, staff, data, contracts, operational discretion, and remedy routes needed to govern that outcome are split elsewhere; the archive should therefore test formal authority against operative levers before calling a body a government, a failure, a fix, or a thing ready to abolish.

## Why this matters

The archive now has strong tests for cross-boundary formation, waist capture, supplier dependency, plural-order authority, soft-law hardening, database alerts, and municipal handback. The next failure mode is more ordinary and more dangerous: a board, authority, partnership, commission, hub, or secretariat exists; it has meetings, a mission, dashboards, plans, an executive director, public frustration, and sometimes a large budget; yet the practical levers for the social problem it is said to solve sit across other departments, elected offices, grant conditions, property markets, courts, police, health systems, nonprofit providers, state law, and private operators.

That body is not automatically fake. It may be the right connector, grant administrator, data home, provider-contracting waist, or temporary backbone. But it is not a general-purpose government just because the public problem is visible. It is also not a fair accountability target if the archive cannot separate what the body controls from what it merely coordinates, requests, reports, funds, or inherits.

The symbolic-authority docket exists to prevent two bad readings at once. The first bad reading is **mission-statement government**: the body says it leads a system, so readers assume it owns the whole outcome. The second bad reading is **failure by headline**: the public problem persists, so readers assume the body failed even where the live levers are held by others. A serious reconstruction must distinguish mission, mandate, means, record, recourse, and transition.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start with the public-problem owner map

Before praising or condemning an authority, list the practical owners of the problem.

| Lever | Question | Symbolic-overload signal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| legal duty | who is legally required to act? | a coordinating body is blamed for a duty held by a county, city, ministry, court, or state |
| money | who appropriates, conditions, advances, withholds, or reallocates funds? | the body administers grants but cannot set the funding architecture |
| land / assets | who controls land, facilities, rights-of-way, buildings, shelters, clinics, or infrastructure? | the body is blamed for supply it cannot site, build, license, or acquire |
| workforce | who hires, disciplines, transfers, and directs staff? | the body convenes providers but cannot command field capacity |
| contracts | who writes, monitors, pays, amends, terminates, and enforces provider contracts? | contracting exists but is not tied to performance, data quality, or service verification |
| data | who owns the record system, data standards, data correction, and public dashboards? | the body reports numbers it cannot validate or that providers do not enter reliably |
| operating discretion | who decides referrals, eligibility, prioritization, dispatch, placement, inspection, or enforcement? | the body is called the leader while daily decisions occur elsewhere |
| remedies | who can hear complaints, correct records, order repair, recover funds, or compensate harm? | affected people have a help desk but not a reconstructable public route |
| transition | who can abolish, shrink, recharter, absorb, or replace the body? | reform is announced without a migration receipt for records, contracts, staff, and obligations |

The docket should not move to a form verdict until this map is complete.

### 2. Distinguish six authority states

A symbolic-authority review should classify each function separately.

| State | Meaning | Appropriate holding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| direct authority | body can decide, spend, command, enforce, or deliver | judge performance against duty and record |
| delegated authority | body acts for a principal under a charter, grant, or agreement | judge by delegated scope and principal oversight |
| coordinating authority | body convenes, aligns, reports, and routinizes shared action | do not treat coordination as command |
| contracting waist | body funds or contracts providers but does not itself deliver all services | test contract monitoring, payment, verification, and provider capacity |
| data / observatory waist | body collects, publishes, or validates system facts | test data quality, correction, dashboard limits, and public interpretation |
| symbolic forum | body absorbs public attention while key levers are elsewhere | downshift, recharter, or publish a no-blame / no-credit boundary |

Most real bodies occupy more than one state. The archive should not collapse them.

### 3. Run the mandate-capacity table

| Test | Pass evidence | Repair if failed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| mandate text | charter, statute, agreement, settlement, grant, or board act names the function | rewrite public-facing claims so they do not exceed the legal mandate |
| capacity proof | staff, systems, contracts, money, and authority match the named function | narrow the claim, add resources, or move the function to a body that owns the levers |
| data proof | source system and validation route can reconstruct the outcome claim | block performance claims until record and data-quality defects are repaired |
| contract proof | payments, deliverables, monitoring, corrective action, and closeout can be traced | pause expansion or reprocurement until provider-accountability files are complete |
| affected-party route | user, resident, provider, worker, and taxpayer complaints go to a real remedy path | add a public complaint / appeal / correction map before claiming accountability |
| political-owner proof | elected or legally responsible principals are named for choices beyond the body | stop hiding principal decisions behind the technical body |
| transition proof | abolitions, withdrawals, integrations, or restructures have transfer receipts | do not treat reorganization as reform until records, staff, funds, and obligations migrate cleanly |

### 4. Separate outcome failure from governance failure

A body can be surrounded by catastrophe without being the sole cause of it. A body can also have genuine internal governance defects even when the larger public problem has causes outside its mandate.

The docket should therefore split diagnosis into three lanes.

| Lane | Question | Example finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| structural insufficiency | did the body ever have the powers needed to solve the whole problem? | no; it was a coordinator and grant / contract waist, not a housing-production government |
| internal failure | did the body fail at the functions it actually owned? | yes; contract monitoring, payment, records, data quality, or dashboard discipline failed |
| external mismatch | did principals ask the body to absorb blame for choices elsewhere? | yes; elected, fiscal, land-use, health, legal, or market levers remained outside the body |

A responsible holding can find all three at once.

### 5. Treat reorganization as a new case, not automatic repair

A transition from an authority to a department, from a joint body to a single-principal body, or from a shared waist to direct contracting may be justified. It may also move opacity rather than cure it.

Every reorganization should carry a **transition receipt**:

| Receipt field | Required content |
| --- | --- |
| transferred functions | what moves, what stays, and what ends |
| records | where files, grants, contracts, dashboards, and audit trails go |
| people | which staff transfer, which roles dissolve, and what rights attach |
| money | which funds, advances, liabilities, reserves, and grant conditions move |
| providers | how contracts, invoices, monitoring, and corrective actions migrate |
| users | how affected people keep service continuity and complaint routes |
| dashboards | how metrics stay comparable across old and new structures |
| principals | which elected or legal actor now owns each decision |
| sunset | when the old body, residual body, or transition office ends |

Without that receipt, reorganization is only a press release with an org chart.

### 6. Publish a no-blame / no-credit boundary

When a body is only one lane in a larger system, public communication should say what it cannot honestly claim. The boundary should name:

- outcomes the body directly controls;
- outcomes it influences but does not control;
- outcomes held by other principals;
- indicators that are systemwide but not body-owned;
- missing data that prevents performance claims;
- claims that must be reviewed before budget, abolition, renewal, or transfer decisions.

A no-blame / no-credit boundary is not defensive bureaucracy. It is public epistemic hygiene.

## Failure modes

- **mission-statement government**: a coordination body is treated as if its mission statement gives it command over land, money, health, police, courts, providers, or housing supply.
- **headline failure**: persistence of the public problem is used as proof that the body failed, without separating direct authority from external levers.
- **dashboard sovereignty**: the body owns a public dashboard or count, so the dashboard becomes the apparent state of the system even when source data are incomplete, stale, or provider-entered.
- **grant-administrator scapegoat**: elected principals delegate funding mechanics and then blame the administrator for policy choices that remain with the principals.
- **contract fog**: service delivery is performed by providers, but payments, deliverables, corrective action, and verification are too weak to reconstruct performance.
- **reorganization halo**: creating a new department, office, authority, or hub is treated as accountability before transfer receipts, continuity, data, and remedy routes are proven.
- **abolition theater**: a weak body is dissolved, but its functions, records, liabilities, and users move into a less visible place.
- **no-owner crisis**: everyone calls the issue cross-cutting, so no one publishes a principal-by-principal decision map.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Can a resident tell which public actor owns the next concrete decision?
2. Does the body have the legal power to command the outcome for which it is being praised or blamed?
3. Are money, land, workforce, data, contracts, and remedies mapped separately?
4. Can payments be tied to deliverables and service verification?
5. Can system counts and dashboards be replayed from source records?
6. Can an affected person correct a record or challenge a service failure without falling into provider-private limbo?
7. Does public communication distinguish coordination, administration, contracting, delivery, and political choice?
8. Are elected principals prevented from hiding behind the joint body when the body lacks their powers?
9. Does a proposed reorganization include transfer receipts for records, staff, contracts, funds, users, liabilities, and dashboards?
10. Has the archive separated structural insufficiency, internal failure, and external mismatch before issuing a form holding?

## Reconstruction instruction

Add symbolic-authority review to the `861` negative-control family and the `860` manual. Use this note when a public body is accused of failing, credited with success, reorganized, absorbed, or abolished despite obvious mismatch between its formal mission and its operative levers. It should be especially active for homelessness, housing, emergency coordination, public-health delivery, regional economic development, climate partnerships, anti-poverty systems, and other problems where many actors can point at one visible waist.
