# 869 — Applied municipal handback case packet for Detroit Financial Review Commission: post-bankruptcy waiver, reactivation clock, and no local democracy by dormant receivership

## One-line thesis

The Detroit Financial Review Commission case is best classified as a decreased-oversight municipal distress handback monitor: active state approval powers have been waived after repeated fiscal certifications, but the public-law form remains a live waiver / reactivation / eventual-release waist rather than either permanent receivership or ordinary local self-government with no residue.

## Why this matters

The archive needed an applied handback case. Previous case packets mostly tested how to build cross-boundary forms. This packet tests how to stand one down without pretending the intervention has disappeared before the legal and fiscal residues are closed.

Detroit is useful because the case has multiple layers at once: bankruptcy exit, state-created fiscal review commission, budget and contract approval powers, annual waivers of active oversight, local elected budget processes, pension and retiree-benefit residue, contract-publication duties, public meetings, and an explicit future release story. It therefore tests `861` and `868` better than a clean success story would. The case forces the archive to ask: what counts as handback when a commission is still meeting, reporting remains mandatory, reactivation remains possible, and final release depends on future conditions?

The packet's answer is deliberately narrow. It does not say Detroit's recovery is complete, nor that state oversight was democratically harmless, nor that fiscal control boards are generally legitimate. It says the honest current form is a **decreased-oversight municipal distress handback monitor** whose legitimacy depends on transparent waiver conditions, fiscal evidence, democratic-damage memory, reactivation clarity, local-capacity proof, and a final release path.

### 2. Form verdict

The honest form is **decreased-oversight municipal distress handback monitor**.

That form is thinner than emergency receivership or active control, because ordinary city budget and management choices have returned to elected local institutions subject to continuing fiscal discipline. It is thicker than ordinary self-government, because the commission remains a public-law actor, meeting and monitoring, with waiver conditions and reactivation stakes still live.

The form is acceptable only if its public file keeps four distinctions visible:

1. **active oversight** versus **decreased oversight**;
2. **waiver** versus **final release**;
3. **balanced-budget certification** versus **service and capacity recovery**;
4. **local budget authority** versus **residual bankruptcy, pension, debt, and statutory conditions**.

### 7. Source-currentness warning

The packet must be read by year. A 2018 waiver, a 2024 waiver packet, a 2025 waiver resolution, and 2026 meeting minutes do not say the same thing. The source-currentness class is therefore **implementation clock / annual waiver clock**. Any claim about current status should cite the latest FRC meeting page, latest waiver resolution, latest city financial report, and any current court / plan-of-adjustment status.

### 8. Holding

The Detroit FRC case should be cited for **handback discipline**, not as a general model of fiscal emergency governance. Its reusable lessons are:

- handback can be staged;
- waiver is not dissolution;
- annual monitoring can be legitimate only if reactivation and release rules are visible;
- fiscal recovery should not be allowed to erase democratic damage;
- balanced budgets do not automatically prove service, capacity, or resident recovery;
- local autonomy after distress must carry debt, pension, contract, and record residues in public view.

### 9. Repair surfaces

| Surface | Required repair |
| --- | --- |
| waiver file | publish annual resolution, compliance exhibit, continuing duties, and reactivation conditions |
| final-release file | publish statutory condition map, remaining waivers, plan-of-adjustment residue, and expected release date if conditions hold |
| pension file | isolate pension / retiree-benefit cliffs from general balanced-budget celebration |
| budget file | connect local council process, mayoral proposal, four-year plan, and FRC review calendar |
| contract file | preserve public contract posting and commission approval / waiver history |
| service file | supplement fiscal balance with resident-facing service and infrastructure indicators |

| opposition file | include residents, retirees, workers, creditors, local elected officials, state officials, and service users |

## Failure modes

- **dormant receivership theater**: active oversight is waived, but the public cannot tell what state powers remain, what would revive them, or when the commission exits.
- **full-local-control theater**: officials describe restored democracy without acknowledging waiver, reporting, plan, pension, debt, and reactivation residues.
- **balanced-budget halo**: fiscal certifications are allowed to stand in for resident service recovery, neighborhood investment, infrastructure, and administrative capacity.
- **pension-cliff delay**: legacy obligations are treated as solved because short-term budgets balance.
- **meeting-minutes minimalism**: public minutes exist but do not carry enough analysis for residents to understand why waiver or release is justified.
- **reactivation ambiguity**: the return of active oversight depends on broad discretion rather than objective triggers.
- **historical amnesia**: democratic displacement during bankruptcy / emergency periods is forgotten once the city produces clean fiscal documents.
- **release-date fantasy**: projected release is presented as guaranteed even though future waivers, plan conditions, and court residues remain live.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Can a resident distinguish active oversight, decreased oversight, waiver, final release, and dissolution?
2. Does the latest packet name the statutory or plan conditions that remain before final release?
3. Are the commission's current powers and the city's current local powers both listed?
4. Are waiver conditions tied to evidence rather than generalized recovery rhetoric?
5. Are pension, debt, and plan-of-adjustment residues separated from annual balanced-budget claims?
6. Does the public record show what would reactivate active oversight?
7. Are city council budget hearings and commission review calendars connected so residents can see where decisions are actually made?
8. Does the packet preserve democratic-damage memory while recognizing genuine fiscal repair?
9. Is service recovery tested separately from fiscal recovery?
10. Does the archive identify what evidence would justify release, reactivation, or a finding of permanent-capture risk?
