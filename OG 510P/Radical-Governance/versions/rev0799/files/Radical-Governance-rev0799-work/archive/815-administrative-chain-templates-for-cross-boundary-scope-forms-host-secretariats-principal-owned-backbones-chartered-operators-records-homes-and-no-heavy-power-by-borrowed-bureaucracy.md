# 815 — Administrative-chain templates for cross-boundary scope forms: host secretariats, principal-owned backbones, chartered operators, records homes, and no heavy power by borrowed bureaucracy

## One-line thesis

Once the archive has said **which cross-boundary institutional form is honest**, **what legitimacy chain belongs to it**, and **what fiscal chain belongs to it**, it should also be able to say **what administrative chain belongs to it**: thin connectors should use small secretariats with no hidden command over member administrations, shared-service backbones should keep declared principals, employer-of-record rules, records homes, and service agreements, bounded functional authorities should have chartered operating cores with explicit staffing, asset, procurement, and incident-command authority, and general-purpose territorial tiers should normally own their own civil-service, records, and operating spine rather than ruling through borrowed host bureaucracy.

## Why this matters

`790` says what the default stack is. `810` says how to assemble threshold proof before saying a metropolitan, county-pooling, regional-platform, fiscal-floor, or legitimacy-thickness line is really crossed. `812` says which institutional form is honest once the burden is real. `813` says who should sit in the room and which decisions require which legitimacy key. `814` says what money constitution belongs to that form. `816` says what oversight chain belongs to that form once the question becomes who scrutinizes, audits, investigates maladministration, or hears review routes. `818` says what information chain belongs to that form once the question becomes where common indicators, observatories, publication lanes, and shared facts should live. `822` says what asset-and-liability chain belongs to that form once the question becomes who owns the durable plant, books reserves, or takes the handback on exit.

What the recent scope stack still lacked was one compact answer to the next question:

**where does the real administrative spine live?**

That gap matters because many constitutional failures are not failures of abstract form alone. They are failures of **borrowed bureaucracy**.

One failure is **host drift**: a connector or pooled backbone is said to be joint, but one host municipality quietly becomes the real employer, operator, and records sovereign for everyone else.

Another failure is **shell authority**: a bounded authority or metropolitan body is given public tasks, money, and controversy, but has no direct staffing, procurement, incident-command, or records spine of its own and must beg line agencies or member governments for every serious move.

A third failure is **double-shadow administration**: the public is told one body is accountable, while another body actually holds the staff, systems, files, continuity plans, and operational keys that determine what happens in practice.

Official material supports a more disciplined administrative ladder. The Council of Europe’s Charter on local self-government says local authorities should be able to determine internal administrative structures adapted to local needs and that conditions of service should allow recruitment of high-quality staff on merit and competence. OECD work on inter-municipal shared services emphasizes clear legal forms and agreements covering functions, responsibilities, structure, cost-sharing, duration, termination, and reporting, and it warns that host-municipality models create distinctive risks. OECD metropolitan work also shows that metropolitan governance bodies vary materially in legal status, powers, budgets, and staff rather than sharing one standard operating shell. World Bank metropolitan-governance work likewise treats administrative design as model-sensitive and warns by implication against pretending that advisory forums, indirectly composed bodies, and real operating authorities all carry the same administrative burden. The archive should compress those lessons into one rule: **match administrative thickness to the real operating burden, but stop relying on borrowed host machinery once power, continuity, or consequence have become substantial.**

## Pattern pack

### 1. Use the administrative-chain note after form, legitimacy, and fiscal questions are separated

This note does not decide whether a threshold is crossed.

Use `810` for that.

This note does not decide whether the honest form is a thin connector, shared-service backbone, bounded authority, or general-purpose territorial tier.

Use `812` for that.

This note does not decide who should sit in the room or which decisions require an ordinary board, ratification, or second key.

Use `813` for that.

This note does not decide what revenue base, equalization rule, reserve, or capital lane belongs to the body.

Use `814` for that.

This note applies after those questions are separated and the remaining live issue is:

- who employs the staff,
- who owns the systems and service contracts,
- where the public record and case home sit,
- who carries continuity and incident command,
- and when reliance on a host or parent administration is still honest.

Where the unresolved issue is not who operates the machinery but where the shared facts, observatories, dashboards, method notes, and publication discipline should live, use `818` instead of stretching the administrative-chain note beyond its real job.

### 2. Distinguish four recurrent administrative chains before inventing hybrids

The archive should usually begin with four recognizable administrative chains.

| Form | Default administrative chain | What it may legitimately borrow | What it must not hide |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Thin connector** | small secretariat, limited programme office, or rotating support cell | meeting support, analysis, joint drafting, temporary secondees | undeclared command over member administrations or hidden file sovereignty |
| **Shared-service backbone** | pooled operating center serving declared principals under service agreements | host payroll, facilities, procurement, or IT where openly bounded | covert sovereignty transfer to the host or operator |
| **Bounded functional authority** | chartered operator with direct management of the named system | transitional support from parent bodies, targeted shared services | shell authority with no real operating spine |
| **General-purpose territorial tier** | own civil service, treasury interfaces, records home, procurement, and continuity architecture | transitional technical assistance or narrow outsourced support | permanent rule through borrowed bureaucracy |

The archive should not begin with bespoke institutional poetry until one of these four administrative chains has first been ruled out honestly.

### 3. Thin connectors should stay administratively light and visibly non-sovereign

A thin connector is honest when its administrative spine is limited to:

- agenda setting,
- meeting support,
- common analysis,
- shared data or dashboards,
- strategy drafting,
- voluntary project preparation,
- and minor communications work.

It may rely on a host secretariat or rotating support cell, but the archive should require visible answers to four questions:

- who keeps the official records,
- who may commit the connector publicly,
- who may hire or direct staff,
- and how a host handoff occurs without memory loss.

A thin connector becomes administratively dishonest once it starts:

- directing line staff across member governments,
- keeping the only operative records of binding decisions,
- running enduring operational systems,
- or holding the sole continuity plan for a critical public function.

At that point the archive should stop describing it as only a secretariat.

### 4. Shared-service backbones should preserve principal ownership while pooling machinery

A shared-service backbone exists because several governments keep political title to a function while pooling the machinery needed to run it well.

Its administrative chain should therefore begin from one simple premise:

**the principals remain visible even when the machinery is pooled.**

That usually means the archive should require a declared answer on:

- employer of record,
- delegated manager authority,
- service catalogue and service levels,
- records home and case-home routing,
- incident escalation and business continuity,
- procurement signature authority,
- data access and audit trails,
- and entry, exit, and unwind procedures.

The backbone may be hosted, jointly owned, or separately incorporated. What matters is not the label but whether the public can still say who owns the duty, who runs the machinery, and where the operative file really lives.

### 5. Bounded functional authorities need real operators, not decorative headquarters

A bounded functional authority becomes the honest answer when one cross-boundary system needs continuous operations, asset stewardship, technical balancing, or 24/7 continuity.

Its administrative chain should therefore be heavier than a backbone’s.

Typical honest ingredients include:

- direct executive authority over the named system,
- operating staff or contracted operators under a hard charter,
- asset and maintenance responsibility,
- incident command or dispatch discipline where relevant,
- procurement and contract-management authority,
- records systems appropriate to the field,
- and continuity planning that does not disappear if one member government loses interest.

The archive should resist two common evasions here:

- **headquarters theater**, where a body has a board, logo, and budget line but no direct operating grip on the system it supposedly governs;
- **borrowed-command dependence**, where real operations always revert to a host ministry, member city, or state utility whenever the stakes rise.

A bounded authority can use seconded staff or transitional shared services for a time. It cannot remain honest forever if it has no real operator chain of its own.

### 6. General-purpose territorial tiers should normally own a public administrative spine

Once the archive has concluded that the honest answer is a general-purpose territorial tier, it should usually stop searching for permanent administrative shortcuts.

At that point the administrative chain should normally include:

- its own civil-service home,
- permanent records and archiving responsibilities,
- its own procurement and contract-management discipline,
- an intelligible cabinet or executive-department structure where relevant,
- administrative complaint and review routing,
- and continuity arrangements tied to the tier’s own legal identity rather than to a host body’s grace.

Outsourcing, shared corporate services, or delegated implementation may still exist at the margins. But the archive should treat them as supplements, not as the constitutional body itself.

The core rule is simple: **once the politics is truly territorial and ongoing, the operating spine should normally live inside the territory’s own accountable administrative home.**

### 7. Separate five administrative titles even when one body holds several of them

Later archive work should usually ask five distinct questions:

- who is the legal principal,
- who is employer of record,
- who is operator of the service or system,
- where the public record and case home sit,
- and who holds continuity or incident command.

In a thin connector, several of these titles may be tiny or even nil.

In a shared-service backbone, the principal and operator may diverge while the records home remains distributed or function-specific.

In a bounded authority, the operator and continuity titles may be central even where some employment or support services are shared.

In a general-purpose tier, all five titles are usually real and should be named.

The archive should still separate them explicitly. Silence is how borrowed administration hardens into shadow sovereignty.

### 8. Use second-key administrative gates for high-consequence operating moves below the full-tier threshold

`813` already says when a body should not act through an ordinary internal vote alone.

This note gives the operating version of that rule.

Even below the full-tier threshold, the archive should usually require a second administrative key for moves such as:

- transfer of employer-of-record status,
- migration of records home or case home,
- outsourcing of core operations,
- closure or relocation of critical control rooms, depots, or service centers,
- major continuity-plan redesign,
- operator substitution,
- or expansion of the body’s service catalogue beyond the original charter.

A narrow authority or backbone can remain narrow only if its heaviest operating moves do not quietly become ordinary internal housekeeping.

### 9. Publish administrative-upgrade triggers when lighter forms start carrying heavier reality

An administrative chain is not permanent merely because it was once appropriate.

Later archive work should say what would force an upgrade, such as:

- a connector becoming the real co-ordinator of live operations,
- a host secretariat becoming the only place with institutional memory,
- a shared-service backbone accumulating field-level discretion over priorities among principals,
- a bounded authority taking on around-the-clock operations without a stable operator core,
- or a general-purpose tier still depending on another body’s staff and systems for its ordinary life.

When those triggers appear, the archive should stop saying only that the form or budget has matured. It should also say that the **administrative chain** has matured and now requires thicker staffing, records, procurement, continuity, or direct operating capacity.

### 10. Name downshift tests as well

The archive should also guard against permanent administrative over-thickening.

If a crisis operations center has returned to a planning secretariat, if direct operating authority has narrowed back to a service-support backbone, or if a temporary field command has expired, then a heavier administrative chain may no longer be necessary.

Permanent operating bureaucracy should not survive merely because it once solved a harder problem.

### 11. Require one short administrative-chain holding in applied verdicts

When `811` is used for a live case, the archive should normally be able to add one short holding such as:

- **honest current form is a shared-service backbone; municipalities remain principals, the pooled center may operate payroll and procurement under service agreements, and each municipality keeps its own political title plus declared record-routing rules**;
- **honest current form is a bounded transit authority; the authority now needs direct operating staff, contract-management authority, and incident-command continuity rather than continued host-city command by informal secondment**;
- **honest current form is now a metropolitan tier; ordinary administration, records, procurement, and continuity should move into the tier’s own civil-service home rather than remain borrowed from the core city or the state**.

That keeps applied judgments from stopping at the words **authority**, **metro**, or **backbone** without saying where the actual bureaucracy lives. Where the unresolved issue is not the operator chain itself but the independent scrutiny and redress chain that must sit around it, the packet should also carry a short oversight-chain holding under `816`. Where the unresolved issue is instead where common indicators, service telemetry, observatories, and publication discipline should live, the packet should also carry a short information-chain holding under `818`. Where the unresolved issue is instead who owns the durable assets, bears the long-tail liabilities, or takes the handback when the operator changes, the packet should also carry a short asset-and-liability-chain holding under `822`.

## Guardrails

- Do not let a connector’s host secretariat become the undeclared operator of member governments.
- Do not let a shared-service backbone hide who is principal, who is employer of record, and where the file or case home sits.
- Do not let a bounded authority govern a critical system without direct operating grip, continuity planning, and contract-management authority proportionate to the task.
- Do not let a general-purpose territorial tier remain a decorative chamber that rules through borrowed staff, borrowed files, and borrowed procurement.
- Do not migrate records homes, control rooms, or operator status as though they were only technical moves when they change real accountability.
- Do not confuse secondees, consultants, or temporary programme staff with a stable public administrative spine.
- Do not widen operating power without widening the legitimacy and fiscal chains that should accompany it.
- Do not keep crisis administration alive after the underlying operating burden has materially narrowed.

## Failure modes

- **host drift** — a nominally joint arrangement quietly becomes whatever the host administration is willing to carry.
- **shell authority** — a body has public title but no real staff, systems, files, or operating grip.
- **double-shadow administration** — the accountable body and the real operating body are not the same, and the public cannot tell which one governs.
- **record exile** — operative files and records live only inside a host or contractor environment outside the body’s visible constitutional home.
- **secondment state** — an arrangement survives indefinitely on borrowed staff with no stable civil-service or operator chain.
- **incident-command void** — when the system is under stress, nobody can say who is actually in charge.
- **operator substitution by stealth** — outsourcing or host takeover changes the constitutional reality without an explicit second-key decision.
- **bureaucratic overhang after downshift** — temporary heavy operating machinery survives after the need for it has narrowed.
