# 751 — Regional-tier necessity tests: territorial platforms, shared rule, asymmetry, collapse, and no map inheritance as constitution

## One-line thesis

A regional, provincial, state, or similar tier should be treated as **necessary** only when at least one of four conditions is real — a major territorial platform, a federal or plurinational shared-rule bargain, a very large sparse-area catchment, or a durable asymmetry the constitution must recognize — and otherwise the archive should prefer either stronger municipalities, metropolitan government, bounded functional authorities, or honest scope collapse over decorative regionalism.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a dense set of notes on scope allocation, metropolitan government, regions, asymmetry, collapsed stacks, and ideal governments by scope (`580`, `606`, `607`, `661`, `699`, `749`, `750`). But one recurring decision still benefits from a cleaner test:

**when is a regional tier actually needed at all?**

That question matters because many countries inherit provinces, departments, counties, prefectures, governorates, oblasts, regions, or states whose constitutional status is historically thick but functionally mixed. Some are indispensable. Some are merely inherited map furniture. Some should be asymmetric. Some should collapse or become functional overlays rather than remain fake general governments.

Without a necessity test, debates about regions drift toward slogans: pro-decentralization romance, anti-bureaucratic centralism, or uninspected loyalty to inherited borders.

The archive needs a firmer answer.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Ask first whether the region is carrying a real territorial platform

A regional tier is often justified when it governs a platform too large for municipalities and too place-shaped for the national center to run honestly.

Typical examples include:

- hospital and referral systems,
- higher education and research platforms,
- basin and landscape management,
- regional rail or corridor planning,
- large-scale economic development,
- and territorial infrastructure sequencing.

If several of these functions are real and recurrent at one geography, the region may be necessary.

### 2. Treat shared rule as an independent reason for a regional tier

Even if platform work is thinner, a regional tier may still be necessary where constituent territories are part of the constitutional bargain itself.

That includes federations, plurinational settlements, islands with entrenched autonomy, indigenous or historic territorial compacts, and other cases where self-rule plus shared rule is not just an efficiency choice but a legitimacy requirement.

In such cases, the region is not merely an administrative convenience. It is one of the polity’s democratic homes.

### 3. Treat sparse-area catchment as a third necessity pathway

Large, thinly settled territories often need a regional or provincial tier not because they are politically federal, but because service backbones, transport reach, emergency logistics, ecological management, and technical staffing cannot be governed honestly through small municipalities alone.

The archive should therefore recognize **territorial vastness and service catchment depth** as a serious reason for regions.

### 4. Treat durable asymmetry as a fourth necessity pathway

Sometimes a country does not need uniform regional government everywhere but does need stronger subnational self-government somewhere.

The archive should prefer **asymmetric regionalism** when one or more territories have distinct platform, capacity, language, legal history, island geography, or political compact that makes uniform design dishonest.

A region may therefore be constitutionally necessary in one part of a country and unnecessary in another.

### 5. If none of the four tests is met, do not keep a region merely because the map already has one

A region should not survive by historical inertia alone.

If it has no real platform work, no shared-rule bargain, no sparse-area necessity, and no durable asymmetry to carry, then the archive should presume that one of four other answers is better:

- stronger municipalities,
- a metropolitan tier where the real problem is urban and spillover-heavy,
- bounded functional authorities for basins, corridors, grids, or utilities,
- or honest scope collapse into another tier.

### 6. Judge regions by complete-kit honesty, not by label alone

A region that passes the necessity test should still be checked for kit honesty.

Ask whether it has:

- a decision forum with real authority,
- an executive and civil-service spine,
- a budget and predictable fiscal lane,
- review and redress routes,
- direct civic entry,
- and a coherent public-law identity.

A necessary region without a complete enough kit may still be underconstitutional.

### 7. Prefer collapse over decorative regionalism where the work is not there

If a polity cannot honestly sustain a general regional tier, it should collapse the layer openly rather than maintain ceremonial provinces or departments that mainly rebadge central ministries.

This is especially important in city-states, very small countries, and highly metro-dominant states.

### 8. Prefer functional overlays over fake regions when the footprint is not truly territorial

Sometimes a government keeps or invents a regional tier to solve a problem that is actually basin-shaped, corridor-shaped, grid-shaped, or border-shaped.

In those cases, the archive should usually prefer a bounded functional authority to a pretend territorial general government.

### 9. Force a periodic rereview where regions survive mainly by inheritance

Even when abolishing or redesigning regions is politically difficult, the archive should insist on visible rereview where the region’s necessity case is weak.

The question should not be “does the province still exist?” but “what indispensable work would be lost if it did not?”

### 10. Keep the answer reversible

A region can become more necessary over time as infrastructure, hospitals, corridors, ecological risk, or political settlement change. It can also become less necessary as metros strengthen, functions decouple, or state capacity collapses into other arrangements.

The archive should therefore treat regional necessity as a standing constitutional judgment, not a one-time metaphysical truth.

## Necessity table

| Necessity test | What counts as proof | Default answer if proof is weak |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Territorial platform** | recurrent region-scale hospitals, universities, basins, corridors, landscapes, development sequencing | use municipalities, metros, or functional authorities instead |
| **Shared rule** | federal, plurinational, autonomous, island, or other constitutional self-rule bargain | treat the unit as administrative, not constitutional |
| **Sparse-area catchment** | long distances, thin density, large emergency or service backbones, minimum viable specialist platforms | use county pooling or national backstops rather than fake regions |
| **Durable asymmetry** | one territory genuinely needs more or different powers than others for stable reasons | avoid uniform regionalism by historical reflex |

## Guardrails

- Do not infer regional necessity from inherited boundaries alone.
- Do not abolish a region just because its current design is weak; first ask whether the work is real but the kit is dishonest.
- Do not use the language of regional identity to hide a purely metropolitan or functional problem.
- Do not force uniformity where asymmetry is the more honest constitutional answer.
- Do not keep decorative regional tiers that mainly rebadge central administration.

## Failure modes

- **map inheritance constitutionalism**: old boundaries are treated as self-justifying even after their work has disappeared.
- **anti-regional reflex**: weak current institutions are used to deny real platform or shared-rule needs.
- **uniformity theater**: every part of the country gets the same regional design despite very different geographies and bargains.
- **metro disguise**: a region is maintained mainly to avoid admitting that a city-region needs its own democratic tier.
- **functional miscasting**: basin, grid, corridor, or border problems are forced into a general regional shell they do not fit.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a note says a country **has** or **should have** a regional tier but cannot state which necessity test it passes — territorial platform, shared rule, sparse-area catchment, or durable asymmetry — the regional claim is still too loose for ideal-government design work.
