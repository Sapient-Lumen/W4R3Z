# 725 — Ideal governance stack from block to planet: territorial tiers, functional authorities, compact unions, and no empty-tier fetish

## One-line thesis

An ideal governance stack from block to planet is not a neat ladder of identical states but a mixed constitutional order: micro-local bodies for witness and stewardship, municipalities for ordinary place government, metropolitan and regional tiers where urban systems or territorial platforms are real, nations for equal citizenship and solidarity, continental compacts for recurrent cross-border problems, global institutions for peace, alerting, commons, and interoperability, and bounded functional authorities in between whenever a watershed, corridor, grid, basin, port, or referral network plainly outruns ordinary administrative geography.

## Why this matters

The archive already has a serious territorial ladder for **ideal governments by scope**. What it still needed was one tighter answer to the phrase **and everything in between**.

That phrase matters because real governance almost never fits a perfectly nested picture of neighborhood, city, region, nation, and globe alone. Between those territorial tiers sit many of the systems that actually decide whether life is governable: watersheds, commuter sheds, hospital referral networks, freight corridors, grids, ports, flood systems, housing markets, and cross-border technical regimes.

If the archive does not name those in-between forms clearly, it risks two recurring mistakes.

The first is **empty-tier fetish**: every problem is treated as proof that a new full territorial government should exist.

The second is **workaround constitutionalism**: powerful special-purpose bodies get created to manage real systems, but they remain under-legitimated, under-reviewed, fiscally foggy, and publicly hard to reach because nobody admits they are now exercising public power of constitutional significance.

A better answer is not more layers for their own sake. It is a sharper distinction between:

- **general-purpose territorial governments**, which should exist where everyday democratic life, shared territory, and broad distributive choice genuinely live; and
- **special-purpose functional authorities or treaty regimes**, which should exist where a real system footprint outruns those territories but does not justify cloning a full sovereign stack.

This note compresses that distinction into one reusable scope ladder.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate territorial governments from functional authorities

Not every public problem deserves a new general-purpose tier.

A serious constitution should first ask whether the question is about:

1. a place where people live together across many issues,
2. a functional system whose footprint crosses several places,
3. or a cross-border regime that needs comparable rules rather than one domestic chain of command.

General-purpose governments belong mainly to the first category. Functional authorities and treaty regimes belong mainly to the second and third.

### 2. Keep micro-local bodies close, direct, and light

At the scale of the building, block, village, ward, or neighborhood, the ideal institution is usually a direct, low-cost, high-trust body for witness, upkeep, petition, and escalation.

Its jobs are to surface neglect early, steward tiny commons, provide a direct participation lane, and force higher tiers to see what they would otherwise miss. It may hold a micro-budget or participatory budget, but it should not pretend to be a miniature sovereign state.

### 3. Treat the municipality as the first complete government

The municipality is the first level that should usually carry a full democratic and administrative kit: legislature or council, executive leadership, professional civil service, public budget, public records, complaint routes, and ordinary judicial review under higher law.

This is the scale for streets, sanitation, libraries, permitting, ordinary local safety prevention, park systems, local service counters, and parcel-scale rulemaking. In rural settings, the relevant first complete government may be a county, district, or amalgamated municipality rather than a dense city, but the principle is the same: ordinary place government deserves one visible civic front door.

### 4. Create metropolitan government when the city-region is already real

Where labour markets, transit, housing pressure, freight, trunk infrastructure, flood risk, or air quality already operate at metropolitan scale, the ideal answer is not endless bargaining among municipalities alone.

A city-region should get a metropolitan authority once the urban system is making recurrent regional tradeoffs. That authority needs public-legible legitimacy, a budget spine, planning and implementation capacity, and review lanes proportionate to the power it holds.

If metropolitan government is binding in substance, it should stop pretending to be only co-ordination in form.

### 5. Use functional authorities for systems that do not map cleanly to territory

Some in-between institutions should be neither fake governments nor casual committees. They should be **bounded functional authorities**.

Typical cases include:

- watershed and basin authorities,
- corridor and freight authorities,
- grid and market operators under public law,
- port and airport systems,
- hospital referral and trauma networks,
- flood-control and drainage systems,
- ecosystem or commons boards,
- and interoperable registries or standards bodies.

These bodies should be narrow, typed, and reviewable. They may need technical civil service, inspection power, levy authority, or capital planning, but they should not quietly absorb broad distributive or constitutional powers that belong to a real representative government.

### 6. Upgrade functional authorities once they start making broad territorial bargains

An in-between authority should not stay permanently underconstitutional once it starts doing the work of a real government.

If it repeatedly decides who grows and who bears loss, who gets infrastructure first, how housing burdens are shared, how fiscal transfers move, how land-use conflict is settled, or how emergency burdens are allocated across a territory, then the archive should ask whether it has crossed from functional administration into shared territorial government.

At that point it may need stronger legislature-governance, budget-governance, participation, and judicial or tribunal review than a technical board usually carries.

### 7. Reserve regions for major territorial platforms and durable self-rule

The regional, provincial, state, or similar tier should exist where a larger territorial platform is real.

That includes hospital systems, higher education, basin-scale development, major transport backbones, ecological landscapes, disaster logistics, and constitutionally meaningful self-rule inside a federation or plurinational order. A real region deserves a legislature, executive, civil service depth, fiscal capacity, and public-law identity that fit those tasks.

Regions should not exist only to mirror the national map or to host patronage without platform work.

### 8. Reserve the nation for guarantees, solidarity, and constitutional settlement

The nation remains indispensable, but not because it should run everything. Its ideal role is to guarantee equal citizenship, rights floors, redistribution, social insurance, macro-stability, national public goods, common legal status, and constitutional arbitration across the rest of the stack.

National government is strongest when it acts as guarantor, equalizer, and backstop. It becomes dangerous when it treats every local, metropolitan, regional, or functional problem as proof that central ministries should become the ordinary operator.

### 9. Treat continental bodies as competence-typed compact unions

Between nation and globe, the ideal layer is often a compact union or plurilateral regime rather than a full supranational state.

This scale is useful for movement rules, common markets, energy or transport interconnection, basin treaties, research compacts, industrial standards, financial rails, and selected rights or review guarantees that member states have chosen to share. Such bodies need clear competences, amendment rules, dispute settlement, compliance review, budget discipline, and visible exit or adjustment routes.

They should be strong where the problem is recurrently cross-border and weak where domestic self-government should remain primary.

### 10. Keep the global layer thin, real, and problem-typed

The ideal global layer should remain narrow-waisted but institutionally real.

It is the level for peace and conflict restraint, public-health alerting, selected commons protection, technical interoperability, scientific panels, reporting frameworks, maritime and aviation safety baselines, and other matters that plainly outrun any one state.

Its institutions should usually be assemblies, treaty secretariats, courts, registries, inspection or reporting systems, and specialised agencies. The archive should reject both world-state monoculture and the fantasy that voluntary goodwill alone can govern shared planetary risks.

### 11. Attach every layer to money, review, and participation

Scope arguments become theatrical when they name only where power sits.

Every serious layer — territorial or functional — should answer at least five questions:

1. who decides,
2. who staffs,
3. who pays,
4. who reviews,
5. and how the governed can petition, contest, or influence it.

If an in-between body cannot answer those questions, it is probably exercising public authority through constitutional haze.

### 12. Make upgrade and exit rules explicit

Every added layer should carry one of two disciplines.

If it is a permanent territorial government, it should say why the scope has a durable vocation.

If it is a functional authority or compact regime, it should say its problem class, mandate boundary, review route, fiscal logic, and what would trigger either constitutional upgrade or decommissioning.

That stops provisional workaround bodies from quietly becoming immortal.

## Guardrails

- Do not build a new tier merely because existing tiers are badly coordinated.
- Do not force a special-purpose body to carry broad distributive conflict without matching democratic legitimacy.
- Do not leave a real metropolitan or regional system governed only by intergovernmental bargaining if it already makes binding territorial choices.
- Do not let national ministries absorb ordinary local and metropolitan life by administrative impatience.
- Do not let continental or global institutions widen without a clear problem class, lawful competence, and review path.
- Do not let functional authorities become permanent workaround constitutions without upgrade or exit tests.

## Failure modes

- **empty-tier fetish**: every mismatch produces a new general-purpose territorial government whether or not a real vocation exists.
- **workaround constitutionalism**: powerful in-between authorities govern materially important systems without matching legitimacy, participation, or review.
- **territorial overreach**: a municipality, region, or nation tries to govern a basin, corridor, grid, or metropolitan system as if boundaries and system footprints were the same thing.
- **functional overreach**: a technical body quietly takes on broad territorial distribution, land-use, or rights tradeoffs it was never designed to carry.
- **central defaultism**: the nation claims every unresolved problem because it can move fastest, not because it is the right scope.
- **treaty romanticism**: continental or global co-operation is praised in the abstract without saying who decides, who pays, who reviews, and how member polities can contest drift.
- **administrative geography amnesia**: map lines are treated as timeless truth even when the real service, ecology, or market system plainly runs elsewhere.

## Practical tests

A proposed governance layer is probably honest if the answer to each question is yes:

1. Does this layer solve a problem class that clearly exists at its claimed scope?
2. Is it visibly territorial government, visibly functional authority, or visibly treaty regime rather than a blurred hybrid by convenience?
3. Can it name its decision forum, executive or staffing model, budget logic, review route, and participation channel?
4. If it is special-purpose, are its mandate boundary and upgrade or exit tests explicit?
5. If it makes repeated distributive bargains for a territory, has its legitimacy been upgraded accordingly?
6. If it is global or continental, are its competences narrow enough to remain treaty-bound and reviewable?

## Compression rule for the archive

When future notes ask what the ideal government is **at a given scope or between scopes**, do not default to one cloned state form. First decide whether the problem needs a general-purpose territorial government, a bounded functional authority, or a treaty regime. Then specify the smallest honest institution that can see the problem, finance it, review it, and be reached by the people governed through it.
