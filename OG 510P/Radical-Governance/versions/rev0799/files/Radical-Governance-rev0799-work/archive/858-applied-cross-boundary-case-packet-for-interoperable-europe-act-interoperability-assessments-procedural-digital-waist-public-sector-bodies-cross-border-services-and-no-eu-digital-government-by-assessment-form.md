# 858 — Applied cross-boundary case packet for Interoperable Europe Act interoperability assessments: procedural digital waist, public-sector bodies, cross-border services, and no EU digital government by assessment form

## One-line thesis

The Interoperable Europe Act case should be classified as a procedural digital interoperability waist: strong enough to require assessments before new or substantially modified binding requirements for trans-European digital public services, but too narrow to become an EU digital government, a general software regulator, or a substitute for domestic legal authority.

## Why this matters

The archive needed a digital cross-boundary case that is neither a service-delivery authority, nor an asset-heavy utility, nor a treaty emergency system, nor a coercive inspection regime. The Interoperable Europe Act supplies that test.

Its key move is procedural. Before certain public bodies or Union entities decide on new or substantially modified binding requirements for trans-European digital public services, they must assess interoperability. That is real governance: it changes the design calendar, documentation expectations, cross-border compatibility record, and reuse discipline. But it is not a new general digital ministry. It does not itself operate every service, own every register, decide every benefit, procure every system, or adjudicate every user complaint.

This makes the case useful for the archive because it tests a thin but legally meaningful waist: a cross-border assessment discipline that should improve public-service interoperability without swallowing ordinary public administration.

The case is about a legal assessment obligation and support architecture, not about central operation of all digital public services.

### 2. Trigger test

The dispatcher should activate when all of these are present:

| Trigger | Test |
| --- | --- |
| cross-border public service | the service requires interaction across Member State borders or between Union entities and public bodies |
| binding requirement | a public authority is deciding on a requirement that shapes the digital public service |
| new or substantial modification | the change is not merely routine maintenance |
| interoperability effect | legal, organisational, semantic, or technical choices may impair reuse, data exchange, or cross-border functioning |
| pre-decision timing | assessment happens before the binding requirement is adopted |

If these triggers are absent, the archive should downshift to ordinary domestic digital-service governance, procurement discipline, or internal architecture review.

### 5. Form holding

The honest form is **procedural digital interoperability waist**.

The case supports a binding pre-decision assessment discipline for cross-border digital public-service requirements, plus support tools, guidance, reusable solutions, monitoring, and collaborative governance. It does not support a general EU software approval board, a central procurement monopoly, a universal data model for all public administration, or the replacement of national, regional, or local legal authority over the underlying services.

### 6. Rival interpretations

| Rival rule | Why it is tempting | Why this packet limits it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| purely domestic digital autonomy | protects local and national service design | fails when local choices break cross-border rights, mobility, or administrative cooperation |
| central EU digital ministry | promises uniformity and reuse | overreads a procedural assessment waist into general service government |
| vendor-led interoperability | fast implementation through dominant platforms | risks lock-in, proprietary standards, and public-law opacity |
| standards-only governance | avoids legal bureaucracy | weak when binding requirements create cross-border incompatibility before standards are considered |
| assessment-form compliance | easy to audit | can become paperwork if not tied to design choices, publication, reuse, and correction |
| procurement-first solution | treats interoperability as a buying problem | misses legal, organisational, semantic, and rights effects |

The preferred rule is assessment before binding cross-border digital-service requirements, with visible scope, evidence, reuse, and exit discipline.

### 7. Repair surfaces

| Surface | Required repair |
| --- | --- |
| scope label | identify whether the service is trans-European and why |
| binding-requirement file | name the requirement, decision owner, and adoption date |
| assessment record | preserve interoperability issues, alternatives, mitigation, and residual risk |
| public route | show where assessment information is available |
| reuse ledger | identify reusable solutions, standards, or common specifications considered |
| procurement interface | state whether vendor choices affect interoperability or exit |
| data-correction route | preserve who can correct shared data, identifiers, or service metadata |
| review clock | reopen on substantial modification, guidance change, or service failure |

### 8. Review clock

Reopen this packet when any of the following occurs:

- assessment guidelines, templates, decision trees, or publication rules change;
- a new or substantially modified binding requirement for a major cross-border digital public service is adopted without a visible assessment;
- interoperability regulatory sandboxes materially change how public bodies test new services;
- common specifications or reusable solutions become de facto mandatory;
- procurement frameworks or vendors turn interoperability into lock-in;
- citizens, businesses, or administrations experience denial, delay, duplicate filing, or contradictory records because the assessment waist failed;
- the Commission, Board, or portal practice changes the boundary between support, monitoring, and practical command.

## Failure modes

- **assessment paperwork**: completing a form after design choices are effectively settled.
- **EU-digital-government inflation**: treating a procedural assessment obligation as central control of all digital administration.
- **local autonomy theater**: invoking domestic competence while building systems that break cross-border services.
- **semantic lock-in**: letting one data model or platform become the practical legislature of interoperability.
- **portal sovereignty**: turning the portal or reusable-solution catalogue into an invisible procurement gate.
- **stakeholder erasure**: treating interoperability as an IT issue while citizens and businesses face repeated filing, denial, or inconsistent records.
- **currentness drift**: relying on old assessment guidance after legal clocks, sandboxes, or implementation tools have changed.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Can the file state whether the requirement is in scope and why?
2. Was the interoperability assessment completed before the binding requirement was adopted?
3. Can the public see the assessment record or at least its required public-facing elements?
4. Did the assessment consider legal, organisational, semantic, and technical interoperability rather than only APIs?
5. Can affected users identify where duplicate filing, contradictory data, or cross-border service denial can be challenged?
6. Does the procurement record prevent vendor lock-in from becoming the real interoperability rule?
7. Is there a fresh assessment when the binding requirement is substantially modified?
8. Can the archive explain what the Interoperable Europe Act does not centralize?
