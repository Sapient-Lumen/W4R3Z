# 500 — Political competition by scope: civic lists, durable parties, public finance, spending discipline, and no incumbency self-dealing

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should make political competition open enough for renewal and structured enough for governability: local elections should allow civic lists, independents, and low-friction entry where that fits; larger scales need durable parties that aggregate interests across territory and time; and public finance, spending discipline, timely disclosure, equal treatment, and anti-incumbency rules should keep wealth and state machinery from becoming the real ballot language.

## Why this matters

Representation design is only half the democratic question. The other half is **how political competition is organized and financed**. A beautiful chamber design can still produce oligarchic politics if parties are hollow shells, ballot access is cartelized, campaigns are privately financed arms races, or incumbents quietly convert state resources into campaign equipment.

Different scales also need different competitive forms. Hyper-local politics can tolerate more independent candidacies, neighborhood slates, or civic lists because issues are concrete and party labels carry less of the information load. Large territorial polities need organizations that can recruit candidates, build coalitions, discipline agendas, and translate dispersed public demands into governable programs. The archive therefore needs a scale-matched note on political competition rather than pretending one model of party organization or campaign law fits every layer.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat parties as constitutional intermediaries, not merely private brands

At city, regional, and national scale especially, parties are not just campaign vehicles. They are institutions through which citizens organize, recruit leaders, aggregate preferences, and sustain accountability over time. The archive should therefore reject both extremes: total state suffocation of parties and total refusal to regulate them as if they were ordinary private clubs.

### 2. Fit competitive form to scale

Micro-local and some municipal elections may legitimately leave room for independents, civic lists, ward associations, tribal or village groupings, and low-barrier coalition making. Larger legislatures, federal chambers, and executive contests usually need more durable party structures. The ideal system should not force nationalized party machinery onto every village, nor should it romanticize permanent non-party localism at scales where long-horizon coalition discipline is essential.

### 3. Keep party registration and ballot access fair, stable, and non-cartelizing

Barriers to entry should protect electoral seriousness, not entrench incumbents. Registration, signature, nomination, and reporting rules should be clear and workable. Thresholds may be justified to prevent fragmentation in some systems, but the archive should prefer thresholds and ballot rules that are visible, proportionate, and resistant to manipulation against new entrants or minority representation.

### 4. Use public support and non-cash access tools to reduce oligarchic dependence

Ideal democracies should not force parties and candidates to rely overwhelmingly on large private donors. Reasonable public financing, free or fairly allocated media time where applicable, transparent in-kind support rules, and basic campaign infrastructure access can reduce pay-to-play dynamics while still leaving room for citizen support and organizational competition. The point is not to freeze the party system; it is to prevent plutocratic selection.

### 5. Pair donation and spending rules with timely disclosure

Donation caps, source restrictions, expenditure discipline, and real-time or near-real-time disclosure all address a common problem: unequal political voice translated through money. The archive should prefer political-finance rules that make major support visible before voting ends, distinguish campaign from ordinary party activity clearly enough to regulate both sensibly, and cover third-party intervention where otherwise the rules become a shell game.

### 6. Block incumbents from converting state resources into campaign assets

Public staff, official communications channels, state advertising, public vehicles, procurement favors, and discretionary grants should not become campaign tools. Anti-incumbency safeguards matter at every level, including local and regional elections where misuse of administrative resources can be especially hard to challenge socially. A government should not referee its own election with one hand while campaigning with the other.

### 7. Give oversight bodies real independence, information, and sanction power

Political-finance and party-law enforcement should not depend on voluntary honesty alone. Oversight bodies need audit access, timely filings, investigatory authority proportionate to the task, visible sanctions, and the institutional independence to enforce rules against ruling parties as well as challengers. Underpowered oversight is often worse than no oversight because it launders distrust through a formal facade.

### 8. Use competition rules to widen inclusion without scripting ideology

A good party and election regime should widen the feasible participation of women, minorities, persons with disabilities, youth, and geographically dispersed groups through lawful design tools — such as funding incentives, nomination rules, accessibility duties, or list design where the system uses lists. But the archive should remain wary of party laws that drift from fairness and transparency into ideological licensing by the state.

### 9. Keep local experimentation compatible with common democratic floors

Subnational variation in electoral calendars, local ballot forms, or municipal competition rules may be legitimate. But common democratic floors should still guard equal suffrage, fair administration, lawful campaign conduct, and anti-corruption standards in political funding. Federalism should not become a license for some territories to operate as patronage enclaves.

## Guardrails

- Do not regulate parties so lightly that money quietly becomes the real membership test.
- Do not regulate them so heavily that incumbents can weaponize registration law against challengers.
- Do not let public support become a permanent cartel subsidy with no room for renewal.
- Do not allow campaign disclosure to arrive only after voters have already cast ballots.
- Do not tolerate misuse of administrative resources as merely normal incumbency advantage.

## Failure modes

- **cartel democracy**: ballot access and public funding rules freeze the existing party map.
- **plutocratic competition**: private money becomes the dominant translator of political voice.
- **shadow campaigning**: third parties, unofficial committees, or state-linked actors spend around the formal rules.
- **administrative incumbency**: officeholders quietly turn public resources into campaign infrastructure.
- **hollow parties**: organizations exist for branding and patronage but not for durable membership, aggregation, or accountability.

## Practical tests

A scale-matched competition regime passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does the polity distinguish the competitive forms that make sense at micro-local, municipal, regional, and national scale rather than forcing one template everywhere?
2. Are registration, nomination, and ballot-access rules clear and proportionate enough that challengers can realistically enter?
3. Do public support, disclosure, and spending rules reduce dependence on concentrated wealth without freezing renewal?
4. Are incumbents credibly barred from using state resources, staff, or publicity for partisan advantage?
5. Can the oversight body detect, verify, and sanction violations in time to matter politically rather than only historically?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can describe **how votes are counted** but cannot show **how parties enter, how campaigns are funded, how incumbents are restrained, and how wealth is stopped from becoming the dominant ballot language**, then it still has **elections without a competition constitution**.
