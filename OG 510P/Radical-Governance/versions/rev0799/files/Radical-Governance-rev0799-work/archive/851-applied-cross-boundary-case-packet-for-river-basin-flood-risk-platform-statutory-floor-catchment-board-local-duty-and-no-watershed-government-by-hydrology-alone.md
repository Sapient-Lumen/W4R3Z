# 851 — Applied cross-boundary case packet for river-basin flood-risk platform: statutory floor, catchment board, local duty, and no watershed government by hydrology alone

## One-line thesis

A river-basin and flood-risk system should usually be governed as a **regional risk platform under a statutory floor with local delivery duties**, not as a full watershed government: hydrology justifies shared maps, programmes, levies, evidence, and risk coordination, but it does not by itself erase municipalities, planning authorities, land duties, user routes, or national environmental guarantees.

## Why this matters

`849` gave the archive a thick positive case: a continuous metropolitan transport network can justify a bounded functional authority inside a visible democratic shell. `850` gave the archive a thin negative case: many cross-boundary capacity problems need only a member-owned shared-service backbone. The missing middle case is a boundary-hard natural-risk system.

River basins and flood plains do not respect municipal borders. Risk is cumulative, evidence-heavy, and often asset-linked: upstream land use, drainage, river condition, reservoirs, coastal defence, surface water, emergency response, insurance, planning, and ecological status all interact. But a basin is not automatically a people, a legislature, or a general government. The honest institutional form must be stronger than informal local cooperation and weaker than hydrological sovereignty.

This packet uses the English river-basin / flood-risk pattern as the first middle-form test: Environment Agency river-basin and flood-risk planning, regional flood and coastal committees, lead local flood authority responsibilities, catchment partnership input, and statutory review cycles. The verdict is **regional risk platform**, not metropolitan authority and not thin back-office compact.

This packet rejects two opposite mistakes. The first is **municipal hydrology denial**: pretending each local authority can manage water and flood risk as if upstream and downstream effects stop at the boundary. The second is **watershed sovereignty theater**: pretending the catchment map supplies a complete democratic mandate.

### 2. Trigger proof under `848`

| Trigger field | Basin / flood-risk packet answer |
| --- | --- |
| boundary mismatch | water bodies, flood pathways, catchments, drainage systems, coastal processes, reservoirs, surface-water risk, and ecological programmes cross municipal and sometimes national boundaries |
| harmed population | residents, businesses, farms, infrastructure users, ecosystems, and downstream communities exposed to water pollution, drought, flood, erosion, failed drainage, delayed warnings, or uncoordinated investment |
| incumbent failure | purely local action fails when upstream action creates downstream risk, data is fragmented, cost-benefit choices span authorities, and asset maintenance or planning choices depend on a basin-scale evidence base |
| form family | statutory regional risk platform, river-basin plan, flood-risk-management plan, regional committee, catchment partnership, or coordinated programme board under national law |
| lower-form insufficiency | a thin shared-service hub is too weak when the live question is risk maps, investment sequencing, cross-authority duties, ecological objectives, and public consultation across a hydrological system |
| upper-form excess | a general-purpose watershed government is too thick unless the body taxes, legislates, coerces, owns major assets, or resolves distributive choices at a scale that already has democratic authorization |
| upgrade test | thicken only if the platform begins making binding land-use, fiscal, infrastructure, enforcement, or displacement choices that exceed plan coordination and require stronger democratic, fiscal, asset, or remedy machinery |
| handback test | downshift only if the risk field becomes decomposable into local duties without upstream / downstream loss, shared evidence loss, or flood / ecological externality loss |

The central test is whether the body coordinates **risk responsibility** or exercises **territorial rule**. Basin platforms need enough institutional weight to make risk legible and coordinated; they do not automatically need full legislative sovereignty.

### 5. Form holding

The honest form is **regional risk platform under statutory floor**. It may publish and maintain risk maps, river-basin objectives, flood-risk plans, programmes of measures, funding priorities, local-duty interfaces, committee records, consultation materials, catchment partnership routes, progress reports, and project delivery ledgers. It may coordinate responsible authorities and make risk visible across municipal boundaries.

It must not silently become a general-purpose watershed government. It should not settle housing growth, land value, managed retreat, farm practice, infrastructure routing, insurance exposure, local taxation, or displacement burdens by technical plan alone. When those choices become unavoidable, the packet must escalate to the correct democratic, fiscal, asset, contract, regulatory, or judicial surface instead of hiding the decision inside basin expertise.

### 7. Rival interpretations

| Rival rule | Why it is tempting | Why this packet rejects or limits it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| municipality-only flood governance | preserves local planning accountability | fails when water, risk, evidence, and cost run upstream / downstream across boundaries |
| expert agency decides everything | centralizes technical competence | risks turning science and engineering into unreviewable distributive choice |
| elected basin government | matches ecological geography | overbuilds unless the basin body genuinely exercises broad legislative, fiscal, coercive, or distributive power |
| voluntary catchment partnership only | maximizes collaboration and trust | too weak for statutory review cycles, asset priorities, public risk maps, and residual-risk accountability |
| national department control | supplies legal floor and finance | can miss catchment evidence, local exposure, and community repair unless paired with regional / local risk surfaces |

The preferred rule is **statutory floor plus regional risk coordination plus local delivery accountability**.

## Failure modes

- **watershed sovereignty theater**: treating a catchment boundary as if it creates a complete territorial government.
- **municipal hydrology denial**: insisting that local autonomy means upstream / downstream risk can be handled separately.
- **expertise laundering**: using technical flood or water evidence to settle distributive choices without the right public route.
- **fiscal evaporation**: naming resilience ambitions without saying who pays, who waits, who maintains, and who absorbs remaining risk.
- **map worship**: publishing risk maps that are not linked to duties, warnings, projects, records, or remedies.
- **consultation sponge**: absorbing catchment participation without showing response, change, or disagreement.
- **asset invisibility**: discussing basin strategy while hiding who owns, maintains, insures, repairs, or retires defences and water assets.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Can a resident tell which authority handles main-river, surface-water, groundwater, coastal, drainage, planning, emergency-warning, and complaint questions?
2. Can an upstream community see how its land, drainage, or development choices affect downstream risk?
3. Can a downstream community see which projects, maintenance duties, or residual risks remain unfunded?
4. Can committee or agency records show why one flood-risk or water-quality action outranked another?
5. Can consultation responses be traced to plan changes or explicit rejections?
6. Can a major defence or nature-based project show its owner, liability, contract, maintenance, and handback path?
7. Can the platform name what it cannot decide without elected, fiscal, judicial, or regulatory escalation?
