# 864 — Plural-order authority and Indigenous co-governance: legal personality, consent, settlement mandate, relational jurisdiction, and no sovereignty by consultation theater

## One-line thesis

Plural-order authority should not be flattened into ordinary decentralization, consultation, or stakeholder management; when Indigenous law, treaty redress, legal personality, relational territory, and state public law meet, the archive must name each authority lane, consent threshold, record duty, operational owner, and remedy route before it calls the arrangement legitimate.

## Why this matters

The archive has strong tools for municipal, metropolitan, regional, national, continental, and global scope. But those tools can still smuggle a state-shaped assumption: that every legitimate authority must be a public corporation, elected tier, ministry, treaty secretariat, regulator, or service board.

Plural-order cases break that habit. A river, mountain, forest, or ancestral territory may be recognised as a legal person. An iwi, hapū, tribe, nation, clan, or customary institution may hold authority that is not merely advisory. A state agency may remain operationally responsible while no longer being the only public-law protagonist. A settlement instrument may settle old Crown breaches while creating a continuing governance relationship rather than a one-time compensation event.

The archive therefore needs a test that protects both sides: it must not romanticise legal personality as automatic justice, and it must not demote Indigenous authority into consultation theatre.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start with an authority-lane map, not a level-of-government map

A plural-order packet should begin by separating the lanes of authority.

| Lane | Core question | Evidence to preserve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Indigenous / relational authority | who speaks from the affected people, place, ancestor, water, mountain, or customary order? | representative institution, mandate record, tikanga / customary value statement where appropriate, conflict route |
| settlement / treaty authority | what historical breach, bargain, or redress instrument creates the arrangement? | deed, statute, apology, settlement scope, claims boundary, continuing obligation |
| legal-person authority | is the natural entity a legal person, and who is its human face or voice? | vesting rule, powers / duties / liabilities, guardian or representative appointment, conflict rule |
| state public-law authority | which minister, department, regulator, local authority, court, or board still holds formal power? | statutory power, retained duty, delegated function, review route |
| operational authority | who actually budgets, manages, maintains, permits, enforces, records, and communicates? | operating plan, budget line, staff home, concession / permit route, records schedule |
| public / third-party rights | which public-access, private-property, aboriginal title, customary, concession, environmental, and user rights persist? | boundary rule, rights-preservation rule, notice and appeal path |

A plural-order case is not solved by saying “co-management.” Co-management is the question, not the answer.

### 2. Distinguish consultation, consent, co-decision, and authority

The archive should not use consultation words as if they were all equivalent.

| Label | Meaning in the packet | Failure mode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| notice | affected people are informed | notice after commitment becomes theatre |
| consultation | affected people can shape the decision | consultation with no effect becomes extraction |
| consultation to obtain consent | the process is directed toward agreement, even if domestic law defines the final legal effect | consent language is laundered into a checkbox |
| consent / approval | the relevant representative institution can say yes or no to a specified act | consent holder is misidentified or bypassed |
| co-decision | two or more authorities must decide together | joint signature hides unequal resources or veto asymmetry |
| direct authority | an Indigenous or legal-person representative body holds statutory or customary power | state treats authority as advice after losing the argument |

The packet should state which label applies to each decision type. A single arrangement may use several labels at once: consultation for ordinary operations, co-approval for management plans, consent for adding land, ordinary ministerial decision for specified concessions, and court review for legality.

### 3. Legal personality is a public-law waist, not magic sovereignty

When a natural entity is given legal personality, the archive should ask:

| Field | Question |
| --- | --- |
| personhood object | what exactly is the legal person: river, catchment, mountain, park, forest, ecosystem, or named whole? |
| physical / metaphysical boundary | does the legal person include physical and spiritual / metaphysical elements, and how is that made legible to state law? |
| face and voice | who acts and speaks for the legal person? |
| asset ownership | does the legal person own land, minerals, funds, records, or other assets? |
| operational manager | who manages day-to-day work? |
| retained public law | which conservation, environmental, property, access, and administrative laws continue to apply? |
| affected rights | what happens to private rights, public access, customary rights, permits, concessions, and aboriginal title? |
| remedy | who can challenge a decision, correct a record, enforce duties, or represent the legal person in a dispute? |

Personhood can dignify a relationship, but it can also create ownership fog. The archive should not allow a government to celebrate “the land owns itself” while hiding who budgets, permits, excludes, audits, records, or litigates.

### 4. Treat relational boundary as a jurisdictional fact

Plural-order authority often follows ancestry, kinship, whakapapa, use, water, mountain, migration, burial, food, ceremony, language, and memory. These are not merely cultural context. They can be jurisdictional facts.

The packet should therefore map four boundaries:

| Boundary | Required statement |
| --- | --- |
| legal boundary | statutory area, park boundary, catchment, reserve, administrative unit |
| relational boundary | peoples, hapū / iwi / tribes / nations / clans, sites, ancestors, waters, seasons, use, obligations |
| ecological boundary | hydrology, habitat, species, erosion, fire, pest, climate, or landscape field |
| service / visitor boundary | who uses the place and which public services or access duties are triggered |

Where these boundaries do not match, the mismatch is a governance issue rather than a drafting inconvenience.

### 5. Preserve state duties after recognition

Recognition of Indigenous authority, legal personality, or relational status should not become a way for the state to shed duties quietly.

The packet should preserve:

- environmental and conservation duties;
- public record duties;
- administrative-law duties;
- funding and capacity duties promised in the settlement or statute;
- rights-preservation duties for private, public, customary, and aboriginal interests;
- court, tribunal, ombuds, audit, or supervisory routes;
- safe channels for internal Indigenous disagreement where the arrangement itself requires a representative institution.

Plural-order governance fails if recognition is used as unfunded delegation.

### 6. Add a mandate-and-difference file

A plural-order arrangement must identify the representative institution without pretending that the affected people are politically uniform.

Minimum file:

| Field | Required content |
| --- | --- |
| representative institution | who is authorised to speak or appoint? |
| basis of mandate | treaty, settlement, statute, election, customary process, ratification, trust deed, or agreed protocol |
| internal difference | how hapū, iwi, clans, families, elders, youth, women, off-territory members, and other affected groups can raise disagreement |
| conflict route | how appointment, mandate, or decision disputes are resolved |
| public interface | what outsiders can rely on without erasing internal complexity |

Do not demand that Indigenous peoples demonstrate impossible unanimity. Do not allow state officials to exploit internal difference to bypass representative institutions.

### 7. Create a plural-order evidence lane

The claim ledger should add a plural-order evidence type when a case uses this note.

| Evidence lane | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| settlement law | deed, statute, apology, redress package, claims boundary |
| Indigenous authority | representative institution, values, customary law / tikanga statement, mandate record |
| legal-person record | personhood provision, face / voice body, asset vesting, authority to sue or act |
| operations | department role, budget, staffing, management plan, permit / concession procedure |
| rights preservation | private rights, public access, customary rights, aboriginal title, environmental protections |
| participation | consultation, consent, co-decision, public planning, affected-party route |
| remedies | court, tribunal, minister, ombuds, audit, dispute, correction, enforcement |

### 8. Put the plural-order test into cross-boundary packets

A case packet should activate this note when any of the following are true:

- a natural entity receives legal personality;
- Indigenous or customary authority is part of the legal form;
- a treaty / settlement instrument creates ongoing governance rather than one-time compensation;
- consultation or consent is used as a legitimacy claim;
- ordinary state boundaries miss the relational, ecological, or historical boundary;
- a public authority remains operationally responsible but no longer owns the place or narrative alone.

## Failure modes

- **consultation theater**: engagement is used to legitimate a decision already made.
- **personhood fog**: legal personality is celebrated while ownership, budget, operations, records, and remedies are unclear.
- **state-duty escape**: recognition becomes unfunded delegation or a way to avoid conservation, record, or service duties.
- **mandate bypass**: officials consult convenient individuals while ignoring the representative institution required by the arrangement.
- **unanimity trap**: internal Indigenous disagreement is used to deny authority altogether.
- **customary-law flattening**: tikanga, custom, relationship, or spiritual connection is reduced to aesthetic preamble.
- **public-access panic**: ordinary users are told that recognition necessarily erases public access even when the legal arrangement preserves it.
- **ownership fiction**: vesting land in a legal person is described as full self-government even while operational power remains elsewhere.
- **rights collision opacity**: private rights, customary rights, public access, conservation duties, and concession interests are not mapped together.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Can a reader identify who speaks for the legal person or Indigenous authority?
2. Is the representative institution named, and is its mandate distinguishable from generic stakeholder status?
3. Are consultation, consent, approval, co-decision, and direct authority separated by decision type?
4. Are state duties retained where they have not been lawfully transferred?
5. Are public access, private rights, customary rights, aboriginal title, concessions, and environmental duties mapped rather than rhetorically waved away?
6. Is the operating manager different from the legal owner or face / voice body, and is that difference visible?
7. Is there a budget or capacity lane for the new authority role?
8. Is there a remedy or dispute route for affected persons, internal mandate disputes, and the natural entity itself?
9. Does the arrangement protect the Indigenous relationship without pretending that legal personhood alone restores the whole relationship?
