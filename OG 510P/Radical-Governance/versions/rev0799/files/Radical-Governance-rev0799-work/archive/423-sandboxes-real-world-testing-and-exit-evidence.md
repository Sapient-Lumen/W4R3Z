# 423 — Sandboxes, real-world testing, and exit evidence

## One-line thesis

Public-interest sandboxes and real-world tests should be governed as **temporary, consent-bound, reversible exceptions** with registration, time limits, suspension triggers, and explicit exit evidence.

## Why this matters

A sandbox can be either disciplined governance or polished loophole. Without hard boundaries, the same pattern repeats:

- experimentation stretches into semi-permanent deployment,
- people are exposed without meaningful choice or visibility,
- harms during testing get treated as acceptable because the system is “only a pilot”,
- nobody can later say what the test proved, what failed, or why scaling was justified.

Current official rules are stricter than the usual rhetoric. The EU AI Act frames regulatory sandboxes and real-world testing as controlled, time-limited settings; requires registration, informed consent in ordinary cases, reversibility or disregarding of outputs, oversight by qualified people, incident reporting, suspension or termination when mitigation fails, and notification of final outcomes. The archive should therefore treat testing as a heavily governed exception rather than an innovation halo.

## Design rule

Any public automation sandbox or real-world test should have an explicit **entry rule**, **operating rule**, and **exit rule**. The minimum public record should show:

- the test objective,
- the participating organisations,
- the scope and duration,
- the people or workflows exposed,
- the safeguards in place,
- the conditions that pause or terminate the test,
- the evidence required to stop, revise, or scale.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat sandbox status as a narrow exception, not a soft permission slip

A sandbox should be limited in scope, duration, and purpose. It should exist to learn something precise under controlled conditions, not to bypass ordinary governance until the system becomes too entrenched to stop.

### 2. Register every test with a durable identifier

Each test should have a unique identifier and dated record. Even if some operational details must remain restricted, the archive should prefer a trackable object over informal experimentation by email and slide deck.

### 3. Bound time and extension rules in advance

Tests should have a default end date and a high bar for extension. Any extension should require:

- a written reason,
- an updated risk picture,
- confirmation that safeguards still hold,
- a fresh statement of what remains unresolved.

### 4. Keep participation informed and reversible

Where people are directly exposed, consent and understandable notice should be the default. Participants should know:

- the nature and purpose of the test,
- the duration,
- the risks and inconveniences,
- how to withdraw,
- how to request reversal or disregard of outputs where relevant.

Reversibility matters because testing should not silently create irreversible civic facts.

### 5. Protect vulnerable groups and keep alternatives available

Do not use sandbox status to shift disproportionate burden onto people with fewer alternatives or less bargaining power. If the test affects an essential public workflow, keep a workable non-test path available unless law clearly requires otherwise.

### 6. Build suspension and recall into the test itself

A test should have named triggers for:

- immediate mitigation,
- suspension,
- termination,
- prompt recall or disabling of the tested system,
- notification of authorities and relevant participants.

Stopping safely is part of the test design.

### 7. End every test with an outcome decision, not a fade-out

At the end of the sandbox or real-world test, the operator should publish or record a clear outcome:

- stop and discard,
- revise and retest,
- narrow and continue under a new approval,
- promote toward broader deployment.

A trial that simply fades into routine use has failed its governance purpose.

## Guardrails

- Require a written plan before the test begins.
- Keep the duration short enough that “temporary” still means temporary.
- Ensure harmful outputs can be reversed or disregarded where the framework requires it.
- Record final outcomes and not just launch conditions.
- Do not let confidentiality erase the very existence of the test.

## Failure modes

- **sandbox laundering**: experimentation language masks ordinary live deployment.
- **consent theater**: people are nominally informed but cannot realistically refuse.
- **extension creep**: a temporary trial is repeatedly prolonged without real re-approval.
- **non-reversible testing**: the system creates durable consequences while still labelled experimental.
- **exit fog**: no one can later state what evidence the test produced or why scaling followed.

## Practical tests

A sandbox regime passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is every test registered, scoped, and time-limited?
2. Are participants informed and able to withdraw where the framework requires it?
3. Can outputs be reversed or disregarded if the test goes wrong?
4. Are suspension, termination, and recall triggers explicit?
5. Does every test end with a documented decision rather than quiet continuation?

## Compression rule for the archive

Whenever a public operator says “this is only a sandbox” or “just a pilot”, ask:

**What exception is being granted, who is exposed, how long does it last, what stops it, and what evidence must exist before it can become ordinary governance?**

If those answers are soft, the sandbox is already becoming a loophole.
