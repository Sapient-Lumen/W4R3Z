# 568 — Land administration and cadastre by scope: community witnessing, municipal parcel service, regional coordination, national tenure rules, and no government by documentary dispossession

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat land administration as a scale-matched constitutional infrastructure rather than a clerical property office: community and customary institutions should help witness real people-to-land relationships, municipal or county offices should be the ordinary parcel-service front door, metropolitan or regional layers should pool cadastre and geospatial coordination where fragmentation would break the map, national or constitutional-state law should own tenure forms, identifiers, interoperability, and register integrity, and continental or global bodies should provide standards and support rather than pretending a world cadastre exists.

## Why this matters

The archive already has strong notes on **land and housing**, **taxation**, **public knowledge**, **official statistics**, **civil registration**, **indigenous self-government**, **water**, and **extractives**. But it still lacked a compact doctrine for the institutions that tell a polity **who holds which land relationship, under what evidence, on which map, with which public consequences**.

That gap matters because weak land administration is not only a technical nuisance. It is often how exclusion becomes ordinary government. An untitled settlement disappears from service plans. A customary territory appears as “vacant state land.” A parcel exists for taxation but not for tenure protection. A building has an address for billing but not for legal security. A city permits, values, taxes, acquires, and services land through one set of records while courts, utilities, lenders, and communities rely on another.

The right archive stance is therefore **recognize reality early, formalize honestly, integrate records visibly, and keep final authority reviewable**. Land administration should be close enough to lived land relations to see them, structured enough to connect law and map, and public enough that it cannot quietly become a machine for documentary erasure.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from real people-to-land relationships, not only from already-perfect title

The smallest scale often knows first who actually occupies, uses, stewards, inherits, grazes, shares, cultivates, or crosses land. Communities, customary authorities, block associations, indigenous institutions, and frontline local officials can often witness real tenure relations long before a centralized registry can describe them cleanly.

The archive should therefore reject the idea that land administration begins only when the state is ready to issue a fully precise parcel title. Good systems start by **recording reality in forms proportionate to current capacity**: occupancy, possession, use, collective stewardship, customary claims, overlapping rights, shared spaces, and social boundaries. Participatory enumeration, local witnessing, and community evidence can be constitutionally valuable at this stage, especially where conventional titling would otherwise leave most people outside the record.

But the archive should also avoid romantic localism. The smallest scale should help surface and witness tenure reality; it should not become an unreviewable monopoly over final adjudication, especially where local elites, landlords, clans, or political brokers can dominate the process.

### 2. Make municipalities or counties the ordinary service face for parcels, addresses, buildings, and updates

For most people, land administration should not feel like a distant sovereign mystery. The ordinary front door should usually sit at municipal, county, or similarly reachable scale: a place where people can file transactions, update addresses, correct building or parcel data, seek extracts, navigate first registration, and connect land records to planning, permitting, valuation, taxation, utilities, and local dispute-prevention.

This scale is usually best placed to keep the everyday parcel fabric legible because it already lives close to streets, addresses, building control, neighborhood change, and visible occupation patterns. It is also the level most capable of linking land records to lived service delivery rather than treating the cadastre as a detached archive.

The archive should therefore prefer **local service proximity with standardized back-office discipline**. The service face should be near. The data model, evidence classes, and appeal routes should still be governed clearly enough that access does not depend on informal favor.

### 3. Pool cadastral and geospatial capacity above the smallest municipality when fragmentation would break the map

Not every municipality can sustain survey capacity, parcel-fabric maintenance, orthophoto programs, adjudication support, archive protection, or geospatial infrastructure on its own. In fragmented metropolitan areas or in federal systems with many weak local units, forcing each jurisdiction to maintain its own quasi-sovereign cadastre can produce gaps, overlaps, incompatible identifiers, and broken boundary logic.

An ideal constitutional design therefore allows a metropolitan, regional, provincial, or state layer to carry shared cadastral and geospatial functions when scale really helps: base mapping, parcel identifiers, survey control, dispute-support capacity, archive protection, regional service hubs, and cross-boundary coordination. This is especially important where labor markets, watersheds, transport systems, or indigenous territories cross many municipal lines.

The key rule is not “always centralize the cadastre.” It is **do not let territorial fragmentation destroy map integrity**. Where one continuous urban region or one constitutional state needs one coherent parcel fabric, the archive should prefer pooling over municipal cartographic feudalism.

### 4. Keep the legal register, cadastre, and property identifiers integrated — or force them to coordinate as if they were

A mature land administration system has both legal and spatial components: records of rights, responsibilities, and restrictions, plus the mapped units to which those records attach. The archive should therefore treat the split between cadastre and land registry as a constitutional design question, not a mere administrative habit.

Where possible, the polity should prefer one integrated system or one authoritative institutional arrangement that keeps legal and spatial data aligned. If constitutional structure requires separate institutions, then strong mandatory coordination, shared identifiers, synchronized update rules, and interoperable public access are the minimum acceptable substitute.

This is the level where national government — or, in federations, the constitutional state or province that actually owns land law — should usually govern tenure forms, evidentiary hierarchy, unique identifiers, interoperability rules, archive integrity, auditability, and the appeals ladder. The public should not have to guess whether the “real” land truth lives in the deed office, the map office, the tax roll, the planning portal, or a court file.

### 5. Recognize customary, collective, informal, and indigenous tenure without forcing every right through one narrow property template

Land governance fails when the state can only “see” rights that look like one stylized individual freehold parcel. Many societies rely on group, layered, seasonal, common, indigenous, or otherwise non-standard tenure relations. A serious land constitution must be capable of recording and protecting these relationships without pretending they are unreal until flattened into a single narrow form.

The archive should therefore prefer systems that can record a continuum of people-to-land relationships and upgrade them over time without erasing the social reality they are meant to protect. That implies legal recognition pathways, record types for collective and customary rights, and institutional routes for indigenous and community authorities to interface with the public record without surrendering their own governance traditions.

The point is not to make every tenure relation identical. It is to keep the public record from becoming a weapon that rewards whoever already fits the registry’s favorite template.

### 6. Treat first recording and regularization as fit-for-purpose statecraft, not as one-shot perfectionism

Many countries or regions still have large areas where rights are partially recorded, informally recorded, or not recorded at all. The archive should reject the fantasy that the only serious answer is immediate high-cost, parcel-perfect, litigation-heavy titling across the entire territory.

A stronger doctrine is **fit-for-purpose progression**: start with methods that are inclusive, affordable, participatory, and good enough to secure people against dispossession; then improve precision, legal weight, and system integration over time. This approach better fits places where capacity, trust, funding, or boundary certainty are limited.

The archive should also prefer low-friction correction and upgrading paths. A first-recording system that cannot cheaply fix errors, register inheritance, capture subdivision, or absorb later survey improvement will decay back into irrelevance.

### 7. Land administration is part of the constitutional public-facts layer, so access, traceability, and anti-corruption controls matter

Because land records shape taxation, service delivery, expropriation, lending, compensation, elections, environmental control, and the distribution of wealth, they should be treated as a live constitutional information system. That means authenticated extracts, change logs, archive protection, role-bounded access, public search rules, fee discipline, conflict-of-interest controls, and auditable trails for who changed what and why.

The archive should resist both extremes: records so closed that ordinary people cannot discover what the state thinks is true about their land, and records so carelessly open that they expose people to predation, violence, or data abuse. The correct design is structured transparency with privacy and safety discipline.

### 8. Above the nation-state, provide standards, geodesy, and support — not a fantasy of one world deed office

Continental and global institutions do matter in land administration, but mainly as standards, technical-support, geospatial-reference, anti-corruption, and post-conflict support layers. They can help with common models, responsible land-governance principles, geodetic reference frames, satellite and mapping support, professional capacity, and learning across jurisdictions.

What they should not do is pretend that a thin treaty or international secretariat can replace domestic tenure law, local witnessing, constitutional property guarantees, or courts. The archive should therefore treat global land administration as a **supportive narrow waist**, not as a supranational title office.

## Guardrails

- Do not let the absence of perfect title become an excuse to deny that real tenure relations exist.
- Do not make land administration so centralized that ordinary people cannot reach it, understand it, or correct it.
- Do not let every municipality run an incompatible parcel fabric when one shared cadastral layer is plainly needed.
- Do not keep cadastre, deed registry, planning, valuation, and tax identifiers so separate that the state cannot reconcile its own land facts.
- Do not force customary, collective, indigenous, or informal rights through one narrow property form just to make the database comfortable.
- Do not let first registration or regularization become a one-time spectacle with no durable update path.
- Do not make land records either opaque patronage assets or unrestricted exposure engines.
- Do not pretend international frameworks can substitute for domestic legality and local legitimacy.

## Failure modes

- **documentary dispossession**: the state treats unrecorded or differently recorded land relations as if they do not exist.
- **parcel-fabric fracture**: neighboring jurisdictions maintain incompatible maps, identifiers, or update rules for one continuous territory.
- **registry-map split**: the legal record and the cadastral record drift apart and citizens must guess which one governs.
- **template tyranny**: only one formal property type is legible, so collective, customary, or layered rights vanish on entry.
- **titling perfectionism**: the state delays protection because it waits for expensive, litigation-heavy, high-precision completion before recording anyone.
- **local elite capture**: community witnessing is real, but final recognition is monopolized by powerful local actors without review.
- **back-office opacity**: changes to land records occur without visible logs, reasons, or accessible correction routes.
- **global-cadastre fantasy**: international institutions are asked to do the work of domestic law, local recordation, and constitutional adjudication.

## Practical tests

A land administration constitution is scale-matched and publicly usable when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can the system record real people-to-land relationships, including customary, collective, or informal ones, before full formalization is complete?
2. Is the ordinary service face reachable at local scale even when some authoritative functions are pooled regionally or nationally?
3. Where multiple governments touch the same territory, is there one coherent parcel and identifier logic rather than incompatible local maps?
4. Are legal and spatial records integrated enough that the public can tell what unit exists, what rights attach to it, and what changed?
5. Are correction, appeal, archive protection, and anti-corruption controls strong enough that the record can be trusted without becoming unchallengeable?
6. Above the nation-state, do support institutions supply standards and assistance without pretending to own the land register of the world?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **who may buy, tax, permit, evict, compensate, inherit, mortgage, or police a parcel** but cannot say **how real people-to-land relationships are first recognized, mapped, updated, appealed, and protected across scales**, then it still has **land power without a land administration constitution**.
