# 651 — Scope prescription limits, delay bars, adverse possession, and no stack change by time alone

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should not let **mere duration, delayed challenge, or long unlawful exercise** manufacture constitutional title: materially stack-altering arrangements may need continuity management, evidentiary caution, or prospective settlement after long operation, but they should not acquire lawful authority simply because they lasted, were hard to unwind, or went unchallenged for too long.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a fairly complete chain for multilevel scope honesty. It says how a scope fit is assessed (`602`, `628`-`630`), how drift is triggered for review (`632`-`635`), how proposals are classified, cleared, overridden, interpreted, launched, observed, settled, retired, and reactivated (`636`-`643`), and how defects, pauses, severability, remand, relabeling, waiver, and estoppel should be handled (`644`-`650`).

That already closes many of the obvious loopholes. A sponsor cannot simply relabel a scope change as guidance. It cannot claim valid waiver from silence, platform dependence, or signature by the wrong organ. It cannot lean on sunk cost or accepted benefits and call that constitutional title.

But one loophole still remains large enough to matter.

A ministry says: perhaps the original rescoping was imperfect, but it has now been operating this way for seven years. A regional authority says: perhaps the metropolitan board never got the right consent, but everyone has behaved as if it did for a whole electoral cycle. A platform owner says: perhaps this began as an emergency workaround, but the old municipal discretion has not been exercised for so long that the question is no longer realistic. A reviewer says: perhaps the claim is strong in theory, but the challenge comes too late for the constitutional stack to be reopened now.

All of those arguments share a single hidden premise: **time itself can mint title**.

Sometimes time does matter. Evidence decays. People make plans. Services become hard to unwind cleanly. Legal systems often need filing windows, repose doctrines, finality rules, and orderly closure. Not every old defect can justify instant retroactive disruption, and not every late challenge should have the same remedial reach as an early one.

But none of that requires the stronger claim that long use or delayed objection should itself decide who lawfully governs what.

If the archive left that move unanswered, then even after rejecting waiver by silence and estoppel by dependence, a sponsor could still say: **very well — then call it title by duration**.

That is the role of **scope prescription limits**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Keep timeliness rules separate from title rules

The first rule should be simple: a polity should distinguish **when and how a claim may be heard or remedied** from **whether the underlying constitutional title was ever honestly acquired**.

Delay may affect:

- available remedies,
- evidentiary confidence,
- retroactive reach,
- transitional burdens,
- or the need for staged correction.

Delay should not, by itself, answer the deeper question of whether a materially stack-altering arrangement ever travelled through the right lane.

### 2. No adverse possession of protected competence

A ministry, region, metro body, emergency cell, shared-service host, or platform steward should not acquire protected competence merely by occupying it for a long time.

Long exercise may show that something operationally important happened. It should not convert:

- tolerated practice into lawful reassignment,
- emergency residue into ordinary title,
- borrowed administration into owned competence,
- or managerial convenience into constitutional settlement.

No actor should be able to say: **we governed this function long enough that it became ours**.

### 3. Filing windows should not start from hidden or mislabeled change

If a materially stack-altering move was never clearly surfaced as such — because it was buried in guidance, code, grant conditions, procurement, pilot language, or emergency residue — a polity should be cautious about claiming that challenge clocks have already run.

The stronger the archive becomes on packet, opinion, override, register, and clear-statement discipline (`635`-`639`), the weaker the excuse becomes for saying affected actors should somehow have challenged a scope event that was never openly presented as one.

A fair system should not award repose to a sponsor precisely because the sponsor kept the stack change hard to see.

### 4. Distinguish personal reliance and continuity from constitutional settlement

Late challenges can raise real reliance concerns for residents, workers, contractors, and counterpart bodies. That matters. But those concerns belong first to **continuity management**, not to the question of constitutional title.

Where long duration makes correction harder, the honest response is usually:

- narrower remedy,
- staged unwind,
- prospective-only repair,
- or a fresh lawful settlement route.

It is not to say that long operation itself perfected the original defect.

### 5. Require a stronger public act for permanent settlement than simple passage of time

The archive already says that authorized stack changes should move from provisional to settled status through one visible **scope-settlement certificate** (`641`).

Scope prescription limits add the companion rule: where no such public promotion occurred, time alone should not be treated as an adequate substitute. A polity should not let “everyone got used to it” do the work that a lawful settlement act was supposed to do.

### 6. Protect core self-government interests more strongly against repose logic

Not every scope question has the same weight. Some disputes concern operating detail. Others touch the protected core of self-government: own competences, supervisory chains, territorial status, representative control, or the ordinary location of coercive authority.

The deeper the constitutional interest at stake, the more reluctant a polity should be to let ordinary delay logic foreclose honest classification. Time may still shape remedy. It should have less power to fabricate title where protected self-government is at issue.

### 7. Do not confuse non-use with abandonment

A lower tier may fail to exercise a power robustly for many reasons: lack of capacity, intimidation, fiscal pressure, platform dependency, ambiguous law, emergency crowd-out, or simple fear that resistance will fail.

That non-use should not be casually reinterpreted as abandonment of the competence itself.

A polity should therefore avoid saying that a municipality, region, or community “lost” a protected role merely because it:

- stopped exercising it for a period,
- delegated temporarily under pressure,
- accepted operational assistance,
- or lacked the institutional capacity to force the issue earlier.

Formal reassignment still needs the proper lane.

### 8. Put the burden on the sponsor when invoking lateness as a constitutional defense

If a sponsor argues that a scope claim should fail because it comes too late, the sponsor should have to show more than elapsed time.

At minimum, the sponsor should explain:

- when the stack change was openly surfaced as such;
- which public packet, opinion, override, or register act made the change legible;
- what review route existed in practice;
- what third-party reliance now requires protection;
- and why narrower remedial tools could not address the delay without converting it into title.

That burden matters because lateness defenses are easiest to abuse when the original scope event was itself only half-visible.

### 9. Use prospective cure, not fictional historical title

Where an arrangement has lasted a long time and immediate unwind would be destructive, the polity may decide to regularize it prospectively.

That can be honest. But if so, the archive should say openly that the polity is:

- curing prospectively,
- freshly authorizing,
- reclassifying through the right lane,
- or choosing a new settlement from today forward.

It should not pretend that the arrangement was valid all along merely because the polity now prefers to keep it.

### 10. Every repose or lateness disposition should update the live stack honestly

If a reviewer concludes that delay changes the remedy, that conclusion should still feed the public stack machinery.

The resulting public object should say:

- whether title remains disputed;
- whether the defect is being left in place only prospectively;
- whether continuity protection or prospective cure applies;
- whether a fresh packet or settlement act is now required;
- and what this means for the canonical scope register and later review.

That prevents a quiet slide from “challenge came late” to “everyone may now speak as though the original path had been lawful.”

## Guardrails

- Do not let elapsed time do the work of lawful authorization.
- Do not start repose or filing clocks from hidden, mislabeled, or half-legible stack changes.
- Do not confuse narrower remedy with stronger title.
- Do not treat non-use, temporary delegation, or capacity weakness as abandonment of protected competence.
- Do not let continuity concerns silently convert late review into constitutional surrender.
- Do not regularize prospectively while narrating the result as if the past had always been lawful.

## Failure modes

- **adverse-possession federalism**: one tier acquires competence by occupying it long enough.
- **repose-by-obscurity**: the sponsor hides the stack change inside softer instruments, then claims the challenge came too late.
- **elapsed-time ratification myth**: ordinary duration is treated as if it were a public settlement act.
- **non-use-as-abandonment**: weaker or pressured tiers are said to have lost title because they could not exercise it effectively.
- **late-remedy/title collapse**: a reviewer moves from “retroactive unwind is hard” to “therefore the title must have become valid.”
- **prospective-cure revisionism**: a new authorization is granted today but narrated as proof the old arrangement was always legitimate.
- **constitutional amnesia by electoral cycle**: one or two full budget or election cycles are treated as enough to erase the question of lawful origin.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Did the challenged arrangement ever become visible as a scope event through the packet, opinion, override, or register chain, or is the sponsor asking for repose from an event it never clearly disclosed?
2. Is the lateness argument really about evidentiary loss or third-party continuity, or mostly about the sponsor’s desire to keep a now-familiar arrangement?
3. Would the polity still believe the title was strong if the same arrangement had lasted only six months instead of six years?
4. Is the reviewer narrowing remedy because retroactive unwind would harm others, or accidentally narrating that narrower remedy as proof of valid title?
5. Has non-use by the affected tier been wrongly reinterpreted as abandonment when capacity, pressure, ambiguity, or dependency may explain it better?
6. Could the arrangement be regularized prospectively through a fresh lawful act rather than fictionalized as historically valid?
7. Does the dispute touch a protected core interest where repose logic should be especially weak?
8. If a filing or review clock is being invoked, what public act actually started it?
9. Would the polity accept the same repose argument if the original stack change had been openly labeled and heavily contested from day one?
10. Has the live scope register been updated honestly to show whether the result is validated title, prospective cure, continuity protection, or unresolved defect?

## Closing image

A constitutional stack should not let one actor say: **we may never have received this power honestly, but we held it long enough that the calendar made it ours**. Time can counsel caution, sequencing, and prospective repair. It should not govern by itself. The honest answer is: **protect continuity where needed, but make any lasting settlement through explicit public authority rather than through constitutional adverse possession**.
