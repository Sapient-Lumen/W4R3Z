# 867 — Supplier-dependency review dockets: admin privileges, exit rehearsals, benefit claims, and no contract confidence by assertion

## One-line thesis

A public body using cloud, SaaS, AI, identity, analytics, or managed-platform infrastructure should keep a supplier-dependency review docket that proves authority ownership, processor boundaries, privileged access, data and model location, product purpose, benefit claims, exit rehearsal, fallback continuity, redress, and opposition handling before procurement confidence, security assurance, or supplier reputation becomes a substitute for public governance.

## Why this matters

`859` named the supplier layer as a governance surface, and `866` showed why an applied public-health data-platform case needs more than reassurance language. The missing object is a reusable docket: a small evidence structure that can travel across cloud hosting, AI model services, identity systems, data platforms, case-management tools, procurement frameworks, and public analytics.

The archive already has many ingredients: source-backed summaries, public records, vendor registers, model cards, impact dossiers, role logs, release windows, fallback channels, defeat tests, and waist-capture analysis. But supplier dependency needs its own review plane because a system can be secure, procured, and useful while still being public-governance fragile. The proof question is not only “was a contract awarded?” or “does the supplier obey instructions?” It is whether public authority remains reconstructable, portable, contestable, and operational if the supplier changes behaviour, loses trust, fails, is terminated, or becomes too central to replace.

## Pattern pack

### 1. The one-page supplier-dependency docket

| Field | Minimum entry |
| --- | --- |
| public function | the public service, decision, workflow, data product, or infrastructure role supported by the supplier |
| public owner | agency, local body, controller, accountable officer, programme board, or statutory authority that owns the public function |
| supplier role | processor, sub-processor, SaaS provider, cloud host, model provider, integrator, support desk, reseller, or strategic partner |
| authority boundary | what the supplier may do, must not do, and may do only under written instruction or emergency authorisation |
| public record | logs, tickets, data-flow records, model/version records, product notices, DPIAs, audit files, and change records |
| affected parties | users, staff, residents, patients, regulated parties, claimants, travellers, students, or others affected by supplier-shaped operation |
| access proof | roles, privileged sessions, support access, emergency access, sub-processor access, location, and review cadence |
| benefit proof | claims, methodology, comparator, local validation, independent challenge, correction route, and confidence label |
| exit proof | export, migration, step-in, termination, data deletion, records preservation, public skills, and fallback continuity |
| opposition proof | rival architecture, staff / user / public concerns, supplier-risk brief, and response record |

A docket is failed if it cannot answer who controls the action, who can reconstruct the evidence, and who can continue the public service after supplier exit.

### 2. Separate six kinds of confidence

| Confidence type | Useful question | Anti-laundering rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| procurement confidence | was the supplier selected lawfully and competitively? | does not prove the platform is legitimate for every later product or use case |
| security confidence | are baseline security controls and monitoring in place? | does not prove legal authority, public trust, fairness, explainability, or exit |
| privacy confidence | are data-protection duties, minimisation, DPIAs, and rights routes mapped? | does not prove operational value, clinical / frontline suitability, or vendor portability |
| performance confidence | does the service meet uptime, latency, support, and recovery commitments? | does not prove public benefit or absence of downstream harm |
| benefit confidence | are claimed outcomes reconstructable against a method and comparator? | does not prove the supplier is the only or best route |
| trust confidence | do affected parties have reason to accept the system? | cannot be manufactured by communications if access, exit, opposition, and redress records are weak |

Never let one confidence type launder another.

### 3. Public-owner and supplier-role labels

| Label | Meaning | Required warning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| public owner | owns the public function and legal basis | cannot shift public-law accountability to the supplier |
| controller | decides purposes and means for personal data | must preserve data-subject rights and product-specific legal basis |
| joint controller | shares control over specified processing | must publish allocation of responsibilities |
| processor | processes on written instructions | still requires access, sub-processor, audit, breach, and deletion controls |
| sub-processor | supports the processor | cannot appear without approval and a public record |
| integrator | connects systems, transforms data, or configures workflows | must preserve interface-control, test, and change records |
| model provider | supplies model behaviour, updates, refusals, tools, and safety settings | must preserve model/version/configuration and retest clocks |
| support desk | sees or can alter operational state | must preserve minimisation, ticket, session, and after-action records |

A supplier may be legally subordinate but practically powerful. Both facts must appear in the same docket.

### 4. Material-change clocks

A supplier-dependency docket must reopen when any of these occur:

| Trigger | Reopen reason |
| --- | --- |
| new product or use case | authority, legal basis, data category, affected parties, and redress may change |
| new data category | privacy, confidentiality, minimisation, and opt-out boundaries may change |
| new supplier, sub-processor, or support role | access, location, accountability, and exit may change |
| new privileged or admin access class | the public record may no longer reconstruct who touched what |
| new model, automation, or decision-support feature | human responsibility, source hierarchy, abstention, and appeal route may change |
| new jurisdiction, region, or hosting architecture | data location, law, recovery, and access by foreign or third-party actors may change |
| extension, renewal, or break decision | the state must retest market options, performance, trust, benefits, and exit feasibility |
| serious incident or loss of trust | temporary assurance may no longer support live operation |
| merger, insolvency, sanctions, acquisition, or supplier strategic pivot | continuity, access, and public mission fit may change |

Renewal without reopening is supplier capture by calendar.

### 5. Exit rehearsal is not an exit clause

| Artifact | Weak form | Strong form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| contract exit clause | says termination is possible | tested export and migration record with named destination and time estimate |
| data export | dump of tables or files | complete case / appeal / audit packet with data dictionary and provenance |
| knowledge transfer | generic training | public staff can operate, audit, and modify the service or manage a replacement |
| step-in right | legal right on paper | rehearsed credentials, runbooks, service levels, and access controls |
| fallback channel | manual workaround named | tested continuity for essential users during outage, dispute, or termination |
| deletion / return | vendor promise | verified return, deletion, retention, litigation hold, and audit record |

A public authority that cannot rehearse exit has not actually retained control.

### 6. Benefit claims need adversarial evidence

Benefit claims should be docketed in four columns:

| Column | Question |
| --- | --- |
| claimed benefit | what improvement is asserted, by whom, and over what period? |
| method | what comparator, baseline, exclusion, confounder, and confidence level supports it? |
| rival explanation | what else could explain the outcome: staffing, policy change, local process, coding, backlog variation, or selection bias? |
| public consequence | what decision will rely on the claim: expansion, renewal, budget, decommissioning, staff mandate, or public reassurance? |

A benefit claim can justify continued review. It does not by itself justify lock-in.

### 7. Trust and opposition are evidence

Treat trust as an operating condition, not a sentiment. Opposition by patients, service users, staff, local bodies, Indigenous authorities, unions, clinicians, professional groups, civil society, auditors, or legislators may be wrong, exaggerated, or politically motivated. It is still governance evidence if it affects data quality, adoption, consent, opt-out behaviour, staff use, litigation, recruitment, institutional legitimacy, or continuity.

A docket should preserve:

- the strongest opposition brief;
- the public owner's response;
- the rival architecture or lower-form option;
- what would falsify the opposition claim;
- what would falsify the programme's reassurance claim;
- which decision will proceed despite unresolved opposition.

### 8. Supplier-dependency verdicts

| Verdict | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| acceptable support tool | supplier does not control consequential public outcomes, evidence, access, or continuity |
| governed infrastructure dependency | supplier layer is mapped, logged, portable, monitored, and publicly reviewable |
| fragile dependency | one or more access, record, exit, benefit, or opposition surfaces is incomplete but repairable |
| capture-prone dependency | public function depends on supplier-controlled evidence, workflow, model behaviour, or migration barrier |
| unlawful / unsafe delegation risk | supplier effectively decides, blocks, or erases public rights without public authority and remedy |
| emergency-only dependency | temporary use is allowed only under sunset, fallback, and handback conditions |

A verdict should include a review clock and the event that reopens it.

## Failure modes

- **contract-confidence theater**: assuming a lawful award and negotiated terms prove operational legitimacy.
- **security-assurance laundering**: treating security controls as proof of legal basis, fairness, trust, benefit, or exit.
- **processor invisibility**: saying the supplier only follows instructions while hiding privileged access, support, product design, or migration dependence.
- **benefit monoculture**: letting a dashboard of improvements silence method, rival explanations, negative effects, and local variance.
- **exit clause mythology**: mistaking contractual termination language for rehearsed public continuity.
- **admin-role fog**: privileged users can change or see too much without named session records and after-action review.
- **sub-processor sprawl**: support and infrastructure actors multiply faster than the public record can explain.
- **trust laundering**: using engagement pages to stand in for hard changes to access, records, opposition, and public rights.
- **model update surprise**: a supplier-side model or policy change alters public behaviour without a retest clock.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Can a reviewer state the public function, public owner, supplier role, legal basis, and affected parties in one paragraph?
2. Can privileged support access be reconstructed by person, purpose, time, data class, action, and approval?
3. Can every major benefit claim be tied to method, comparator, rival explanation, and decision consequence?
4. Can the public authority run an exit rehearsal without losing case evidence, audit logs, user access, or service continuity?
5. Can a user challenge a supplier-shaped error through a public route rather than only through a private help desk?
6. Can new products, data categories, sub-processors, AI features, or extension decisions trigger mandatory reopening?
7. Can staff or public opposition appear in the official decision file with a serious response and falsifier?
8. Can procurement, security, privacy, performance, benefit, and trust confidence be separated rather than collapsed into one reassurance story?
9. Can the authority explain what it would do if the supplier failed, refused, was terminated, became politically unacceptable, or changed its strategic direction?
