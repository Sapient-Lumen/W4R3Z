# 613 — Participatory homes for shared territorial power: petitions, hearings, mini-publics, territorial notice, and no metropolitan participation by postcode

## One-line thesis

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, it needs an honest **participatory home** as well as a democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, and intergovernmental one: territory-wide notice, petition and question routes, public hearings and consultations, bounded co-prioritisation and participatory-budget lanes where suitable, representative deliberative processes for complex trade-offs, and visible response duties — so residents can influence the shared tier directly rather than relying on borrowed municipal channels, host-city publicity, or consultation theatre.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a fairly complete constitutional-upgrade ladder for shared territorial power. It can say when co-operation has become real government, what democratic home should follow, what fiscal base it needs, what operating core it needs, what accountability stack it needs, what public-law form it should inhabit, and how it should be wired into the surrounding intergovernmental order.

What it still lacked was the **participation analogue**. A metropolitan or regional tier may have an assembly, a budget, staff, audit, legal personality, and standing consultation with the state, yet still remain civically half-built if ordinary residents can only reach it indirectly through their home municipality, through sporadic project consultations, or through host-city channels that do not cover the whole territory.

That gap matters because much of the work done by shared territorial government is exactly the kind of work that demands broader civic input: growth strategies, transport networks, water systems, housing targets, climate adaptation, waste systems, large capital investment, and spatial trade-offs that cross municipal lines. If those choices are really made at metropolitan or regional scale, then the public should not have to guess which town hall, operator, or ministry might still be listening.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine for the **participatory home of shared territorial government**: when a real metropolitan or regional tier must stop borrowing participation legitimacy from its component governments and build its own visible civic-entry routes.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat participation as a direct route into the shared tier, not as a side effect of member-government access

Once a metropolitan or regional body governs real territory, residents should be able to influence it **as that tier**. They should not have to rely only on lobbying their municipality and hoping the issue survives several bargaining tables.

The archive should therefore prefer direct participation lanes at the shared territorial tier once its power is real: publishable notice rules, hearing routes, petitions, consultation channels, and response duties that attach to the metropolitan or regional body itself.

### 2. Build participation around the territory actually being governed

Shared territorial power usually arises because the real geography of the problem outruns one municipality: a labour market, travel shed, growth corridor, basin, resilience zone, or logistics region. Participation design should follow that same geography.

The archive should reject systems in which the governing territory is metropolitan or regional, but the participation architecture remains purely municipal and fragmented. That is how residents end up able to speak only from their postcode while the consequential bargain is struck somewhere else.

### 3. Give the tier its own notice, records, and public-hearing spine

A participatory home begins with basic civic legibility. People must be able to tell when a question is live, what stage it is at, what materials exist, where hearings or consultation windows are open, and what happened after the public spoke.

The archive should therefore prefer a visible **territorial notice spine**: one public calendar, one publication route for agendas and draft plans, one hearing register, one consultation docket, one place to find responses and follow-up. A shared territorial government should not require residents to monitor several municipal portals, contractor websites, and ministry announcements just to learn that a metropolitan decision is underway.

### 4. Keep several participation lanes distinct, but attach each to a response duty

The shared territorial tier should not collapse all public input into one bland “engagement” label. It should distinguish at least:

- **petition and question routes** for surfacing issues or demanding response,
- **hearings and consultations** for draft plans or rule-shaping choices,
- **co-prioritisation or participatory-budget lanes** for bounded discretionary envelopes where suitable,
- **representative deliberative processes** for complex trade-offs that need civic learning and collective judgement.

The archive should then insist that each lane has an explicit response rule. A petition should receive an answer. A hearing should produce a response note or revision memo. A deliberative process should trigger a formal public answer from the elected or responsible body. A participatory-budget lane should publish the spend envelope, winning projects, and delivery status.

### 5. Use representative deliberation for shared trade-offs that cannot be handled honestly by open-mic ritual alone

Metropolitan and regional choices often involve long-horizon trade-offs that ordinary hearings handle poorly: people with more time and organization dominate the microphone, while the hardest cross-boundary bargains remain under-processed.

The archive should therefore prefer **representative deliberative processes** for especially complex or polarised territorial questions: climate adaptation packages, corridor redesign, housing and growth trade-offs, airport or port expansion, basin resilience, or other issues where place, evidence, and distributive consequences collide.

But deliberation must remain a complement, not a constitutional substitute. The shared tier should not use citizens’ assemblies or other mini-publics as legitimacy theatre while keeping the real decisions elsewhere.

### 6. Do not let host cities or large member governments monopolize the participation channel

One recurrent distortion in metropolitan governance is **host-city ventriloquism**: the largest municipality holds the best communications machine, the biggest hearing rooms, the strongest media pull, and the easiest public-recognition route, so the public experiences the whole metropolitan question as if it belonged to that city alone.

The archive should therefore prefer designs in which the shared territorial tier has its own public channel and territorial outreach obligations, including meetings, materials, and response paths that are not captured by the dominant municipality.

### 7. Make participation accessible across the territory, not only technically open in theory

An honest participatory home must be reachable by people across the whole governed territory. That means more than a web form.

The archive should therefore prefer:

- online and offline entry routes,
- materials in plain language and relevant languages where needed,
- hybrid hearings where distance is real,
- disability-accessible formats,
- timings and locations that do not assume one urban center,
- and outreach to places, groups, and commuters who are routinely governed by the metropolitan or regional tier but weakly represented in ordinary participation rituals.

A body that technically permits comment but functionally centers one district, one class of repeat player, or one professional advocacy circuit has not built a territorial participatory home.

### 8. Use co-prioritisation and participatory budgeting only for bounded envelopes, not to allocate basic territorial rights

At metropolitan and regional level, participatory budgeting or similar co-prioritisation tools can help shape small capital envelopes, neighborhood interfaces to larger systems, environmental improvements, cultural investments, or other bounded discretionary choices.

The archive should strongly resist, however, asking residents to ration core entitlements or territorially basic services through competitive participation rituals. A shared territorial government should not offload structural inequalities in transport access, flood protection, water quality, or housing fairness into a public contest for scraps.

### 9. Keep participation visibly linked to the representative and scrutiny chain

Participation channels are most honest when people can see where their voice goes next. Petition and consultation routes should connect to a chamber, committee, executive, or named officer with a duty to answer. Deliberative recommendations should meet a recorded vote, response memorandum, or equivalent formal uptake path. Participatory-budget outputs should enter the real budget and delivery system.

The archive should reject **participation without docking** — processes that collect civic voice but never visibly attach to the responsible representative or administrative chain.

### 10. Treat major territorial plans as requiring a participation architecture, not a one-off hearing minimum

Large metropolitan and regional plans often structure housing, mobility, land use, environmental risk, and major capital decisions for years. The archive should therefore prefer a participation architecture proportionate to that consequence: staged notice, early issue-framing, draft consultation, hearing phases, targeted outreach to affected places, structured response documents, and visible revision before adoption.

This is not participation maximalism. It is a recognition that a long-horizon territorial plan cannot be made democratically honest by one comment window at the end.

## Participatory-home matrix

| Question | Thin or dishonest arrangement | Honest participatory home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **How do residents know a metropolitan or regional decision is live?** | scattered notices across member portals and project sites | one territorial notice and consultation spine |
| **How can people surface problems or demand response?** | must lobby a municipality and hope it relays the issue | direct petition and question routes to the shared tier |
| **How are complex territorial trade-offs discussed?** | open-mic ritual or expert-only planning | representative deliberation plus formal response |
| **Who controls the public channel?** | host city, ministry, or dominant operator | the shared territorial tier itself, with visible outreach duties |
| **What happens after participation?** | acknowledgement without uptake path | response memo, recorded vote, revised draft, or delivery follow-through |
| **Who can realistically take part?** | insiders near the center or skilled repeat players | multi-channel, accessible, territory-wide participation design |

## Failure modes

- **participation by postcode**: the real governing scale is metropolitan or regional, but people can only influence it through fragmented municipal routes.
- **host-city public monopoly**: the largest municipality effectively becomes the public face and hearing gatekeeper for the whole territory.
- **consultation theatre at scale**: major territorial decisions carry public-input rituals but no visible response duty or revision path.
- **assembly substitution by mini-public**: deliberative bodies are used to excuse a weak representative chain rather than complement it.
- **territorial notice maze**: agendas, hearings, plans, and response papers are scattered across too many portals for ordinary residents to track.
- **center-of-gravity bias**: participation is formally open, but practically clustered around one city, language group, or professional civic class.
- **budget contest cruelty**: participatory tools are used to ration basic territorial services or equal-rights floors.
- **participation without docking**: public input never clearly reaches the chamber, executive, or budget process that must answer for it.

## Practical tests

A shared territorial body has an honest participatory home when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can residents reach the metropolitan or regional tier directly, rather than only through component governments or informal backchannels?
2. Is there one intelligible territorial notice-and-records route for live hearings, consultations, draft plans, and responses?
3. Are petition, hearing, consultation, deliberative, and bounded co-prioritisation lanes kept distinct and linked to explicit response duties?
4. Does the participation architecture cover the whole governed territory rather than mainly the dominant municipality or administrative center?
5. Are accessibility, language, timing, digital/offline parity, and distance handled seriously enough that participation is usable in practice?
6. Are complex territorial trade-offs routed, where appropriate, into representative deliberative processes rather than only open-mic ritual or insider bargaining?
7. Do participation outputs visibly dock into the chamber, executive, budget, planning, or scrutiny chain that must answer for them?
8. Are participatory-budget or co-prioritisation tools bounded so they do not turn basic rights or territorial fairness into a competition for leftovers?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a metropolitan or regional tier now governs real territory, but residents still cannot plainly say **where they are notified, where they can petition or be heard, how complex territorial bargains receive structured civic input, and which body must publicly answer**, then it still lacks **a participatory home for shared territorial power** and is drifting into **metropolitan participation by postcode**.
