# 664 — Obligation and funding formulas by problem class: capacity to pay, equalization, polluter-pays, beneficiary-pays, risk solidarity, and no one-formula federalism

## One-line thesis

Ideal multilevel and global governance should match each shared obligation to the right **apportionment rule** — capacity to pay for common institutions, equalization for rights floors, polluter-pays for harm control, beneficiary-pays for optional service gains, risk-based pooling for insurance and emergency reserves, and differentiated duties where histories and capabilities materially differ — so solidarity is explicit and reviewable rather than smuggled through arbitrary shares, annual bargaining, or one formula pasted across every tier and problem.

## Why this matters

The archive already has notes on **fiscal equalization** (`489`), on **mandate-finance congruence** (`581`), on the **canonical government matrix by scope** (`660`), and now on the **global institutional toolkit** (`662`). What it still lacked was one tighter doctrine for the question that recurs whenever several governments must pay, contribute, or carry obligations together:

**by what formula?**

That sounds technical, but it is constitutional.

A higher-scope institution becomes dishonest when it asks members to contribute money, carry refugees, cut emissions, host infrastructure, maintain reserves, or absorb losses without saying why the burden is shared that way. The formula determines whether the regime is an equal club, an insurance pool, a redistributive union, a harm-correction mechanism, or a beneficiary scheme. When that distinction stays implicit, politics curdles into resentment, ad hoc bargaining, or ritual accusation that someone else is free-riding.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine for **apportionment honesty**. Different public problems justify different burden rules. Good government should declare the rule family openly and review it as conditions change.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate "who benefits", "who caused harm", "who can afford more", and "who faces higher need"

These are not the same question.

A regime may be tempted to compress them into one contribution key, but that usually blurs unlike moral and institutional logics:

- **capacity to pay** asks who can contribute more without comparable sacrifice;
- **equalization** asks where revenue capacity and expenditure need diverge from acceptable service floors;
- **polluter-pays** asks who should bear the cost of preventing, controlling, or remedying harm they impose;
- **beneficiary-pays** asks who gains from a service, asset, or amenity and should therefore help finance it;
- **risk pooling** asks who should contribute to a common reserve against uncertain shocks;
- **differentiated responsibility** asks whether history, capability, development stage, or structural exposure justify non-uniform duties.

The archive should force designers to name which family they are using, rather than hiding behind generic solidarity language.

### 2. Use capacity to pay for the standing costs of common institutions

Some shared institutions exist as common constitutional machinery: assemblies, secretariats, courts, registries, inspection systems, and other core bodies that make the regime possible at all.

For these, **capacity to pay** is often the cleanest rule. United Nations assessed contributions are grounded in this logic. The point is not that every member benefits equally or caused the same harm. The point is that a common institution should be sustainably financed without overburdening weaker members.

The archive should therefore usually prefer capacity-to-pay rules for the **standing overhead** of real common institutions.

### 3. Use equalization where self-government exists under common rights floors

A decentralized polity that expects municipalities, regions, or states to provide comparable public floors cannot simply say "raise your own revenue" and walk away. OECD work on fiscal equalization makes the basic point: different territories face different fiscal capacities and service costs, and equalization exists to offset those differences so public services do not become a postcode lottery.

The archive should therefore prefer **equalization formulas** when the purpose is to reconcile territorial self-government with universal rights floors, comparable tax effort, or remoteness and cost disadvantages. Equalization is not charity. It is the fiscal companion of shared citizenship.

### 4. Use polluter-pays where the object is harm prevention, control, or remediation

Not every shared burden should be socialized.

Where a party imposes measurable environmental or analogous harm, the archive should generally prefer **polluter-pays** logic for prevention and control costs. OECD has long treated this as a basic cost-allocation principle. Climate, pollution, hazardous waste, and some congestion regimes lose honesty when the injurer's role is hidden inside general-budget rhetoric.

This does not mean polluter-pays solves every problem alone. But the archive should not let a regime call itself fair while forcing the general public or exposed territories to subsidize avoidable externalization.

### 5. Use beneficiary-pays where the gain is optional, localized, or user-specific

Some costs are better allocated not by guilt or solidarity but by gain.

A transport extension that sharply raises nearby land values, a specialized export corridor, a premium local amenity, or a water-management improvement may justify **beneficiary-pays** or user-pays contributions. OECD water-finance work treats this as a real complement to polluter-pays.

The archive should therefore prefer beneficiary logic where:

- gains are concentrated,
- users can be identified,
- and full socialization would obscure who is receiving the extra value.

But beneficiary-pays should not be used to price basic rights out of reach. It is better suited to optional gains than to minimum dignified floors.

### 6. Use risk pooling for low-frequency, high-severity shocks

Some burdens are neither routine services nor cleanly attributable harms. They are uncertain events whose costs explode if each government stands alone: disasters, insurance backstops, macro shocks, epidemics, financial crises, or major crop failure.

There the honest formula is usually some mix of:

- pre-agreed contributions into a common reserve,
- contribution keys tied to exposure and capacity,
- clear payout triggers,
- and ex post solidarity where the reserve proves insufficient.

The archive should therefore treat **risk solidarity** as its own formula family. Insurance logic should not be confused with equalization, and emergency burden sharing should not be improvised each time catastrophe strikes.

### 7. Use differentiated duties where history and capability make strict symmetry dishonest

Some common problems are formally shared but materially unequal.

The UN climate regime uses the language of **common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities** to capture exactly this reality: all parties bear obligations, but not all obligations are identical, because histories, capabilities, and circumstances differ.

The archive should therefore allow differentiated schedules, timetables, reporting burdens, or financing duties where asymmetry is real and publicly justified. But differentiation should be criteria-based and reviewable. Otherwise it degenerates into permanent exception politics.

### 8. Use mixed formulas openly when the regime really has several jobs at once

Many regimes combine several functions:

- a standing secretariat,
- a rights-floor transfer system,
- a pollution-control rule,
- and an emergency reserve.

In that case, the archive should resist the temptation to invent one grand magic key. The more honest move is often to separate the budget into typed layers:

- one **capacity-to-pay** key for core institutional overhead,
- one **equalization** formula for territorial floors,
- one **polluter-pays** stream for harms,
- one **beneficiary** contribution for optional gains,
- and one **risk pool** for shocks.

A mixed regime should show that mix visibly.

### 9. Review formulas periodically, because geography, wealth, exposure, and technology move

A good formula can become a bad one by drift.

Population changes, climate risk shifts, extraction patterns move, transport networks mature, local tax bases widen or collapse, and capabilities change. A regime that never revisits its contribution key can slowly convert a once-honest bargain into a stale constitutional wound.

The archive should therefore require visible review periods, updated data, and challenge lanes for material drift. No formula should become sacred merely because it is old.

## Formula matrix

| Problem class | Primary honest formula | Typical use | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Standing constitutional overhead of a shared institution | capacity to pay | secretariat, court, registry, inspection backbone | equal nominal shares that crush weaker members |
| Common rights floors across self-governing territories | equalization by fiscal capacity and expenditure need | local government floors, remoteness compensation, universal service | pretending local autonomy can survive with no fiscal correction |
| Preventing or remedying harm imposed by identifiable actors | polluter-pays | emissions, effluent, hazardous waste, congestion externalities | socializing avoidable externalities into general revenues |
| Financing optional or concentrated gains | beneficiary-pays or user-pays | premium amenities, land-value gains, export corridor benefits | making the general public subsidize private or localized upside |
| Low-frequency, high-severity shared shocks | risk-based pooling plus solidarity backstop | disaster funds, insurance pools, epidemic reserves | annual improvisation after the shock arrives |
| Shared duty under unequal history and capability | differentiated responsibility | climate obligations, transition timelines, reporting flexibilities | rigid symmetry that ignores capacity and responsibility gaps |
| Regimes with several distinct functions | mixed typed formulas | unions, compacts, common funds with several missions | one opaque contribution key pretending to do every job |

## Guardrails

- Do not use one formula merely because it is administratively convenient.
- Do not fund equal citizenship or minimum rights floors through beneficiary logic alone.
- Do not hide pollution or extraction costs inside generic solidarity rhetoric.
- Do not turn differentiated responsibility into unreviewable permanent privilege.
- Do not let insurance-style regimes pretend they are ordinary equalization systems.
- Do not let common institutions survive on annual bargaining if they plainly require standing assessed finance.

## Failure modes

- **one-formula federalism**: the same contribution key is pasted across overhead, equalization, remediation, and emergency reserve functions.
- **solidarity fog**: burdens are shared, but the regime cannot say whether it is using capacity, need, harm, benefit, or risk logic.
- **postcode austerity by autonomy rhetoric**: lower tiers are told to self-govern while equalization is starved.
- **pollution socialization**: the public pays for harms that identifiable actors should control or remedy.
- **beneficiary camouflage**: concentrated gains are financed as though everyone benefited equally.
- **frozen asymmetry**: differentiated responsibility becomes a permanent political carve-out with no review.

## Practical tests

A burden-sharing regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Has it named the exact formula family used for each major obligation or budget stream?
2. If it funds common institutions, is the core overhead financed by a sustainable capacity-sensitive rule?
3. If it promises comparable territorial service floors, is there a real equalization mechanism tied to capacity and need?
4. If the regime addresses a harm, does the cost allocation visibly reflect polluter responsibility where feasible?
5. If the regime finances concentrated gains, are beneficiary or user contributions considered openly?
6. If the regime is about catastrophic or uncertain shocks, is there a real reserve or pooling design rather than ex post improvisation alone?
7. If responsibilities are differentiated, are the criteria, review period, and adjustment path public?
8. If several formula families coexist, are they budgeted and explained separately?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity or treaty can describe **what everyone is supposed to contribute or carry** but cannot say **which formula family justifies each burden, what data updates it, and how drift or dispute will be reviewed**, it still has **shared obligations without an honest burden constitution**.
