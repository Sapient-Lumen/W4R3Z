# 585 — Conditional grants, matching funds, competitive programs, and no government by grant maze

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat **intergovernmental grants as constitutional instruments rather than administrative weather**: ordinary local discretion and equal citizenship should rest mainly on own-source revenue plus predictable general and equalization transfers; earmarked, matching, performance, capital, or competitive grants should be reserved for narrow cases where rights floors, spillovers, transition priorities, or shared investment genuinely justify them; co-financing duties should scale to fiscal capacity; application, reporting, and audit rules should be harmonized and limited; and no higher tier should be allowed to govern municipalities, metros, or regions mainly by forcing them to live inside a permanent grant maze.

## Why this matters

The archive now has stronger rules for **scope allocation** (`579`), **mandate-finance congruence** (`581`), **capability congruence** (`582`), **intergovernmental machinery** (`542`), and **preemption by higher tiers** (`584`). What it still lacked was one compact doctrine for a very common real-world operating system of centralization: **government by grant program**.

That failure appears in several forms. A constitution promises local self-government, but ordinary local services depend on dozens of earmarked grants whose rules are rewritten every budget cycle. A ministry says a function is “decentralized,” yet municipalities can act only if they win a competitive bid written around national priorities. Matching-fund requirements quietly exclude poorer places from capital programs. Reporting, procurement, and audit rules multiply grant by grant until thin local administrations spend more time feeding funding machinery than solving local problems. A higher tier avoids saying it has reclaimed a power, but it achieves the same result by attaching operationally irresistible conditions to money.

The result is a recurring pathology: **government by grant maze**. Local and regional governments remain nominally responsible, but real governing discretion migrates upward into application templates, approval letters, release conditions, and ever-thickening compliance files. That is not neutral public finance. It is a constitutional technique.

The archive therefore needs a narrower rule. **Grant design should follow the constitutional logic of the function being financed.** Money meant to preserve local discretion should arrive in ways that actually preserve discretion. Money meant to secure a common national floor should say so openly. Money meant to coordinate shared investment should not quietly become permanent micro-management.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish the grant types before arguing about their virtues

The archive should force the polity to say what kind of grant it is using.

- **general-purpose or block transfers** support broad local or regional discretion;
- **equalization transfers** protect equal citizenship across unequal tax bases and needs;
- **specific-purpose or earmarked grants** fund a named function or program;
- **matching grants or co-financing grants** require the receiving government to contribute funds of its own;
- **performance grants** tie some allocation or release to measured institutional or service results;
- **competitive grants** allocate money through proposals, contests, or discretionary selection rather than through stable formulas;
- **capital grants** fund infrastructure, transition investments, or other major one-off or multi-year projects.

These are not interchangeable. A constitution that treats all transfers as one blurred bucket cannot tell whether it is protecting self-government, coordinating a shared objective, or displacing lower-tier discretion by stealth.

### 2. Fund ordinary local discretion mainly through own-source revenue, general grants, and equalization

Where the function is genuinely local or regional in judgment and adaptation — routine place services, ordinary maintenance, local public space, neighborhood facilities, many elements of service delivery, and comparable daily responsibilities — the default should not be a permanent stack of earmarked grants.

The archive should continue to prefer a base made of:

- visible own-source fiscal room,
- stable formula-based general grants,
- and equalization strong enough to stop inherited tax base from becoming inherited citizenship.

That is the grant form that matches self-government. If a higher tier says a place may decide locally, but most of the money arrives only through tightly specified program grants, the polity is not financing autonomy. It is staging autonomy.

### 3. Reserve conditional grants for narrow and publishable reasons

Conditional grants do have a constitutional role. The archive should not abolish them. It should narrow them.

A higher tier can justify conditional or earmarked grants where the grant is clearly serving one or more of the following:

- a national rights floor or universal service guarantee,
- a genuine spillover or system-integration problem,
- a transition priority that requires temporary alignment,
- a large capital program where sequencing and interoperability matter,
- a temporary capability-building or reform effort,
- or a shock response where speed and protected use are necessary.

The key discipline is that the reason must be explicit. “Because the center prefers control” is not a constitutional category.

### 4. Do not use competitive grants for core entitlements or routine viability

Competitive funds can sometimes surface local initiative, pilot innovation, or allocate genuinely scarce one-off capital. But the archive should strongly resist building ordinary territorial viability around grant competitions.

A municipality should not need to win a national contest to keep a clinic open, repair basic water lines, maintain statutory records, or meet routine service obligations. Competitive grants are a poor fit for basic entitlements because they reward proposal-writing capacity, consultant access, and political legibility more than need, legal duty, or territorial fairness.

The archive should therefore prefer formulas for:

- equalization,
- ordinary service floors,
- predictable maintenance support,
- and recurring capital renewal lanes.

Competitions should sit at the margin for experimental or distinctly discretionary initiatives, not at the core of subnational survival.

### 5. Scale matching and co-financing requirements to fiscal capacity, or they become exclusion devices

Matching grants can be useful when the purpose is to elicit local commitment, discipline project selection, or reflect the reality that benefits and costs are shared. But a flat matching requirement often punishes exactly the places the program claims to help.

The archive should therefore prefer co-financing rules that are:

- lower or waived for fiscally weaker, remote, fragile, or distressed places,
- calibrated to objective fiscal-capacity indicators,
- and published in advance rather than negotiated ad hoc.

Otherwise the center announces a universal opportunity that wealthier jurisdictions can absorb and poorer ones can only admire. The grant then widens territorial inequality while pretending to solve it.

### 6. Make conditions outcome-facing and system-facing, not line-item colonial

Where conditions are justified, they should focus on what the higher tier is legitimately entitled to protect: service floors, rights compliance, interoperability, financial integrity, delivery milestones, maintenance of the asset, or measurable reform steps that are genuinely attributable to the receiving government.

The archive should resist hyper-detailed line-item and process control when a lighter instrument would do the job. A central government usually does not need to specify every staff title, every procurement sub-step, every output format, or every local sequencing choice in order to protect a lawful national interest.

A good grant condition therefore tends to be:

- relevant to the stated public interest,
- proportionate to the grant type,
- objectively verifiable,
- and compatible with lawful local adaptation.

Otherwise a grant becomes a disguised operating manual for subnational government.

### 7. Harmonize calendars, applications, reporting, audit, and data rules across grant streams

A great deal of fiscal centralization now happens through administrative burden rather than through open legal override. The archive should therefore treat grant-system coherence as a constitutional matter, not a back-office nicety.

Where several grant programs coexist, the polity should align as much as possible:

- budget calendars and release schedules,
- application windows and documentation requirements,
- procurement and financial-management rules,
- reporting frequencies and indicator definitions,
- audit expectations,
- and digital submission interfaces.

The aim is not one giant uniform bureaucratic machine. The aim is to prevent subnational governments — especially small or thin-capacity ones — from being trapped inside dozens of inconsistent micro-regimes whose main common feature is their indifference to local administrative limits.

### 8. Pay for the administrative work and technical assistance that grant systems demand

A serious grant constitution should be honest that application-writing, procurement, data collection, reporting, and assurance work cost real time and money. If a higher tier wants elaborate conditions, multi-stage approvals, or performance measurement, it should not pretend that local compliance is free.

The archive should therefore prefer grant systems that:

- allow reasonable administrative-cost coverage,
- include technical-assistance lanes where capacity is thin,
- finance shared back-office support where appropriate,
- and publish whether compliance costs are expected to come from grant proceeds, local budgets, or higher-tier support.

Unfunded compliance is just another unfunded mandate wearing a managerial accent.

### 9. Separate temporary reform grants from permanent operating constitutions

Some grant programs properly exist to change a system: digitize records, build a treatment plant, consolidate a fragmented service platform, raise public financial management standards, or support a time-bounded transition. Those grants should behave like transition machinery, not like immortal constitutional furniture.

The archive should therefore require every reform or transition grant to say:

- what ordinary fiscal lane replaces it afterward,
- which conditions expire with the transition,
- what ongoing costs remain,
- and what happens if the reform partially succeeds but does not fully close.

Without that discipline, temporary grants become a perpetual substitute for durable fiscal settlement.

### 10. Treat grant conditions as override-capable instruments and review them accordingly

The archive has already tightened the doctrine of hidden preemption by money. This note extends that rule. If a grant is so essential that a lower tier cannot realistically refuse it, then its conditions should be treated as **override-capable constitutional instruments**.

That means the polity should be able to ask:

- what lawful interest the condition protects,
- whether the condition is proportionate,
- whether the same goal could be reached through a general grant plus standards or consultation,
- what discretion remains below,
- and what remedy exists if the condition effectively displaces a lower-tier power.

No constitution should permit hard recentralization merely by routing commands through treasury vocabulary.

### 11. Keep formula logic public and discretionary grant decisions reviewable

Grant systems should be unusually legible. For formula grants, the formula, weights, update rules, floors, ceilings, hold-harmless measures, and data sources should be public. For discretionary or competitive grants, the program should publish criteria, scoring, assessor identity or class, conflict safeguards, and reasons for major decisions.

The archive should resist opaque allocation because money is one of the fastest ways to rewrite a territorial constitution without admitting it. If no one can explain why one municipality received a grant, why another failed, or why conditions changed midstream, then the grant system is doing constitutional work in the dark.

### 12. Protect a right to consultation before major grant redesign or re-earmarking

Because redistributed resources shape the real scope of self-government, large changes in grant architecture should not be sprung on subnational governments as a completed fact. The archive should therefore prefer a visible consultation duty before:

- major re-earmarking of general grants,
- redesign of equalization or matching formulas,
- merger or abolition of large grant programs,
- introduction of new reporting or digital compliance systems,
- or conversion of broad grants into competitive bidding regimes.

Consultation should occur early enough to affect the design, not only late enough to stage polite resentment.

### 13. Build handback, consolidation, and simplification clocks into the grant system itself

A healthy polity should periodically ask whether multiple grant programs could be merged, whether conditionality can be lightened, whether a temporary special lane can be absorbed into a general formula, and whether mature local or regional capacity now allows more discretion than before.

The archive should therefore prefer review clocks that ask:

- does this condition still solve a live problem,
- can two or more grants be consolidated,
- can reporting be reduced,
- can performance conditions be simplified,
- and has the case for a competitive lane weakened enough that a formula would now be fairer?

Otherwise every historical compromise becomes a permanent administrative sediment layer.

## Guardrails

- Do not fund ordinary local self-government mainly through proliferating earmarked and competitive programs.
- Do not use flat matching requirements where fiscal capacity differs sharply across places.
- Do not turn every grant into a separate reporting and audit universe.
- Do not treat temporary reform grants as immortal substitutes for durable fiscal settlement.
- Do not claim decentralization while governing essential services through irresistible grant conditions.

## Failure modes

- **grant maze**: subnational governments survive by navigating overlapping application, reporting, release, and audit regimes rather than by governing.
- **competitive survivalism**: basic territorial viability depends on winning contests instead of receiving predictable lawful support.
- **matching exclusion**: poorer places self-exclude from capital and transition programs because the required local contribution is structurally out of reach.
- **line-item colonialism**: higher tiers control minute local operating choices through earmarks and release conditions rather than through explicit law.
- **temporary-permanent drift**: a reform or emergency grant becomes a standing fiscal constitution by habit.
- **soft recentralization by treasury language**: ministers deny preemption while using money to erase subnational discretion.

## Practical tests

A polity is handling intergovernmental grants honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can it distinguish clearly between general, equalization, earmarked, matching, performance, competitive, and capital grants?
2. Are ordinary local and regional discretionary functions financed mainly through own-source room plus stable formula transfers rather than through recurring competitions?
3. When grants are conditional, is the protected national or shared interest stated explicitly?
4. Are co-financing rules scaled to fiscal capacity so poorer places are not structurally excluded?
5. Are applications, reporting, audit, and release rules harmonized enough that local governments are not buried in avoidable administrative duplication?
6. Does the grant system finance or otherwise support the compliance work it demands?
7. Can the polity explain which grant conditions are temporary and what permanent lane replaces them?
8. Is there consultation and review when major grant architecture changes narrow subnational discretion?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **which level formally owns a function and how much money it transfers**, but cannot also show **which grant type is being used, why any condition is justified, how poorer places are protected from matching exclusion, how the administrative burden is contained, and when temporary grant controls expire or hand back into ordinary finance**, then it still has **intergovernmental finance without a constitutional doctrine of grant rule**.
