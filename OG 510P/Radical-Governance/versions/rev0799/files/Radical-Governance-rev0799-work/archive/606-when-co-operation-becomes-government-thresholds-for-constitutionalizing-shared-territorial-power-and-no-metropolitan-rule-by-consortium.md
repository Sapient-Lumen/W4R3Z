# 606 — When co-operation becomes government: thresholds for constitutionalizing shared territorial power, and no metropolitan rule by consortium

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should let municipalities and regions co-operate freely for shared capacity and service delivery, but once a joint body starts making repeated cross-boundary trade-offs across several sectors, raising compulsory money, issuing debt, binding land use or major infrastructure choices, or functioning as the real political center of a territory, it should be constitutionalized as honest metropolitan or regional government rather than left as a consortium, summit, or quango stack.

## Why this matters

The archive already contains notes on intermunicipal co-operation, special-purpose authorities, ideal governments by scope, diagnosis of scope misfit, and repair ladders for a bad multilevel stack. What it still lacked was one compact threshold doctrine for the hardest practical line-drawing question in this family: **when is a shared body still just co-operation, and when has it become undeclared government?**

That gap matters because live systems often hide constitutional choices inside apparently technical arrangements. A transport consortium begins to decide housing access. A development authority starts to shape land use, utility routing, resilience spending, and debt across several municipalities. A mayoral council becomes the place where the real metropolitan budget bargain happens, even though no metropolitan electorate can throw it out. A field that began as shared service delivery turns into a territorial politics engine without ever being admitted as such.

The result is not merely administrative untidiness. It is a legitimacy error. Residents are governed by a body whose powers, money, and strategic reach now exceed the narrow co-operation or single-purpose rationale that first justified it. The archive therefore needs one compact rule for the moment when a polity should stop pretending it is only coordinating and instead build an honest democratic home for the work.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat co-operation as a real institution, but not as a universal constitutional solvent

Intermunicipal and intergovernmental co-operation are healthy parts of a decentralised system. They let small jurisdictions pool staff, procure jointly, run shared facilities, and solve spillover problems without forced merger or quiet recentralisation.

But the archive should reject the habit of treating co-operation as a device that can absorb any amount of territorial power without changing institutional form. Co-operation is a good instrument. It is not a permanent excuse for avoiding the question of who really governs a shared territory.

### 2. Start from the functional geography, then ask whether the politics has also become shared

The first reason to pool power is usually geographic: commuting zones, watersheds, corridors, hospital catchments, waste sheds, labour markets, flood systems, or growth areas do not stop at one municipal boundary. That functional geography can justify co-operation.

The next question is whether the relevant **politics** has also become shared. If the body is no longer coordinating one bounded service but is repeatedly arbitrating distribution, growth, pricing, risk, and investment across the territory, then the problem is no longer only technical. It has become constitutional.

### 3. Keep the co-operation lane for narrow, bounded, and reversible tasks

The archive should continue to prefer ordinary co-operation where the arrangement remains recognisably narrow. Typical signs that the co-operation lane is still honest include:

- one or a few well-bounded functions,
- limited and explainable geography,
- member governments retaining the real political mandate,
- finance based mainly on transparent contributions or service charges,
- easy public explanation of who decides, who pays, and how the arrangement could be changed.

This is the natural zone for shared procurement, joint technical offices, dispatch centers, treatment plants, specialist staff pools, and other capacity-sharing tools.

### 4. Constitutionalize once the body makes multi-sector territorial trade-offs

A body has crossed the threshold toward real government when it no longer manages one narrow system but repeatedly arbitrates across several sectors that shape the same territory. Transport plus land use. Housing plus infrastructure plus resilience. Economic development plus major capital planning plus utility routing. Public safety plus data systems plus emergency coordination. These are not merely parallel technical tasks. They are trade-off fields that change who benefits, who pays, what gets built, and which places grow or decline.

At that point the polity should ask whether it is still looking at co-operation or at a hidden metropolitan or regional constitution.

### 5. Constitutionalize once money becomes compulsory, debt-bearing, redistributive, or system-shaping

The threshold is crossed more clearly when the shared body can:

- impose compulsory charges or tax-like levies,
- issue significant debt or bind future revenue,
- redistribute resources among municipalities as a governing choice,
- or determine investment sequencing that materially advantages some places over others.

These are not just operating details. They are core public-government decisions. A body that can make them needs a legitimacy, audit, review, and representation structure proportionate to them.

### 6. Constitutionalize once the arrangement binds people who cannot realistically exit or consent route-by-route

Voluntary co-operation among municipalities can remain honest even when residents are indirectly affected through their own local governments. But the design becomes harder to defend once the shared body governs people who cannot meaningfully avoid its rules and who have no clear route to contest or replace its leadership.

If a territorial body can determine mandatory service conditions, spatial-development outcomes, pricing structures, or emergency restrictions for a whole functional area, then the democratic question can no longer be answered merely by saying that some member governments sit around the table.

### 7. Constitutionalize when the body becomes the real forum of territorial bargaining

A practical signal matters as much as formal legal power: where do the real bargains happen? If mayors, ministries, utility chiefs, developers, and agencies all know that the true decisions for a territory now happen in the metropolitan transport board, growth cabinet, or regional compact council, then the archive should treat that body as politically consequential whether or not legal texts still describe it as co-operation.

When the room that makes the deal is no longer the same room the public can actually judge, the stack has drifted into shadow constitutionalism.

### 8. Treat authority proliferation as evidence that a general-purpose territorial tier may now be the honest answer

One consortium or one utility district may be sensible. But when a territory is run through a growing package of transport bodies, planning boards, housing corporations, climate authorities, resilience platforms, and pooled finance vehicles, the problem may no longer be fragmentation alone. The problem may be that the polity is refusing to admit the need for a metropolitan or regional government.

The archive should therefore treat **multi-body accumulation** as a threshold signal. At some point, stitching together several narrow entities produces a wider territorial constitution without one visible democratic home.

### 9. When the threshold is crossed, upgrade to a minimum complete democratic kit

Once a shared body is doing real government, the honest response is not necessarily maximum institutional grandeur. But it does require a **minimum complete kit**: a public forum or assembly with intelligible membership and scrutiny powers, an executive chain that can be named and blamed, transparent finance, public records, lawful review routes, and a mandate sized to the territory and functions actually being governed.

In some polities that may mean a real metropolitan government. In others it may mean a stronger regional tier, a directly elected metropolitan assembly, or another hard-law body with clearer democratic anchoring. The crucial point is not the label. It is the end of rule by workaround.

### 10. Preserve a review path for arrangements that remain below the threshold

Not every joint body should be upgraded. But every one should know the test it would fail if it outgrew its original rationale. Charters should therefore publish review questions such as: has the remit become multi-sector, have compulsory levies or significant debt appeared, is the body now the real arena of territorial bargaining, and are residents effectively governed without a matching democratic home?

A co-operation arrangement that can never trigger constitutional reconsideration is too easy a vehicle for hidden state-building.

## Threshold matrix

| Signal | Still honest co-operation | Threshold crossed toward government |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Function** | one narrow or clearly bundled service | repeated cross-sector trade-offs shaping the territory |
| **Geography** | bounded service area | de facto governing area for growth, mobility, resilience, or redistribution |
| **Finance** | transparent member contributions / service charges | compulsory levies, major debt, redistributive or sequencing power |
| **Politics** | member governments remain visible principals | joint body becomes the real deal-making arena |
| **Citizen relation** | indirect effects mediated through local governments | whole-area residents are bound without a clear democratic home |
| **Institutional ecology** | one or few limited bodies | proliferating boards and authorities make up a shadow territorial constitution |

## Guardrails

- Do not treat intermunicipal co-operation as proof that no stronger tier is needed.
- Do not call a body “technical” once it is repeatedly making distributive or developmental trade-offs.
- Do not let compulsory charges, major debt, or cross-territorial investment sequencing sit inside weakly anchored consortium machinery.
- Do not answer the democratic question solely by saying elected mayors or ministers sit on the board.
- Do not use a stack of narrow authorities to avoid admitting the need for a general-purpose metropolitan or regional tier.
- Do not abolish useful co-operation arrangements merely because constitutionalization is sometimes necessary; many should remain narrow and bounded.
- Do not let threshold review disappear into one-time founding documents; it should recur.

## Failure modes

- **metropolitan rule by consortium**: a shared urban region is really governed through a mayoral or interagency compact that lacks a matching democratic home.
- **technicality theater**: institutions describe strategic distributive decisions as mere coordination work.
- **shadow constitutionalism**: the effective territorial constitution lives in boards, councils, and funding tables rather than in visible public government.
- **quango laddering**: governments keep adding narrow authorities to avoid one honest institutional upgrade.
- **consent fiction**: compulsory or unavoidable territorial rules are defended as voluntary because municipalities nominally joined the arrangement.
- **democracy lag**: functional and fiscal integration advance, but representation and scrutiny remain stuck at an earlier stage.

## Practical tests

A polity is drawing the line honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can it explain which shared bodies are still narrow co-operation and why?
2. Can it name the exact threshold tests that would force reconsideration of that status?
3. If a joint body makes cross-sector trade-offs, compulsory financial decisions, or territory-shaping investment choices, is there a democratic home proportionate to those powers?
4. Can residents tell where the real territorial bargains happen and how to contest them?
5. If several narrow bodies now govern the same functional area, has the polity tested whether a general-purpose metropolitan or regional tier would be the cleaner answer?
6. Are review clocks, charter limits, and upgrade paths visible before shadow government hardens into habit?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity says a shared body is **only co-operation**, but that body now makes the consequential bargains on land, mobility, housing, infrastructure, risk, money, or growth for a whole territory, then the polity is no longer coordinating. It is governing — and it should build **an honest democratic home for that territorial power**.
