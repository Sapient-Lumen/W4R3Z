# 679 — Shared-regime interpretation constitutions: authentic texts, ordinary meaning, context, object and purpose, authoritative interpretations, guidance, and no binding law by helpdesk

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed interpretation constitution** that separates authentic text, ordinary interpretation, subsequent agreement or practice, member-adopted authoritative interpretation, adjudicative clarification, and secretariat guidance — so legal meaning does not drift between frozen literalism and quiet rulemaking by FAQ.

## Why this matters

The archive now has notes on entry and exit (`669`, `670`), representation and decision rules (`671`, `672`), officers and documentary spine (`673`, `674`), initiation and subsidiary bodies (`675`, `676`), external participation and integrity (`677`, `678`), and earlier notes on differentiated commitments, change ladders, and compliance (`666`, `667`, `668`).

That is enough machinery to make and run a shared regime. It is not yet enough to keep interpretation honest.

Every consequential regime eventually faces disputes about **what the text means now**. A phrase looks clear in one language and ambiguous in another. A secretariat publishes a technical note and people start acting as if it were binding. A dispute body clarifies one question in one case and actors begin treating every paragraph as if it were a universal amendment. Members adopt a consensus decision, but nobody can tell whether it is guidance, interpretation, or stealth modification.

Without a typed interpretation constitution, five familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **helpdesk law**: the practical meaning of obligations migrates into FAQs, circulars, slide decks, or staff email.

The second is **frozen-text theater**: actors pretend the regime can only read bare words literally, even where the regime itself has lawful interpretive tools.

The third is **translation opportunism**: one language version, unofficial translation, or convenience summary is quietly treated as controlling.

The fourth is **adjudication drift**: case-specific clarification is mistaken for institution-wide authoritative interpretation.

The fifth is **change laundering**: members or officers use interpretation rhetoric to do work that really belongs to amendment, annex update, or new decision lanes.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **shared-regime interpretation discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Publish one interpretation map for the regime

A serious regime should say in one place:

- which texts are authentic,
- which translations are official, courtesy, or informational only,
- what default interpretive rules apply,
- which organ may adopt an institution-wide authoritative interpretation,
- what adjudicators may clarify in cases,
- what legal status guidance notes or secretariat answers have,
- and when a claimed interpretation is really a change that belongs in the version ladder.

If users must infer legal status from logos, file design, or who sent the email, the regime has not built an interpretation constitution.

### 2. Separate authentic text from convenience text

The archive should prefer a rule that distinguishes:

- authentic language versions,
- officially published translations,
- consolidated or annotated working texts,
- explanatory summaries,
- and unofficial convenience copies.

The point is not pedantry. It is to stop practical governance from moving onto whichever copy is easiest to forward.

### 3. State the default interpretive rule instead of leaving it to folklore

A serious regime should say whether ordinary meaning, context, object and purpose, later agreement, later practice, technical annex logic, or other named rules govern interpretation by default.

This matters because regimes should not oscillate opportunistically between literalism, purposivism, and “everyone knows what this really means” depending on who is speaking.

### 4. Distinguish authoritative interpretation from dispute clarification

A regime should keep separate at least three lanes:

- member-adopted authoritative interpretations with institution-wide effect,
- case-specific adjudicative clarification for resolving a dispute,
- and non-binding explanatory or administrative guidance.

These lanes can interact. They should not collapse into one another.

### 5. Force every guidance product to carry a legal-status label

Circulars, FAQs, implementation handbooks, technical notes, and secretariat letters may be useful. They should not acquire binding force by tone.

The archive should therefore prefer a rule that every guidance product says explicitly whether it is:

- legally binding,
- authoritative but not itself binding,
- presumptively persuasive,
- purely operational,
- or informational only.

If the regime cannot label the legal status of its own explanatory products, it is already governing partly by helpdesk.

### 6. Route actual text change out of the interpretation lane

When a claimed interpretation adds new conditions, removes options, changes thresholds, reallocates powers, or narrows rights more than the existing text can honestly bear, the regime should stop calling it interpretation and send it to the change ladder (`668`).

Interpretation should clarify the existing settlement. It should not quietly refound it.

### 7. Treat later agreements and later practice as typed evidence, not ambient vibes

Members may later agree on interpretation, or repeated practice may become relevant to interpretation. But those facts should be visible.

The archive should therefore prefer one register that distinguishes:

- formal interpretive decisions,
- later inter-member agreements relevant to interpretation,
- documented common practice,
- and unilateral statements that may illuminate but do not settle the law by themselves.

### 8. Control cross-language interpretation openly

Where several authentic languages exist, the regime should say:

- how divergences are flagged,
- who may issue concordance notes,
- whether one working language is operationally privileged without displacing equal authenticity,
- and what happens when a translation conflicts with an authentic text.

No multilingual regime should leave meaning to translation folklore.

### 9. Keep secretariat advice useful but constitutionally modest

Secretariats often answer practical questions. That is compatible with good governance only if the advice lane is typed.

The regime should therefore say:

- when advice is merely administrative,
- when legal services opinions are internal only,
- whether advisory letters may be published,
- whether members may rely on them,
- and what happens if later adjudication or authoritative interpretation points another way.

### 10. Maintain one public interpretation register

A serious regime should be able to show, in one place:

- the text interpreted,
- the interpretive instrument or case,
- the organ or actor,
- the legal status,
- the effective date,
- supersession if any,
- and the relationship to later amendment, protocol, or annex update.

Without that register, the regime will eventually confuse living interpretation with accumulated paper residue.

## Working matrix

| Interpretive object | Honest rule | Why | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| treaty or founding text | apply named default interpretive rules to authentic text and context | keeps interpretation principled | opportunistic switching among interpretive styles |
| member-adopted interpretation | issue through named organ and threshold with institution-wide status | distinguishes law for all from case outcome | treating consensus notes or press lines as equivalent |
| dispute ruling | clarify only what is needed to resolve the case unless the regime says more | preserves adjudicative discipline | converting every case paragraph into general amendment |
| secretariat guidance | carry an explicit legal-status label and citation to authority | keeps help useful without constitutional drift | binding law by FAQ, helpdesk, or workshop slide |
| multilingual divergence | flag and resolve through typed concordance and authentic-text review | protects equal authenticity and legal certainty | letting the most convenient language win by habit |
| apparent interpretive innovation | escalate to change ladder when clarification becomes redesign | preserves lawful adaptation | laundering amendment through interpretation rhetoric |

## Guardrails

- Do not let guidance products hide their legal status.
- Do not let unofficial translations or convenience consolidations displace authentic text.
- Do not treat adjudicative clarification as the same thing as member-adopted authoritative interpretation.
- Do not use interpretation to add duties or remove rights the existing text cannot honestly bear.
- Do not confuse later agreement or later practice with one actor’s unilateral memo.
- Do not let multilingual regimes improvise around cross-language conflict.

## Failure modes

- **binding law by helpdesk**: operational advice quietly becomes the real constitution.
- **translation opportunism**: actors cherry-pick the language version or summary that helps them most.
- **interpretive gaslighting**: the regime claims that an obvious redesign was “always there in the text”.
- **case-to-constitution slippage**: parties treat a dispute ruling as if members had adopted a universal interpretation.
- **frozen-text theater**: the regime denies its own lawful interpretive tools until politics change.
- **registerless drift**: no one can reconstruct which instrument currently carries what interpretive weight.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it publish one interpretation map naming texts, organs, and legal-status lanes?
2. Are authentic texts, official translations, and convenience copies visibly distinct?
3. Is there a named default interpretive rule rather than folklore?
4. Are authoritative interpretations, dispute clarifications, and guidance products typed separately?
5. Does every FAQ, circular, or handbook carry an explicit legal-status label?
6. Is there a clear line where interpretation must escalate into the regime’s change ladder?
7. Are later agreements and later practice captured as typed interpretive evidence rather than rumor?
8. Is there a public register linking interpretations to text, actor, date, status, and supersession?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can say **what its texts are** but cannot say **which copies are authentic, what interpretive rule applies, who may issue institution-wide interpretations, what a case decision does, and why its guidance is or is not binding**, it still has **formal law without an honest interpretation constitution**.
