# 567 — Public borrowing and debt by scope: no neighborhood debt, municipal capital discipline, metro infrastructure, national stabilization, and no government by hidden guarantees

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat borrowing as a scale-matched constitutional power rather than a general permission slip: micro-local bodies should usually not borrow at all, municipalities and regions should borrow mainly for long-lived capital under hard disclosure and restructuring rules, national governments should own macro-stabilization and sovereign debt management openly rather than laundering risk through off-book vehicles, and continental or global borrowing should remain narrow, charter-bound, and formula-backed instead of pretending a thin treaty body is already a full fiscal state.

## Why this matters

The archive already says a great deal about **taxation**, **equalization**, **public investment**, **budgeting**, **public enterprises**, and **financial stability**. What it still lacked was a compact constitutional doctrine for **who should borrow, for what, on what balance sheet, with what rescue boundaries, and with what failure-handling route**.

That absence matters because debt is often where constitutional design turns into theater. A city that cannot tax honestly may hide borrowing in utilities, development corporations, or PPP shells. A region can promise infrastructure without naming the repayment base. A national government can call contingent liabilities “private” until a crisis drags them back on budget. And a federation can preach local autonomy while encouraging everyone to behave as if the center will quietly rescue them at the last minute.

The right archive stance is therefore **hard-budget-constraint constitutionalism without service-collapse nihilism**. Borrowing is not forbidden. It is typed. Its lawful uses should differ by scope. Its risks should stay visible. Its rescue rules should exist before the crisis. And no level of government should be allowed to mint political upside today by pushing opaque debt, guarantees, or refinancing cliffs onto someone else tomorrow.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Do not make the smallest scale a debt issuer: neighborhood and micro-local bodies should usually operate on grants, contributions, and prepaid budgets rather than borrowing

Blocks, neighborhoods, villages, estates, and other genuinely micro-local bodies are usually too small, too informal, and too politically intimate to sustain responsible debt issuance. They rarely have diversified revenue, professional treasury capacity, or a credible path for independent market scrutiny. The archive should therefore treat borrowing at this scale as an exception that must be specially justified rather than as an ordinary power.

The default micro-local design is simpler: small formula grants, member contributions, participatory allocation, maintenance reserves, and transparent project phasing. If a small body needs a roof, pump, lane repair, or hall upgrade, the constitutional answer is usually co-funding, intermunicipal support, or municipal capital planning — not turning a neighborhood committee into a miniature debtor.

### 2. Municipal borrowing is for durable capital, not for disguising operating weakness

Municipalities are the first level at which public borrowing can make durable constitutional sense. They own and maintain long-lived assets — water systems, streets, schools, housing, parks, depots, fleet, and civic buildings — whose benefits span decades. Borrowing can therefore be appropriate when it aligns costs over time and prevents current taxpayers from either overpaying immediately or neglecting needed infrastructure.

But the archive should insist on a hard distinction between **capital finance** and **operating-gap concealment**. Municipal debt should usually be tied to clearly defined capital plans, asset lives, repayment paths, and debt-service affordability. Ordinary payroll, routine maintenance backlogs, and structurally recurring operating deficits should not be pushed into loans, lease gimmicks, supplier arrears, or public-private partnership obligations just because those feel less embarrassing than admitting the budget is broken.

### 3. Metropolitan and regional debt is justified when the asset is genuinely network-scale and the repayment base is visibly shared

Metropolitan and regional governments often manage the kinds of systems that are too large for one municipality but too place-specific for the national treasury to run well: transit networks, bulk water, wastewater systems, flood defenses, energy distribution upgrades, hospitals, vocational platforms, and intercity corridors. Borrowing at these levels can therefore be more appropriate than fragmented local borrowing because the governed asset and the governed repayment base actually line up.

The archive should prefer debt at this scale when three conditions hold together: the project serves a real shared system, the revenue or transfer basis for repayment is explicit, and governance is strong enough that one core city or faction cannot quietly externalize cost onto the rest. Metropolitan or regional debt should sit on a visible public balance sheet with one treasury logic, not in a thicket of special vehicles created mainly to dodge democratic scrutiny or statutory debt limits.

### 4. Every subnational system needs both ex ante borrowing rules and an ex post insolvency or restructuring lane

Borrowing discipline cannot rest only on optimistic rules written for normal times. Even well-designed subnational governments can hit distress through macro shocks, disasters, interest-rate jumps, major employer exits, corruption, or reckless political cycles. An ideal constitutional order therefore needs both **ex ante constraints** and **ex post failure procedures**.

Ex ante, the archive should prefer transparent borrowing authorization, debt-service and liquidity monitoring, medium-term capital plans, stress testing, and clear publication of guarantees, PPP obligations, and enterprise debt that may flow back to the public. Ex post, the archive should insist on an orderly municipal or subnational insolvency or restructuring route that preserves essential services, stops creditor chaos, allocates losses openly, and restores fiscal health without pretending every distress case is either a morality tale or a surprise.

The key principle is **no automatic bailout, but also no improvisation in crisis**. A center that always rescues subnational borrowers teaches everyone to overborrow. A center that refuses any restructuring framework and waits for collapse teaches everyone to hide problems until the last possible minute. The constitution should instead predeclare who can intervene, what services are protected, how debts can be restructured, and what political or fiscal consequences remain with the debtor government.

### 5. National governments own macro-stabilization, sovereign debt strategy, and explicit contingent-liability governance

The national level is different because it typically owns countercyclical fiscal policy, lender-of-last-resort institutions, sovereign debt management, national tax bases, and the legal architecture around guarantees, state-owned enterprises, and banking-sector backstops. The archive should therefore treat national borrowing not as merely a larger municipal balance sheet but as a distinct constitutional function.

An ideal national debt constitution can borrow for recession response, war or emergency subject to law, long-lived public investment, and transition programs that no lower tier can credibly smooth alone. But it should do so on the public balance sheet, through a visible debt-management strategy, with transparent maturity and refinancing choices, and with honest recording of contingent liabilities. Governments should not treat guarantees, PPP commitments, state enterprise debt, supplier arrears, or quasi-fiscal central-bank operations as a shadow zone where real obligations stop counting.

The archive should also prefer sovereign borrowing structures that reduce rollover fragility rather than maximize short-term political convenience. Cheap short maturities, foreign-currency dependence, and hidden guarantees can flatter the present while loading the future with refinancing cliffs and crisis politics.

### 6. Public enterprises, development banks, utilities, and PPP vehicles cannot be used as constitutional escape hatches from debt law

Much bad public borrowing does not appear first as “government debt.” It appears as a utility, land bank, transit company, housing corporation, development finance vehicle, or PPP project that has public purpose, public risk, and eventual public rescue expectations but is narrated as though it were someone else’s balance sheet.

The archive should therefore treat public-sector debt broadly. If a vehicle depends on tax power, monopoly power, public guarantees, mandatory patronage, or a nearly certain bailout expectation, its obligations are part of the real fiscal constitution whether or not officials prefer a narrower headline number. The ideal design is not to ban all enterprise borrowing. It is to stop fake separation. Public corporations may borrow where their mandate and revenue model justify it, but the sponsoring polity should disclose exposures, bound guarantees, and prevent off-budget entities from becoming debt laundries.

### 7. Continental and global borrowing should stay narrow, chartered, and repayment-specific

Above the nation-state, borrowing authority should remain sparse. Continental unions, development institutions, and global facilities may sometimes need debt capacity for shared infrastructure, emergency liquidity, climate or resilience investment, or backstop lending. But the archive should reject the fantasy that every treaty body should become a treasury just because cross-border problems are real.

The constitutional test is strict. Borrowing above the national level should attach to a narrow mandate, an explicit legal basis, a named repayment or callable-capital structure, and a governance model proportionate to the risks imposed on member publics. A thin global secretariat with weak democratic roots should not quietly accumulate open-ended leverage. Nor should a continental body borrow expansively while hiding whether the real guarantor is a shared budget, richer member states, user charges, or future political improvisation.

### 8. Debt transparency is not a statistics hobby; it is part of the live constitutional order

A borrowing constitution fails when no one can answer four simple questions in real time: **who owes, on what terms, for what purpose, and who else is really on the hook?** The archive should therefore treat debt registries, guarantee registers, PPP exposure tables, maturity ladders, and consolidated public-sector reporting as first-class governance surfaces rather than back-office technicalities.

This is especially important in decentralized systems. Subnational governments, utilities, school districts, hospital authorities, transit entities, and special-purpose vehicles often make the same public promise on different ledgers. The ideal constitution should publish those exposures both entity-by-entity and in consolidated form, so local autonomy does not become an accounting trick that blinds the wider polity to its own liabilities.

## Guardrails

- Do not let neighborhood or village bodies normalize borrowing when they lack diversified revenues and professional treasury capacity.
- Do not use municipal debt to hide recurring operating deficits, deferred maintenance, or politically convenient under-taxation.
- Do not split metropolitan or regional infrastructure finance across off-book authorities simply to evade democratic debt limits.
- Do not allow guarantees, PPP obligations, and enterprise debt to sit outside the public risk picture because they are not labeled sovereign bonds.
- Do not promise local autonomy while practicing routine discretionary bailout.
- Do not wait for default to invent the insolvency, intervention, or restructuring rules.
- Do not let continental or global bodies borrow without a narrow mandate, visible repayment basis, and bounded member exposure.

## Failure modes

- **tiny-debtor syndrome**: micro-local bodies take on obligations they cannot professionally price, monitor, or refinance.
- **operating-deficit laundering**: current spending is pushed into loans, arrears, lease structures, or PPP commitments that imitate capital finance.
- **SPV fog**: governments create authorities, utilities, or project companies whose debt is formally separate but politically guaranteed.
- **soft-budget federalism**: subnational actors borrow on the expectation that the center will rescue them because failure is politically inconvenient.
- **service-collapse insolvency**: debt distress is handled through brute austerity or institutional chaos because no restructuring lane protects essential services.
- **guarantee invisibility**: public guarantees and contingent liabilities accumulate off to the side until they suddenly become headline debt.
- **rollover cliff politics**: the state overuses short-term or fragile debt structures and later discovers that refinancing conditions now govern democratic choice.
- **supranational treasury theater**: a regional or global institution borrows far beyond its clear legal and political mandate and then relies on ambiguity about who ultimately bears the risk.

## Practical tests

A borrowing constitution is scale-matched and democratically honest when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. At each level, is borrowing limited to purposes that match the level’s real revenue base, asset portfolio, and constitutional role?
2. Are capital borrowing, operating finance, guarantees, PPP obligations, enterprise debt, and emergency support exposures all visible enough to evaluate together?
3. Does every subnational tier face both ex ante borrowing discipline and an ex post restructuring route that preserves essential services?
4. Are bailout expectations bounded by published rules rather than left to destructive political suspense?
5. Where borrowing exists above the national level, is the mandate narrow and the repayment basis explicit enough that leverage does not outrun legitimacy?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can name its debt stock but not **which level may borrow, for which class of asset or shock, on whose balance sheet, under which rescue boundary, and through which restructuring lane**, then it still has **public debt without a debt constitution**.
