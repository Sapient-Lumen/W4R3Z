# 668 — Shared-regime change ladders: review conferences, amendments, protocols, annex updates, tacit acceptance, version registers, and no regime rewrite by secretariat FAQ

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed change ladder** that distinguishes political guidance, interpretive guidance, implementing decisions, annex or technical updates, formal amendments, optional protocols, and refounding texts — each with its own adoption body, consent rule, objection or non-acceptance lane, entry-into-force logic, version register, and domestic landing path — so the regime can evolve without either paralysis or quiet constitutional rewrite by circular, dashboard, or FAQ.

## Why this matters

The archive now has compact doctrines for integration depth (`663`), burden constitutions (`664`), domestic landing (`665`), compliance (`666`), and differentiated commitments (`667`). But one more design object is needed if the archive is going to speak honestly about long-lived shared regimes:

**how does a treaty system or compact actually change over time?**

Every serious regime eventually faces some mix of the following pressures:

- scientific or technical updates,
- operational detail that cannot wait for full refounding,
- deeper commitments among only some members,
- clarifications after implementation reveals ambiguity,
- and occasional need for real constitutional amendment.

Without a typed change ladder, regimes tend to swing between two bad poles.

At one pole lies **frozen-text governance**: everyone knows the system needs updating, but the only visible lane is a full formal amendment, so the regime decays while practice improvises around it.

At the other pole lies **governance by unofficial accretion**: secretariats, conference conclusions, FAQ pages, guidance notes, or software defaults begin functioning as if they were treaty change even though the legal lane for change was never opened.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **shared-regime version discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Publish one visible ladder of change instruments

A serious regime should not force readers to guess whether a conference declaration, annex revision, protocol, or amendment carries legal weight.

The archive should require one visible ladder naming at least:

1. political declarations or conference conclusions,
2. interpretive or technical guidance,
3. implementing decisions within delegated authority,
4. annex or schedule updates,
5. formal amendments to the parent text,
6. optional or supplementary protocols,
7. and full replacement or refounding instruments.

Each rung should declare what it can and cannot lawfully do.

### 2. Match the change device to the weight of the change

Not every update needs the heaviest lane. But not every update belongs in a technical annex either.

The archive should therefore ask a simpler question before any change: **does this proposal merely maintain the existing bargain, operationalize a delegated detail, extend an optional module, or alter the constitutional balance of obligation itself?**

Technical maintenance should not need refounding. Constitutional change should not be smuggled through maintenance machinery.

### 3. Use annex and schedule updates for bounded technical maintenance only

Annexes, schedules, lists, and similar attached instruments are often the right place for technical standards, controlled substances lists, reporting templates, or similar bounded details that need more frequent updating than the parent treaty text.

But the archive should insist that the regime state clearly:

- which matters may be updated by annex,
- who adopts the update,
- whether parties can object or file non-acceptance,
- when the update becomes effective,
- and what domestic implementation step follows.

Annex machinery becomes dangerous when it starts changing the core political bargain instead of maintaining an already delegated technical field.

### 4. Use formal amendment for core obligations, institutions, and voting or competence rules

When the proposed change alters:

- who is bound,
- what the primary obligations are,
- who decides,
- how money is raised,
- how compliance works,
- or how members enter, exit, or are reviewed,

then the archive should presumptively treat it as amendment-level or higher change.

That means explicit proposal, adoption rule, acceptance or ratification lane where required, entry-into-force rule, and version tracking. The regime should not pretend that constitutional surgery is just a technical update.

### 5. Use optional protocols for deeper modules rather than overloading the parent text

Protocols and side instruments are often the honest way to add a deeper module, narrower issue-area package, or higher-commitment club without forcing every existing member into immediate full acceptance.

The archive should value protocols when they preserve legibility:

- the parent instrument remains readable,
- the deeper obligations are clearly ring-fenced,
- participation conditions are explicit,
- and later accession remains possible where intended.

A protocol is preferable to hiding a second treaty inside meeting conclusions or interpretive practice.

### 6. Use tacit acceptance or simplified update only for tightly bounded technical fields with notice and objection lanes

Some regimes legitimately use tacit acceptance or simplified procedures so technical amendments can keep pace with real-world change.

That can be governance rather than drift — but only where the field is clearly bounded, notice is real, objection or non-acceptance rights are visible, and the simplified lane does not rewrite the regime's constitutional core.

The archive should be especially skeptical when "simplified" procedures start changing distributive obligations, major institutional powers, or rights-sensitive baselines.

### 7. Keep guidance, FAQs, and secretariat documents in their lane

Shared regimes often need guidance, explanatory notes, model forms, and operational manuals.

Those can be useful. But the archive should insist that they remain visibly subordinate unless the treaty or compact explicitly delegates binding effect through a named legal lane. Guidance may explain, coordinate, or standardize practice. It should not quietly amend the regime by browser page or circular letter.

### 8. Link every external change to domestic landing and live version control

A change adopted internationally or intergovernmentally is not yet live domestically simply because the conference voted.

The archive should therefore pair every change rung with its domestic landing type:

- direct effect,
- transposition,
- technical notice,
- administrative update,
- legislative approval,
- or no domestic effect until further act.

It should also keep one public version register showing which text, annex edition, protocol, or amendment is in force for which members and territories on which date.

### 9. Schedule review conferences without pretending every review is legal change

Periodic review is a strength. It lets members revisit scientific baselines, institutional performance, and implementation gaps.

But a review conference, stocktake, or summit should not itself be confused with amendment unless the regime's own law says so. The archive should distinguish sharply between:

- review that recommends,
- decision that implements delegated power,
- and amendment that changes the bargain.

## Change ladder matrix

| Change rung | Honest use | Consent / effect logic to declare | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| political declaration / conference conclusion | signal intent, priorities, future work, or shared interpretation without changing legal text | adopting body, legal status, follow-up lane | presenting politics as binding law |
| guidance / model forms / secretariat note | explain practice or support implementation | issuer, authority basis, non-binding or binding status, supersession path | regime rewrite by FAQ |
| implementing decision within delegated authority | fill in bounded details already delegated by the base instrument | decision rule, scope limit, review or challenge path, effective date | using delegated detail to change the constitutional core |
| annex / schedule update | maintain technical lists, standards, templates, or similar bounded content | adoption lane, objection or non-acceptance rule, entry into force, version number | stuffing major political tradeoffs into technical annexes |
| formal amendment | change core obligations, institutions, competences, voting, finance, or compliance architecture | proposal rule, adoption body, acceptance or ratification rule, entry into force, who is bound when | treating constitutional change as housekeeping |
| optional protocol / supplementary instrument | add deeper or narrower module for some or later-participating members | participation rule, relation to parent text, accession path, institutions and compliance lane | burying a second treaty inside practice notes |
| refounding / replacement text | rebuild the regime when accumulated patching is no longer honest | transition rule, succession of obligations, continuity and sunset path, canonical text register | endless patchwork with no canonical current text |

## Guardrails

- Do not let secretariat guidance impersonate amendment.
- Do not use annex updates for changes that alter the regime's constitutional core.
- Do not force every technical tweak through refounding if the regime already delegated bounded update authority.
- Do not use tacit acceptance where notice, objection rights, or scope limits are weak.
- Do not adopt a new external text without saying how and when it lands domestically.
- Do not let several current versions coexist with no public register of who is bound by which one.
- Do not confuse review conference outputs with legal change unless the base instrument expressly does so.

## Failure modes

- **regime rewrite by secretariat FAQ**: guidance or browser text becomes the operative rule without the legal change lane ever opening.
- **annex overload**: politically weighty changes are hidden in technical update machinery.
- **frozen-text drift**: the formal text remains old while the real regime mutates through practice and workaround.
- **version fog**: no public register shows which amendment, annex edition, or protocol is in force for which member.
- **silent domestic gap**: an external update is adopted, but no one states whether domestic law or operations changed.
- **review inflation**: every summit communique is narrated as if the treaty itself changed.

## Practical tests

A shared regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it publish one visible ladder of change instruments from guidance through amendment and protocol?
2. For each rung, does it state who adopts it, what it may change, and what consent or objection rule applies?
3. Are annex or simplified procedures confined to clearly bounded technical fields?
4. Are core changes routed through amendment-level or stronger lanes?
5. Are optional deeper modules placed in protocols or equivalently legible side instruments rather than hidden in practice?
6. Is there one current version register showing what text is in force for each member and territory?
7. Does every adopted change declare its domestic landing path and live effective date?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can say **that it evolves** but cannot say **what change rung a given update used, who accepted it, when it entered into force, who is bound by that version, and how it landed domestically**, it still has **adaptation rhetoric without an honest version constitution**.
