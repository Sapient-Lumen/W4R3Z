# 778 — Administrative-justice timeliness and delay-remedies necessity tests for ideal governments: priority lanes, case management, clock publication, and no rights by queue decay

## One-line thesis

Ideal governments should treat **time in administrative justice as constitutional substance rather than an operational afterthought**: complaint, review, appeal, and enforcement lanes should publish meaningful clocks, triage urgency visibly, give adjudicators active case-management powers, maintain surge and backlog disciplines, and provide acceleratory and where needed compensatory remedies for unreasonable duration, so legality does not decay into rights by queue endurance.

## Why this matters

The archive now says much more clearly how a person should complain (`772`), obtain a second look (`773`), aggregate widespread harms (`774`), seek compensation (`775`), survive cost barriers (`776`), and force execution against the state (`777`). What it still lacked as one compact operating rule was a cleaner answer to a simpler but more universal question:

**what happens when the remedy exists, but time itself becomes the hidden constitution of who receives it?**

Administrative and public-law systems often fail less by open denial than by managed drift. A benefit appeal is heard after the eviction. A licensing dispute is decided after the season or tender window has passed. A school-placement challenge is resolved after the academic year. A procurement challenge is heard after the contract is already entrenched. A planning or demolition challenge becomes moot because the site is altered before judgment. A non-response case is routed through so many preliminary steps that the state wins without ever clearly winning on the merits.

In those settings, delay is not neutral. It reallocates power. It advantages the repeat institutional actor over the person with a perishable need, a small balance sheet, or a short practical horizon. A formally generous review architecture can therefore remain substantively thin if the system does not say **which matters are urgent, what clocks run from which event, who may accelerate a case, how adjournments and dormant intervals are policed, what metrics are published, and what remedy exists when the state or the justice system itself has taken too long**.

Ideal-government design therefore needs a doctrine of **administrative-justice timeliness necessity**. The point is not to demand identical deadlines for every case type. It is to insist that the public-redress system take time seriously enough to type urgency, publish clocks, manage cases actively, and repair delay when reasonable-time guarantees have already been breached.

The live design question is not whether a constitution or convention somewhere says proceedings must occur within a reasonable time. It is whether the polity can show a usable architecture for **priority routing, caseflow control, delay diagnosis, acceleratory relief, compensatory repair, and public duration data**.

## Necessity table

| Necessity test | What counts as proof | Honest design consequence | Default answer if proof is weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Need for visible clocks from legally meaningful start points** | rights-bearing delay occurs before anyone can tell when the case really started | define clock triggers for complaint, review, appeal, remand, execution, and reopened matters | let “pending” hide dead time and start-date ambiguity |
| **Need to type urgency rather than queue everything alike** | some matters become moot or irreparable if heard too late | create explicit priority lanes and expedition triggers for time-sensitive case classes | first-in-first-out folklore or informal favours decide urgency |
| **Need for active case management rather than passive file drift** | delay is driven by adjournment culture, dormant intervals, or unmanaged evidence cycles | empower adjudicators or case managers to set timetables, narrow issues, and control postponements | parties and institutions may stretch time without discipline |
| **Need for a remedy against excessive duration itself** | proceedings regularly outlast usefulness or breach reasonable-time guarantees | provide acceleratory orders, escalation routes, and where justified compensation for completed delay harm | treat duration as unfortunate but legally inert |
| **Need to see backlog structurally, not anecdotaly** | delay clusters by case type, agency, stage, or tribunal | publish duration and backlog data by lane, stage, urgency class, and outcome | rely on aggregate averages that hide where time is being lost |
| **Need to stop delay transfer between institutions** | time saved in one stage is lost in referral, remand, or execution | align clocks and handoff duties across agencies, tribunals, courts, and treasury/execution bodies | each office claims it was quick inside its own silo |
|
## Pattern pack

### 1. Start clocks where the right-bearing burden actually begins

Timeliness design should specify the legally meaningful start point for each lane: complaint filed, request received, decision notified, file completed, appeal lodged, remand returned, or execution due. Systems that cannot say when the clock started are usually systems that cannot say who consumed the time.

### 2. Publish urgency classes and expedition triggers openly

Not every matter needs the same pace. But the archive should prefer systems that openly type urgency for matters such as imminent removal, demolition, homelessness, subsistence benefits, education placement, election access, health access, procurement standstill windows, disclosure with perishable relevance, or environmental harm. Priority should not depend on who knows whom.

### 3. Give adjudicators and review bodies active case-management powers

Good design equips the deciding body to set schedules, require focused pleadings, consolidate similar matters where fair, insist on file production, limit avoidable postponements, and decide threshold issues early. Passive file accumulation is not neutrality; it is queue government.

### 4. Distinguish unavoidable complexity from unmanaged dead time

Some matters legitimately take longer because the record is large, expertise is needed, or participation rights are broad. Timeliness architecture should therefore separate genuine complexity from idle intervals, recurrent remands for missing basics, chronic non-appearance, unexplained file transfer delay, or agency failure to produce the record.

### 5. Provide acceleratory remedies while the case is still alive

If proceedings are drifting unreasonably, the system should allow a party to seek expedition, reassignment, timetable-setting, supervisory direction, or another acceleratory order before the whole matter becomes moot. A right to complain about delay only after the right has withered is a weak right.

### 6. Provide compensatory or equivalent repair where unreasonable duration has already occurred

Some delay cannot be undone. In those cases, ideal governments should provide compensation or equivalent redress for completed unreasonable duration, especially where the delay itself inflicted measurable harm or breached a reasonable-time guarantee.

### 7. Treat remand and handoff time as part of the same justice obligation

A system should not improve apparent timeliness by bouncing cases between agency, tribunal, and court. Clocks should continue across remand, referral, expert report commissioning, file transmission, and execution follow-through, or at least remain linked in public reporting.

### 8. Use backlog triage and surge tools before crisis becomes the normal constitution

When incoming volume spikes or an institution is structurally overloaded, the system should have lawful tools for surge staffing, temporary panels, simplified tracks for repetitive issues, grouped hearings, templateable issues, or transfer rules that do not sacrifice independence or fairness.

### 9. Publish duration, adjournment, and dormant-interval data by lane and stage

Average case duration alone is too coarse. Timeliness reporting should show intake-to-first-action, first-action-to-hearing, hearing-to-decision, remand-to-redetermination, and decision-to-execution. It should also track adjournments, reasons for postponement, and dormant periods attributable to specific actors or institutions.

### 10. Give reasons when expedition is denied

If a person seeks urgent treatment or complains of unreasonable delay, the system should say why acceleration is refused, what timetable remains, and what further step is available. Silence about time usually protects only the institution already consuming it.

## Guardrails

- Do not let queue order or internal convenience substitute for a typed urgency policy.
- Do not measure timeliness only by court averages while ignoring agency delay, remand delay, or non-execution after judgment.
- Do not allow adjournments, expert referrals, or file-transfer gaps to disappear into non-attributed dead time.
- Do not offer only post hoc compensation where an acceleratory remedy could still save the case.
- Do not let emergency fast tracks become the hidden norm for everything while ordinary lanes remain structurally starved.
- Do not permit private access, political salience, or media pressure to function as the real expedition rule.

## Failure modes

- **rights by queue decay**: the claimant technically had a remedy, but only after the need expired.
- **average-time camouflage**: headline duration statistics improve while urgent or complex sub-lanes quietly collapse.
- **dead-time laundering**: no institution owns the months lost in transfer, remand, or file production.
- **expedition by influence**: only represented, visible, or connected claimants get accelerated treatment.
- **delay-without-remedy**: the system acknowledges unreasonable duration but offers no way to accelerate or repair it.
- **backlog constitutionalism**: temporary volume spikes harden into the real rule of law without public choice.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can tell you **that decisions are reviewable and judgments are binding** but cannot also show **when the clock starts, which matters qualify for urgency, who actively manages drift, what remedy exists against unreasonable duration, how delay data are published by stage and lane, and how remands and execution remain inside the same timeliness duty**, then it still has **public redress without a time constitution**.
