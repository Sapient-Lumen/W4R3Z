# 689 — Shared-regime territorial-presence constitutions: headquarters, regional offices, country offices, liaison posts, service hubs, host instruments, review clocks, and no regime by landlord permission

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed territorial-presence constitution** that separates headquarters, regional offices, country offices, liaison posts, service hubs, hosted outposts, and temporary mission footprints — with a named host instrument, mandate fit, reporting line, support package, review clock, and closure path for each — so institutional presence stays functional and reviewable instead of hardening into landlord permission, prestige geography, or office sprawl by habit.

## Why this matters

The archive now explains how shared regimes obtain legal personality and functional insulation inside host states (`687`) and how they handle private-law claims when their operations harm or burden others (`688`). That is enough to explain **how a regime exists in law** and **how it should answer for private harms**. It is still not enough to explain **where the regime is actually planted in territory, which kinds of offices it may create, what each office is for, and how host-state dependence is kept constitutional instead of improvised**.

That gap matters because many shared regimes drift into territorial confusion long before they drift into legal crisis.

A headquarters becomes the de facto only mind of the institution even when regional or country discretion is mission-critical. Regional offices accumulate permanent political influence without a visible test for what belongs there rather than at headquarters or country level. Liaison posts begin coordinating, negotiating, and signaling commitments even though they were constituted only for representation. Service centres quietly become operational bottlenecks for finance, travel, procurement, or IT but are still described as mere back office. Temporary emergency footprints remain open after the emergency because nobody typed review or closure discipline. Co-located offices start borrowing one another's staff, records, and badges until nobody can say which organization or level is acting.

Without a typed territorial-presence constitution, six familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **office sprawl by habit**: sites survive because closing them is politically awkward, not because their function is still justified.

The second is **mandate blur**: representation, coordination, operations, technical support, and service processing are all mixed together inside the word office.

The third is **host dependence opacity**: real operability depends on visas, utilities, premises, IT, and local relationships that live in side letters, favors, or habit instead of in a visible constitutional packet.

The fourth is **shadow hierarchy**: headquarters, regional, and country offices bargain informally over who really decides, while the formal chart says almost nothing.

The fifth is **symbolic presence inflation**: the institution opens or keeps a site because the signal matters, even though the office lacks a coherent authority map.

The sixth is **closure denial**: temporary, pilot, or emergency presences become immortal because nobody wrote a review clock or exit route.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **shared-regime territorial-presence discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Publish one typed presence map instead of treating every site as just an office

A serious regime should publish a single presence map that distinguishes at minimum:

- headquarters or principal seat,
- regional or subregional offices,
- country offices,
- liaison or representation offices,
- service centres or administrative hubs,
- technical, data, research, or logistics hubs,
- hosted or embedded outposts,
- and temporary mission, surge, or emergency footprints.

This is the archive's first anti-sprawl rule: geography should travel through named institutional types, not through one sentimental word.

### 2. Tie each site to a mission-specific reason for being

Each presence type should carry a visible functional test.

Headquarters should exist to house the institution's common leadership, constitutional organs, enterprise functions, and archive spine. Regional offices should exist only where cross-border coordination, quality assurance, pooled expertise, or scale-specific support genuinely sit between global and country levels. Country offices should exist only where on-the-ground planning, implementation support, monitoring, convening, or response work cannot honestly be done remotely. Liaison offices should exist for representation and intergovernmental interface, not as a disguised operational ministry. Service centres should exist for transaction processing and enterprise support, not as unreviewed political centers.

### 3. Bind every presence to a host instrument

Each territorial footprint should point to its legal landing instrument: headquarters agreement, host country agreement, standard assistance agreement, exchange of letters, memorandum of understanding, lease packet, or emergency access instrument as applicable.

The archive should prefer one visible answer to the question: **what gives this site its lawful standing here?**

### 4. Separate location from authority

No site should gain policy authority merely because senior people sit there, because the building is prestigious, or because the office interacts often with the host state.

A serious regime should publish, for each presence type:

- what decisions may be made there,
- what decisions must be escalated,
- what delegated authorities are held locally,
- and what remains centrally reserved.

That is how the archive prevents geography from becoming a stealth constitution.

### 5. Type reporting lines and cross-level support duties

Headquarters, regional offices, country offices, and hubs should not merely appear on an organigram. The regime should say what support travels downward, what reporting travels upward, what peer-coordination expectations exist laterally, and what quality-assurance or escalation role each layer holds.

Where country differentiation is real, it should be explicit rather than hidden in unequal informal access to headquarters.

### 6. Distinguish representation posts from operational posts

Liaison offices at multilateral capitals, host-country capitals, or other political centres may be justified. But they should be typed as representation and coordination lanes unless the regime expressly grants them operational authority.

This prevents the archive from normalizing **government by corridor access**.

### 7. Keep service hubs administratively powerful but constitutionally narrow

Shared regimes often need payroll centres, travel centres, logistics hubs, IT centres, or training campuses. Those are real institutions. But they should remain narrow in mandate.

A service hub may process transactions, provide enterprise tooling, or stage supplies. It should not quietly become the political brain of the regime just because operational dependency runs through it.

### 8. Type hosted and co-located presences explicitly

A regime may be hosted within another international body, a state facility, a university campus, or a shared compound. It may also colocate multiple regime components in one campus.

Those arrangements should come with explicit firebreaks for:

- records and archives,
- IT administration,
- HR authority,
- procurement and payment authority,
- external communications,
- and privileges, immunities, or access credentials.

Co-location should not mean constitutional fusion.

### 9. Put review clocks and closure routes on every non-permanent footprint

Temporary, pilot, surge, and emergency presences should carry:

- an opening trigger,
- a review date,
- continuation criteria,
- a closure authority,
- and a records, assets, staff, and liability handback path.

This is how the archive prevents humanitarian or crisis presence from turning into immortal infrastructure by inertia.

### 10. Publish one minimum operating packet for every site

For each territorial footprint, the regime should be able to point to one minimum packet covering:

- host instrument,
- mandate and scope,
- office head and reporting line,
- staff profile,
- legal status,
- premises and facilities basis,
- records and IT systems,
- financial and procurement support,
- security and incident contacts,
- and review or closure clock.

That packet is the difference between visible institutional presence and symbolic office theater.

## Working matrix

| Presence type | Honest function | Must be visible | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| headquarters | common leadership, constitutional organs, enterprise backbone | seat basis, reserved powers, support duties | treating the seat as universal operational superiority |
| regional office | cross-border coordination, pooled expertise, quality assurance | territorial remit, delegation map, support lane | building a second headquarters by drift |
| country office | on-the-ground planning, implementation support, monitoring, response | host instrument, local delegation, escalation path | permanent local symbolism without real mandate |
| liaison post | representation, negotiation support, forum access | speaking role, limits on operational authority | corridor government by access and proximity |
| service hub | payroll, IT, travel, logistics, training, shared transactions | service catalogue, decision limits, continuity duties | back-office site becoming a shadow cabinet |
| hosted or temporary outpost | bounded support, surge, or embedded function | firebreaks, review clock, closure path | landlord permission as constitutional title |

## Guardrails

- Do not call every site an office and pretend the word is self-explanatory.
- Do not let location confer authority by prestige, proximity, or habit.
- Do not allow liaison posts to make durable operational commitments without an explicit delegation map.
- Do not let service centres turn into hidden governing cores because everyone depends on them.
- Do not keep temporary or emergency presences open without review clocks and closure routes.
- Do not confuse co-location with merged authority.
- Do not let host-state favors or landlord tolerance substitute for a visible host instrument.

## Failure modes

- **office sprawl by habit**: sites persist because nobody wrote a closure lane.
- **mandate blur**: representation, operations, and service processing are all mislabeled as office work.
- **host dependence opacity**: real operability lives in favors, side letters, and personal access.
- **shadow hierarchy**: the true headquarters-region-country bargain exists only in practice.
- **symbolic presence inflation**: geography is preserved for signaling after the function has thinned out.
- **closure denial**: pilots and emergency footprints become permanent by inertia.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it publish one typed map of headquarters, regional, country, liaison, service, hosted, and temporary presences?
2. Does each site have a named functional reason for being rather than a vague visibility rationale?
3. Is the host instrument or legal landing basis visible for each territorial footprint?
4. Can the regime distinguish what authority is local, regional, central, or merely representative?
5. Are reporting lines and support obligations visible across headquarters, regions, countries, and hubs?
6. Are liaison posts and service centres constitutionally prevented from drifting into shadow government?
7. Are co-located or hosted presences covered by explicit firebreaks for records, IT, procurement, and communications?
8. Do temporary or emergency footprints have review clocks, closure triggers, and handback routes?
9. Can every site point to one minimum operating packet covering staff, systems, facilities, finance, and security contacts?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can name **where it is** but not **what each site is for, what legal instrument lands it there, what authority travels with it, what support and security packet sustains it, and when it must be reviewed or closed**, then it still has **territorial presence without an honest territorial-presence constitution**.
