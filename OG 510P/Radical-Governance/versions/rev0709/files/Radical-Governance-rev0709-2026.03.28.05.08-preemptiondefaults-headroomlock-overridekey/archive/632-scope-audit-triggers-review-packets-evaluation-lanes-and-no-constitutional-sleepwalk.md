# 632 — Scope audit triggers, review packets, evaluation lanes, and no constitutional sleepwalk

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should keep a standing **scope audit registry** that uses territorial-impact, fiscal, democratic, and operational tripwires to trigger a typed **review packet** whenever real responsibility drifts away from the nominal map, so repair begins from evidence and laddered choice rather than from crisis improvisation or silent constitutional sleepwalk.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a much sharper doctrine for multilevel design than it did even a few revisions ago. It says **which scope normally fits which class of problem** (`579`, `602`, `603`). It says **how to diagnose misfit and choose repairs in sequence** (`604`, `605`). It says **when shared territorial co-operation becomes real government and needs constitutional upgrade** (`606`-`627`). And it now says **how claims of ideal government should be tested, presumed, moved, and handed back** (`628`-`631`).

What it still lacked was the operating discipline that turns those doctrines into a live maintenance practice: **how does a polity know when it must re-open the scope question at all?**

That gap matters because most multilevel stacks do not become dishonest only through one dramatic constitutional event. They drift. A metropolitan labour market becomes real long before the law admits it. A regional ministry keeps accumulating approvals because “temporary” support never quite ends. A local function remains formally municipal but is now almost entirely scripted by conditional grants, vendor defaults, or national templates. Emergency command decays into ordinary hierarchy. Several special-purpose bodies collectively become the real government of a territory while nobody ever says so in public.

The archive should therefore add a compact doctrine of **scope audit triggers**. Good constitutional design is not only about where powers should start and how they may move. It is also about maintaining one visible registry of the signals that say the current allocation is no longer honest enough to leave unexamined.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat scope fit as a standing maintenance question, not only as a crisis question

A healthy multilevel polity should not revisit responsibility assignment only after failure, scandal, or territorial panic. It should maintain a standing practice of checking whether the nominal map still matches where decisions, money, coercion, and blame actually live.

The archive should therefore prefer **routine scope auditing** over constitutional attention deficit. Review does not mean constant redesign. It means the system has a lawful way to notice when reality is escaping the declared stack.

### 2. Use tripwires, not ambient unease

Scope review should begin from visible triggers rather than from elite vibes. A trigger is not yet a reform decision. It is a reason to open a typed review packet.

Typical tripwires include:

- a shared functional geography becoming real enough that several municipalities can no longer govern it honestly alone,
- repeated emergency or distress interventions that stop looking exceptional,
- grant or platform dependence swallowing lower-tier discretion,
- duplicate chains or approval loops across levels,
- chronic unfunded mandates or rising vertical fiscal imbalance,
- special-purpose or consortium accumulation that is performing general territorial government in practice,
- recurring boundary mismatch between service catchments, planning geographies, electorates, and tax bases,
- and sharp increases in complaint, litigation, delay, or democratic contestation caused by scale confusion rather than by the underlying policy dispute.

The archive should reject both extremes: permanent constitutional complacency and hair-trigger reorganisation.

### 3. Every trigger should launch a typed review packet, not a predetermined answer

A scope trigger should open one visible packet that names:

- the function or field under review,
- the ordinary legal home,
- the actual operational holders of power,
- the trigger condition,
- the territorial-impact and evidence window,
- the affected residents and authorities,
- the immediate protections needed while review runs,
- and the menu of candidate repairs.

This matters because a trigger is only a notice that fit may have degraded. It should not predetermine recentralisation, merger, upgrade, or abolition before the evidence is assembled.

### 4. Distinguish drift types before choosing the lane of repair

Not all scope drift is the same. The review packet should classify the pathology before the polity debates remedies. Useful categories include:

- **paper fit / real mismatch**: the law says one thing, but the live geography or workflow says another,
- **soft upper-tier capture**: grants, templates, approvals, or software defaults hold the real discretion above,
- **underbuilt correct home**: the assigned level is still right but lacks capability or money,
- **shadow territorial government**: several co-operative or special-purpose bodies together are doing real government without a matching public constitution,
- **temporary holdover**: an emergency or distress intervention is lingering past the point of honest exception,
- **boundary mismatch**: electorate, plan, budget, service, and redress geographies no longer line up.

The point is to stop every scope argument from collapsing into one blunt question about “centralise or decentralise.”

### 5. Keep the first remedy on the repair ladder unless the evidence clearly demands a deeper move

Once a trigger opens review, the archive should still respect the repair ladder. Many trigger events will justify a lane fix, connector repair, kit completion, or handback rather than a territorial upgrade or map change. Others will prove that a metropolitan or regional constitutionalization is now overdue.

The key rule is that **the review packet should compare candidate remedies in order of depth** and say why lighter instruments are insufficient when deeper surgery is proposed.

### 6. Consultation should attach to the packet early, not after the answer is chosen

Because scope review often affects local or regional self-government, affected authorities and residents should be involved early enough to shape the diagnosis, not merely to react to a nearly closed settlement. That includes consultation on the trigger itself, the evidence base, the territorial framing, and the range of candidate responses.

The archive should reject review packets that ask municipalities, regions, or communities for comments only after a ministry has already chosen merger, takeover, or redesign.

### 7. Distinguish temporary performance rescue from proof of permanent re-scoping

A central intervention or shared-service workaround may improve service quality in the short run. That alone does not prove that the function belongs permanently at the higher or pooled level. Review packets should therefore separate:

- evidence that the original scope was wrong,
- evidence that the scope was right but the kit was incomplete,
- and evidence that the temporary instrument performed well only because it had exceptional powers, money, or political attention that ordinary arrangements would not keep.

Without that distinction, every rescue becomes a self-justifying case for permanent upward retention.

### 8. Publish unresolved triggers instead of letting them fade back into folklore

Not every trigger will end in redesign. Some will end in “monitor,” “repair connectors,” “complete the kit,” or “maintain for now.” But unresolved or deferred triggers should stay on a visible registry with a next review point.

The archive should prefer **open pending status** over administrative amnesia. A polity should be able to see which territories or functions are under repeated concern even when no immediate constitutional action is taken.

### 9. Pair trigger review with metrics, but never let metrics replace political judgment

Useful indicators include catchment mismatch, service delay, appeal volume, cost-shifting, grant dependence, repeat emergency use, staff vacancy rates, capital underdelivery, litigation, and complaint routing failure. But no single metric should automatically redraw the stack.

The archive should therefore prefer **indicator-supported constitutional judgment** rather than metric rule by spreadsheet. Numbers help surface pattern; they do not decide institutional morality on their own.

### 10. Let the audit registry cover downward as well as upward correction

Scope review should not be biased toward central accumulation. The same registry should surface functions that can move downward, capacities that can be restored, special measures that can end, and special-purpose bodies that can be narrowed or dissolved.

Otherwise “review” becomes just another word for searching upward for the next place to park power.

### 11. Keep one historical trail of trigger, diagnosis, action, and outcome

Each trigger should leave behind one durable record linking:

- the initial signal,
- the review packet,
- consultation inputs,
- the chosen remedy,
- the implementation path,
- the follow-up review,
- and whether the trigger closed, persisted, or reopened.

That historical trail matters because multilevel systems forget quickly. Without it, the same constitutional problem returns every few years and each government pretends it is seeing the issue for the first time.

### 12. Use different audit rhythms for different kinds of scope risk

Not all fields need the same review clock. Emergency holdings may need very short review cycles. Metropolitan and regional fit may justify periodic territorial review tied to planning, census, or capital cycles. Special-purpose accumulation may need scheduled portfolio pruning. Fiscal-congruence triggers may need annual monitoring. The point is not one universal calendar but **declared rhythms matched to the risk of drift**.

## Trigger matrix

| Trigger type | Typical sign | First review question | Possible honest outcomes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| functional-area growth | real labour market, basin, corridor, or catchment outruns current map | is this still several local functions or now one shared territorial system? | connector repair, co-operation, special-purpose narrowing, metropolitan or regional upgrade |
| soft upper-tier capture | grants, approvals, templates, or systems absorb local discretion | is the center carrying a temporary support role or the real function? | simplify conditions, restore discretion, formal rescoping, or handback |
| repeated emergency holdover | the same temporary override keeps renewing | is this still emergency governance or undeclared redesign? | handback, phased restoration, or explicit permanent redesign route |
| duplicate chains and delay | several levels sign, inspect, or veto the same action | is the issue role blur rather than wrong territory? | lane fix, cut duplicate approvals, lead-lane typing, or deeper reallocation |
| boundary mismatch | plan, electorate, budget, and service geographies diverge | which map is now lying about the real government? | connector repair, territory realignment, constitutionalization, or special-purpose pruning |
| democratic strain | rising complaint, litigation, or legitimacy conflict caused by scale confusion | is power sitting where people can no longer see or contest it honestly? | scrutiny upgrade, clearer review routes, representative upgrade, or rescoping |

## Guardrails

- Do not wait for constitutional panic before reviewing obvious scope drift.
- Do not let triggers dictate answers before a review packet is built.
- Do not treat improved short-run performance under emergency or central support as automatic proof that upward retention is permanently justified.
- Do not use metrics alone to redraw the stack.
- Do not let pending triggers disappear because they are politically inconvenient.
- Do not make scope review a one-way search for upper-tier control.
- Do not consult affected authorities only after the preferred redesign is already written.

## Failure modes

- **constitutional sleepwalk**: real power migrates across levels for years without any visible review trigger.
- **tripwire without packet**: a problem is acknowledged, but no evidence bundle or remedy menu is created.
- **metric theater**: dashboards create the appearance of objectivity while hiding the political choices behind threshold design.
- **review as ambush**: consultation begins only after a ministry has already chosen the answer.
- **upward-bias audit**: every trigger is treated as evidence for recentralisation, while downward restoration or pruning never receives the same attention.
- **temporary-success fallacy**: a rescue instrument performs well under extraordinary support and is misread as proof of the ideal permanent constitution.
- **registry amnesia**: unresolved triggers vanish from view and recur later as if no one had seen them before.

## Practical tests

A polity is auditing scope fit honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is there a standing public registry of scope triggers rather than only ad hoc reform episodes?
2. Does each trigger open a typed review packet that names the legal home, actual holders, evidence window, territorial impact, and candidate repairs?
3. Are drift types classified before remedies are debated?
4. Do review packets compare lighter and deeper remedies in repair-ladder order?
5. Are affected authorities and residents consulted early enough to shape the diagnosis?
6. Can the polity distinguish temporary rescue success from evidence for permanent re-scoping?
7. Do unresolved triggers remain visible with a next review point?
8. Does the registry surface downward restoration and pruning opportunities as well as upward accumulation?
9. Is there one historical trail linking trigger, review, decision, implementation, and follow-up outcome?
10. Are review rhythms matched to different kinds of scope risk rather than left entirely undefined?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can explain **which scope currently holds a function** and even **how that allocation could be changed**, but cannot also show **what signals would force a fresh review, what packet opens when those signals appear, and how unresolved drift stays visible over time**, then it still has **scope doctrine without a doctrine for noticing constitutional drift before the stack sleepwalks into dishonesty**.
