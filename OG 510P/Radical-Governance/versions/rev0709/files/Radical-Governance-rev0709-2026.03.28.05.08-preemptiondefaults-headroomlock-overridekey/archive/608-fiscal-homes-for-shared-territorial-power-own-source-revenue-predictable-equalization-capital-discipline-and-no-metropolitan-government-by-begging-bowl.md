# 608 — Fiscal homes for shared territorial power: own-source revenue, predictable equalization, capital discipline, and no metropolitan government by begging bowl

## One-line thesis

Once shared territorial power has become real metropolitan or regional government, the polity should give it an honest fiscal constitution: responsibilities commensurate with resources, a visible base of own or rule-based shared revenue, diversified and buoyant funding, equalization that protects weaker places without destroying discretion, grants used for clear national or supralocal purposes rather than everyday dependence, and borrowing bounded mainly to capital investment rather than operational drift.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a threshold doctrine for when co-operation becomes government and a doctrine for what democratic home should follow. What it still lacked was the **fiscal doctrine for the same threshold**.

That gap matters because territorial government can remain democratically upgraded on paper while fiscally infantilized in practice. A metropolitan assembly may exist, but still live on annual contributions extracted from municipalities through bargaining. A regional body may have a statute, but no meaningful revenue discretion, no stable equalization rule, and no honest borrowing lane. A transport-and-housing territory may be politically real, but still forced to patch together its budget from earmarked project grants, developer deals, farebox hopes, and off-book vehicles.

That is not a mature constitution. It is a begging bowl with ceremonial trim. If a body is trusted to make durable trade-offs across a shared territory, it needs the financial means to do so in a way that the public can see, judge, and contest. Otherwise the real fiscal constitution remains elsewhere, and the supposedly empowered territorial government is only a partial facade.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish co-operation finance from government finance

Narrow shared-service co-operation can honestly be financed through member contributions, service charges, contract schedules, and project-specific cost sharing. That is normal because the body is derivative and bounded.

But once the body is making recurrent territorial choices about distribution, sequencing, land use, network investment, resilience, or growth, contribution bargaining stops being a neutral funding device. It becomes a constitutional choke point. The archive should therefore distinguish sharply between **co-operation finance** and **government finance**. A real territorial government should not have to beg its member governments every year for permission to exist.

### 2. Give real territorial government a visible revenue base of its own or a rule-bound shared yield

An honest metropolitan or regional tier does not always need a wholly independent tax base with total rate freedom. But it does need a revenue base that is visibly attached to its legal responsibilities and not merely to annual political pleading.

That revenue base can take several forms: genuine own-source taxes or charges, assigned shares of broader taxes distributed by stable rule, or a mixed basket. The key requirement is that the body can present a public budget whose main operating logic does not disappear into improvised municipal contributions or grant-chasing. A territory that governs should be able to say where its ordinary money comes from without narrating a perpetual rescue story.

### 3. Match the revenue instrument to the geography and politics of the territory

The archive should reject revenue design by habit. The right fiscal base depends on what kind of territory is being governed.

- A narrow service authority may live mainly on user charges and member contributions.
- A metropolitan body that shapes land use, transport, housing access, and spatial sequencing usually needs at least some broad territorial revenue rather than farebox dependence or project-by-project bargains.
- A regional government that carries wider service, development, or solidarity duties may need a thicker mix of taxes, shared taxes, formula transfers, and borrowing room.

The design question is not “which tax do metros always get?” but **which revenue instruments fit the scale, externalities, redistributive burden, and democratic claim of the body?**

### 4. Keep resources commensurate with responsibilities

The archive should continue to treat mandate-finance congruence as a constitutional principle, but this note sharpens it for shared territorial government. If a metropolitan or regional body is expected to plan, co-ordinate, invest, and arbitrate across a territory, the same settlement should state how those functions are financed on a durable basis.

Bodies with broad duties but only episodic project money are under-constitutionalized. Bodies with major promises but no operating base are invitation letters to blame-shifting. The rule should remain simple: when a territory gets real work, it should get real money.

### 5. Diversify the revenue basket enough to track changing costs and shocks

A territorial government should not be trapped in one brittle revenue stream if its duties are ongoing and system-wide. The archive should therefore prefer a **diversified and reasonably buoyant** basket: some mix of taxes, shared yields, charges, transfers, and capital-finance access calibrated to the work.

This is not a plea for fiscal sprawl. It is a plea against constitutions that assign dynamic territorial responsibilities but fund them through one static or politically fragile source. A body that must absorb population growth, climate stress, infrastructure maintenance, or cyclical downturns needs a revenue system that can move with the territory rather than a formula designed for yesterday’s calm.

### 6. Use equalization to protect weaker places without erasing territorial discretion

Once a metropolitan or regional government spans places with unequal tax bases, unequal infrastructure burdens, or unequal service costs, some equalization question appears whether the constitution admits it or not. The archive should prefer explicit equalization over hidden subsidy politics.

Equalization should correct structural imbalance and prevent richer components from turning the shared tier into a club for places that already win. But it should not flatten every difference into central tutelage or erase the recipient government’s own sphere of discretion. The honest goal is **solidarity without fiscal puppetry**.

### 7. Use grants for clear purposes, not as the ordinary oxygen of the territory

Grants remain legitimate. National governments may use them for equalization, transition support, major capital programmes, emergency response, rights floors, or strategic goals with clear spillovers. Continental or supranational funds may also be appropriate for named cross-border purposes.

But the archive should reject **metropolitan or regional government by grant maze**. A body that lives mainly from earmarked programmes, competitions, discretionary allocations, or ministry-by-ministry applications remains fiscally subordinate even if its statute sounds grand. Grants should supplement or steer. They should not become the everyday oxygen of a territorial government that is supposed to exercise its own judgment.

### 8. Keep borrowing mainly for capital, with visible debt discipline and no off-book bypass

Shared territorial governments often need capital-heavy tools because transport, utility, housing-supporting infrastructure, flood protection, and spatial adaptation all involve long-lived assets. Borrowing can therefore be entirely legitimate.

But the archive should keep the line already visible elsewhere: borrowing belongs mainly to capital investment tied to a lawful budget and public debt discipline, not to chronic operating gaps disguised as sophistication. It should also reject the habit of shifting debt into authorities, development corporations, holding companies, or special vehicles merely to preserve the illusion that the real government has stayed debt-light. A territorial government that benefits from the asset should not hide the liability in institutional shrubbery.

### 9. Put the whole territorial bargain on one visible budget spine

Once a metropolitan or regional tier is real government, its fiscal constitution should not be scattered across the parent municipalities, a clutch of authorities, a national project office, and opaque special funds. The public should be able to see one intelligible budget spine: what ordinary revenues the body receives, what transfers and equalization rules apply, what debt is outstanding, which capital commitments are binding future budgets, and which component governments still finance separate parallel machinery.

Without that budget spine, democratic representation and fiscal reality drift apart. The assembly debates one slice while the consequential bargaining occurs elsewhere.

### 10. Publish fiscal-upgrade triggers when co-operation grows into territorial government

A polity should say in advance what fiscal upgrade follows institutional upgrade. If a consortium acquires broad planning authority, compulsory levies, major investment sequencing power, or a democratically stronger representative home, its funding model should be reviewed too.

The archive should therefore prefer **visible fiscal triggers**: when powers deepen, contributions-only finance is reconsidered; when debt authority appears, treasury and audit rules thicken; when redistribution becomes real, equalization is formalized; when the body becomes the main territorial bargain forum, the public budget should move into that forum as well. This keeps fiscal design from lagging several constitutional steps behind the reality of power.

## Fiscal-home matrix

| Condition | Contributions / contracts fit | Mixed territorial revenue fits | Full territorial fiscal constitution fits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Institution type** | narrow consortium or shared-service body | stronger metropolitan or regional co-ordination body with recurring system tasks | real metropolitan or regional government making consequential bargains |
| **Ordinary revenue** | member contributions, service charges, project money | contribution base plus assigned/shared revenues, formula transfers, limited own charges | own-source or rule-bound shared taxes/charges, predictable transfers/equalization, public budget authority |
| **Discretion** | low to moderate; mostly derivative of members | moderate; some territorial prioritisation | high enough to support accountable territorial choice |
| **Equalization need** | limited or ad hoc | visible but still developing | formal and publishable where territorial disparities matter |
| **Borrowing lane** | rare or project-specific | bounded capital finance | regular capital-market or public-finance access under debt rules and audit |
| **Main risk if misused** | contribution veto and paralysis | blurred accountability between grants and own choices | debt drift, hidden guarantees, or local-democracy erosion if fiscal powers are not clearly divided |

## Guardrails

- Do not pretend annual contribution bargaining is a complete fiscal constitution for a body that already governs a territory.
- Do not assign broad territorial duties without naming a durable operating revenue base.
- Do not fund dynamic system responsibilities through one brittle or politically discretionary source alone.
- Do not use equalization to erase the policy discretion of the receiving territorial government.
- Do not use earmarked or competitive grants as a permanent substitute for ordinary fiscal autonomy.
- Do not hide territorial debt in authorities, corporations, or vehicles that preserve cosmetic innocence while binding the same public.
- Do not let the democratic home of a territory debate only a ceremonial slice of the money while the real fiscal bargain happens elsewhere.
- Do not deepen a shared body’s powers without publishing the fiscal review trigger that should follow.

## Failure modes

- **begging-bowl metro**: a metropolitan body governs in practice but survives on annual municipal pleas and side bargains.
- **statute without treasury**: a regional or metropolitan government exists in law but lacks an ordinary fiscal base proportionate to its tasks.
- **grant-oxygen dependency**: the body can move only when higher tiers open an earmarked programme or competition.
- **farebox fantasy**: broad system government is financed as though it were merely a user-paid service line.
- **equalization denial**: richer component places use constitutional ambiguity to avoid visible solidarity.
- **equalization puppetry**: solidarity exists, but every transfer is so controlled that local or regional discretion becomes fictive.
- **off-book metro debt**: liabilities are shifted into affiliated entities so the territorial government can deny responsibility for its own infrastructure bargain.
- **budget decoupling**: the representative forum exists, but the consequential territorial money map lies outside it.

## Practical tests

A shared territorial government has an honest fiscal home when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can it explain the ordinary revenue basis for its standing responsibilities without relying mainly on annual bargaining or discretionary rescue?
2. Are its resources commensurate with the responsibilities the constitution and law assign to it?
3. Is the revenue basket diversified enough to cope with real cost evolution, growth, and shocks?
4. If territorial disparities matter, is there a visible equalization rule rather than opaque subsidy politics?
5. Are grants used for named purposes rather than as the body’s everyday life support?
6. If it borrows, is the borrowing lane tied mainly to capital investment, public budget authority, audit, and visible debt discipline?
7. Can the public see one intelligible budget spine for the territorial bargain rather than a maze of parallel vehicles and side funds?
8. If the body’s powers deepen, is there a published trigger for fiscal redesign as well as institutional redesign?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a metropolitan or regional body now makes the real bargains of a shared territory but still depends mainly on annual contributions, discretionary grants, or off-book vehicles for its ordinary life, then it still lacks **a fiscal home for shared territorial power** and is drifting toward **metropolitan government by begging bowl**.
