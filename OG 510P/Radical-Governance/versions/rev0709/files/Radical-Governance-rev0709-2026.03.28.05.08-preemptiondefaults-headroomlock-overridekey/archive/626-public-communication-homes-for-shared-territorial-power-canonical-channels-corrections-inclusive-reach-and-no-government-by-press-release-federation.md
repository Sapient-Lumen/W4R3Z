# 626 — Public-communication homes for shared territorial power: canonical channels, corrections, inclusive reach, and no government by press-release federation

## One-line thesis

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, it should acquire an explicit **public-communication home**: a named official communications function distinct from party or campaign messaging; one canonical set of channels for notices, updates, warnings, and explanations; correction and version trails; multilingual and accessible publication discipline; clear docking between general information and legal notice; and archival capture of official statements so the territory does not govern through host-city press offices, operator fragments, screenshots, and rumor.

## Why this matters

The archive already says that real shared territorial power needs democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, participatory, knowledge, service, planning, capital, regulatory, contract, workforce, digital, emergency, redress, territorial, and temporal homes. What it still lacked was the **public-communication analogue**.

That omission matters because many metropolitan and regional bodies become publicly real before they become communicatively honest. Residents meet the shared tier through service alerts, fare changes, consultation pages, emergency warnings, project maps, enforcement announcements, election material, social media posts, mayoral press conferences, or operator notices. Yet the official voice behind those surfaces is often constitutionally thin. One city communications office may speak for the whole region by habit. A contracted operator may become the most trusted source about a public service it does not constitutionally own. A minister, mayor, or board chair may blur institutional information with political argument. Public corrections may happen silently, or not at all. The result is not merely poor branding; it is a failure to make public authority legible.

A real shared tier therefore needs more than a website. It needs an explicit answer to the question: **what counts as the official public voice of this government, how is it corrected, how does it reach everyone affected, and how is it kept distinct from political messaging, operator messaging, and legal notice?**

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish public communication from political communication

The archive should keep a bright line between **public communication in the service of the common good** and party, electoral, or leader-centred political communication. A real shared tier may have elected leadership and contested politics, but its official public-communication home should exist to inform, explain, listen, warn, and respond on behalf of the institution, not to turn the public record into campaign collateral.

### 2. Give the shared tier one canonical public-channel spine

Once territorial power is real, residents should be able to identify the tier’s official channels without guessing which mayor, ministry, operator, contractor, or board member is currently most vocal. The archive should therefore prefer one canonical channel spine: official web domain, official publication or document register where needed, verified emergency channels, verified service-status channels, and explicit rules about which social or third-party platforms are merely mirrors.

If the real public route is still “whoever posts first” or “whatever host government currently carries the message”, the polity has not built an honest communication home.

### 3. Separate general information, service updates, emergency warnings, and legal notice

Not every message has the same legal or civic weight. The archive should distinguish:

- general public information and explanation,
- service updates and operational status,
- emergency warnings and crisis instructions,
- participatory notices and consultation invitations,
- and legally consequential notice that starts clocks or changes rights.

These may travel through related channels, but they should not be allowed to collapse into one generic feed where no one knows what is authoritative, what is advisory, and what triggers legal consequences.

### 4. Make corrections visible instead of letting edits disappear into the feed

A real government should not quietly edit away mistakes in public-facing material when the message affected rights, planning assumptions, public safety, or public understanding of what the institution was doing. The archive should prefer correction discipline: timestamped updates, visible correction notes, version history where the stakes justify it, links from superseded material to current material, and preservation of the original statement in the public record or archive.

Otherwise governance migrates into screenshot folklore and adversarial archival labor.

### 5. Keep channel parity and inclusive reach

A communication home is dishonest if it assumes everyone can use the same channel, language, literacy level, or device. The archive should therefore prefer accessible formats, clear language, multilingual coverage where the territory’s population requires it, parity between digital and assisted-digital routes, and explicit provision for people who rely on call centres, in-person counters, disability accommodations, or partner organisations for access.

This is not a decorative accessibility norm. It is part of whether the government can actually reach the public it governs.

### 6. Dock communication to records, evidence, and discoverability

Official communications are not just publicity. They are part of the evidence-bearing surface of government. The archive should prefer durable URLs, archival capture of consequential statements, links to the underlying decision or record where possible, searchable publication histories, and an explicit distinction between live updates, archived notices, and later interpretation.

If a territory can make consequential public claims but cannot later show what it said, when, where, and on what authority, its communication constitution is still thin.

### 7. Give operators and member governments communication roles, but not constitutional ownership of the message

In metropolitan and regional systems, operators, utilities, municipalities, and ministries will often communicate with the public. That can be appropriate. But the archive should resist letting those bodies become the de facto constitutional voice of the shared tier. The communication home should specify when partners may issue information in their own name, when they must relay or mirror territorial messages, when co-badging is required, and who owns correction authority when messages conflict.

Otherwise the public learns the territory through federation residue rather than through a visible public institution.

### 8. Require an answer-and-listen lane, not only broadcast

OECD public-communication practice treats communication as a two-way government function. The archive should therefore prefer public-contact routes that can receive questions, route them, and answer them; clear response norms; and visible escalation where communication failures expose deeper service or governance failures.

A communication home that only broadcasts is still incomplete. Residents must be able to tell the institution that its message is wrong, unclear, missing, inaccessible, or contradicted by reality.

### 9. Keep crisis communication predeclared rather than improvised

Emergency note 622 already says a real shared tier needs exercised warning and recovery doctrine. The communication-home analogue is narrower: the territory should know in advance who speaks in a crisis, which channels are canonical, what fallback channels exist, how member governments and operators mirror or localise messages, and how corrections are issued when early information is incomplete.

Otherwise public communication in crisis collapses into leader improvisation, media brokerage, or conflicting operator alerts.

### 10. Protect the boundary between explanation and propaganda

A real shared tier may explain policy, service changes, taxes, or institutional redesign. But the archive should prefer visible rules against using official channels and resources to blur institutional explanation into personal credit-claiming, opposition attack, or pre-election image management. The question is not whether the institution may explain itself. The question is whether residents can still recognise when they are hearing the government as a public authority rather than the incumbent as a campaigner.

### 11. Treat communications capability as a permanent operating function, not a last-minute decoration

A polity that governs across a territory should not discover too late that it has no staffed route for public explanation, correction, translation, accessibility review, audience segmentation, rumor response, or public archiving. The archive should therefore prefer a small but explicit communications function with authority, staffing, routing rules, and continuity arrangements of its own.

## Communication-home matrix

| Question | Thin or dishonest arrangement | Honest public-communication home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Who speaks officially?** | host-city press office, ministerial habit, or operator voice by default | named institutional communication function with clear authority |
| **Which channels count?** | scattered feeds, copied PDFs, screenshots, unofficial reposting | canonical channel spine with mirrors clearly labeled |
| **What kind of message is this?** | advisory, emergency, legal, and political messages blur together | message classes are explicit and routed accordingly |
| **How are mistakes corrected?** | silent edits, deletions, and post hoc denial | visible corrections, timestamps, and links to superseded material |
| **Who can actually receive it?** | one language, one app, one literacy assumption | accessible, multilingual, assisted-digital, and parity-minded reach |
| **Can the message be reconstructed later?** | no durable archive, no authority trail | preserved official statements linked to records and dates |
| **How do partners communicate?** | operators and member governments improvise inconsistent messages | roles, mirroring rules, and correction ownership are explicit |
| **Can the public answer back?** | broadcast only; no routing for questions or complaints | two-way contact, routing, and response discipline |

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse polished branding with an official communications constitution.
- Do not let social-media virality determine what counts as the authoritative message.
- Do not let service operators become the public sovereign merely because they own the app or feed people watch most.
- Do not treat silent edits as acceptable when public understanding, rights, or safety were affected.
- Do not collapse legal notice into ordinary publicity or ordinary publicity into legal notice.
- Do not assume one language, one medium, or one device is enough for a governed territory.
- Do not allow official institutional channels to become campaign channels by drift.
- Do not leave crisis communication to whoever happens to reach the microphone first.

## Failure modes

- **government by press-release federation**: the shared tier exists in public only as a bundle of host-government, operator, and mayoral messages.
- **channel ambiguity**: nobody can tell which website, feed, or alert stream is actually authoritative.
- **silent correction**: consequential public errors disappear without visible correction trail.
- **notice blur**: advisory content, legal notice, operational updates, and emergency warning are mixed together.
- **access-by-device**: people effectively lose access because the message only works for those with the preferred app, language, or literacy.
- **operator sovereignty by interface**: the vendor or operator that owns the front-end becomes the real public face of government.
- **archive amnesia**: consequential statements cannot later be reconstructed, cited, or challenged.
- **propaganda seepage**: institutional channels drift into incumbent self-promotion or party signalling.

## Practical tests

A polity is handling public communication honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can residents identify one canonical public-channel spine for the shared tier without needing local insider knowledge?
2. Is it clear which messages are general information, service updates, emergency warnings, participatory notices, and legally consequential notice?
3. Does the institution maintain visible correction and supersession practice for consequential public statements?
4. Are official communications archived or otherwise reconstructable with durable dates, locations, and authority trails?
5. Are multilingual, accessibility, and assisted-digital needs treated as operating requirements rather than afterthoughts?
6. Can operators, municipalities, and ministries explain exactly when they speak for themselves, when they mirror the shared tier, and who owns corrections if messages conflict?
7. Is there a real public-contact route for questions, clarifications, and communication failure reports?
8. Are official institutional communications protected from drift into electoral or partisan messaging?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared metropolitan or regional tier exercises real public power but residents still cannot say **which channels are official, what class of message they are seeing, how corrections are made, whether the message is archived, or whether they are hearing the institution rather than a host government, operator, or campaign voice**, then the polity has not built an honest public-communication home. It is still governing by **press-release federation**.
