# 784 — Administrative consistency, equal treatment, and reasoned departure necessity tests for ideal governments: like cases alike, public memory, and no random-walk administration

## One-line thesis

Ideal governments should require **administrative decisions to treat like cases alike, keep usable memory of prior positions, and explain material departures from settled guidance or practice**: consistency should discipline discretion without freezing error forever, and change should occur through visible reasons, prospective clarification, and fair transition rather than through office lottery or silent drift.

## Why this matters

The archive already says that permit systems need clear criteria and visible procedural grammar (`573`), that appeals need reasons and file access (`773`), that administrative guidance must not become hidden law (`782`), and that legitimate expectations can arise when people order their lives around what the state has said or done (`783`). What it still lacked as one compact operating rule was the bridge between those notes:

**when must administration decide today in a way that coheres with what it decided yesterday?**

Every real administration accumulates positions. Front desks learn informal thresholds. inspectors settle into recurring interpretations. benefits offices form repeat practices. tribunals see the same fact patterns over and over. even where no formal precedent doctrine exists, the state creates expectations and comparative baselines through its own behaviour. If two materially similar applicants receive opposite treatment because they saw different officials, filed in different months, or landed in different regional offices, the problem is not only inefficiency. It is a rule-of-law problem.

But the opposite mistake is also common. Officials become so afraid of inconsistency that they copy prior error forever. One mistaken earlier approval becomes a perpetual entitlement. Internal digests harden into pseudo-law. Or an agency changes course for good reasons but refuses to admit it has changed course, leaving the unlucky transition cohort punished for being early, visible, or politically unconnected.

Ideal-government design therefore needs a doctrine of **administrative consistency necessity**. The point is not to import a full judicial doctrine of stare decisis into every permit desk. It is to require a more honest administrative grammar: the system should be able to show what counts as a materially similar case, what prior positions matter, what legal status prior decisions and guidance carry, when equal treatment requires alignment, when unlawfulness blocks repetition, when policy change should be prospective, how departures must be reasoned, and which institutions can detect drift across offices before randomness becomes the true constitution of administration.

## Necessity table

| Necessity test | What counts as proof | Honest design consequence | Default answer if proof is weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Need to keep like cases from turning on office lottery** | materially similar people receive different outcomes across officials, regions, or time without stated reasons | require consistency checks, comparison discipline, and departure reasons | let discretion dissolve into postcode or desk lottery |
| **Need to preserve legal equality without freezing first error forever** | prior decisions are relevant but some are plainly unlawful or factually distinguishable | use prior decisions as comparators, not blind replicas, and explain why an unlawful error will not be repeated | copy mistakes indefinitely in the name of fairness |
| **Need for visible policy evolution rather than silent drift** | agencies change interpretation, evidence demands, or enforcement posture over time | announce changes, date them, preserve old positions, and apply transition or reliance discipline where appropriate | change practice through quiet internal mood shifts |
| **Need to coordinate multi-office administration** | decentralised offices apply the same law differently | maintain digests, escalation channels, and central clarification lanes for recurring questions | let each office become its own small sovereign |
| **Need to make review institutions able to see inconsistency** | courts, ombuds, or auditors cannot tell whether similar cases were treated alike | keep decision records, searchable guidance history, and stated departure reasons | deny review because administrative memory is missing |
| **Need to preserve case-specific judgment** | officials claim consistency requires mechanical repetition even when facts differ materially | require comparison on relevant facts only and preserve proportional, individualized assessment | flatten judgment into checklist absolutism |
| **Need to prevent insiders from monopolising tacit practice** | practical success depends on brokers who know the unofficial rule | publish recurrent interpretations and convert stable practice into visible guidance or formal rulemaking where needed | govern by internal folklore and brokerage |
| **Need to distinguish lawful precedence from de facto lawmaking** | prior decisions are used as if they silently bind everyone else | type the status of prior decisions honestly and move broad norms into guidance or law when they become general | let accumulation of examples substitute for public rulemaking |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from a like-cases-alike presumption, not a same-answer-always reflex

Administrative consistency begins with a rebuttable presumption that materially similar cases should not produce materially different outcomes without explanation. The key word is **materially**. Equal treatment does not require ignoring real factual, legal, temporal, or risk differences. It requires the administration to identify such differences honestly rather than hiding them behind conclusory discretion language.

### 2. Keep a usable memory of recurring positions

Where recurring fact patterns or interpretive questions arise, administrations should maintain internal digests, comparator banks, advisory compilations, or other usable memory tools. If those tools materially affect outsiders, the outward-relevant parts should migrate into public guidance, public examples, or formal rulemaking rather than remaining permanent internal folklore.

### 3. Distinguish prior decisions, public guidance, and binding law by status

A prior permit, ruling, or benefit decision is not necessarily binding on all future cases. Public guidance is not enacted law. An internal desk manual is not the same as either. Ideal governments should force these categories to be named honestly so administrators and affected people know whether a prior position is a comparator, a presumptive interpretation, a binding legal rule, or only one data point.

### 4. Require express reasons for material departure

If the administration reaches a different result from its own recent practice, public guidance, prior assurance, or office norm in a materially similar case, it should say so expressly and explain why. Silent inconsistency is usually worse than acknowledged change, because it destroys both predictability and reviewability.

### 5. Do not repeat clear unlawfulness just to preserve visual symmetry

Equal treatment does not require “equal illegality.” If an earlier decision was clearly ultra vires, fraudulent, or based on obvious error, the state may refuse to repeat it. But it should say that this is what it is doing, correct its public materials if needed, and where reliance or transition issues arise address them openly rather than hiding behind bare assertions that “each case is different.”

### 6. Move stable broad norms out of example-accumulation and into public guidance or law

If an administration finds itself repeatedly deciding the same issue the same way, that is often evidence that a more explicit public rule or guidance document is needed. An ideal system does not let hundreds of near-identical case outcomes do the work that one visible clarification should do.

### 7. Use central clarification lanes when decentralised offices diverge

In distributed systems, some divergence is inevitable. What matters is whether there is a lawful route to detect and correct it: central interpretive circulars, review boards, legal services opinions, ombuds recommendations, appellate decisions, audit findings, or structured cross-office case conferences. Without a correction lane, federal or regional pluralism turns into random walk administration.

### 8. Make policy change prospective where fairness requires

When administration changes course on a recurring issue, the fairest response is often not to pretend nothing changed. It is to announce the new interpretation, date it, archive the old position, and where needed provide transition windows, grandfathering, or case-by-case reliance relief. That keeps consistency from becoming a trap for both the state and the public.

### 9. Monitor inconsistency as an operational signal, not just a litigation event

Agencies should use appeals, complaints, ombuds findings, audit, and simple outcome analytics to detect unexplained divergence across offices or staff. Consistency failures are usually visible in the data and complaint stream before they become jurisprudence.

### 10. Preserve individualized proportionality and mercy where law allows

Consistency should not erase the possibility of waiver, proportional adjustment, hardship accommodation, or other individualized judgment where the law permits it. The design aim is principled coherence, not dehumanised repetition.

## Guardrails

- Do not let materially similar cases yield different outcomes without reasons.
- Do not copy prior unlawful decisions just to avoid visible asymmetry.
- Do not let internal practice settle broad public norms that never migrate into visible guidance or law.
- Do not change course by quiet desk drift while pretending the old position never existed.
- Do not treat “consistency” as a license for algorithmic or checklist absolutism where the law still requires judgment.
- Do not make comparison impossible by failing to keep decision memory, version history, or explanation trails.

## Failure modes

- **random-walk administration**: outcomes depend more on official, office, or filing date than on lawfully relevant facts.
- **equal illegality**: one earlier mistake is repeated indefinitely in the name of fairness.
- **folklore consistency**: insiders know the real office norm, but outsiders only see the formal text.
- **silent policy drift**: practice changes without public acknowledgement, archive trail, or transition discipline.
- **pseudo-precedent creep**: accumulated examples become binding in practice without legal-status clarity.
- **checklist equality**: consistency is purchased by suppressing proportionality, hardship accommodation, and case-specific judgment.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can tell you **that agencies issue decisions and sometimes guidance** but cannot also show **how like cases are compared, when prior decisions matter, how departures are reasoned, how decentralised offices are kept from diverging, when unlawfulness blocks repetition, and how broad recurring positions are converted into visible guidance or law**, then it still has **administrative discretion without a consistency constitution**.
