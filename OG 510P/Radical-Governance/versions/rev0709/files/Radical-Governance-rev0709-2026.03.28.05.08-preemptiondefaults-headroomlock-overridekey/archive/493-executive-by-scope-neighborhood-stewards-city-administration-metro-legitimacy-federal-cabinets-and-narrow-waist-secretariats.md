# 493 — Executive by scope: neighborhood stewards, city administration, metro legitimacy, federal cabinets, and narrow-waist secretariats

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should not use one executive template at every scale: it should rely on collegial stewardship and recallable coordinators in the smallest units, elected councils backed by professional administration in municipalities, directly legitimated executives for metropolitan systems that can actually bind the region, politically accountable cabinets where broad shared policy must be driven, and narrow secretariats rather than pseudo-sovereign executives in plurilateral and global bodies.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a sharper answer to **what each scale should govern**, **how it should be represented**, **how boundaries should adapt**, and **how scope conflicts should be routed**. It still needs a compact answer to a different design question: **who actually runs the machinery at each scale, and in what form?**

That question is not trivial. Executive design determines whether decisions are coherent, legible, and reversible or instead drift into personal rule, committee fog, or technocratic shadow government. A block association does not need a miniature presidency. A metropolitan transit-and-housing region should not be ruled only by back-room bargaining among indirectly delegated officeholders. A nation cannot safely improvise its executive form without regard to legislative support. A global body should coordinate and implement agreed mandates, not quietly mutate into an unbounded sovereign.

The ideal answer is therefore scale-matched executive design. Executives should be strong enough to coordinate at their own level, but not so overgrown that they swallow representation, review, or lower-tier self-government.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Do not clone one executive model across every scale

Strong-mayor logics, council-manager logics, cabinet government, collegial boards, and treaty secretariats solve different coordination problems. The archive should reject executive monoculture just as it rejects one-size-fits-all representation.

### 2. Keep micro-local executive authority light, collegial, and recallable

At the block, village, ward, tribal, or neighborhood level, the executive function should usually be modest: convening meetings, coordinating small works, maintaining shared spaces, publishing decisions, and escalating issues that exceed local competence. This level is generally best served by:

- chairs or convenors with limited terms,
- small collegial committees,
- recallable delegates,
- and narrow stewardship mandates rather than broad personal discretion.

The smallest units need operational coordination, not miniature sovereigns.

### 3. Give municipalities professional administration under elected direction

Municipal government usually works best when elected representatives set priorities and approve budgets while a professional administration runs service delivery, staffing, procurement, inspection, and continuity. Exact constitutional form can vary, but the archive should strongly prefer a visible separation between:

- democratic direction,
- professional administration,
- and independent review or audit.

This is the scale where execution must be competent every day, not reinvented every election.

### 4. Require direct democratic legitimacy for metropolitan executives with real binding power

Where a metropolitan authority can tax, zone, allocate housing obligations, run transport trunks, or coordinate major water and waste systems, its executive leadership should not depend entirely on informal inter-municipal brokerage. It should have democratic legitimacy proportionate to its actual power, whether through direct election or another electorally legible route anchored in the public rather than only in municipal bargaining.

### 5. Use regional executives for platforms and landscapes, not for ornamental duplication

Regional, provincial, state, basin, and similar executives justify themselves when they run functions that genuinely live at that scale: hospital systems, watershed plans, grid corridors, tertiary education platforms, regional development, and disaster logistics. They should not exist merely as duplicative prestige layers between city and nation.

### 6. Fit the national executive to the party system and legislature, not to constitutional fashion

At the national level, executive design must be evaluated together with party fragmentation, electoral rules, bicameral structure, judicial review, and emergency powers. The archive should distrust executive designs that create two rival democratic command centers without a clear correction path, or that combine direct personal mandates with legislatures too fragmented to sustain governable coalition or accountability.

### 7. Keep federal executives compatible with partial self-rule

In federations and heavily regionalized states, the central executive should be capable of common action without treating constituent units as branch offices. That means:

- clear limits on central override,
- lawful intergovernmental forums,
- transparent use of conditional funding,
- and operational coordination rules that do not quietly erase subnational autonomy.

### 8. Make plurilateral and global executives secretariat-like and mandate-bound

Continental unions, treaty systems, and global bodies need people who can coordinate, monitor, convene, publish, and implement agreed rules. But these executive organs should usually be narrow-waist secretariats or commissions with explicit mandates, not free-ranging governments of the world. Their legitimacy comes from scope clarity, member consent, transparency, and problem fit.

### 9. Build handback, audit, and review into executive power from the start

Every level should make it easy to tell:

- who may act,
- on what authority,
- for how long,
- subject to what review,
- and how powers revert when the task or emergency ends.

An executive that cannot hand power back cleanly is not merely energetic. It is structurally unsafe.

## Guardrails

- Do not install presidential theatre at neighborhood scale.
- Do not let elected municipal politics substitute for professional administrative capacity.
- Do not leave metropolitan systems to informal bargaining when they exercise binding public power.
- Do not choose a national executive form without checking its fit with the legislature and party system.
- Do not let treaty or global secretariats acquire de facto open-ended executive authority by drift.

## Failure modes

- **micro-sovereignism**: tiny jurisdictions acquire oversized personal executive offices detached from real function.
- **committee fog**: nobody can tell who is accountable for execution because authority is endlessly diffused.
- **metro brokerage rule**: urban systems are governed by inter-municipal dealmaking rather than public-legible executive responsibility.
- **dual-mandate collision**: the national constitution creates rival democratic executives without a reliable correction path.
- **secretariat creep**: narrow international implementing bodies quietly become unbounded political executives.

## Practical tests

An executive architecture passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is executive form matched to the scale and problem class rather than copied from another tier?
2. Are municipal services run through competent professional administration under democratic direction?
3. Do metropolitan executives with real coercive authority have public legitimacy strong enough for their task?
4. Is national executive design evaluated together with legislative structure and party-system reality?
5. Are regional, plurilateral, and global executives tightly scoped to functions they can justify?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity uses **the same executive imagination for a block board, a city, a metro, a federation, and a treaty body**, it is probably not designing government by scale; it is repeating **constitutional furniture where institutional fit is required**.
