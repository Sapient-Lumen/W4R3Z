# 792 — Stress-test and drill cards for ideal governments of each scope: essential-service continuity, command legibility, fiscal buffers, rights discipline, rescoping signals, and no paper constitution by calm weather

## One-line thesis

The archive should pair its ideal-government answer key and benchmark cards with one compact **stress-test and drill-card format** for each scope — essential-service continuity, command and escalation legibility, reserve and surge discipline, intergovernmental handoff integrity, legality and rights restraint under pressure, public communication, and recovery or handback triggers — so governments are judged not only by their steady-state design but by whether they remain honest under flood, blackout, outbreak, fiscal shock, cyber disruption, or constitutional strain.

## Why this matters

`790` gives the archive a short answer key for what the ideal government at each scope should be. `791` gives a compact card for benchmarking whether a real constitution or proposal matches that ideal stack in ordinary time. `632` gives scope-audit triggers for when the live allocation has drifted far enough to reopen the settlement. `757` gives the archive a standing anti-theater rule for emergency powers, and `567` already says that debt and hidden guarantees should be stress-tested rather than discovered at the cliff edge.

What the archive still lacked was one compact object for a different question:

**does the arrangement still tell the truth about who governs what once conditions become adverse?**

That matters because many governments look coherent in calm weather and collapse into constitutional fog under stress. A municipality that appears competent in normal service cycles may have no continuity lane for water, notice, payroll, or emergency procurement. A metropolitan arrangement that looks collaborative may revert to host-city command the moment rail, housing, or flood systems are stressed. A region may discover that its health or logistics platform depends on informal ministerial rescue. A nation may claim guarantor status while actually carrying no disciplined way to surge money, protect rights, preserve legislative scrutiny, or hand powers back after the shock. Treaty bodies and global institutions often face the reverse pathology: they are blamed for failures that were really implementation or state-capacity failures below them, because nobody stated the drill grammar in advance.

The archive therefore needs a short **stress-test card**. Not a thick resilience manual. Not a generic crisis-playbook archive. One compact device that asks the same few questions of every scope, so later notes can judge whether an institution remains constitutional, reviewable, and properly scoped when reality turns hostile.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Use stress-test cards after design and benchmark work, not instead of them

A stress-test card is not the archive’s first instrument.

Use:

- `700` when the archive is stating a proposed ideal-government packet,
- `701` when rival packets must be compared,
- `790` when a reader needs the short answer-key stack,
- `791` when a real arrangement needs a steady-state benchmark,
- and this note when the archive is asking **whether that arrangement keeps constitutional honesty under pressure rather than only on paper in ordinary time**.

### 2. Separate calm-weather adequacy from shock-weather honesty

A government can meet the ordinary benchmark and still fail the stress card.

That is because the shock question is different. The archive is no longer asking only whether the minimum kit exists. It is asking whether the kit stays legible when ordinary assumptions break: staff absence, utility interruption, market exit, disease spread, flood, wildfire, cyber outage, border friction, supply failure, court backlog surge, or sudden revenue collapse.

A stress-test card should therefore complement a benchmark card, not duplicate it.

When the archive instead needs to say **how often** those drills should occur, how they connect to routine benchmark refresh, or what publication and closure rhythm follows each exercise, it should use `793` rather than overloading the stress card itself. When recurring drill failure needs to become a phased redesign or handback pathway, it should use `794` rather than stretching the card into a transition plan. When the archive needs to say **what public incident or drill summary must remain visible after the test**, it should use `795` rather than leaving the stress result inside expert notes.

### 3. Ask the same seven stress questions at every scope

Every stress-test card should ask the same seven questions.

1. **Essential-service continuity** — what must keep working, at degraded but real level, through the shock?
2. **Command and escalation legibility** — who leads, who supports, who can requisition, and where do unresolved failures escalate?
3. **Reserve and surge discipline** — what fiscal, staffing, mutual-aid, procurement, or technical buffers exist before improvisation begins?
4. **Intergovernmental handoff integrity** — can information, authority, money, and assets pass cleanly across levels without orphan gaps or command overlap?
5. **Legality and rights restraint** — what must still be published, reviewable, challengeable, and time-limited even when decisions accelerate?
6. **Public communication and witness continuity** — how will residents, counterpart institutions, and affected workers know what changed, for how long, and with what remedies?
7. **Recovery, handback, and rescoping signals** — what triggers restoration to ordinary governance, and what evidence would instead force durable redesign under `632`?

These seven questions let the archive compare stress performance without pretending every shock is the same.

### 4. Keep the drills typed, short, and scenario-agnostic enough to reuse

The archive should not explode into one bespoke drill note for every hazard class unless a later domain note truly needs it.

A stress-test card should therefore stay at the constitutional layer. It should usually name a small reusable hazard family such as:

- flood, wildfire, earthquake, or other place shock,
- blackout, water, network, or other infrastructure disruption,
- outbreak, contamination, or other public-health event,
- fiscal cliff, revenue crash, or supplier failure,
- cyber or registry compromise,
- or constitutional strain such as prolonged emergency rule, caretaker drift, or intergovernmental deadlock.

The point is to see whether the government’s **constitutional routing and minimum kit remain honest** under pressure, not to build a full emergency-services handbook inside the archive.

### 5. Distinguish underpreparedness from mis-scope under stress

A failed drill may reveal two very different problems.

Sometimes the scope is still right, but the government is underprepared: too few reserves, no continuity plan, weak mutual aid, stale records, thin procurement lane, or no backup command site.

Other times the drill reveals mis-scope: the municipality is carrying a metropolitan system alone, the metro has no democratic authority over the system everyone expects it to stabilize, the region is decorative while the nation improvises everything, or the global body is being asked to implement functions that belong to states.

A stress-test card should separate **underprepared** from **mis-scoped under stress** rather than treating all failure as mere lack of planning.

### 6. Make legality visible inside the drill, not after it

Stress-testing is not merely about keeping pumps running or data moving.

The archive should insist that every drill card state what remains legally visible under pressure: emergency authority used, publication duty, time limit, legislative or council renewal point, complaint entry, review lane, procurement trace, notice rule, or record capture requirement. `757` already supplies the emergency-powers discipline. This note says the discipline should be tested, not merely declared.

### 7. Force every drill to end with a rescoping question

The archive should never treat stress drills as pure operational rehearsal.

Every drill must end with one constitutional question: **did the shock reveal a repairable gap inside the current scope, or did it prove that the function has outgrown its current home?**

That is where this note links back to `632`. The drill should produce either:

- no rescoping signal,
- a kit-completion or connector-repair task,
- a temporary support-with-handback task,
- or a formal trigger to reopen the scope allocation.

### 8. Treat recovery and handback as part of the constitutional test

Many institutions do not fail at the peak of crisis. They fail in the tail.

Temporary hosts remain in control too long. Emergency procurement never re-enters normal discipline. Central command lingers after local capability returns. Special funds become permanent shadow budgets. Crisis data systems keep operating without ordinary publication, retention, and review rules.

A stress-test card should therefore test **exit honesty**, not just response speed.

### 9. Keep cards short enough to compare across scopes and cases

The archive should be able to put several stress cards side by side.

That means one card should usually fit on one compact table or one short list. If a case needs many pages before anyone can tell whether the government held constitutional shape under stress, the archive probably needs a domain-specific note later. The starter constitutional card should still be short.

## Stress-card grammar

| Field | What the card should say | Typical outputs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Archetype claimed** | which scope the arrangement says it is | municipal, metropolitan, regional, national, continental, global |
| **Shock family** | the reusable adverse condition being tested | flood, blackout, outbreak, fiscal shock, cyber outage, constitutional strain |
| **Essential-service continuity** | what had to keep operating and whether continuity was real | holds / degraded but real / continuity gap |
| **Command and escalation legibility** | whether lead, support, requisition, and escalation routes were visible | clear / conditional / fogged |
| **Reserve and surge discipline** | whether reserves, surge staff, backup procurement, or mutual aid existed before improvisation | prepared / thin / improvisational |
| **Intergovernmental handoff integrity** | whether authority, information, money, and assets crossed levels cleanly | clean / conditional / broken |
| **Legality and rights restraint** | whether emergency acts stayed published, time-limited, reviewable, and challengeable | disciplined / strained / shadow |
| **Public communication and witness continuity** | whether affected people and institutions could still see what changed and where to seek remedy | visible / patchy / opaque |
| **Recovery / handback / rescoping signal** | whether the result implies restore, repair, temporary support, or formal rescoping review | restore / repair / support-with-handback / rescope |
| **Overall stress result** | compact synthetic judgment | holds / underprepared / mis-scoped under stress / shadow response |

## Scope pass signs under stress

| Scope | Pass signs | Failure smell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Micro-local** | witness and mutual-aid cell still reachable; local signal capture continues; clear upward escalation; no fake sovereign freelancing | panic theater, rumor routing, or tiny-body command cosplay |
| **Municipal** | water, sanitation, counters, notices, and local service triage continue at degraded but real level; council or delegated scrutiny remains visible; mutual aid and reserves exist | service continuity collapse, invisible emergency procurement, or center-by-default takeover with no handback path |
| **Metropolitan** | trunk systems, shared mobility, basin risk, and region-wide allocation tradeoffs stay coordinated through a legible metro command lane | host-city capture, summit improvisation, or operator patchwork with no public constitutional home |
| **Regional / provincial / state** | hospital, corridor, grid, disaster logistics, and territorial-platform functions surge coherently with real finance and clear support to municipalities | decorative region collapse, ministerial rescue by improvisation, or platform work revealed as national folklore |
| **National** | equalization, macro-fiscal shock absorption, constitutional publication, legislative renewal, judicial or review continuity, and national mutual-aid routing still operate | permanent emergency absorption, rights blackout, or solidarity language masking operational chaos |
| **Continental / plurilateral** | treaty bodies coordinate cross-border information, corridor rules, procurement or mobility compatibility, and dispute handling without pretending to be domestic government | competence creep by panic, or blame shifting to a compact body that lacks implementation powers |
| **Global** | alerting, reporting, scientific synthesis, interoperability, and treaty-bound coordination continue with visible implementation boundaries | world-state theater, empty forum ritual, or domestic implementation failure falsely narrated as global-command failure |

## Example card grammar

| Field | Example entry |
| --- | --- |
| **Archetype claimed** | metropolitan |
| **Shock family** | flood and trunk-transit disruption |
| **Essential-service continuity** | degraded but real: main transit spine, evacuation routing, and basin pumping remained coordinated across the urban area |
| **Command and escalation legibility** | conditional: metro incident room led system allocation, but procurement still depended on host-city legal cover |
| **Reserve and surge discipline** | thin: surge staffing existed through operator mutual aid, but reserve finance for extended disruption was weak |
| **Intergovernmental handoff integrity** | clean between metro and municipalities for service routing, but broken upward for rapid capital authorization |
| **Legality and rights restraint** | disciplined: emergency traffic orders were published, time-limited, and challengeable, though committee scrutiny lagged |
| **Public communication and witness continuity** | patchy: service maps were visible, but compensation and complaint routes for displaced riders were hard to find |
| **Recovery / handback / rescoping signal** | repair plus possible upgrade: fix procurement lane now; reopen metro fiscal kit if borrowing and emergency allocation powers remain deep |
| **Overall stress result** | underprepared rather than mis-scoped |

The point is not this exact wording. The point is that the archive should be able to compare several governments or reform proposals on whether they stay constitutionally honest under pressure without writing a disaster manual each time.

## Guardrails

- Do not use a stress card before the arrangement’s archetype is clear enough to name.
- Do not confuse operational slowness with constitutional failure unless the lag reveals command, scope, legality, or continuity dishonesty.
- Do not let emergency effectiveness excuse rights opacity, unpublished powers, or nonreviewable orders.
- Do not treat central rescue as proof that the center should permanently own the function.
- Do not treat every failed drill as mis-scope when some failures are kit-completion or reserve problems.
- Do not score treaty or global bodies as though they were supposed to be national states.
- Do not let recovery and handback disappear from the card once the immediate shock passes.

## Failure modes

- **paper constitution by calm weather** — the arrangement looks coherent only when nothing abnormal happens.
- **response admiration without legality** — speed or heroics hide publication, review, or rights failure.
- **host-of-last-resort drift** — temporary support bodies quietly become permanent rulers after repeated shocks.
- **mis-scope disguised as poor planning** — the wrong level keeps failing and the archive blames preparedness alone.
- **drill without handback** — crisis entry is rehearsed but restoration to ordinary government is not.
- **implementation blame fog** — continental or global institutions are credited or blamed for tasks that legally belonged elsewhere.
- **invisible tail failure** — the crisis peak passes, but emergency budgets, data systems, or command chains remain outside normal discipline.

## Compression rule for the archive

If the archive wants to say that a real constitution or reform proposal is not only well designed in ordinary time but **robust under shock**, it should usually be able to do so with one short stress-test card naming the shock family, essential-service continuity, command and escalation legibility, reserve and surge discipline, intergovernmental handoff integrity, legality and rights restraint, public communication and witness continuity, and recovery or rescoping signals. If it cannot, the resilience claim is still too atmospheric to count as a stable archive judgment.
