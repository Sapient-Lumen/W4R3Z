# 826 — Mandate-source defaults for ideal governments of each scope: assembly voice, council confidence, metro public mandates, regional investiture, national confidence, and treaty delegation

## One-line thesis

The archive should answer the question **where should governing authority at each scope get its mandate?** with scale-matched defaults rather than a direct-election fetish: assembly voice and recallable stewardship at micro-local scale, elected councils as the primary democratic source in municipalities, whole-territory public mandates for metropolitan governments that can bind a real urban system, assembly-invested regional government where a region genuinely carries platform functions, parliamentary or assembly-confidence national government as the default source of executive authority, and treaty- or charter-derived delegation with bounded parliamentary and public complements for plurilateral and global bodies.

## Why this matters

The archive already has a stronger answer to **what each scope is for** (`790`), **what regime form usually fits each scope** (`825`), **how executives should be designed** (`493`), **how representation and electoral systems should vary by scale** (`490`, `566`), **how governments are formed and removed** (`752`), and **how cross-boundary legitimacy chains should be composed once a form exists** (`813`).

What it still lacked as one compact note was a sharper answer to a narrower but constantly recurring constitutional question:

**when a body is real, from what democratic or delegated source should its authority actually flow?**

That gap matters because many constitutional arguments smuggle legitimacy in through one crude shortcut: **direct election equals democratic seriousness**. That shortcut is often false. A neighborhood does not become more legitimate because it elects a tiny president. A municipality does not become more democratic because a charismatic mayor is directly elected while the council weakens and the administrative state blurs. A metropolitan body that can tax, zone, borrow, and allocate obligations may indeed need direct territorial authorization, because residents are bound as a region rather than only as members of separate municipalities. A region with real platform functions usually needs democratic authority too, but not necessarily a separately elected personal executive. A nation should not lightly create two rival popular mandates when one confidence chain would govern more honestly. A continental or global body does not become legitimate merely by fantasizing a giant undifferentiated plebiscite across a public that the institution cannot yet honestly organize, answer to, or enforce against.

The archive therefore needs a doctrine of **mandate-source fit**. Mandate source is not identical to regime form, electoral formula, or review design. It is the answer to a prior question: **who authorizes this body, through what route, and with how much directness, derivation, or confidence linkage?** That question deserves its own compression layer because later scope notes should not have to rediscover it piecemeal from elections, executives, and legitimacy-chain design every time.

## Scope table

| Scope | Default mandate source | Why this fit is usually best | What it must not become |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Micro-local** | open assembly, simple local election, or recallable delegate mandate | keeps authority intimate, legible, and easy to reverse | miniature personal presidency |
| **Municipal** | elected council as primary democratic source, with manager or executive tied lawfully to it and only bounded mayoral exceptions | matches everyday government to plural local representation plus professional administration | municipal plebiscitary sovereignty |
| **Metropolitan / county / functional urban area** | whole-territory public mandate for chamber and a directly elected or publicly invested executive where powers are materially binding | prevents regional rule by incumbent cartel or host-city proxy | metro government without a metro demos |
| **Regional / provincial / state** | elected assembly plus investiture / confidence for the executive, with territorial second keys only where shared rule truly requires them | gives regions democratic thickness without automatically duplicating national presidentialism | regional nation cosplay |
| **National** | parliamentary or assembly-confidence mandate for government by default; separate direct presidential mandate only by strong positive case | gives one visible route from election to governable executive responsibility | dual democratic command by habit |
| **Continental / plurilateral** | treaty ratification, member-state delegation, and bounded parliamentary / public complements | matches mixed legitimacy to problem-bound shared rule below full statehood | superstate legitimacy theater |
| **Global** | charter and treaty delegation through states plus universal assembly, reporting, judicial, and public-accountability complements | fits a narrow-waist order for irreducibly global tasks | planetary executive plebiscite fantasy |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat mandate source as its own design layer

The archive should stop treating mandate source as an incidental by-product of elections or office titles. It is its own constitutional design choice.

Two governments can share the same broad regime label while drawing authority from very different places. A metropolitan executive may be directly elected or assembly-invested. A municipal mayor may be directly elected or chosen from council. A regional premier may arise from assembly confidence while another region imitates national presidentialism. A continental body may have a real parliamentary element while still drawing most of its authority from treaty delegation. Those are not cosmetic differences. They shape who can claim to speak for whom, who can be removed by whom, and whether conflict appears as ordinary politics or as a collision between rival democratic titles.

### 2. Micro-local mandate should come from voice, proximity, and reversibility

At the smallest scales, the archive should usually prefer authority grounded in **direct participation or extremely low-cost electoral authorization**:

- open assemblies,
- neighborhood or village votes,
- recallable delegates,
- rotating stewardship,
- and simple replacement rules.

This is the scale where legitimacy is strongest when residents can see, challenge, and reverse authority quickly. The archive should therefore distrust any design that seeks democratic gravitas here through a separately elected executive with a personal mandate. The right constitutional gain at micro-local scale is not mandate thickness. It is **civic closeness with easy correction**.

### 3. Municipal mandate should usually be council-centered, even where mayors are directly elected

Municipalities are the first scale where full everyday government becomes honest. That makes them more institutionally serious than neighborhoods, but not automatically more plebiscitary.

The archive should therefore usually treat the **elected municipal council** as the primary democratic source of authority. This means:

- the council should authorize budget and law,
- professional administration should operate under lawfully visible political direction,
- and any manager, chief administrative officer, or executive team should trace its democratic title through that council relationship.

A directly elected mayor may still be justified in some municipalities. But the archive should treat that as **one possible municipal executive arrangement**, not as the default democratic source of local legitimacy. Where a mayor is directly elected, the constitution should still prevent the office from claiming to be the sole embodiment of the city. The council remains the plural representative body of the municipality.

### 4. Metropolitan bodies with real binding power need a whole-territory public mandate

Metropolitan governance is the clearest place where the archive should resist both direct-election fetish and direct-election denial.

A metro that merely co-ordinates lightly among member governments may rely on delegated municipal participation. But once a metropolitan body can:

- tax or levy charges,
- bind land-use or housing obligations,
- run trunk transport,
- borrow materially,
- allocate infrastructure burdens,
- or exercise durable planning and service-shaping authority,

it should no longer govern mainly through indirect brokerage among incumbent mayors or ministers.

At that point the archive should usually prefer **a whole-territory public mandate**:

- directly elected metropolitan chamber or council,
- territorial electoral logic that treats the real urban region as the political unit,
- and an executive that is either directly elected across that territory or invested through a publicly legible chamber-confidence route.

The key point is not that every metro must mimic the same mayoral model. It is that the people bound by metropolitan decisions should be able to see a mandate that belongs to the metropolitan public itself rather than to a cartel of pre-existing officeholders.

### 5. Regions should usually get democratic thickness through assembly investiture, not a second personal mandate

Where a region truly carries platform functions or shared-rule weight, the archive should prefer democratic authority there. But it should usually source executive authority through **the elected regional assembly**, not through an automatically separate popular executive mandate.

That normally means:

- direct election of the regional legislature,
- formation of the executive through investiture, confidence, or equivalent assembly authorization,
- and territorial second-key devices only where the wider constitutional settlement truly requires them.

This default fits many regional cases because the region often needs stable coalition government, intergovernmental bargaining, and a visible legislative-executive chain more than it needs a separately personalized democratic apex. Regional presidentialism should therefore be treated as an exception that must show why a second direct mandate solves more than it breaks.

### 6. National executive authority should default to legislative confidence, not parallel popular title

At national scale the archive should begin from a hard presumption that executive authority should usually come from **legislative confidence** rather than from a separate direct personal mandate.

That presumption does not deny that presidential or semi-presidential systems can work. It says something narrower and more demanding: the archive should not treat them as the natural democratic baseline.

Why? Because at national scale:

- party systems are complex,
- coalition bargaining is often inevitable,
- law and budget require legislative partnership,
- and deadlock becomes system-shaping when two institutions both claim direct popular title.

The archive should therefore usually prefer a national mandate chain in which voters elect a legislature and the government draws authority from that legislature’s confidence. That gives one visible route from election to executive power. Direct presidential mandate should be treated as a positive-case exception that must justify the collision risk, the deadlock-repair architecture, and the party-system fit.

### 7. Distinguish a direct public mandate from a symbolic or arbitral office

This note should also reinforce a discipline already present elsewhere in the archive: **not every high office needs its own democratic mandate**.

Where a separate head of state exists, the archive should usually prefer a symbolic, civic, or bounded arbitral source of authority rather than a competing direct popular mandate. This is especially important at national scale and almost always decisive below it.

The question is not whether the office is important. The question is whether the office needs an independent democratic title in order to do its work. In most cases the archive’s answer should be no.

### 8. Continental and plurilateral bodies should draw authority from mixed but explicit delegation

At continental or plurilateral scale, the archive should resist the temptation to solve legitimacy anxiety by pretending there is already one simple electorate waiting to authorize a superstate.

The more honest default is **mixed but explicit delegation**:

- treaty ratification through national constitutional systems,
- member-state representation in councils or chambers,
- bounded parliamentary or assembly elements where lawmaking or budget roles justify them,
- and public reporting, review, and amendment routes that are visible enough to inspect.

Some unions may properly include direct parliamentary elections. The archive should allow that. But even then, the deeper constitutional mandate usually remains mixed: part peoples, part states, part treaty commitment, part shared-rule bargain. The archive should prefer saying that openly over laundering derivative authority into faux unitary sovereignty.

### 9. Global authority should remain charter-derived, not plebiscitary by aspiration

At global scale the archive should be especially strict.

A universal direct mandate for a world executive or one undifferentiated planetary legislature may sound democratic in the abstract. In practice it often outruns:

- the institution’s actual competence,
- the existence of one coherent enforceable demos,
- the available review and accountability routes,
- and the still-central role of state consent in global legal order.

The archive should therefore prefer a **charter- and treaty-derived mandate structure**:

- states authorize through charter and treaty commitments,
- universal assembly forums provide public and diplomatic legitimacy,
- secretariats implement bounded mandates,
- courts and compliance systems review lawful commitments,
- and affected publics gain visibility through reporting, participation, scrutiny, and norm-building complements.

That is not anti-democratic resignation. It is institutional honesty about the current source of global authority.

### 10. Avoid stacking rival mandates unless the constitution can explain why

Across all scales, the archive should ask one hard question whenever designers propose an additional direct mandate:

**what constitutional work does this extra source of democratic title perform that the existing chain cannot?**

If the answer is vague — seriousness, visibility, leadership, identity, prestige, or “people should choose” in the abstract — the archive should usually reject the extra mandate. Rival mandates are costly. They create collision, ambiguity, and temptation to govern through public symbolism rather than through one legible chain of responsibility.

### 11. Use stronger directness where people are directly bound as a public, not where symbols are thin

The archive should also invert one common mistake. Directness is not most needed where a tier wants to feel important. It is most needed where **a real public is directly bound together by shared coercive or distributive power**.

That is why direct public mandate is often stronger at the metropolitan level than many designers admit. Residents of a real metro may be bound together by transport, land, water, housing, borrowing, and resilience decisions every day. By contrast, a highly derivative or narrow treaty body may wield important but still bounded functions that do not justify pretending it already speaks with one undifferentiated popular voice.

### 12. Mandate source should stay aligned with removal and review

Finally, mandate source must remain compatible with the archive’s formation, removal, review, and contestability rules.

A body whose executive is assembly-invested should not quietly behave as though it had a personal plebiscitary mandate. A directly elected metro executive should still be answerable to law, review, and bounded competence. A treaty secretariat should not claim democratic latitude that belongs only to ratifying states, parliamentary bodies, or explicit treaty amendment.

Mandate source is therefore not only about entry. It is about the whole constitutional story the body is allowed to tell about itself.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat direct election as the universal solvent of legitimacy.
- Do not give micro-local units personal executive mandates they do not need.
- Do not let directly elected municipal mayors erase the council as the city’s plural democratic source.
- Do not keep a real metropolitan government on delegated municipal mandates once residents are directly bound region-wide.
- Do not duplicate national-level democratic mandates without a named correction path.
- Do not pretend continental or global bodies possess one simple democratic source when their legitimacy is openly mixed and derivative.

## Failure modes

- **plebiscitary prestige drift** — designers add direct election mainly to make an institution feel weightier.
- **council eclipse** — a city keeps electing a council but the constitution behaves as though only the mayor carries democratic title.
- **metro proxy rule** — a whole urban region is governed through the prior mandates of lower-tier incumbents rather than through its own public authorization.
- **regional duplicate apex** — a region copies national presidential logic without proving why the second popular title is worth the cost.
- **dual-command nationalism** — a national constitution multiplies rival mandates and then expects courts, emergencies, or charisma to repair the collision.
- **delegation laundering** — treaty-derived bodies present themselves as though their authority were immediate and popular rather than mediated and bounded.
- **global ballot theater** — planetary voting symbolism outruns actual competence, enforcement, and accountability architecture.

## Practical tests

A mandate source is probably right when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does the source of authority match the scale and directness of the public actually being bound?
2. Is the mandate chain legible enough that ordinary people can tell who authorized the governing apex?
3. Does the design avoid multiplying rival democratic titles unless the constitution can defend the gain?
4. Where the body is materially regional or metropolitan, does its mandate belong to that territory rather than only to predecessor officeholders?
5. Where the body is treaty- or charter-based, does the archive describe its authority honestly as mixed or delegated rather than laundering it into faux sovereignty?
6. Do removal, review, and correction routes still fit the mandate source chosen?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a proposed constitution can say **who won the election** but cannot clearly say **from whom this tier actually receives its authority and why that source fits the scope**, then it still has **electoral theater without a mandate constitution**.
