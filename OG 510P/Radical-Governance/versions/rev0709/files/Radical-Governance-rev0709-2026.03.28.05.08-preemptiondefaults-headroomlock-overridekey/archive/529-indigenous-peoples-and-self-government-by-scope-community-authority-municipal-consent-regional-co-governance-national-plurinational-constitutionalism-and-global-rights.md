# 529 — Indigenous peoples and self-government by scope: community authority, municipal consent, regional co-governance, national plurinational constitutionalism, and global rights

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat Indigenous peoples as peoples and governing authorities rather than as cultural stakeholders inside someone else’s map: Indigenous communities and nations should retain authority over identity, membership, internal institutions, culture, and many matters of everyday law according to their own traditions and evolving consent; municipalities should share space, services, permits, and practical coexistence through negotiated respect rather than zoning override or paternal inclusion; regional bodies should co-govern watersheds, forests, transport, health, education, and other shared systems whose real catchments outrun one settlement; nations should constitutionally recognize Indigenous peoples, lands, treaties, restitution claims, fiscal powers, and free, prior and informed consent rather than forcing all authority through a unitary citizenship mold; and the global layer should remain narrow but real in rights norms, anti-extractive scrutiny, and cross-border Indigenous solidarity rather than pretending a world body can govern each people’s internal life.

## Why this matters

Many states speak about Indigenous inclusion while keeping the constitutional operating system unchanged. Indigenous peoples are invited to participate as consultees, heritage holders, or vulnerable populations, but the state still assumes that all final authority begins and ends with settler institutions. That arrangement turns self-determination into advisory theater.

The archive already has notes on land, biodiversity, culture, migration, and constitutional change. What it still needs is one compact note on the **plurinational constitution** that ties those pieces together. Indigenous governance is not only about land or ceremony. It is also about treaty relationships, legal personality, language, education, jurisdiction, fiscal powers, urban belonging, and the right to refuse or reshape projects that would alter territories, livelihoods, or sacred relations.

An ideal government therefore allocates Indigenous self-government by scope. It keeps internal community authority close to the people, scales co-governance to the ecosystems and service catchments that are actually shared, reserves hard constitutional recognition and fiscal settlement to the nation, and uses the global layer only where rights norms, anti-colonial scrutiny, and transboundary solidarity clearly outrun domestic law.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat Indigenous peoples as peoples, not as interest groups

A good polity should begin from self-determination, self-identification, and the continuing political existence of Indigenous peoples. It should reject the premise that Indigenous nations are merely ethnic minorities to be consulted after core decisions are already made elsewhere.

### 2. Keep internal authority closest to the people themselves

Questions of membership, identity, family and kinship rules where applicable, internal leadership selection, customary governance, cultural continuity, and many everyday matters of community life should remain under Indigenous authority, subject to rights-safe evolution and internal contestability. The state should not use recognition as a tool for micromanaging who a people is.

### 3. Give municipalities a coexistence role, not an extinction veto

Municipal government is often where land-use interfaces, service access, streets, utilities, markets, and public institutions meet Indigenous residents and territories. Cities and towns should therefore negotiate service arrangements, shared infrastructure, local accommodation, and permit interfaces in good faith. They should not use zoning, local permitting, or administrative convenience to erase Indigenous jurisdiction or force communities to bargain from dependency.

### 4. Build regional co-governance where ecosystems and service systems are shared

Watersheds, forests, fisheries, transport corridors, hospitals, schools, and labour markets often cut across one municipality or one reserve boundary. Regional bodies should therefore support treaty implementation, shared-resource governance, cultural and linguistic ecosystems, and service compacts wherever one-jurisdiction fragmentation would otherwise reproduce colonial conflict.

### 5. Reserve treaty settlement, land restitution, and hard constitutional guarantees to the nation

Recognition of Indigenous peoples, treaty and land-rights settlement, restitution and compensation frameworks, fiscal powers, court access, nationwide consultation baselines, and the legal force of free, prior and informed consent belong mainly at the national constitutional level. Those guarantees require durable status, redistributive capacity, and insulation from local majoritarian capture.

### 6. Treat free, prior and informed consent as a governing rule, not a listening exercise

An ideal plurinational constitution should distinguish information sessions from rights-bearing consent. Consent must be free of coercion, sought prior to authorization, grounded in adequate information, and routed through peoples’ own representative institutions. Projects affecting lands, resources, relocation, hazardous materials, or legislative measures with major Indigenous impact should face real consent thresholds and real remedies.

### 7. Include urban Indigenous life rather than confining recognition to remote territory

A large share of Indigenous life is urban or mobile. Municipal and national institutions should therefore make urban residency, service access, cultural presence, and political participation compatible with Indigenous identity and authority, rather than acting as if recognition only exists in distant territories convenient to the dominant map.

### 8. Keep the global layer narrow: rights, monitoring, and solidarity

The global layer should not administer Indigenous nations or harmonize every treaty settlement. Its legitimate role is narrower: sustain rights norms on self-determination and consent, scrutinize dispossession and extractive abuse, protect cross-border peoples and knowledge, and support solidarity, language survival, and comparable standards.

## Guardrails

- Do not reduce self-determination to one consultation meeting after the real decision is already made.
- Do not let municipal zoning, utility dependence, or service contracts function as a quiet extinguishment power.
- Do not offer symbolic recognition without land, revenue, remedy, or jurisdictional consequences.
- Do not force Indigenous peoples to choose between cultural authenticity and urban citizenship.
- Do not treat consent as waived merely because a state defines a project as nationally important.

## Failure modes

- **stakeholderization**: Indigenous peoples are heard but never allowed to govern.
- **recognition without consequence**: constitutions praise Indigenous identity while keeping all decisive power elsewhere.
- **extractive federalism**: higher governments use regional or national necessity to override peoples whose lands bear the cost.
- **map erasure**: official boundaries pretend existing Indigenous territories, mobility, and treaty geographies do not exist.
- **urban disappearance**: Indigenous rights are acknowledged only in rural enclaves while city institutions behave as if Indigenous people are administratively ordinary and politically singularized.

## Practical tests

A scale-matched plurinational constitution passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can Indigenous peoples determine membership, leadership, and internal governance without state identity policing?
2. Are shared lands, resources, and service systems governed through real co-governance at the regional scale where spillovers actually sit?
3. Are treaty standing, land rights, fiscal powers, restitution, and consent thresholds guaranteed nationally rather than left to local grace?
4. Does free, prior and informed consent change authorization, remedy, and project design rather than serving as consultation theater?
5. Does the system recognize urban Indigenous life and cross-border solidarity without pretending only remote territory is politically real?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can describe **its recognition statements, heritage programmes, or consultation protocols** but cannot show **which powers Indigenous peoples hold directly, where co-governance sits, what consent can actually stop or reroute, and how treaty or land claims bind the national constitution**, it still has **reconciliation rhetoric without plurinational government**.
