# 817 — Participation-chain templates for cross-boundary scope forms: open dockets, hearings, user councils, deliberative mini-publics, response ledgers, and no heavy power by consultation theater

## One-line thesis

Once the archive has said **which cross-boundary institutional form is honest**, **who owns it**, **how money moves through it**, **where its operating spine lives**, and **where scrutiny and redress live**, it should also be able to say **what participation chain belongs to that form**: thin connectors should publish open dockets and simple input windows rather than staging fake popular mandates, shared-service backbones should run structured user and accessibility input without pretending pooled operations are a substitute for elected politics, bounded functional authorities should carry recurring hearings and response duties plus deliberative methods where long-horizon tradeoffs justify them, and general-purpose territorial tiers should own a full participation constitution rather than borrowing legitimacy from consultation theater.

## Why this matters

`790` says what the default stack is. `810` says how to assemble threshold proof before saying a metropolitan, county-pooling, regional-platform, fiscal-floor, or legitimacy-thickness line is really crossed. `812` says which institutional form is honest once the burden is real. `813` says who should sit in the room and which decisions require which legitimacy key. `814` says what money constitution belongs to that form. `815` says where the real bureaucracy, records home, and continuity chain belong. `816` says where scrutiny, audit, complaint handling, legality review, and final remedy should live. Earlier archive work in `405` and `597` already covers participation operations and local participation rights in broader terms.

What the recent scope stack still lacked was one compact answer to the next question:

**what public-input chain honestly belongs to each cross-boundary form?**

That gap matters because many institutional failures are not failures of legal form alone. They are failures of **borrowed legitimacy**.

One failure is **consultation theater**: a connector, authority, or regional platform conducts surveys, town halls, or roadshow events and then treats that visibility as if it were a public mandate, even when the body has no declared duty to respond, no clear decision path, and no lawful ownership of the tradeoff.

Another failure is **service-user substitution**: a shared-service backbone does useful user research, accessibility testing, or service co-design and then quietly implies that this operational listening can substitute for the elected principals who still own the political title to the function.

A third failure is **deliberation cosplay**: a body convenes a mini-public or citizens’ assembly with no clear mandate, no published evidence base, no response duty, and no rule for how recommendations affect the actual decision. The participation object then becomes a legitimacy costume rather than a constitutional component.

Official material supports a more disciplined ladder. The Council of Europe’s Additional Protocol to the European Charter of Local Self-Government says states shall secure to everyone within their jurisdiction the right to participate in the affairs of a local authority and provide means of facilitating that right. Council of Europe Recommendation CM/Rec(2018)4 says governments should frame a participation policy, improve the legal framework, enable local and regional authorities to use a wide range of participation instruments, and co-operate across levels. The OECD Recommendation on Open Government defines open government through transparency, integrity, accountability, and stakeholder participation and treats this culture as relevant across branches and levels of government. The OECD Guidelines for Citizen Participation Processes add operational discipline: there should be a real decision problem, clear expected results, an identified group to involve, a method fit to the task, inclusive design, and a visible feedback loop showing how input was used. Council of Europe guidance on deliberative democracy likewise stresses that deliberative processes should enhance representative institutions rather than replace them, and that they need lawful design, fair representation, and accountability. The archive should compress those lessons into one rule: **match participation thickness to real power, and stop letting any body claim democratic depth merely because it staged engagement around decisions it never clearly owned or never clearly answered.**

## Pattern pack

### 1. Use the participation-chain note after form, legitimacy, fiscal, administrative, and oversight questions are separated

This note does not decide whether a threshold is crossed.

Use `810` for that.

This note does not decide whether the honest form is a thin connector, shared-service backbone, bounded authority, or general-purpose territorial tier.

Use `812` for that.

This note does not decide who sits in the room or which decisions require ordinary board power, ratification, or a second key.

Use `813` for that.

This note does not decide what revenue base, equalization rule, reserve, or capital lane belongs to the body.

Use `814` for that.

This note does not decide who employs the staff, owns the systems, keeps the files, or carries continuity.

Use `815` for that.

This note does not decide who scrutinizes, audits, hears complaints, supervises legality, or provides final judicial remedy.

Use `816` for that.

This note applies after those questions are separated and the remaining live issue is:

- what type of public-input channel belongs to the form,
- which participation methods are proportionate to the decision,
- who must be heard,
- what response duty exists,
- and how participation should inform decisions without impersonating a different legitimacy chain.

### 2. Distinguish four recurrent participation chains before inventing hybrids

The archive should usually begin with four recognizable participation chains.

| Form | Default participation chain | What it may legitimately carry | What it must not hide |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Thin connector** | open agendas, public dockets, submission windows, and routed testimony through member bodies | notice, comments, light hearings, common issue mapping, observer access | a fake popular mandate for a body that mainly aligns others |
| **Shared-service backbone** | user panels, accessibility testing, service co-design, civic monitoring, and published service-response loops | service standards input, lived-experience evidence, complaint pattern learning, participatory improvement of delivery | substitution of service listening for elected ownership of the policy title |
| **Bounded functional authority** | recurring public hearings, corridor or basin consultation, stakeholder forums, and deliberative processes where tradeoffs are durable and distributive | route design, fare/service tradeoffs, capital sequencing, resilience priorities, siting and mitigation design | legitimacy borrowed from one-off consultations with no response duty |
| **General-purpose territorial tier** | full participation constitution with petitions, hearings, initiative or referendum rights where lawful, participatory budgeting where appropriate, and representative deliberation for long-horizon tradeoffs | agenda-setting, major plan participation, distributive debate, civic monitoring, issue-specific mini-publics | consultation branding standing in for democratic government |

The archive should not start with bespoke participation poetry until one of these four chains has first been ruled out honestly.

### 3. Separate five participation titles even when one legal order houses several of them

Later archive work should usually ask five separate questions:

- who gets **notice and docket visibility**,
- who gets **to speak, testify, or submit evidence**,
- who joins **structured user or stakeholder co-design**,
- when a matter needs **representative deliberation or a mini-public**,
- and who owes the public a **published response table or participation ledger** after the process closes.

One legal order may house several of these titles together. The archive should still separate them explicitly. Silence is how a generic “consultation” label gets mistaken for a real participation constitution.

### 4. Thin connectors should open the file and hear input without pretending to own a direct mass mandate

A thin connector is honest when it exists mainly for:

- common diagnosis,
- agenda setting,
- early alignment,
- non-binding planning,
- and preparation for later principal-side decisions.

Its participation chain should therefore stay light but visible.

Typical honest ingredients include:

- published agendas, work programmes, and membership,
- public issue dockets or submission windows,
- hearings or roundtables when a common plan or draft standard is being assembled,
- and clear routing of consequential public demands back to the member councils, assemblies, ministers, or authorities that actually own the decision.

What the archive should reject here is the move where a connector runs a large outreach exercise and then treats attendance, applause, or aggregated comments as if they settled burdens, map choices, or budget commitments the connector cannot itself lawfully own.

If the connector is becoming the real place where tradeoffs are set, then the problem is not merely weak participation design. The archive may also have misclassified the form and should revisit `812` and `813`.

### 5. Shared-service backbones should center user and operator learning without displacing principal politics

A shared-service backbone exists because several governments keep political title to a function while pooling the machinery needed to run it well.

Its participation chain should therefore begin from one premise:

**service-user voice improves pooled operations, but it does not replace elected principals.**

That usually means the archive should require visible answers to questions such as:

- how users can report friction, exclusion, and service failure,
- whether there is structured accessibility and inclusion testing,
- whether service standards or redesign choices are opened for user comment,
- whether civic-monitoring or co-production channels exist for implementation learning,
- how participation findings are routed back to the principals that still own policy choice,
- and how the backbone publishes what changed because of user input.

A backbone becomes constitutionally dishonest when user councils, customer boards, or digital feedback loops are used as a rhetorical substitute for the elected bodies that still set entitlement, burden, or territorial priority.

### 6. Bounded functional authorities should carry recurring hearing and response machinery proportionate to durable system tradeoffs

A bounded functional authority becomes the honest answer when one cross-boundary system needs continuous operation, stewardship, capital sequencing, or resilience planning.

Its participation chain should therefore be heavier than a backbone’s.

Typical honest ingredients include:

- recurring hearings tied to plans, tariffs, routes, service levels, or capital programs,
- place-sensitive consultation for corridors, basins, siting, mitigation, or phased works,
- standing advisory forums or stakeholder assemblies where the field has durable affected groups,
- representative deliberative processes when the question is long-horizon, distributive, legitimacy-sensitive, and not reducible to technical comment alone,
- and a published response table showing what was accepted, rejected, deferred, or escalated and why.

The archive should reject the pattern where a major authority with real distributive and infrastructure power relies on one consultation round at project launch and then treats that event as if it discharged its continuing public-input duty forever.

### 7. General-purpose territorial tiers should own a full participation constitution rather than borrowing legitimacy from outreach programs

A general-purpose territorial tier makes recurring choices across sectors, burdens, budgets, land, and long horizons.

Its participation chain should therefore be treated as a durable constitutional layer.

That can include:

- petition and agenda-entry rights,
- ordinary hearings in legislative and executive processes,
- public participation on major plans and budgets,
- participatory budgeting where the amount and scope are honest,
- civic monitoring and community review channels,
- and representative deliberative processes for especially difficult cross-sector tradeoffs.

Where the legal order provides initiatives or referendums, those should be handled through the archive’s broader participation-rights discipline rather than improvised inside the cross-boundary note stack. The core rule here is simpler: a territorial tier should not try to satisfy democratic depth through a communications office, a consultation portal, or occasional assemblies alone.

### 8. Escalate participation thickness with irreversibility, distributive burden, and lived territorial effect

The archive should usually thicken the participation chain when one or more of the following are true:

- the decision distributes burdens or benefits across places,
- the effects are long-horizon or hard to reverse,
- the decision visibly shapes land use, access, fares, housing, mobility, or environmental exposure,
- the field has recurring affected communities rather than one-off commenters,
- or the archive is already relying on participation to strengthen legitimacy around a cross-boundary tradeoff.

Under those conditions, generic comment windows are usually too thin.

### 9. Require a response duty strong enough to close the loop

A participation chain is not honest if the archive cannot say what happened to the input.

Later case work should usually require some public response object proportionate to the method, such as:

- a response table,
- a participation ledger,
- a citizens’ assembly reply memorandum,
- a plan revision note,
- or a published explanation of why a recommendation was not taken.

A body that gathers public attention without publishing a response discipline is not yet running a participation chain. It is running intake theater.

### 10. Keep method fit tighter than prestige

The archive should not over-value whichever participation instrument is most fashionable.

Use:

- open information and simple submissions when the public mainly needs notice and correction,
- hearings when testimony, local experience, and challenge matter,
- user panels and co-design when service quality and accessibility are the live issue,
- civic monitoring when implementation truth matters,
- and representative deliberation when the tradeoff is hard, legitimacy-sensitive, and broader than organized stakeholder bargaining.

A mini-public is not automatically better than a hearing, and a hearing is not automatically better than a user panel. The method should fit the actual decision and the actual form.

### 11. Do not let participation channels impersonate elections, complaints, or courts

Participation is not the same thing as:

- electoral authorization,
- maladministration complaint handling,
- judicial review,
- or independent scrutiny.

Those chains may inform one another, but they are not interchangeable.

A connector cannot upgrade itself into a democratic tier through comment counts. A service backbone cannot absorb the principal’s mandate by running user research. A deliberative process cannot become a secret substitute court for unresolved rights questions. And a body with poor complaint or audit machinery cannot cure that deficit by staging more engagement.

### 12. Use one minimum participation-chain table before treating the design as settled

The archive should prefer a compact minimum packet like this:

| Field | Minimum question |
| --- | --- |
| **Form** | thin connector / shared-service backbone / bounded authority / general-purpose tier |
| **Decision class** | what kind of decision or tradeoff is the participation object attached to? |
| **Primary method** | notice window / hearing / user panel / civic monitoring / consultation / mini-public / other |
| **Affected public** | who is invited or entitled to participate, and why? |
| **Response duty** | what public object closes the loop? |
| **Escalation trigger** | what makes the chain thicken or reopen? |
| **Anti-cosplay limit** | what would make this participation chain a legitimacy costume rather than a real constitutional component? |

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse visibility with mandate.
- Do not use service-user participation to erase elected responsibility.
- Do not launch a deliberative process without a clear mandate, balanced evidence, and a response duty.
- Do not use one public hearing to legitimate years of distributive power.
- Do not let the most organized stakeholders stand in for the whole affected public.
- Do not mistake complaint handling, litigation, or audit for participation.

## Failure modes

- **consultation theater**: engagement is staged, but no decision path or response duty exists.
- **user-voice substitution**: operational listening is used to displace elected political ownership.
- **deliberation cosplay**: a mini-public is run without lawful mandate, representativeness, or follow-through.
- **comment-window minimalism**: generic consultation is used for durable distributive and territorial choices that need thicker public input.
- **participation capture**: the loudest organized actors are treated as if they exhausted the affected public.
- **feedback-loop failure**: people can speak, but nobody can see what the institution did with what they said.

## Compression rule for the archive

If the archive cannot answer the question **what public-input chain belongs to this form and this decision?** in one sentence naming the **method, affected public, response duty, and anti-cosplay limit**, then the participation design is still too vague to count as settled.
