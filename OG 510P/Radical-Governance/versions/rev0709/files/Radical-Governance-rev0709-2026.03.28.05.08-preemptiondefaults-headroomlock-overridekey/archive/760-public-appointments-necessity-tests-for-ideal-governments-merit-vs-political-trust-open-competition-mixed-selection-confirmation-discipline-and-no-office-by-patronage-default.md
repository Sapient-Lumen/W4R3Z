# 760 — Public-appointments necessity tests for ideal governments: merit vs political trust, open competition, mixed selection, confirmation discipline, and no office by patronage default

## One-line thesis

Ideal governments should treat public appointments as a **typed constitutional lane rather than one generic power of disposal**: elected offices should be filled by voters, political-trust offices should be few and plainly political, most administrative and service-delivery posts should be filled through open merit-based recruitment, constitutionally sensitive or independence-bearing offices may justify mixed nominating and confirmation arrangements, and no polity should let patronage, vacancy manipulation, or prestige theatrics become the real appointments constitution.

## Why this matters

The archive already says a great deal about **civil service and public workforce design** (`496`, `549`), **executives** (`493`), **legislatures** (`548`), **public enterprises** (`546`), **electoral administration** (`552`), **ombuds institutions** (`554`), **human-rights institutions** (`555`), **regulators** (`545`), **government formation and removal** (`752`), **head-of-state design** (`754`), **constitutional review** (`756`), and **independent institutions** (`759`).

What it still lacked was one compact doctrine for an earlier and more general constitutional question:

**how should important public offices actually be filled, and what kind of appointment lane is honest for each office type?**

That gap matters because many constitutions speak loftily about neutrality, independence, or democratic legitimacy while leaving the real staffing order vague. Ministers then fill boards as a reward system, parties treat regulators and watchdogs as spoils, vacancy management becomes a covert appointments tool, and governments collapse the distinction among elected mandate, political leadership, professional administration, and protected referee institutions.

Ideal-government design therefore needs a doctrine of **appointment typing**. The live question is not who formally signs the instrument. It is whether the office is being filled through the lane that matches its democratic and constitutional job.

## Necessity table

| Necessity test | What counts as proof | Honest design consequence | Default answer if proof is weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Need for direct democratic authorization** | the office sets political direction in its own right or is meant to embody popular choice | election by the electorate or by the representative body lawfully chosen for that tier | executive appointment to a quasi-elective office |
| **Need for political confidence and programme alignment** | the office must translate an elected government's platform into strategy and can honestly turn over with government | political appointment with visible tenure-at-pleasure or cabinet-confidence logic | fake merit language for an office that is plainly political |
| **Need for professional administration and continuity** | the office delivers law, services, records, procurement, or operational management across electoral cycles | open merit-based recruitment with competency criteria and ordinary performance discipline | ministerial or partisan patronage appointment |
| **Need for neutral refereeing or independence** | the office oversees elections, audit, rights, regulation, prosecution, or adjudication and would be compromised by direct partisan control | mixed or multi-actor appointment, protected tenure, transparent qualifications, and reviewable legality | single-actor executive fill by ordinary discretion |
| **Need for temporary continuity only** | a vacancy or short incapacity would otherwise interrupt lawful operations | narrow acting or succession lane with time limits and a live permanent-appointment clock | indefinite provisional officeholding as a substitute constitution |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from office type rather than from the vanity of who wants to choose

The archive should begin by sorting offices into different constitutional classes instead of pretending that one appointments power can honestly fill all public roles. At minimum, ideal governments should distinguish:

- **elective offices** chosen by voters or representative bodies;
- **political-trust offices** such as ministers and a bounded strategic layer;
- **merit offices** in the civil service and operational public administration;
- **independence-bearing offices** such as judges, auditors, electoral commissioners, ombuds, and similarly protected referees;
- **temporary continuity offices** filled only to bridge a vacancy or incapacity.

If the polity cannot say which class an office belongs to, it is already inviting capture or confusion.

### 2. Keep political appointments few, named, and visibly political

Not every public appointment is suspect. Democratic governments need some offices that turn over when elections change the governing programme. Ministers, political advisers, and a bounded strategic leadership layer can therefore be honestly political.

But the archive should reject the habit of calling plainly partisan choice “merit” simply because the appointee is credentialed. The honest test is whether the office should lawfully move with political confidence. If yes, call it political and limit its footprint. If no, move it out of the spoils lane.

### 3. Make merit the default for administration, service delivery, and operational management

Where the office is mainly about lawful implementation, case handling, budgeting, inspection, records, service continuity, procurement, technical advice, or professional management, the default should be open merit-based recruitment.

That means published role criteria, lawful qualification rules, fair competition, conflict screening, and reasonable protection against arbitrary dismissal. The archive should prefer competency-tested recruitment over informal sponsorship, family-network hiring, or ministerial personal trust for jobs that ought to survive electoral turnover.

### 4. Use mixed or widened coalitions where the office must remain contestable to government

Some offices cannot honestly sit under one political hand. Judges, auditors, election commissioners, ombuds leaders, major regulators, prosecutors where institutionally separate, and certain constitutional referees may need appointment designs that widen the coalition required for capture.

The archive should therefore prefer mixed nominating panels, cross-party confirmation, multi-actor appointment shares, or other coalition-widening designs where the function genuinely requires them. But it should do this only where the independence case is real, not as prestige ornament for every board or agency.

### 5. Publish qualifications, incompatibilities, and conflicts before the contest begins

Appointments become easier to manipulate when eligibility and disqualification standards are blurry or retrofitted to the preferred candidate. Ideal governments should therefore state in advance:

- required experience or professional standing;
- disqualifying conflicts and incompatibilities;
- cooling-off rules where appropriate;
- whether partisan office, recent campaign service, lobbying, or regulated-industry ties trigger restrictions;
- and whether diversity, representativeness, language, territorial, or legal-profession criteria lawfully matter for the office.

The archive should reject bespoke eligibility engineering tailored to one person or one coalition.

### 6. Keep hearings and confirmation proportional rather than theatrical

Public hearings, legislative confirmation, and advisory-panel reports can improve legitimacy when the office is constitutionally significant. But they should test suitability, independence, competence, and integrity rather than becoming mere partisan spectacle or hidden veto trading.

The archive should therefore prefer a proportional confirmation grammar: heavier scrutiny for high constitutional and referee offices; lighter or no legislative theatre for ordinary merit posts; and no design that makes confirmation a routine vacancy machine.

### 7. Match term design to the office instead of copying one tenure formula everywhere

Political offices often rise and fall with elections or confidence. Merit offices may use career tenure with performance discipline. Independent offices may require fixed terms, often staggered and sometimes non-renewable, to reduce capture.

The archive should reject tenure monoculture. The honest question is not whether every office has the same length of service, but whether its term structure prevents both partisan sweep and unremovable deadweight.

### 8. Design appointment and removal together, not as separate myths

A good appointments constitution also says how officeholders leave. A merit office can be hollowed out if removal is arbitrary. An independent office is not independent if dismissal is easy. A political office is dishonest if formal removal rules pretend the office is insulated when everyone knows it turns on confidence.

The archive should therefore require that every appointments lane travel with a corresponding discipline or removal lane appropriate to its class.

### 9. Build anti-vacancy and anti-holdover discipline into the appointments constitution

Even a beautiful appointment rule fails if the polity tolerates endless vacancies, expired mandates, serial “acting” renewals, or indefinite holdovers. Those are not minor administrative problems. They are alternate appointment mechanisms.

Ideal governments should therefore publish appointment clocks, vacancy reporting, nomination deadlines where suitable, holdover conditions, and what happens when a confirming body or nominating actor does nothing. A constitution should not silently reward strategic delay.

### 10. Prefer one visible appointments map over office-by-office folklore

The public should be able to tell which offices are elected, political, merit-filled, mixed-appointed, or temporarily bridged; who nominates; who confirms; which qualification and conflict rules apply; how long terms run; and what challenge route exists for illegality.

If that information must be reconstructed from scattered statutes, cabinet custom, party deals, and vacancy press releases, the polity has not built an appointments constitution. It has only inherited appointment folklore.

## Guardrails

- Do not call a plainly political office merit-based merely because the appointee has credentials.
- Do not use discretionary executive appointment as the default for ordinary administrative and service-delivery posts.
- Do not create elaborate confirmation theater for offices that should simply be filled through open competition.
- Do not use “independence” as a prestige reason for mixed appointment if the function does not genuinely require insulation.
- Do not let qualifications, conflicts, or incompatibilities be rewritten to fit a preferred candidate.
- Do not separate appointment design from removal, vacancy, and holdover design.
- Do not allow strategic delay to become a hidden method of capture.

## Failure modes

- **patronage default**: discretionary political sponsorship becomes the ordinary way offices are filled.
- **merit camouflage**: governments brand politically convenient appointments as neutral recruitment.
- **confirmation theater**: hearings become partisan performance while competence and legality go untested.
- **sweep design**: one electoral victory fills too many independent or referee offices at once.
- **eligibility tailoring**: qualifications or conflict rules are rewritten around one candidate.
- **vacancy capture**: delay, holdover, or acting service quietly replaces the lawful appointment lane.
- **panel mystique**: advisory commissions obscure rather than clarify who actually decides.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **who signed the appointment** but cannot also say **what class of office this is, why this appointment lane fits that class, what merit or independence rules apply, how conflicts are screened, how the office is removed, and how vacancy games are prevented**, then it still has **officeholders without an appointments constitution**.
