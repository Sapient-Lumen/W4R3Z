# 840 — Preemption defaults for ideal governments of each scope: local headroom, metro primacy, federal bargain locks, national floors, and treaty-bounded override

## One-line thesis

The archive should usually prefer different override grammars by scale: **micro-local bodies should remain subject to municipal law and charter while keeping visible voice lanes; municipalities should enjoy broad headroom above common floors unless preemption is explicit, proportionate, and reviewable; metropolitan governments should hold named primacy inside real shared urban systems; regional governments should keep constitutionally real autonomy except where the federal bargain openly assigns paramount power elsewhere; national governments may impose rights floors, solidarity rules, and interoperability baselines but should face a higher burden before using ceilings or field occupation; and continental or global bodies should override only through explicit treaty commitments rather than ambient upper-tier drift**.

## Why this matters

The archive already knows a lot about subsidiarity, concurrent competences, supervision, and silent override, especially in `579`, `584`, `591`, `637`, `727`, and `839`. But those notes answer several questions at once: who should normally hold a function, when a routing default is rebutted, how concurrent powers should be typed, and how constitutions should change when a bargain is rewritten.

What the archive still lacked was one compact answer-key note for the narrower recurring question: **once the stack exists, when may one scale lawfully override another, and what kind of override belongs where?** Without that compression layer, later archive work risks three familiar mistakes. One is **ambient superiority**, where higher tiers quietly treat all lower authority as revocable administrative convenience. Another is **local absolutism**, where municipalities or regions are described as if shared floors, treaty commitments, or system integrity can never bind them. The third is **system blur**, where metropolitan bodies are given responsibility for shared networks without the primacy needed to keep the networks coherent.

The archive therefore needs one short note on **preemption defaults by scope**. The live question is not whether override should ever happen. It is **what kind of override honestly fits each scale, and what kind should trigger suspicion**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish supervision, floors, ceilings, field occupation, and system primacy

The archive should force constitutions and ordinary statutes to say which override move they are actually making.

At minimum, the archive should distinguish:

- **supervision for legality**, where a higher tier checks whether a lower one acted lawfully;
- **floor preemption**, where a higher tier sets a minimum that lower tiers may exceed but not undercut;
- **ceiling preemption**, where lower tiers are forbidden from going further;
- **field occupation**, where a whole subject is reserved upward;
- and **system primacy**, where one scale gets the last coordinating word inside a named shared system so that the system can actually function.

These moves are not interchangeable. If a constitution cannot say which one it is using, it is probably overriding by atmosphere.

### 2. Micro-local bodies should have voice and stewardship, not veto against ordinary municipal government

Below the municipality, the archive should usually reject any fantasy that every ward, block, or village body is a mini sovereign.

The honest default is:

- protected rights to meet, minute, petition, witness, and escalate;
- local discretion over tiny commons and stewardship tasks where the municipal charter allows it;
- but no standing power to nullify ordinary municipal rules, rights floors, budgetary law, or shared-city obligations.

Micro-local institutions should be easy to hear and hard to erase, yet they should remain **low-veto bodies**. Their constitutional dignity comes from reachability and signal value, not from holding a local supremacy doctrine against the first real government tier.

### 3. Municipal government should get broad headroom above common floors

Because municipalities are the first complete governments of ordinary life, they should usually enjoy the strongest ordinary presumption of **headroom**.

The archive should usually prefer a municipal override constitution in which:

- national or regional law may set rights floors, due-process baselines, interoperability requirements, and minimum service guarantees;
- municipalities remain free to adapt implementation and often to go further than the floor;
- any ceiling preemption or field occupation must be explicit, legally reviewable, and tied to a named protected interest;
- and grant conditions, software platforms, procurement templates, or ministry guidance do not silently erase local discretion.

In other words, the municipality should ordinarily live under **common floors plus local headroom**, not under ambient upper-tier managerial entitlement.

### 4. Metropolitan government should have named primacy inside real shared urban systems

Where a metropolitan tier is constitutionally real, it should not be reduced to begging member municipalities each time a cross-boundary system needs coordination.

The archive should usually prefer:

- explicit metropolitan primacy for the named shared systems that justify the tier in the first place, such as trunk transport, fare integration, flood-control basins, major housing allocation formulas, freight interfaces, or regional utility backbones;
- clearly bounded primacy that does not swallow every municipal matter;
- visible publication of which decisions are metro-final, which require double keys, and which remain local;
- and legality review or constitutional recourse if the metro body tries to convert system primacy into general-purpose local tutelage.

A shared city cannot be governed honestly if the metro tier is real enough to be blamed but never real enough to prevail inside its own named systems.

### 5. Regional or provincial government should keep autonomy where the bargain is real, with explicit paramountcy only where the constitution actually assigns it

Once regional or state government is part of the constitutional bargain, override should be read through that bargain rather than through habit.

The archive should therefore usually prefer:

- explicit assignment of exclusive, shared, and concurrent competences;
- clear paramountcy rules for conflict cases rather than inference by prestige;
- stronger protection against ordinary-law erosion of regional autonomy where territorial self-government, language settlement, or shared rule is part of the founding deal;
- and judicial or constitutional review where one side claims a field it was not actually given.

The key point is that federal or devolved systems should not drift into **silent recentralization by framework law**, nor into total fragmentation because nobody can say who finally decides what.

### 6. National government may set rights floors, solidarity rules, and interoperability baselines, but ceilings and field occupation require a heavier burden

The national tier is the guarantor tier. That gives it a serious override role, but not an unlimited one.

The archive should usually treat national override as strongest when it protects:

- equal citizenship and rights floors;
- redistribution and risk-pooling that cannot be honestly carried at smaller scales;
- macroeconomic and system-integrity baselines;
- national infrastructure interoperability;
- and protection against cross-border harms that lower tiers cannot contain.

But the archive should demand a steeper public justification when the nation tries to convert those interests into full ceilings or field occupation. Uniformity alone is rarely enough. If a floor-plus-headroom design would work, the nation should ordinarily prefer that to total occupation.

### 7. Continental and global bodies should override only through explicit treaty commitment and typed supremacy rules

Above the state, the archive should usually reject the fantasy of ambient upper law that simply hovers into force.

The honest upper-tier default is:

- override only where states have made explicit treaty commitments;
- no secretariat, conference, donor, or emergency practice treated as if it were independent constitutional supremacy;
- treaty obligations performed in good faith once undertaken;
- and any claimed priority over lower domestic law or over conflicting treaty duties stated through the treaty text or recognized supremacy rule rather than insinuated by prestige.

This means the upper tier can be real without becoming mystical. Its override powers come from **named consent and named legal effect**, not from being higher in the air.

### 8. Keep override visible in law, finance, and operations at the same time

A polity does not handle override honestly if it only changes the legal text.

Whenever one scale preempts another, the archive should expect the polity to say:

- what discretion the lower tier still keeps;
- what finance or liabilities move with the override;
- what records, platforms, or implementation tools become controlling;
- what review route remains open;
- and what review clock or repeal route revisits the override later.

Otherwise the constitution gets the worst of both worlds: hidden override in practice and unresolved accountability in public.

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse legality supervision with a standing right to manage everything from above.
- Do not let municipalities or regions call themselves autonomous while core floors, finance, and platforms are silently hard-coded elsewhere.
- Do not create a metropolitan tier for shared systems and then deny it any primacy inside those systems.
- Do not let national uniformity claims erase local headroom where a floor would suffice.
- Do not let treaty bodies widen their effect through guidance, donor leverage, provisionalism, or prestige alone.

## Failure modes

- **ambient superiority**: higher tiers behave as if all lower authority is revocable by default.
- **micro-veto cosplay**: tiny local bodies are treated as if they can block ordinary municipal government.
- **metro without primacy**: the shared-city tier is blamed for systems it cannot finally coordinate.
- **silent recentralization**: regional or municipal autonomy is narrowed through framework law, grants, or platforms without openly naming the override.
- **ceiling inflation**: a legitimate national floor quietly becomes a maximum.
- **treaty aura**: upper-tier institutions are treated as self-widening sources of override without explicit legal commitment.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity cannot say **which rules are mere supervision, which are floors, which are ceilings, which fields are truly occupied, which shared systems carry metropolitan primacy, and which upper-tier overrides exist only by explicit treaty commitment**, then it does not yet have an honest override constitution; it has **preemption by status, software, or drift**.
