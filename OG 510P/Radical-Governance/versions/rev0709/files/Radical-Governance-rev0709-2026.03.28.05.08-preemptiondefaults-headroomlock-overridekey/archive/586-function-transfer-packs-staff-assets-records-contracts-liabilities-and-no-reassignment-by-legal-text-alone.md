# 586 — Function transfer packs, staff, assets, records, contracts, liabilities, and no reassignment by legal text alone

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat **any transfer of a public function across levels of government as a full transfer pack rather than a sentence in a law**: whenever a competence moves, the same settlement should also name the effective date, service standard, staff lane, asset and lease inventory, contract and procurement treatment, records and data custody, ongoing-case handoff, fiscal lane, liability rule, transition authority, dispute route, and review clock, so that decentralisation or recentralisation cannot occur by moving the headline power while leaving the receiving government to inherit an empty shell.

## Why this matters

The archive now has stronger notes on **scope allocation** (`579`), **mandate-finance congruence** (`581`), **capability congruence** (`582`), **subnational distress and handback** (`583`), **override and preemption** (`584`), and **government by grant maze** (`585`). What it still lacked was one compact doctrine for a recurrent implementation fraud: **reassignment by legal text alone**.

That failure appears whenever a parliament, ministry, governor, or constitutional settlement says a responsibility now sits at a different level but leaves the operational body behind. A service is “decentralised,” yet the staff remain employed by the center, the buildings remain in someone else’s register, the software license stays with the old ministry, open cases and archives stay trapped in incompatible systems, union and pension questions are deferred, suppliers do not know who now stands behind the contract, and residents are told to discover the new route by trial and error. The law moves. The function does not.

The same pathology appears in recentralisation. A higher tier reclaims a function in the name of uniformity or rescue, but does not name which liabilities, records, service guarantees, or appeal routes move upward, creating a legal shell game in which lower tiers remain blamed for work they no longer fully control.

The archive therefore needs a tighter rule. **A function is not actually reassigned until the whole operating bundle moves or is explicitly bridged.** Ideal government is not built by changing the column header on an organisational chart and hoping the rest follows.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat every competence move as an explicit transfer pack

The legal act that reallocates a function should be presumed incomplete unless it also specifies the transfer pack that makes the function governable on day one and governable during transition.

At minimum, the pack should name:

- the function and its legal basis;
- whether the move is decentralisation, recentralisation, delegation, deconcentration, or temporary joint administration;
- the source and destination authorities;
- the effective date and any staged cutover dates;
- the service floor that must survive the move;
- the budget and revenue lane;
- the staff, asset, record, contract, and liability treatment;
- the temporary powers that apply during transition;
- the dispute and review route;
- and the handback or post-transfer review clock.

If those elements are absent, the polity is not transferring a function honestly. It is announcing a destination without specifying the vehicle.

### 2. Distinguish ownership, delegation, agency, and emergency stewardship before moving anything

Many transfer failures begin with category confusion. The polity must say whether the receiving level will:

- **own the function as an ordinary self-governing competence**,
- **carry it out as a delegated task on someone else’s legal authority**,
- **host shared operations or back-office support without becoming the law-owning authority**,
- or **exercise temporary stewardship under distress, emergency, or transition rules**.

These are constitutionally different states. They imply different discretion, financing, supervision, appeal routes, and liability structures. A transfer pack that blurs them invites later conflict over who may adapt policy, who pays, who may inspect, and who gets sued.

### 3. Publish the cutover calendar, freeze windows, and who remains on point at each moment

Residents, staff, suppliers, courts, and peer governments need to know **when authority actually changes**.

A good transfer pack therefore publishes:

- the enactment date,
- the operational go-live date,
- any freeze windows for moving cases, budgets, records, or systems,
- which authority receives new matters during each phase,
- and which authority remains responsible for legacy matters until closure or novation.

Without this, everyone learns the new constitution through bounced applications, rejected invoices, and contradictory instructions.

### 4. Move or bridge staff lawfully, visibly, and with continuity conditions stated up front

A public function almost never moves without people. The archive should therefore resist all settlements that pretend labour, professional licensing, management lines, collective bargaining, pensions, disciplinary files, and training obligations are downstream details.

The transfer pack should say:

- which staff move automatically,
- which posts remain shared or seconded for a defined period,
- what happens to pay, seniority, pensions, and accrued rights,
- which employer holds managerial authority during overlap,
- what retraining or certification is required,
- and how conflicts of instruction are resolved while the service is still stabilising.

Otherwise the receiving level inherits a paper competence and the sending level keeps the only people who know how to operate it.

### 5. Inventory and transfer the physical and digital operating estate, not just the policy label

Buildings, counters, vehicles, labs, clinics, server environments, licenses, identifiers, networks, furniture, archives space, and leased facilities are part of the function.

The transfer pack should therefore include a visible asset and facilities schedule naming:

- owned assets,
- leased assets and lease novation or sublease treatment,
- maintenance responsibilities,
- insurance and risk coverage,
- digital systems and admin access,
- cybersecurity and business-continuity obligations,
- and the condition in which assets must be handed over.

A competence that arrives without the means to house, equip, and operate it is not decentralised. It is stranded.

### 6. Move records, registries, data custody, and ongoing case files under explicit continuity rules

The archive has already tightened doctrines on **public records** (`571`), **base registries and interoperability** (`576`), **service delivery** (`577`), and **official notice** (`575`). This note extends them into functional reassignment.

Whenever a function moves, the polity should specify:

- which records remain with the sending authority and which transfer,
- who becomes the authoritative custodian for active files,
- how archives, retention duties, and access rights continue,
- how identifiers and registries are mapped or rekeyed,
- how pending deadlines and notice obligations are preserved,
- and how the public can discover the new records or case route.

A transfer that loses the file, breaks the identifier, or leaves the citizen unable to locate the responsible office is not an administrative transition. It is institutional amnesia.

### 7. Novate or bridge contracts and procurement obligations instead of letting vendors discover the constitution first

Many public functions depend on procurement frameworks, utilities contracts, maintenance agreements, software subscriptions, service concessions, and outsourced delivery arrangements. The legal reassignment of a function does not by itself tell the contractor who now instructs, pays, varies, audits, or terminates the agreement.

The transfer pack should therefore state:

- which contracts move,
- whether they are novated, assigned, jointly managed for a period, or allowed to run down,
- who certifies performance and pays invoices during overlap,
- how dispute resolution changes,
- and what continuity clauses protect the public if the contract architecture lags the constitutional change.

Otherwise suppliers become the first interpreters of the intergovernmental settlement.

### 8. Move the money, debts, receivables, and contingent liabilities with the function or state the bridge explicitly

This note does not replace `581`; it sharpens it for transitions. A transfer pack should say not only how the destination level will finance the function in the long run, but also how the move handles:

- partial-year appropriations,
- arrears and unpaid invoices,
- receivables and fees,
- grants already awarded,
- debt service or lease obligations tied to transferred assets,
- pension and severance liabilities,
- litigation exposure,
- and any contingent liabilities that may crystallise after the move.

When those questions are left vague, sending authorities try to offload liabilities while destination authorities inherit duties without usable balance-sheet truth.

### 9. Protect service continuity with temporary bridges rather than heroic overnight cutovers

Ideal governments do not require every transfer to happen in one immaculate midnight switch. They require honest continuity.

A transfer pack may therefore use temporary bridges such as:

- transitional service agreements,
- shared payroll or finance support,
- temporary shared call-center and front-counter arrangements,
- mirrored case queues,
- regional technical support teams,
- and staged migration of digital systems.

But such bridges should be explicit, time-bounded, and reviewable. A bridge is there to preserve continuity while capability settles. It is not a license for permanent ambiguity about who governs.

### 10. Keep complaint, appeal, court, and ombuds routes legible throughout the move

A resident should not lose a remedy because the state is rearranging itself.

The transfer pack should therefore preserve and publish:

- where complaints go during overlap,
- which authority issues appealable decisions at each stage,
- which court or tribunal remains competent,
- how limitation periods and filing clocks are protected,
- and how ombuds, audit, inspection, or rights bodies can still identify the responsible institution.

Transition is not a valid excuse for making responsibility undecidable.

### 11. Consult the affected governments, workers, and communities before the move hardens

Because function transfers alter real self-government, administration, and lived service access, they should not be treated as one-sided executive logistics.

Before major reassignment, the polity should consult:

- the governments losing and receiving the function,
- relevant local or regional associations,
- affected staff and representative bodies,
- users and communities where access points will move,
- and, where boundaries or territorial structures are implicated, the communities concerned.

Consultation should cover the transition design itself: not only *whether* the move is lawful or desirable, but *how* staff, records, assets, and service continuity will be protected.

### 12. Provide a named transition authority and a fast dispute route

Transfer packs need an interpreter while the system is in motion. The archive should therefore prefer a named transition authority — or a tightly bounded joint board — with power to resolve operational disputes about the move.

Its remit should be narrow and published: staff assignment disputes, contract-control ambiguity, data-custody conflicts, cross-billing, asset handover failure, case-routing questions, and deadline preservation. It should not quietly become a standing extra tier of government.

The point is simple: unresolved transition disputes should not be dumped onto residents or frontline staff as ambient confusion.

### 13. Review the move after cutover and either simplify or repair it

A polity should revisit a major competence transfer after enough time has passed to see what actually happened. The review should ask:

- did the function and the full operating bundle really move,
- did service continuity hold,
- did any bridge become permanent by inertia,
- did assets, contracts, records, and liabilities land where the law said they would,
- and can the settlement now be simplified because temporary overlap is no longer needed?

Without review, transfer packs accumulate as sediment: old overlaps, duplicate files, unclear liabilities, and shared systems that nobody meant to keep forever.

## Guardrails

- Do not transfer a function without specifying staff, assets, records, contracts, fiscal lane, and liability treatment.
- Do not let citizens discover the cutover date by failed service attempts.
- Do not move active cases or legal deadlines without naming who now owns the file and the remedy route.
- Do not use transitional shared administration without an expiry, owner, and dispute rule.
- Do not claim decentralisation or recentralisation success while the operating estate remains somewhere else.

## Failure modes

- **empty-shell decentralisation**: the lower tier receives the duty but not the people, tools, files, or money.
- **liability dumping**: the sending tier pushes debts, claims, or pension burdens downward without the associated resources.
- **file amnesia**: cases, notices, archives, or identifiers are broken by transfer and residents cannot locate responsibility.
- **vendor constitutionalism**: contractors and platforms become the practical arbiters of who governs because novation and instruction rights were left vague.
- **permanent temporary overlap**: transitional service agreements and shared systems never expire, producing durable ambiguity.
- **cutover by folklore**: staff and residents learn the new operating model through rumor, bounced calls, and contradictory front doors.

## Practical tests

A polity is handling functional reassignment honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does every major competence transfer come with a written transfer pack rather than only a headline legal provision?
2. Does the pack distinguish self-governing ownership, delegated execution, agency support, and temporary stewardship?
3. Are cutover dates, overlap periods, and who remains responsible for legacy matters all publicly stated?
4. Are staff, assets, records, contracts, and liabilities all either transferred or explicitly bridged?
5. Can a resident identify where to file, appeal, complain, or obtain records during the transition?
6. Can suppliers and workers identify who now instructs, pays, and answers for the function?
7. Are temporary bridges time-bounded and subject to post-transfer review?
8. Has the polity preserved service continuity without making ambiguity permanent?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **which level owns a function in law**, and even say **how it will be financed**, but cannot also show **who takes the staff, where the assets sit, who holds the files, how contracts are novated, which liabilities move, which authority signs during overlap, and how residents keep service and remedy during cutover**, then it still has **territorial constitutional rhetoric without a governed transfer doctrine**.
