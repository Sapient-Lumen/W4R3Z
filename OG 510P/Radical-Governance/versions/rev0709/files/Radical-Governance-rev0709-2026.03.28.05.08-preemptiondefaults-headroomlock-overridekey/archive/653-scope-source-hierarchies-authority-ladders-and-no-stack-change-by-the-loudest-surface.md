# 653 — Scope source hierarchies, authority ladders, and no stack change by the loudest surface

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should settle disputes about **who governs what** against a declared **source hierarchy** rather than against whichever surface is loudest, newest, easiest to find, or already wired into daily operations: constitutional and statutory text, authoritative judgments, and explicit scope acts should outrank derivative registers, and derivative registers should outrank budgets, contracts, software behavior, dashboards, FAQs, and public-relations residue.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a dense doctrine for scope honesty. It says how functions fit levels (`602`, `628`-`630`), when review is triggered (`632`-`635`), how stack changes are classified and cleared (`636`-`639`), how they are watched, settled, retired, and reactivated (`640`-`643`), and how defects, interim protection, severability, remand, relabeling, waiver, estoppel, prescription, and desuetude should be handled (`644`-`652`).

That already closes many ways of changing the stack too cheaply.

But one operational loophole still remains.

A statute says one thing, the shared platform does another, the budget spreadsheet reflects a third arrangement, the public website still points residents somewhere else, and the canonical scope register has not yet caught up. A court or constitutional body has already limited the arrangement, but the dashboard, grant templates, procurement framework, and help-center script all keep treating the older pattern as live. A ministry insists that the portal reflects the real constitutional position because everyone uses it. A municipality says the statute still controls. A reviewer says the register is newer than the statute and therefore more reliable. An operator says the contract is what really governs the service now. A press office says the public announcement settled the matter politically even if the legal objects lagged.

All of those moves ask the same dangerous question in disguised form: **which artifact gets to act like the constitution in practice?**

Sometimes the answer is straightforward. The law and the judgment are aligned, the register is updated, the portal points to the right route, and the budget and workflow merely implement what the authority-bearing objects already say.

But often the surfaces diverge.

Modern government runs on layers of text and code: constitutional provisions, statutes, regulations, judgments, intergovernmental compacts, registers, diff packets, scope opinions, overrides, budgets, grant terms, procurement clauses, permissions tables, portal copy, dashboards, FAQs, training decks, operator scripts, and public messaging. Those layers do not all have the same legal force. They do not all change title. They do not all speak to the same moment in time. And they do not all deserve equal weight when there is conflict.

If the archive leaves that ordering implicit, then the most visible or operationally dominant surface will tend to win. A portal becomes more authoritative than the law because residents actually use it. A software default becomes more authoritative than the register because staff cannot bypass it. A budget line becomes more authoritative than a judgment because payroll is already flowing. A public announcement becomes more authoritative than the actual packet because no one wants to contradict it in public.

That is how constitutional folklore becomes constitutional fact.

The archive already has a related instinct in its notes on canonical registers (`633`), jurisprudence digests (`634`), clear statements (`638`), conformance (`639`), and public source hierarchies for consequential automated systems (`438`). What it still lacked was one compact, stack-wide doctrine saying that disputes about scope must be settled against a declared **authority ladder**.

That is the role of **scope source hierarchies**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Predeclare one authority ladder for scope disputes

A polity should publish one simple hierarchy for answering scope questions when surfaces conflict.

At minimum, the ladder should distinguish between:

- constitutional or treaty text,
- statute and delegated legislation,
- authoritative judicial or equivalent rulings,
- explicit scope acts such as packets, opinions, overrides, settlements, retirement, or reactivation certificates,
- derivative but official memory layers such as the canonical scope register and jurisprudence digest,
- implementation artifacts such as budgets, grants, contracts, workflow rules, permissions tables, and operating manuals,
- and merely descriptive or communicative artifacts such as portals, FAQs, dashboards, decks, press releases, and screenshots.

The point is not to make every dispute easy. It is to stop the dispute from being decided by improvisation about which surface feels most real today.

### 2. Primary legal sources should outrank derivative summaries and operational residue

The first substantive rule should be simple: **title comes from authority-bearing acts, not from derivative convenience layers**.

Where there is a conflict, constitutional and statutory text, properly binding delegated law, and authoritative adjudication should normally outrank:

- registers,
- summaries,
- dashboards,
- portal wording,
- operator practice,
- budget labels,
- software behavior,
- or public messaging.

Those lower surfaces may be powerful evidence about drift, conformance failure, or lived operation. They should not be allowed to become the source of lawful title just because they are easier to access.

### 3. Canonical registers should be authoritative derivative surfaces, not self-originating constitutions

The archive already treats the canonical scope register as a vital object (`633`). Scope source hierarchies sharpen that point.

A register should be treated as the **best official derivative memory surface** for the live stack. It should be the first public place most people look. But it should not claim self-originating constitutional force when the deeper authority-bearing objects say otherwise.

That means a register should:

- cite the legal basis for each entry,
- point to the governing packet, opinion, override, judgment, or settlement where relevant,
- carry visible time and status labels,
- and declare when an entry is provisional, contested, lagging, or awaiting conforming implementation.

The register matters greatly. It simply should not pretend to be more than it is.

### 4. Judgments and equivalent rulings should update the stack even when operations lag

A common failure mode is to treat a court or equivalent ruling as intellectually important but operationally secondary until budgets, platforms, or ministries catch up.

A cleaner rule is that authoritative adjudication should update the legal stack first, with implementation surfaces then carrying a visible **non-conformance burden** until they are repaired.

That prevents this dishonest sequence:

1. the reviewer or court narrows or invalidates a stack arrangement;
2. the live systems, forms, and scripts continue to act as if nothing changed;
3. months later someone argues that the old arrangement must still be real because operations never stopped.

Under a source hierarchy, operational lag is evidence of implementation debt, not evidence that the higher-authority source lost.

### 5. Budgets, contracts, grants, and code should count as implementation evidence, not automatic title

Many of the most consequential modern scope conflicts arise from artifacts that are undeniably real but not constitutionally self-sufficient:

- budget appropriations,
- grant conditions,
- procurement frameworks,
- concession or hosting contracts,
- permissions tables,
- routing logic,
- case-system defaults,
- service catalogues,
- and staffing or payroll structures.

These artifacts matter because they often show who actually governs in practice. The archive should treat them as **high-value evidence of conformance, drift, or hidden rescoping**.

But unless the legal system expressly gives one of those artifacts title-bearing force in the relevant lane, they should not be allowed to settle who lawfully owns the competence.

Otherwise the stack gets rewritten by finance, procurement, or code simply because those surfaces are closer to daily life.

### 6. Public communications should be linked to authority, not mistaken for authority

Residents often encounter the state through the surface that is easiest to find:

- the portal,
- the helpline script,
- the website notice,
- the dashboard,
- the press release,
- or the mayor's or ministry's public statement.

Those surfaces need not be legally empty. They may create reliance concerns, notice obligations, or accountability consequences. But they should not be allowed to settle scope on their own.

A sound design rule is that public-facing materials should link back to the authority-bearing source or derivative register entry that supports them. When they cannot do that, they should be treated as communicative surfaces that may need correction, not as the place where the constitutional answer is created.

### 7. Where primary sources conflict or are incomplete, force the issue back into the proper lane

A hierarchy is most useful when it reaches a hard case honestly.

Sometimes the constitution, statute, delegated law, and judgment do not yield one clean answer. In that case the archive should resist the temptation to let lower surfaces fill the gap by convenience.

Instead, the polity should force the matter back into the proper lane:

- a scope-diff packet (`635`),
- a scope opinion (`636`),
- an override if necessary (`637`),
- a clear statement repair (`638`),
- or a jurisprudence update (`634`).

In other words: where the primary layers are genuinely unclear, the answer should be **fresh lawful clarification**, not constitutional settlement by portal copy or workflow habit.

### 8. Time and supersession labels should travel with every derivative surface

Source conflicts are often temporal conflicts in disguise. The portal reflects last month's arrangement. The register reflects this week's settlement certificate. The budget line reflects an earlier compromise. The contract still assumes a pre-judgment model. The FAQ was updated by the press team, but the statute was amended again yesterday.

A polity should therefore require derivative surfaces to carry:

- effective dates,
- last-updated dates,
- provisional or settled status,
- superseded links,
- and where applicable, the exact upstream authority they implement.

Without those labels, old surfaces keep winning arguments merely because their currency cannot be judged quickly.

### 9. Every detected source conflict should produce one visible conflict object

When materially stack-bearing surfaces disagree, the archive should not rely on institutional memory or hallway clarification.

There should be one visible conflict object — whether a short register flag, conflict memo, or equivalent — stating:

- which surfaces conflict,
- which source currently governs under the hierarchy,
- whether implementation is non-conforming,
- whether residents or subordinate authorities face immediate routing risk,
- and what update sequence is required.

This turns source conflict into governed work instead of folklore.

### 10. The more rights-bearing the question, the stricter the hierarchy should become

Not every mismatch deserves the same alarm. A stale infographic is not the same as a stale appeal route or a portal that points residents to the wrong sovereign.

The archive should therefore become stricter as stakes rise.

Where the conflict affects:

- who may coerce,
- who may tax or spend,
- who hears complaints or appeals,
- who controls planning, emergency, or police-like powers,
- or which body residents must petition or challenge,

the polity should apply the hierarchy aggressively and prefer the most formal, reviewable, and law-linked source available.

That is where "the loudest surface" becomes most dangerous.

## Guardrails

- Do not let a portal, FAQ, dashboard, or press release outrank law, judgment, or explicit scope acts merely because it is the public's first point of contact.
- Do not let budgets, contracts, workflow defaults, or platform permissions manufacture constitutional title unless the law expressly gives them that force in the relevant lane.
- Do not let the canonical register pretend to self-generate authority; it should point back to the acts and rulings that support it.
- Do not treat operational lag after a judgment or settlement as proof that the older arrangement still governs.
- Do not fill legal ambiguity with software behavior, help-desk scripts, or ministry habit when the honest answer is that clarification is still required.

## Failure modes

- **portal constitutionalism**: the website or intake page becomes the de facto constitution because everyone relies on it first.
- **dashboard supremacy**: management dashboards or scorecards are treated as stronger than the legal objects they summarize.
- **register idolatry**: the canonical register is treated as self-originating title rather than authority-bearing memory tied to upstream acts.
- **budget title laundering**: appropriations and payroll flows are used to argue that operational control has already become lawful constitutional control.
- **code-made-it-real**: permissions tables, routing rules, and platform defaults are treated as the most authoritative answer because they are hardest to bypass.
- **communications settlement theater**: public announcements are used to imply that a stack dispute has been settled when the proper legal lane is still open or unresolved.
- **temporal blur**: stale surfaces win because effective dates, supersession markers, and status labels are missing.
- **judgment lag laundering**: a ruling changes the stack, but implementers keep using the old arrangement and later claim practice proved the ruling unimportant.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Which surface in this dispute is the highest one that actually has authority to create or interpret title here?
2. Is the relied-on artifact authority-bearing, derivative-but-official, implementation residue, or merely communicative?
3. If the portal, dashboard, or workflow were wrong today, what upstream object would prove it wrong?
4. Does the canonical register point to the statute, judgment, packet, or settlement that supports its current entry?
5. If a judgment narrowed the arrangement, are the budget, contract, and platform being treated as non-conforming residue rather than as competing constitutions?
6. Are dates, status labels, and supersession links clear enough to tell whether the apparent conflict is really only a stale-surface problem?
7. If primary legal materials are genuinely unclear, has the polity opened the proper packet or opinion lane rather than letting lower surfaces decide by inertia?
8. Does the public-facing surface link back to an authority-bearing source, or is it asking trust to travel without an anchor?
9. Would the answer change only because one artifact is easier to find, easier to use, or already embedded in daily operations?
10. If the stakes touch coercion, money, appeals, emergency powers, or territorial planning, has the stricter end of the hierarchy been applied?

## Closing image

A good multilevel constitution should not force people to guess whether the real sovereign is the statute, the register, the contract, the portal, the dashboard, or the software switch. It should be able to say, calmly and publicly: **this surface is the memory, this surface is the implementation, this surface is the communication, and this surface is the law** — and the loudest one does not win merely by being loud.
