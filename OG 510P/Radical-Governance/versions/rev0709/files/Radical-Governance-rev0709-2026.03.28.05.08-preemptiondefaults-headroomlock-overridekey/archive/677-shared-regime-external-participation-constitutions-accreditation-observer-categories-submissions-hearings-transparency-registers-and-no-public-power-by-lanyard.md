# 677 — Shared-regime external-participation constitutions: accreditation, observer categories, submissions, hearings, transparency registers, and no public power by lanyard

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed external-participation constitution** that separates membership from observer or accredited-participant status, standing accreditation from session registration, document access from floor rights, written submissions from negotiation rights, and consultation from decision power — so public authority does not drift toward whoever got a badge, a side-room invitation, or a privileged mailing list.

## Why this matters

The archive now has notes on representation (`671`), decision rules (`672`), officer and secretariat power (`673`), records (`674`), initiation (`675`), and subsidiary bodies (`676`).

But once those internal organs exist, regimes still face a recurring constitutional problem one step outside the member table.

Shared regimes are often affected by actors who are not members yet still matter:

- Indigenous peoples and other rightsholders,
- labour and professional bodies,
- scientific and technical communities,
- cities and subnational governments,
- business associations,
- philanthropic funders,
- watchdogs and civil-society organizations,
- and communities living with the direct effects of the regime’s rules.

Without a typed external-participation constitution, four familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **lanyard government**: people with observer badges, private access, or convening proximity acquire practical agenda-shaping power with no visible rule naming what they may actually do.

The second is **consultation theater**: regimes solicit statements and submissions but leave no binding lane showing where those inputs land, who reviewed them, or why they were rejected.

The third is **opaque access capture**: some organizations enjoy durable inside access because of staff familiarity, donor leverage, or long habit while others cannot even discover the threshold for admission.

The fourth is **stakeholder flattening**: communities with rights or direct exposure are treated as interchangeable with generic lobby presence.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **external participation discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate member authority from external participation

External participation is not membership by another name.

The regime should distinguish at minimum:

- members or parties,
- observers with standing status,
- accredited non-state actors,
- invited experts,
- petitioners or submitting organizations,
- affected-rightsholder bodies,
- and media or public audiences.

Each category should carry its own rights bundle. None should quietly inherit voting or adoption rights from physical presence.

### 2. Publish one admission and accreditation lane

A serious regime should not make access depend on backstage familiarity.

It should name:

- who may apply,
- the eligibility criteria,
- required documents,
- the reviewing organ,
- the recommending organ where one exists,
- the deciding organ,
- the timing and deadlines,
- and whether provisional access is possible before final confirmation.

If the regime uses different lanes for intergovernmental organizations, nongovernmental organizations, experts, business associations, philanthropic bodies, and rightsholder institutions, the differences should be explicit rather than hidden in form design.

### 3. Separate standing accreditation from event registration and room access

A standing relationship with the regime is not the same thing as physical access to every room at every meeting.

The archive should therefore prefer a rule that distinguishes:

- admission or accreditation,
- nomination of participants,
- quota or capacity management,
- confirmation of named delegates,
- badge issuance,
- and access to plenary, committee, side-event, or restricted rooms.

Otherwise the regime confuses constitutional status with venue management and turns logistical scarcity into hidden law.

### 4. Type participation rights instead of speaking vaguely about “engagement”

Not all external participants need the same constitutional lane.

The regime should publish, by participant class, whether the holder may:

- access documents,
- submit written comments,
- make oral statements,
- join hearings,
- attend public or open sessions,
- attend committee meetings,
- propose agenda additions through members,
- receive circulations,
- host side events,
- or observe without any floor right.

A regime that says “stakeholders may participate” without typing the specific rights bundle has not built participation; it has built expectation fog.

### 5. Give written submissions a real docket, not a ceremonial inbox

Consultation should leave a visible trail.

The archive should therefore prefer one public or reviewable submissions lane that records:

- who submitted,
- on what item,
- by what deadline,
- under which participant class,
- whether the submission was accepted as complete,
- and where any posted submission, synthesis, or response can be found.

This does not require every regime to publish all submissions without exception. It requires the regime to stop pretending that informal email traffic is a constitutional participation channel.

### 6. Distinguish rightsholder participation from generic stakeholder access

Some external actors are not just interested parties. They are bearers of legal rights, treaty protections, territorial claims, or direct exposure to the regime’s decisions.

The archive should therefore prefer a visible special lane where a regime’s subject matter makes it relevant, especially for:

- Indigenous peoples,
- workers directly governed by a labour or professional regime,
- populations subject to inspection or surveillance,
- and communities that bear concentrated environmental, health, or displacement burdens.

This does not automatically convert rightsholders into voting members. It does mean they should not be flattened into the same category as a general advocacy mailing list.

### 7. Keep observer and accredited access reviewable on a clock

A badge once granted should not become hereditary constitutional capital.

The regime should therefore state:

- whether status is permanent or periodically reviewed,
- what reporting or update duty applies,
- what inactivity or misconduct can trigger review,
- who may recommend suspension or withdrawal,
- and what appeal or reconsideration lane exists.

### 8. Publish one access register with enough real information to detect capture

An honest participation constitution requires more than a list of names.

The register should preferably show, where applicable:

- legal name,
- participant class,
- jurisdiction or region,
- subject-matter competence,
- website or contact,
- current status,
- date of admission or renewal,
- any public plan of collaboration,
- and any relevant declarations on funding, affiliation, or constituency.

The point is not maximum paperwork. The point is that the public should be able to see who is repeatedly present around consequential decisions.

### 9. Keep participation rights subordinate to the member-driven constitution

External participation should matter without becoming a disguised second chamber.

The regime should therefore name how external inputs influence process without confusing them with member powers. Examples include:

- submission dockets,
- hearing rights,
- published syntheses,
- briefing sessions,
- constituency statements,
- and response requirements where the stakes justify them.

But agenda adoption, voting, adoption of binding text, and formal interpretation should remain where the constitution places them unless the regime expressly provides otherwise.

### 10. Publish one external-participation map across the regime

A serious regime should maintain one public map showing:

- every external participant class,
- the admission route for each,
- the rights bundle for each,
- document and submission channels,
- speaking or hearing routes,
- review or renewal clocks,
- suspension rules,
- and the public register location.

If users must reconstruct access law from conference FAQs, side-event portals, invitation habits, and staff memory, the regime has not built an external-participation constitution.

## Working matrix

| External-participation question | Honest rule | Why | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| who may seek standing access | typed classes with published eligibility | prevents access by insider folklore | “stakeholder” as one undifferentiated category |
| who decides admission | named review and decision organs | keeps access from becoming staff patronage | secretariat-only social gatekeeping |
| what accreditation actually grants | standing status separated from event registration and room access | prevents venue logistics from mutating into constitutional power | treating a badge as a general governing mandate |
| how input travels | written submissions, hearings, and statement lanes with visible dockets | makes participation legible and reviewable | ceremonial consultation inboxes |
| who deserves stronger participation lanes | rightsholder and directly affected classes where relevant | avoids flattening rights into generic lobbying | treating all outside voices as interchangeable |
| how status persists or ends | review, renewal, suspension, and appeal rules | prevents inherited access capture | immortal observer status by habit |

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse observer or accredited status with membership or voting authority.
- Do not let event-registration scarcity silently redefine constitutional access rights.
- Do not invite submissions without one visible docket or response lane.
- Do not treat rightsholders and generic interested parties as the same procedural class when the subject matter makes the distinction constitutionally relevant.
- Do not leave standing access unrevised for decades.
- Do not let donor proximity, secretariat familiarity, or chair discretion become the real accreditation system.

## Failure modes

- **lanyard government**: practical influence flows from physical access rather than typed authority.
- **consultation theater**: external input is collected without any visible routing or effect.
- **observer capture**: a stable insider class forms without review, disclosure, or constitutional clarity.
- **stakeholder flattening**: rightsholders are proceduralized as just another lobby category.
- **badge constitutionalism**: venue controls and meeting logistics silently become governance law.
- **access opacity**: outsiders cannot tell how admission, quotas, or speech opportunities are actually determined.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it clearly distinguish members, observers, accredited participants, invited experts, and public audiences?
2. Does it publish eligibility, documents, deadlines, reviewing organs, and deciding organs for standing access?
3. Does it separate standing accreditation from session registration, quotas, and badge issuance?
4. Are document, submission, statement, hearing, and committee-access rights typed by participant class?
5. Is there a visible submissions docket or equivalent reviewable lane?
6. Where relevant, does the regime distinguish rightsholder participation from generic stakeholder access?
7. Are renewal, review, suspension, and appeal routes explicit?
8. Is there one public access register or equivalent transparency spine?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can say **who its members are and how formal votes occur** but cannot say **which outsiders may enter, under what status, through what register, with what submission or speaking rights, and under what renewal or suspension rules**, it still has **public-facing participation without an honest external-participation constitution**.
