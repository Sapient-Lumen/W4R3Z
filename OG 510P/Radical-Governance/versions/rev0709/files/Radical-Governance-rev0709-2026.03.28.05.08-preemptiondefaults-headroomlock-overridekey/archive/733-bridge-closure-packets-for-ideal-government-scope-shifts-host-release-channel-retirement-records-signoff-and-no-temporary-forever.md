# 733 — Bridge-closure packets for ideal-government scope shifts: host release, channel retirement, records signoff, and no temporary forever

## One-line thesis

Once the archive has a transitional-hosting packet (`732`), it should also require one compact **bridge-closure packet** before anybody may claim the scope shift is operationally complete: classify the closure state, retire or rewire every legacy front door, release host-side powers and keys, freeze and hand over the final evidence package, settle the residual exceptions ledger, and refuse to relabel an unresolved dependency as “complete” merely because the cutover date has passed.

## Why this matters

The newer ideal-government scope-shift sequence now answers several hard questions in order.

It can say **where a function should usually live** (`726`). It can say **when to override the default** (`727`). It can say **what minimum constitutional kit a sideways body needs** (`728`). It can say **who must ratify a move** (`729`). It can say **what continuity must survive the move** (`730`). It can say **what witness freezes the handoff moment** (`731`). And it can now say **how a temporary host must behave while the new holder is live in law but still borrowing runtime** (`732`).

What the archive still lacked was the next small object in that chain:

**how does the polity prove that the temporary bridge actually ended, and what happens when it did not?**

That gap matters because transitional hosting is often treated as if it closes itself. A legacy website keeps forwarding longer than planned. Admin credentials remain in the predecessor’s hands “just in case.” Queue triage still happens in the host team’s mailbox. Logs are technically exportable but have never been fully exported. A supplier contract ends, yet one hidden integration keeps the old body in the loop. The public is told the move is finished because the legal title moved months ago, while the actual operating dependency remains.

The archive therefore needs one reusable anti-fiction object: a **bridge-closure packet**. It is narrower than the continuity packet and later than the cutover witness. It does not defend the scope shift, and it does not govern the bridge while it is active. It answers the simpler, sharper question:

**what must be true before a temporary host can honestly stop counting as part of the constitutional operating core?**

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat closure as a named state change, not the absence of fresh complaints

A hosting bridge should not be considered closed merely because nobody raised an alarm. Closure should be a declared state change with its own packet.

That packet should be required whenever a scope-shift bridge is being represented as ended, materially reduced, renewed in narrower form, or replaced by a different dependency. Silence, habit, and lack of incident should not stand in for a closure decision.

### 2. Classify the closure outcome before calling it complete

The packet should not begin with the word “closed.” It should first classify which of a few closure states is actually true.

At minimum, the archive should distinguish:

- **full bridge closure**, where the successor now runs the relevant capability without the transitional host;
- **partial closure**, where some hosted elements have ended but a typed residual dependency remains;
- **renewed hosting**, where the bridge survives and therefore requires fresh clearance rather than decorative optimism;
- and **failed closure**, where the attempted exit exposed defects that require rollback, degraded operation, or a new transition plan.

Without this classification step, “closure” becomes a rhetorical upgrade rather than an operational fact.

### 3. Retire or rewire every legacy public route

No bridge is honestly closed while legacy front doors still behave ambiguously.

The closure packet should therefore state, for every materially consequential legacy route:

- whether it has been retired,
- whether it now only redirects,
- whether it still accepts submissions,
- how misdirected matters are cured,
- what notice head is authoritative,
- and when any remaining relay route expires.

The public should not need folklore to know whether the old door is dead, forwarding, or secretly still live.

### 4. Release host-side powers, keys, and privileged paths explicitly

Temporary hosting often survives through retained privileges rather than through visible public channels. The closure packet should therefore record the disposition of:

- administrator accounts,
- API keys and service credentials,
- signing or publishing permissions,
- emergency-access paths,
- job schedulers and automation hooks,
- deployment or rollback rights,
- queue-priority controls,
- and template or content-publishing permissions.

A bridge is not truly closed if the host can still steer or stop the function through leftover keys.

### 5. Freeze the final host-side evidence bundle before release

The end of hosting should produce one final evidence handback rather than a vague promise of later retrieval.

The packet should therefore identify:

- the last covered operating period,
- the log and audit extracts captured from the host,
- the final records inventory and any exceptions,
- the metadata needed to interpret those materials,
- where the authoritative successor copy now lives,
- and what residual retrieval route exists if later review needs the host-native trace.

This prevents a recurring failure mode: the host relationship ends on paper, but the proof needed to audit it evaporates with the platform, contract, or team.

### 6. Reconcile the exception ledger instead of burying it in the exit memo

A transitional-hosting regime should already have an exceptions ledger (`731`, `732`). Closure should not make that ledger disappear.

The packet should therefore dispose of every live exception by class:

- cured and closed,
- accepted as a bounded residual dependency,
- moved into a new cleared arrangement,
- or escalated as an unresolved blocker.

The archive should resist the temptation to treat unresolved items as “post-closure housekeeping.” If they still shape authority, continuity, or proof, they are part of the closure truth.

### 7. Separate closure of hosting from closure of predecessor accountability

Ending the bridge does not automatically end every predecessor responsibility.

The packet should therefore distinguish clearly between:

- the end of temporary hosting,
- the continuing responsibility for predecessor acts or liabilities,
- the custodian for historical records,
- and the lookup path for legacy complaints, appeals, or investigations.

Otherwise the polity can accidentally launder blame by treating dehosting as if it also erased who must answer for the period before dehosting.

### 8. Close the commercial and funding lane, not just the technical lane

Some transitions fail because the systems moved but the money lane did not, or because an emergency extension was funded informally after the supposed end date.

The packet should therefore state:

- which contract, intergovernmental agreement, or service order ended,
- whether any termination assistance survives,
- whether any paid residual service remains,
- what budget authority funds that residual service,
- and whether new procurement, amendment, or statutory clearance was required.

A bridge is not closed if it still depends on an untyped money drip.

### 9. Publish one successor lookup and one canonical closure head

After closure, the public and downstream institutions should be able to answer one simple question: **who should we go to now?**

The packet should therefore produce one canonical closure head naming:

- the successor public holder,
- the official front door,
- the official record or complaint lookup path,
- the status of legacy channels,
- and the status of any bounded residual dependency still remaining.

No resident, worker, supplier, court, auditor, or ombuds should have to guess which era’s contact point still counts.

### 10. Force rereview when the bridge never actually ended

The archive should refuse the common fiction in which an arrangement remains “temporary” for years while everyone behaves as though it ended.

If a bridge reaches its planned closure point but the successor still materially depends on host-side runtime, keys, evidence custody, or operator capacity, the polity should trigger one of two lanes:

- a **fresh hosting clearance** under the packet in `732`, or
- a **different institutional design choice** if the dependency is no longer transitional but structural.

Temporary should expire into either independence or honesty.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat lack of incident as proof that hosting ended.
- Do not call a bridge closed while the legacy front door still receives or triages consequential work without an explicit rule.
- Do not let admin keys, deployment rights, or emergency-access paths remain with the host by convenience alone.
- Do not end the host arrangement before the final evidence package is captured and reachable.
- Do not bury unresolved exceptions in a generic “future improvements” list.
- Do not confuse ending temporary hosting with ending predecessor accountability for past acts.

## Failure modes

- **temporary forever**: the bridge never ends, but everyone starts speaking as if it already did.
- **ghost privileges**: the successor appears independent while the host still controls keys, publishing paths, or emergency actions.
- **dead-door fiction**: the old channel is described as retired, but the public keeps using it and staff quietly keep acting on what arrives there.
- **proof evaporation at exit**: the host contract or platform ends before the final logs, metadata, and inventories are secured.
- **commercial afterlife**: a supposedly closed bridge continues through informal paid support, retained licenses, or uncited hosting charges.
- **blame wash by dehosting**: predecessor responsibilities become harder to locate because closure language blurred the difference between runtime exit and legal accountability.

## Practical tests

A bridge-closure regime is honest when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does every claimed closure produce one explicit packet rather than relying on elapsed time or managerial confidence?
2. Does the packet classify the result as full closure, partial closure, renewed hosting, or failed closure?
3. Are all legacy public routes retired, redirected, or still-live under explicit rules rather than branding ambiguity?
4. Have host-side keys, privileged roles, and operational override paths been released, transferred, or freshly re-cleared?
5. Has the final host-side evidence bundle been frozen and handed over with enough metadata for later review?
6. Does the packet dispose of every live exception rather than letting unresolved items vanish into narrative?
7. Does the packet separate end-of-hosting from continuing predecessor accountability for legacy acts and files?
8. Are contract, payment, and termination-assistance states visible enough to show whether a residual dependency still exists?
9. Is there one canonical successor lookup path for the public and for oversight bodies?
10. If the bridge did not truly end, did the polity reopen the clearance lane instead of pretending completion?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **the temporary host is no longer needed**, then it should also be able to show one compact packet proving **which routes were retired, which powers and keys were released, what evidence was handed over, what residual dependencies remain, who answers for legacy acts, and whether the bridge really ended or merely changed label**. Otherwise the scope shift remains vulnerable to **temporary forever**.
