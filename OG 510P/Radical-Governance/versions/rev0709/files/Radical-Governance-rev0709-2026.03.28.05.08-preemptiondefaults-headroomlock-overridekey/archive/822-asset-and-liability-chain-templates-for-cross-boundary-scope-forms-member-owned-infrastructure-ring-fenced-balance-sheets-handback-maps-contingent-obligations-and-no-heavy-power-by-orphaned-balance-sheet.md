# 822 — Asset-and-liability-chain templates for cross-boundary scope forms: member-owned infrastructure, ring-fenced balance sheets, handback maps, contingent obligations, and no heavy power by orphaned balance sheet

## One-line thesis

When the archive has already established that a cross-boundary problem is real, chosen the honest form, and said who governs, pays, operates, votes, and hears disputes, it should still require one compact **asset-and-liability chain**: thin connectors should own almost nothing beyond trivial co-ordination tools; shared-service backbones should keep political title with the principals unless a clearly bounded common vehicle is justified; bounded functional authorities may hold and steward system-critical assets and named liabilities inside a ring-fenced public balance sheet; and general-purpose territorial tiers should carry visible asset, debt, reserve, and handback constitutions rather than hiding real sovereignty in host property, off-book obligations, or exit chaos.

## Why this matters

`790` gives the archive a short answer key for what ideal governments at each scope should generally be. `810` says how to assemble threshold proof before saying that a metropolitan, pooling, regional-platform, fiscal-floor, or legitimacy-thickness line is really crossed. `812` says which institutional form is honest once that burden is real. `813` says who should sit in the room and which decisions require which legitimacy key. `814` says what money constitution belongs to the form. `815` says where the operator, employer, records home, and continuity spine should live. `819` says how territory and membership should be entered, changed, exited, and unwound. `820` says what vote, quorum, ratification, deadlock, and emergency grammar belongs to the body. `821` says how disputes should actually travel once the body is real.

What the archive still lacked was one compact answer to the next question:

**once all of that machinery exists, who actually owns the things, holds the debt, carries the lease, books the reserve, and takes the handback if the arrangement changes?**

That gap matters because constitutional dishonesty often hides in the balance sheet rather than in the charter.

One failure is **host capture by property drift**: a host municipality or incumbent operator quietly becomes the real sovereign because the servers, depots, pipes, rolling stock, contracts, or reserve funds sit on its books even after the arrangement has become genuinely shared.

Another failure is **shared-service innocence theater**: the archive treats an arrangement as mere pooling while one common body is already accumulating durable assets, unfunded obligations, or long-lived contracts that outlast the member governments’ ordinary political control.

A third failure is **exit without handback**: accession, withdrawal, merger, or dissolution clauses are discussed as map questions while no one can say who keeps the plant, who repays the debt, what happens to sunk digital systems, who absorbs pension or severance residue, or which liabilities survive past membership.

A fourth failure is **off-book sovereignty**: a formally thin body avoids visible taxation or borrowing, yet it governs through long leases, guarantees, take-or-pay contracts, public-private concessions, minimum-revenue undertakings, or deferred-maintenance obligations that are constitutionally heavy even if the annual operating budget looks light.

Official practice supports a more disciplined chain. Council of Europe material on municipal property treats the ability of local authorities to own material as well as financial assets as an intrinsic component of local self-government, requires that transferred powers be accompanied as far as possible by the assets necessary to exercise them, and warns against transferring burdensome assets that undermine self-government. The EGTC regulation requires conventions and statutes to specify members, contributions, liability arrangements, accounting and budgetary rules, and dissolution conditions, and it makes clear that members may remain liable when the EGTC’s assets are insufficient. OECD work on inter-municipal shared services identifies joint asset management, infrastructure ownership, use, and responsibility division as practical governance problems rather than technical footnotes. OECD infrastructure-governance work treats asset management as a life-cycle discipline tied to accountability and financial management. World Bank local-government finance work likewise treats asset management, debt, and capital planning as core municipal-governance questions. The archive should compress those lessons into one rule: **do not let public power grow heavier than its declared ownership and liability map.**

## Pattern pack

### 1. Use this note after form, money, operator, map, vote, and dispute questions are separated

This note does not decide whether a metropolitan, county-pooling, regional-platform, fiscal-floor, or legitimacy-thickness threshold has been crossed.

Use `810` for that.

This note does not decide whether the honest answer is a thin connector, shared-service backbone, bounded functional authority, or general-purpose territorial tier.

Use `812` for that.

This note does not decide what dues, service payments, assigned revenues, equalization keys, reserve rules, or capital lanes fund the body.

Use `814` for that.

This note does not decide who operates the machinery, employs the staff, or hosts the records home.

Use `815` for that.

This note does not decide who belongs to the body, what territory counts, how accession or amendment work, or when exit and dissolution are lawful.

Use `819` for that.

This note does not decide what vote or dispute route belongs to those questions.

Use `820` and `821` for that.

If the live question is instead whether procurement, concessions, franchises, PPPs, or managed-service contracts are hiding constitutional weight in the body’s commercial perimeter, use `823` rather than stretching the balance-sheet map into a full contracting-boundary judgment.

This note applies after those questions are separated and the remaining live issue is:

- who holds political title to assets used in the arrangement,
- who may grant use rights, leases, concessions, or security interests,
- which liabilities are operational, capital, contingent, or residual,
- what reserve, depreciation, renewal, and decommissioning duties attach,
- and what handback or residue map applies when the body upgrades, exits, splits, or dissolves.

### 2. Distinguish four recurrent asset-and-liability chains before inventing hybrids

The archive should usually begin with four recognizable chains.

| Form | Default asset-and-liability chain | What it may legitimately carry | What it must not hide |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Thin connector** | almost no durable asset title beyond trivial meeting, publication, and co-ordination tools | small working cash, light software, modest secretariat equipment, short contracts | strategic infrastructure, long debt, guarantees, or lock-in through shadow property |
| **Shared-service backbone** | principal-owned core assets with declared shared-use rights, or a tightly bounded common vehicle for named common assets only | pooled systems, back-office platforms, common procurement frameworks, replacement reserves for shared tools, short-to-medium operational liabilities | accidental sovereignty through host-owned critical assets or unpriced exit residues |
| **Bounded functional authority** | ring-fenced public balance sheet for function-critical assets and named liabilities tied to one real system | networks, depots, plants, system-control tools, dedicated capital debt, lifecycle reserves, concession rights, decommissioning duties | drift into general-purpose hidden treasury or asset empire |
| **General-purpose territorial tier** | visible territorial balance sheet with public asset strategy, debt discipline, reserve policy, and boundary-change rules | broad infrastructure portfolio, land and facilities, multi-sector capital program, treasury operations under law | off-book government through shell companies, indefinite concessions, or untracked contingent liabilities |

The archive should not begin with bespoke ownership poetry until one of these four chains has first been ruled out honestly.

### 3. Separate five balance-sheet classes before arguing about title

Later archive work should usually type the object before it argues about ownership.

1. **light operating objects** — software subscriptions, meeting systems, publication tools, office equipment, and short service contracts;
2. **shared administrative infrastructure** — data platforms, call-center systems, procurement tools, common vehicles, laboratories, or buildings used to provide pooled support;
3. **system-critical operational assets** — plants, networks, depots, rights-of-way, rolling stock, control rooms, spectrum-like usage rights, or other infrastructure whose failure stops the service;
4. **financial and contractual obligations** — debt, lease liabilities, guarantees, minimum-revenue undertakings, take-or-pay commitments, pensions, severance, insurance deductibles, and major maintenance provisions;
5. **exit and handback residue** — stranded assets, residual debt, digital lock-in, intellectual property, termination payments, environmental remediation, decommissioning, and records-transfer costs.

The archive should not let one generic phrase such as **shared assets** pretend to settle all five classes equally well.

### 4. Thin connectors should own almost nothing durable

A thin connector is honest only while it remains a forum for co-ordination rather than a shadow sovereign.

Its asset-and-liability chain should therefore stay extremely light.

That usually means:

- ordinary members retain title to the real operational and capital assets;
- the connector may hold only trivial working property and short service contracts needed for scheduling, publication, and common analysis;
- no long borrowing, no major guarantees, and no critical infrastructure leases sit inside the connector;
- if a common secretariat uses host equipment or software, those dependencies are declared as service support rather than hidden connector property.

What the archive should reject here is the move where a supposedly thin body quietly locks members into one vendor, one depot, one digital system, or one reserve fund that no member can realistically leave.

### 5. Shared-service backbones should preserve principal title unless a common vehicle is clearly justified

A shared-service backbone exists to pool capacity while the participating governments retain political title to the duty.

Its asset-and-liability chain should therefore usually begin from a presumption of **principal ownership with declared common-use rights**.

That commonly means:

- core legal title to service responsibilities and function-defining assets remains with the member governments unless one common vehicle is affirmatively justified;
- common administrative infrastructure may sit in a jointly owned company, joint authority, or other declared vehicle where the asset is genuinely indivisible or more efficiently renewed together;
- reserve and replacement policies are written down rather than improvised through year-end bargaining;
- leases, licences, software escrow, and transition assistance are typed in advance so exit is a governed transition rather than a cliff.

The archive should allow a bounded common asset vehicle where it clearly reduces fragmentation, but it should reject the pattern where the host municipality or incumbent provider becomes the real sovereign because everything indispensable sits on its books.

### 6. Bounded functional authorities may own system-critical assets, but only inside a ring-fenced public balance sheet

A bounded functional authority becomes the honest answer when one cross-boundary system requires continuous operation, asset stewardship, and hard technical responsibility.

Its asset-and-liability chain may therefore be substantially heavier than a backbone’s.

Typical honest ingredients include:

- a named asset perimeter tied to the specific function rather than to every adjacent public ambition;
- capital debt and operating liabilities that are explicitly attached to that perimeter;
- lifecycle policies for maintenance, renewal, impairment, and decommissioning;
- publication of major guarantees, minimum-revenue undertakings, and concession obligations;
- clear rules on whether assets revert to members, a successor authority, or the state if the body is dissolved or re-scoped.

The archive should prefer this chain when one real network or plant system needs stewardship. It should be suspicious when a bounded authority starts warehousing unrelated land, cash, or borrowing power simply because it already has a balance sheet.

### 7. General-purpose territorial tiers need visible balance-sheet constitutions, not shadow ownership webs

Once the archive concludes that the honest answer is a real territorial tier, it should stop pretending that asset and liability design can remain mostly contractual.

At that point the body should usually have:

- a visible territorial asset register and capital strategy,
- ordinary law for borrowing, guarantees, reserve use, and asset disposal,
- public rules on what may be delegated to subordinate entities or corporate vehicles,
- and explicit oversight for major concessions, land transactions, and decommissioning liabilities.

Negotiated transfers and service agreements may still matter. They should not become the main constitutional home of capital ownership once the body is a real tier.

### 8. Require handback maps before accession, exit, merger, or dissolution are treated as complete

Whenever `819` is live, the archive should also ask whether the body has a **handback map**.

That map should usually state:

- which assets stay with the body,
- which revert to members or move to a successor,
- how jointly financed improvements are valued,
- who bears residual debt and contingent liabilities,
- how software, data, and intellectual property are transitioned,
- and what happens to maintenance backlogs, environmental duties, or contract break costs.

The archive should not treat a boundary or membership judgment as complete until the corresponding handback map is at least typed.

### 9. Type contingent liabilities explicitly rather than letting them hide in commercial language

Cross-boundary arrangements often look lighter on paper because the heaviest obligations appear as commercial or operational language rather than as explicit debt.

The archive should therefore require later case work to type at least these recurrent classes:

- guarantees and letters of comfort,
- minimum-volume or minimum-revenue undertakings,
- step-in duties and emergency backstop promises,
- termination compensation,
- unfunded major-maintenance obligations,
- pension, leave, and severance residue,
- and environmental or decommissioning liabilities.

If these obligations are material, the archive should treat them as constitutional weight even when the ordinary annual budget looks small.

### 10. Keep operator control, asset title, and liability residue distinct

The same body does not have to hold all three of these roles.

One actor may operate the machinery.

Another may hold legal title to the infrastructure.

A third may bear the residual liability if the arrangement fails.

That is not inherently dishonest.

What becomes dishonest is when the archive lets those roles blur so thoroughly that no one can say:

- who can lawfully dispose of the asset,
- who must finance major renewal,
- who answers if a guarantee is called,
- who books the residual loss on exit,
- or who must keep the system safe during transition.

### 11. Treat asset-chain design as part of public proof

When `811` is used for a real polity, proposal, or case, the packet should normally be able to add one short holding such as:

> **Asset-and-liability-chain holding:** This body may lease and operate shared administrative systems, but strategic service assets remain member-owned and all exit clauses must include a published handback map and liability residue schedule.

or:

> **Asset-and-liability-chain holding:** This authority may own and depreciate function-critical assets inside a ring-fenced balance sheet, but may not warehouse unrelated land, cash, or guarantees outside its named remit.

That keeps applied judgments from stopping at the words **authority**, **backbone**, or **metro** without saying where the durable ownership and residual obligations actually sit.

## Failure modes

- **host-asset capture** — a host municipality or operator becomes the real sovereign because indispensable systems, premises, data tools, or reserves sit on its books without an honest common title rule;
- **off-book treasury creep** — a body claims to be light because it lacks tax power while governing through guarantees, leases, concessions, and deferred renewal obligations;
- **false shared ownership** — members speak of common assets when neither title, use rights, cost shares, nor disposal rules are actually clear;
- **exit without residue map** — withdrawal, merger, or dissolution is declared lawful even though no one has typed stranded assets, break costs, debt residue, or remediation duties;
- **asset empire drift** — a bounded authority keeps accumulating unrelated property and borrowing capacity because nobody enforces remit-limited ownership;
- **digital lock-in without sovereignty check** — software, data, and service continuity depend on one vendor or host in a way that effectively reallocates public power without formal recognition;
- **burden transfer disguised as devolution** — functions or shared-service obligations move downward without the assets necessary to perform them, or with liabilities that cripple the receiving body;
- **publication blackout on capital obligations** — the public can see the operating budget but not the guarantees, concessions, reserve policy, maintenance backlog, or decommissioning liability that make the constitution heavy in practice.
