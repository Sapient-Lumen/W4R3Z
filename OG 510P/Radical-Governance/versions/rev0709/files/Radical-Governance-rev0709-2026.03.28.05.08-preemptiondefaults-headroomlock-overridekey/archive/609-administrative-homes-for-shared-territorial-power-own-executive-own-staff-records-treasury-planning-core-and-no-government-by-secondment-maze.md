# 609 — Administrative homes for shared territorial power: own executive, own staff, records, treasury-planning core, and no government by secondment maze

## One-line thesis

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, it needs an honest **administrative home** as well as a democratic and fiscal one: a legally visible executive center, its own staff and records, a durable core for planning/finance/procurement/legal/data work, interoperable but non-subordinate interfaces with component governments and the state, and enough administrative continuity that the territory is not really governed through borrowed personnel, rotating mayoral bandwidth, consultants, or agency shrubbery.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a stronger line on the threshold at which co-operation becomes real government, on the democratic home that should follow, and on the fiscal constitution that should follow. What it still lacked was the operational doctrine for the same transition.

That gap matters because many live metropolitan and regional systems fail in a recognisable way. They acquire serious territorial tasks — integrated planning, infrastructure sequencing, transport-and-land-use bargains, resilience work, shared borrowing, or redistributive programmes — but keep the administrative form of a light consortium. The board meets. The mayors bargain. A few secondees or borrowed officials try to hold the system together. Strategy lives in one office, finance in another, procurement somewhere else, data in a project unit, and the institutional memory sits inside whichever municipality or ministry currently has the deepest bench.

That is not administrative modesty. It is concealed dependence. A body that exercises durable public power over a territory should not have to impersonate a government using loaned staff and fragile courtesy. If it is real enough to plan, tax, borrow, or bind a territory, it should be real enough to own its executive machinery.

At the same time, the archive should not swing to the opposite error. Not every joint board or service consortium needs a full bureaucracy. Narrow co-operation can remain light. The point is not “build an empire.” The point is “match administrative form to actual governing work.”

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish a co-ordination secretariat from a governing administration

A shared body that merely convenes members, runs a narrow contract, or maintains a bounded service platform can often operate through a small secretariat. That is ordinary co-operation.

But once the body becomes the place where real territorial trade-offs are prepared and executed, the archive should distinguish that lighter secretariat form from a true **territorial administration**. A territorial administration is not just a meeting service. It can prepare decisions, own budgets, keep official records, manage staff, procure lawfully, monitor delivery, carry long-horizon plans, and remain institutionally present even when member governments change political hands.

The archive should therefore reject a familiar disguise: major territorial government presented as though it were still only a co-ordination office.

### 2. Give real territorial government its own executive center

Once a metropolitan or regional body exercises real governing power, it should have an identifiable executive center. The exact form can vary — directly elected executive, assembly-responsible executive, statutory chief executive, regional cabinet, metropolitan manager under public law — but the line of command should be intelligible.

The public should be able to answer simple questions without detective work: who is responsible for implementing the territorial plan, signing the budget execution decisions, directing the administration, answering the assembly, and keeping the institution operational between political meetings? If the only truthful answer is “it depends which municipality, ministry, or special-purpose operator is doing that piece this month,” then the territorial body still lacks an honest administrative home.

### 3. Give the tier its own staff, assets, records, and administrative memory

Real territorial government should not depend entirely on staff on loan from member governments or ministries. Borrowed personnel can support transition or specialist gaps, but they should not be the whole constitutional story.

The archive should therefore prefer that a real metropolitan or regional government have:

- its own legally employed staff,
- control of its own official records and archives,
- custody of its own operating systems and institutional memory,
- and visible administrative ownership of the assets needed for its continuing work.

Without these, continuity collapses whenever elections, intergovernmental relations, or ministerial fashions change. The body then exists politically only when its stronger members feel like feeding it.

### 4. Build a minimum territorial operating core before multiplying agencies

A real metropolitan or regional government does not need every specialist function in-house. But it does need a **minimum operating core** strong enough to keep the institution sovereign over its own work. At minimum, the archive should expect some durable capability in:

- executive coordination and assembly support,
- treasury / budgeting / financial control,
- legal and administrative procedure,
- planning and programme management,
- procurement and contract management,
- data / evidence / mapping / performance support,
- and public records, publication, and resident-facing communications.

This does not mean every capability must be large. It means the body should not be a shell that outsources judgment while retaining only ceremony. Shared expert pools, common service agencies, or framework contracts may still be sensible. But the territorial government should remain the principal that can instruct, review, and integrate them.

### 5. Integrate the cross-sector fields that produce the real territorial bargain

OECD metropolitan work repeatedly shows that metropolitan governance is most active in transport, spatial planning, and regional development, and that fragmentation across institutions weakens implementation. The archive should therefore prefer an administrative design that can actually join up the fields where the territorial bargain lives.

This does not require one mega-department for everything. It does require more than ex post stitching. If transport, land use, housing-supporting infrastructure, resilience, and investment sequencing are all politically shared at metropolitan or regional scale, the administrative home should contain a place where those decisions can genuinely be integrated rather than negotiated only across rival institutional silos.

A territory that is politically integrated but administratively dismembered will keep reproducing the same bargaining failures its reform was supposed to cure.

### 6. Use shared expert platforms as supports, not as substitutes for government

Small or medium jurisdictions will still need shared technical capacity. The archive should continue to allow shared procurement units, engineering pools, data labs, PPP cells, legal-support offices, or planning support services accessible to multiple jurisdictions.

But where the shared territorial tier itself is already real government, those support platforms should feed into its operating core rather than becoming an excuse not to build one. Expert-sharing is a capacity tool, not a constitutional alibi.

The honest rule is simple: shared expertise may supplement the territorial administration; it should not replace the existence of territorial administration.

### 7. Keep intergovernmental interfaces explicit so administrative capacity does not become shadow hierarchy

A metropolitan or regional administration must work with municipalities, counties, regional operators, and state field offices. That is normal. The danger comes when those interfaces become the real chain of command.

The archive should therefore prefer explicit interface rules: what the territorial tier decides itself, what it implements through compacts or contracts, what remains local discretion, what remains state supervision, how data moves, how disputes escalate, and which records belong to whom. This keeps administrative capacity from sliding into either shadow centralization or permanent dependency.

### 8. Treat secondments, consultants, and project units as transitional or bounded tools

Secondments can help start a new administration. Consultants can supply bounded expertise. Project units can accelerate one capital programme. But the archive should reject **government by secondment maze**: a condition in which the real governing intelligence of a territory sits in temporary placements, external advisers, or ring-fenced delivery vehicles that are easier to finance than a durable public administration.

That model produces brittle continuity, blurred loyalty, weak archival memory, hidden cost, and low democratic intelligibility. It is often defended as flexibility, but in practice it means the territory never quite owns its own governing mind.

### 9. Publish administrative-upgrade triggers alongside democratic and fiscal ones

A polity should not wait for administrative collapse before admitting that a shared body has outgrown secretariat form. The archive should therefore prefer visible upgrade triggers.

If a shared body acquires broad planning powers, a standing operating budget, compulsory levies, debt authority, major cross-sector implementation duties, or a directly elected or double-legitimacy democratic home, its administrative form should be reviewed too. The review should ask whether the body now needs its own executive head, permanent staff cadre, records system, treasury core, and legal/procurement/data functions.

This keeps institutional honesty synchronized across representation, money, and administration rather than letting the operating core lag several constitutional steps behind power.

## Administrative-home matrix

| Condition | Light secretariat fits | Shared operating platform fits | Full territorial administration fits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Institution type** | narrow consortium or co-ordination board | stronger co-ordination body with recurring system tasks | real metropolitan or regional government |
| **Executive form** | convenor / rotating chair / small secretariat | permanent management nucleus with bounded delegated authority | identifiable executive center answerable in public |
| **Staff model** | few staff, many member-government inputs | mixed permanent staff plus shared expert pools | own staff as the administrative default, with bounded borrowing only |
| **Core capacities** | meeting support, contracts, reporting | programme management, technical support, some budget/plan capacity | treasury, legal, procurement, planning, data, records, communications, implementation management |
| **Records / memory** | mostly retained by member governments | mixed custody with explicit protocols | official records and institutional memory held by the territorial government itself |
| **Main risk if misused** | paralysis and dependence on strongest members | blurred responsibility between support platform and actual government | bureaucratic overbuild if powers remain narrow or clearly derivative |

## Failure modes

- **board without backbone**: the institution has political visibility but almost no operating core of its own.
- **secondment maze**: the body is run through borrowed staff whose real loyalties, retention, and continuity sit elsewhere.
- **consultant constitution**: long-horizon public judgment is effectively outsourced to advisers and project managers.
- **records exile**: the territorial body cannot fully prove, replay, or inherit its own decisions because the archive lives in component governments or vendors.
- **agency shrubbery**: finance, planning, delivery, and debt are scattered across authorities and vehicles so no one forum actually governs the whole machine.
- **rotating-capacity government**: whichever mayor, ministry, or municipality currently has bandwidth becomes the de facto territorial executive.
- **integration theater**: the statute says the territory plans jointly, but the administrative design still forces transport, land use, investment, and resilience into separate bargaining silos.

## Practical tests

A shared territorial body has an honest administrative home when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it have an identifiable executive center rather than only periodic board gatherings?
2. Does it possess its own staff, records, and administrative memory, rather than depending mainly on borrowed people and files?
3. Can it prepare and execute its core plans and budgets without scattering judgment across unrelated host institutions?
4. Does it retain a minimum internal core in finance, law, procurement, planning, data, and records even when using shared experts or contractors?
5. Are the interfaces with municipalities, agencies, and state offices explicit enough that support does not become shadow command?
6. If it uses secondments, consultants, or project units, are these clearly bounded supports rather than the permanent operating model?
7. If its political or fiscal powers have deepened, has its administrative form been upgraded as well?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a metropolitan or regional body now makes the real decisions of shared territorial government but still relies mainly on rotating chairs, borrowed staff, outsourced judgment, or host-government files to keep itself alive, then it still lacks **an administrative home for shared territorial power** and is drifting toward **government by secondment maze**.
