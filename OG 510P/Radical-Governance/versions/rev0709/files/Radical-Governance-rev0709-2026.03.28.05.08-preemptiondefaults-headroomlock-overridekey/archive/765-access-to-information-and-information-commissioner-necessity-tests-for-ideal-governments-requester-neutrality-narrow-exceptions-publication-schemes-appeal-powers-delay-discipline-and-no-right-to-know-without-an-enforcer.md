# 765 — Access-to-information and information-commissioner necessity tests for ideal governments: requester neutrality, narrow exceptions, publication schemes, appeal powers, delay discipline, and no right to know without an enforcer

## One-line thesis

Ideal governments should treat access to official information as **a standing constitutional right with its own operating system rather than a discretionary transparency favour**: everyone should be able to request records without proving motive, exceptions should be lawful and narrow, publication schemes should proactively release core information, delays should be time-bound and reviewable, and an independent appeal or commissioner lane should be able to order disclosure, monitor compliance, and publish performance data, so the right to know does not dissolve into polite non-response.

## Why this matters

The archive already says a great deal about **public records and archives** (`571`), **open data and public-information reuse** (`578`), **official publications** (`571`), **public communication** (`626`), **administrative procedure** (`573`), and **integrity and anti-corruption** (`499`, `564`). What it still lacked as one compact operating rule was a cleaner answer to a different but adjacent question:

**how does the public obtain information that government did not volunteer, does not want to discuss, or is delaying until political relevance expires?**

Open data and official publication matter, but they are not enough. Governments can publish polished summaries while withholding contracts, notes, emails, logs, impact assessments, instructions, and records that would let people actually test power. A right to request information therefore remains indispensable. Yet that right often fails in practice through broad exceptions, silence, delay, fees, classification sprawl, missing records, or appeal bodies too weak to compel compliance.

Ideal-government design therefore needs a doctrine of **access-to-information necessity**.

## Necessity table

| Necessity test | What counts as proof | Honest design consequence | Default answer if proof is weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Need for requester-neutral access** | information rights should not depend on status, profession, or approved motive | universal right to request official documents | journalist-only or interest-proving access |
| **Need to protect legitimate secrecy without swallowing the rule** | privacy, security, commercial confidentiality, or deliberative space sometimes require restraint | narrow lawful exceptions, harm tests, and public-interest overrides where appropriate | broad discretionary refusal categories |
| **Need to avoid transparency by scavenger hunt only** | core records are repeatedly requested or plainly public-interest material | proactive publication schemes and disclosure logs | request-only transparency |
| **Need for enforceable compliance** | agencies can delay, over-redact, or ignore requests unless checked | independent appeals and commissioner or tribunal powers | internal review only |
| **Need to measure real implementation** | formal rights may exist while performance collapses in practice | statistics, compliance reporting, and decision-time monitoring | no performance visibility |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Make the right universal and motive-blind

Access to information is a public right, not a professional licence. Design should be requester-neutral subject only to practical identity and contact requirements necessary to process the request.

### 2. Define official documents broadly enough to catch real governing records

A right limited to formal final documents is too easy to evade. Coverage should extend to records held by public authorities in whatever format is relevant to governance.

### 3. Keep exceptions specific, harm-based, and reviewable

Secrecy sometimes has a valid home, but limits should be specific, legally typed, and often tested against actual harm and public-interest balancing.

### 4. Use publication schemes and disclosure logs so the right is not purely reactive

If the same information is repeatedly requested, government should learn from the pattern and publish it proactively.

### 5. Enforce time limits and anti-delay discipline

A right that matures after the political moment has passed is only partially a right. Design should therefore set clear acknowledgement and decision clocks, limited extension grounds, and remedies for deemed refusal.

### 6. Provide internal review but do not stop there

First-line reconsideration inside the agency may correct mistakes cheaply, but there should also be a second lane outside the requested body: an information commissioner, tribunal, ombuds-style access body, or court with real powers.

### 7. Give the oversight body powers that match the right

An information commissioner without powers to obtain records, issue binding decisions or equivalent enforceable recommendations, collect statistics, and publish systemic guidance will not reliably change behaviour.

### 8. Align access law with records management, classification, and archives

A request right fails if records do not exist, are badly indexed, are over-classified, or disappear before review.

### 9. Keep fees, format barriers, and portal design from becoming hidden refusal tools

Access should not be frustrated through excessive fees, impossible file formats, inaccessible portals, or response methods that prevent reuse and verification.

### 10. Publish performance data and treat repeated failure as a governance problem

Requests, response times, extension use, appeal outcomes, common refusal grounds, and repeat offenders should be measured and published as constitutional telemetry for the right to know.

## Guardrails

- Do not require requesters to prove a special motive, profession, or personal stake.
- Do not define official documents so narrowly that only polished final outputs are accessible.
- Do not let broad discretionary exceptions swallow the rule.
- Do not treat silence or endless extension as ordinary administration.
- Do not create an information commissioner or appeal body too weak to obtain records or compel correction.

## Failure modes

- **right without an enforcer**: access exists in law but agencies ignore or delay requests with little consequence.
- **delay veto**: requests are answered only after the controversy or decision window has passed.
- **exception creep**: vague exemptions become a general refusal culture.
- **record disappearance**: poor records management makes formal access rights useless.
- **publication theatre**: government points to open-data portals and press releases while withholding the underlying documentary record.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can tell you **what it voluntarily publishes** but cannot also show **who may request inconvenient official records, under what time limits, against what narrow exceptions, through what appeal body, with what binding or equivalent enforcement powers, and with what public performance data**, then it still has **transparency rhetoric without a constitutional right to know**.
