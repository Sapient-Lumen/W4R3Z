# 819 — Boundary-and-membership-chain templates for cross-boundary scope forms: accession gates, territory rules, amendment thickness, exit protocols, dissolution paths, and no map by one-way ratchet

## One-line thesis

Once the archive has said **which cross-boundary institutional form is honest**, **what legitimacy chain belongs to it**, **what fiscal chain belongs to it**, **what administrative spine belongs to it**, **what oversight chain belongs to it**, **what participation chain belongs to it**, and **what information chain belongs to it**, it should also be able to say **what boundary-and-membership chain belongs to it**: thin connectors should use open and reversible participation with no hidden territorial claim, shared-service backbones should declare member classes, accession formulas, notice periods, and unwind rules, bounded functional authorities should use chartered territories plus staged accession and constrained exit, and general-purpose territorial tiers should rely on statutory boundary change with public consultation, transition machinery, and no map by one-way ratchet.

## Why this matters

`790` gives the archive a short answer key for what ideal governments at each scope should generally be. `810` says how to assemble threshold proof before saying that a metropolitan, pooling, regional-platform, or legitimacy-thickness line is really crossed. `812` says which institutional form is honest once that burden is real. `813` says who should sit in the room and which decisions require which legitimacy key. `814` says what money constitution belongs to the form. `815` says where the operator, employer, records home, and continuity spine should live. `816` says what scrutiny and redress chain belongs to the form. `817` says what public-input chain belongs to it. `818` says where the shared facts of the body should actually live. `822` says who owns the strategic assets, bears the liabilities, and takes the residue if the map later changes.

What the recent scope stack still lacked was one compact answer to the next question:

**who is in, what territory counts, how that map may change, and how the body may be entered, exited, split, merged, or buried without constitutional fog?**

That gap matters because many cross-boundary failures are failures of **map honesty**.

One failure is **one-way-ratchet geography**: a metropolitan, county, or regional body may expand easily, but nobody can state the lawful threshold, consultation duty, financial consequences, or exit conditions once the footprint no longer fits reality.

Another failure is **frozen-footprint mismatch**: the labour market, basin, risk shed, or service area has plainly changed, but the body keeps pretending the old territory is still the real one because nobody wants to reopen a politically awkward map.

A third failure is **exit blackmail**: a member can threaten to leave an arrangement that now carries indispensable shared systems, not because exit is really workable, but because the constitution never distinguished opt-in co-ordination from heavy shared operation.

A fourth failure is **charter blur**: territory, membership, remit, borrowing exposure, and voting power all change together through informal accession deals or memoranda that never pass through the amendment gates that should have been triggered.

Official material supports a more disciplined boundary-and-membership ladder. The Council of Europe’s Charter says changes in local authority boundaries shall not be made without prior consultation of the local communities concerned, and it separately protects the right of local authorities to co-operate and form consortia for tasks of common interest. The Charter’s explanatory report likewise treats boundary protection and the right to co-operate as distinct constitutional subjects. The EU regulation on European Groupings of Territorial Cooperation shows what explicit boundary and membership discipline looks like in practice: conventions specify territory, duration, members, organs, dissolution conditions, and amendment procedures; accession of new members follows stated approval routes; and a competent court or authority may wind up a grouping that acts outside its tasks. OECD and World Bank metropolitan-governance material also points the same way: functional urban areas are better captured by city-plus-commuting-zone logic than by inherited administrative lines alone, exact metropolitan delineation matters especially once funding or fiscal-sharing attaches to the area, and metropolitan arrangements tend to evolve as circumstances change. ## Pattern pack

### 1. Use this note after threshold, form, and chain questions are separated

This note does not decide whether a metropolitan, county-pooling, regional-platform, fiscal-floor, or legitimacy threshold has been crossed.

Use `810` for that.

This note does not decide whether the honest institutional answer is a thin connector, shared-service backbone, bounded functional authority, or general-purpose territorial tier.

Use `812` for that.

This note does not decide who sits in the room, what revenue constitution belongs to the body, where the operating spine lives, where scrutiny and remedy sit, what public-input chain belongs to the body, or where the shared facts live.

Use `813`, `814`, `815`, `816`, `817`, `818`, and `822` for those questions. If the remaining live issue is not who belongs or how the map changes but what vote, quorum, unanimity reserve, ratification threshold, or deadlock route those map changes should clear, use `820`.

This note applies after those questions are separated and the remaining live issue is:

- what legal and functional territory the body is actually using,
- who may join or must belong,
- what approval gate exists for accession, merger, split, or territorial change,
- when exit is reversible, staged, or not currently honest,
- and how dissolution, handback, or reconstitution should occur once the body no longer fits the work.

### 2. Distinguish four recurrent boundary-and-membership chains before inventing hybrids

The archive should usually begin with four recognizable chains.

| Form | Default boundary-and-membership chain | What it may legitimately carry | What it must not hide |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Thin connector** | open opt-in participation with reversible project footprints | voluntary participation, observers, rotating working geographies, loose planning perimeters | covert mandatory membership or permanent territorial claim |
| **Shared-service backbone** | declared member list plus accession, withdrawal, and unwind clauses | service-based participation, staged onboarding, notice periods, stranded-cost rules, partial service footprints | host lock-in, silent cross-subsidy, or exit chaos |
| **Bounded functional authority** | chartered service territory plus rule-bound expansion and constrained exit | fixed network or basin footprint, accession tests, task-specific annexes, substitution before withdrawal | territorial creep by memo or instant secession from indispensable shared systems |
| **General-purpose territorial tier** | statutory boundary law plus consultation, transition, and redivision machinery | durable territorial map, formal merger or split procedures, electoral and fiscal transition | one-way annexation ratchet or decorative boundaries detached from lived territory |

The archive should not begin with bespoke map poetry until one of these four chains has first been ruled out honestly.

### 3. Separate six titles even when one legal instrument houses several of them

Later archive work should usually ask six separate questions:

- what the **legal territory** is for powers, finance, and review,
- what the **functional territory** is for planning, comparison, and threshold proof,
- what the **membership classes** are,
- what the **accession and amendment gates** are,
- what the **withdrawal, expulsion, or handback protocol** is,
- and what the **dissolution, merger, split, and asset-liability unwind route** is.

One statute or convention may house several of these titles together. The archive should still separate them explicitly. Silence is how functional catchments are mistaken for democratic territories, or how an open planning perimeter quietly becomes a compulsory fiscal map without a real constitutional step.

### 4. Use open and reversible participation for thin connectors

A thin connector is the right answer when the work is still mainly about:

- alignment,
- common diagnosis,
- relationship-building,
- early agenda-setting,
- or temporary project preparation.

Its boundary-and-membership chain should therefore stay light.

That usually means:

- open or easily joined participation by the relevant governments,
- observer or affiliate status where a full member role would overstate the body,
- problem-specific working geographies rather than one permanently claimed territory,
- easy withdrawal from low-stakes projects,
- and no implication that the connector now owns a tax map, electoral map, or compulsory service perimeter.

A connector becomes constitutionally dishonest once it starts acting as though attendance, map publication, or project coordination has already become a territorial mandate.

### 5. Give shared-service backbones explicit membership economics and unwind rules

A shared-service backbone is still not a general-purpose territorial tier.

But it is thicker than a connector, and its boundary-and-membership chain should say so.

That usually requires:

- a declared member list and service catalogue,
- entry rules for new members,
- notice periods for withdrawal,
- rules for partial participation if not every service is shared by every member,
- asset, liability, reserve, and stranded-cost treatment,
- staff, data, and case handback rules,
- and a continuity plan so one member’s departure does not crash the users of everyone else.

What is not honest here is pretending that a highly pooled backbone is still just a casual club. Once the backbone carries indispensable processing, infrastructure, or specialist capacity, the archive should stop writing withdrawal clauses as if they were costless lifestyle choices.

### 6. Use chartered territory and staged change for bounded functional authorities

A bounded functional authority should usually have a named service territory because one system now genuinely requires it.

Typical examples include:

- a transport trunk,
- a watershed or basin authority,
- a grid,
- a port or corridor,
- or another narrow system whose physical or economic logic plainly crosses ordinary municipal lines.

Its boundary-and-membership chain should therefore normally include:

- a chartered territory or service area,
- objective accession criteria,
- staged territorial expansion rather than casual perimeter drift,
- amendment gates that trigger when territory change also alters power, debt, land leverage, or user burden,
- continuity conditions before any withdrawal becomes effective,
- and a declared wind-down or task-transfer route if the authority ceases to be the honest carrier.

This is the point in the ladder where **exit** and **entry** stop being purely political courtesies. They become questions about continuity, stranded capital, user protection, and the honest allocation of spillovers.

### 7. Treat general-purpose territorial tiers as political maps that require consultation and transition machinery

A general-purpose territorial tier is not merely a service wrapper. It is a political map.

Its boundary-and-membership chain should therefore be the heaviest in the set.

At a minimum, later archive work should expect:

- statutory or constitutional authority for boundary change,
- prior consultation of the affected communities,
- explicit transition boards or cutover machinery where services, elections, or budgets are moving,
- staff, asset, debt, record, and contract allocation,
- electoral-district and representation redesign,
- fiscal equalization or burden-rebalancing treatment where relevant,
- and public clocks for implementation, review, and corrective action.

The archive should be especially suspicious of reforms that speak as though a map can be redrawn first and the political, fiscal, and administrative consequences can be improvised later.

### 8. Thicken the boundary-and-membership chain when five signals appear

The archive should usually move upward from lighter to heavier boundary discipline when several of these signals are present:

1. **funding, fiscal-sharing, or equalization now depends on the map itself**;
2. **exit by one member would impose serious spillovers on others or on users**;
3. **the body carries land, debt, tariff, or binding rule powers tied to territory**;
4. **the functional area has repeatedly outgrown the inherited administrative perimeter**;
5. **membership has become politically consequential enough that observer, affiliate, and full-member status can no longer be blurred honestly**.

These are the moments when a project footprint becomes a constitutional perimeter and the archive should say so openly.

### 9. Require boundary-and-membership fields in applied verdict packets

When `811` is used for a real polity, proposal, or case, the packet should carry a short boundary-and-membership holding whenever the map itself is live.

Typical fields include:

- legal territory,
- functional comparator territory,
- membership basis,
- accession rule,
- amendment gate,
- withdrawal or handback rule,
- dissolution or unwind route,
- and next review clock for the map itself.

That keeps applied judgments from scattering territory, accession, and exit claims across side notes.

## Failure modes

Later archive work should reuse a small boundary-and-membership failure glossary rather than inventing fresh prose each time.

- **one-way-ratchet geography** — expansion is easy, contraction or redesign is constitutionally foggy.
- **frozen-footprint mismatch** — the real functional area has changed but the legal map has not been reviewed.
- **exit blackmail** — a member threatens departure from an arrangement that has become too indispensable for casual exit.
- **charter blur** — accession or perimeter change quietly alters power, burden, or debt without triggering the right amendment gate.
- **permanent provisional geography** — a supposedly temporary coordination perimeter survives so long that it becomes a shadow constitution.

## Compression rule for the archive

If later archive work cannot answer **who is in, what territory counts, how the map changes, and how the body may be exited or wound down** in one short paragraph, then the body’s constitutional perimeter is still too vague to count as honestly designed.
