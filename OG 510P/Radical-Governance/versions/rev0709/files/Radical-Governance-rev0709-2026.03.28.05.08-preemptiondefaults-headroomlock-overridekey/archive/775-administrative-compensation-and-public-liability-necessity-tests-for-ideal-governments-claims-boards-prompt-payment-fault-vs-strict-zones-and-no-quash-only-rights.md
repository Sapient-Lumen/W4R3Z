# 775 — Administrative compensation and public-liability necessity tests for ideal governments: claims boards, prompt payment, fault-vs-strict zones, and no quash-only rights

## One-line thesis

Ideal governments should pair review of unlawful or harmful public action with **a visible public-liability and compensation constitution**: when administrative decisions, omissions, delays, operational failures, dangerous public activities, or non-execution of judgments cause loss, there should be a low-friction route to reparation — by compensation or another appropriate remedy — with typed liability rules, settlement or claims-board capacity, prompt payment, and budget discipline, so people do not win legality on paper while carrying the actual loss alone.

## Why this matters

The archive already covers **remedy stacks** (`494`), **administrative procedure** (`573`, `719`, `720`, `723`), **private-law claims against shared regimes** (`688`), **ombuds** (`772`), and **administrative appeals and tribunals** (`773`). What it still lacked as one compact operating rule was a better answer to another recurring rule-of-law question:

**after the person proves that the administration acted unlawfully, unreasonably, or harmfully, how does actual repair happen?**

Too many systems still split review from reparation in ways that look sophisticated in doctrine and cruel in use. A person can win annulment but not compensation. A tribunal can identify illegality but has no payment power. An agency may be required to reconsider but the claimant must then begin separate civil litigation to recover obvious losses. A judgment for money is obtained but not paid promptly. Or the state treats every request for compensation as a discretionary favor even when the person has already established that public action caused legally cognizable damage.

That gap matters because **quash-only legality** is often a half-remedy. If the benefit was wrongfully withheld for months, if a business was closed under an unlawful order, if property was damaged by public works, if enforcement delayed past usefulness, if detention, exclusion, seizure, demolition, or official misinformation caused concrete loss, a merely declaratory victory may be too late or too thin.

Ideal-government design therefore needs a doctrine of **public-liability necessity**. The point is not to impose one universal strict-liability rule on every administrative act. It is to ensure that the state has a visible answer to when it must repair damage it caused, how the claim is brought, whether the claimant must relitigate illegality, who may settle and pay, and how valid compensation orders are executed without a second war.

The live design question is not whether some remote constitutional principle says the state may sometimes owe damages. It is whether the polity has a usable architecture for **repair after unlawful or harmful administration**, rather than leaving people with righteousness and receipts.

## Necessity table

| Necessity test | What counts as proof | Honest design consequence | Default answer if proof is weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Need for reparation beyond annulment or reconsideration** | unlawful or harmful public action can cause concrete monetary or material loss | create a public-liability route to compensation or equivalent reparation | stop at quashing, apology, or rehearing |
| **Need to type liability grounds honestly** | some harms arise from unlawful decisions, some from negligence, some from dangerous public activity, some from delay or non-execution | distinguish fault, unlawfulness, strict-liability, and special-risk zones by field | one vague compensation promise hides inconsistent outcomes |
| **Need to avoid a second-lawsuit tax after illegality is already established** | claimants first win review, then must relitigate liability from zero | give preclusive or presumptive effect to prior annulment or judgment where appropriate | require full duplicate litigation to turn legality into payment |
| **Need for low-friction claims handling where court process is disproportionate** | many valid claims are modest, repetitive, or operationally routine | provide settlement authority, claims boards, or administrative compensation lanes with review rights | force all compensation claims into ordinary high-cost litigation |
| **Need for real execution of compensation orders** | the state delays or evades payment after liability is recognized | require prompt payment, interest, budget lanes, and execution discipline | compensation right that stalls at treasury or agency level |
| **Need to preserve fairness where public activity imposes concentrated losses** | dangerous, exceptional, or socially useful state action burdens a few people unusually heavily | allow strict or special reparation rules where justice so requires | injured persons bear public burdens alone unless they prove fault in detail |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Pair legality review with a repair lane from the beginning

A modern administrative-justice system should say at the outset what happens if the claimant wins. Some cases need only annulment or reconsideration. Others obviously need money, restoration, replacement, or another material remedy. Review architecture that treats this question as an afterthought usually under-remedies by default.

### 2. Type public liability by harm source instead of pretending one theory fits every case

Ideal governments should distinguish at least:

- unlawful decisions or orders,
- procedural illegality causing loss,
- negligent operational acts or omissions,
- dangerous or exceptional public works or activities,
- wrongful delay or non-response,
- failure to execute judicial or administrative decisions,
- and special fields such as detention, public health, infrastructure, security, or regulatory seizure where the liability logic may differ.

### 3. Do not make people re-prove what a prior review body has already established

When a court or tribunal has already annulled a decision, found unlawful delay, or held that the administration failed to execute a judgment, the compensation lane should not force the person to start from epistemic zero. Causation and quantum may still need proof, but the system should not charge a second full legality suit as the price of repair.

### 4. Provide an administrative settlement or claims-board lane where that is more proportionate than full litigation

Routine property damage, service interruption, road-work loss, operational mistakes, small-value administrative harms, and comparable cases often justify an initial claims authority or settlement board. That lane should be reasoned, reviewable, and bounded by clocks. It should not become an executive courtesy desk immune from appeal.

### 5. Separate legal entitlement from ex gratia mercy

Governments will sometimes make humanitarian or discretionary payments for reasons broader than legal liability. That can be legitimate. But it should remain visibly distinct from claims grounded in legal entitlement. Ex gratia discretion must not become the hidden substitute for a public-liability system.

### 6. Require prompt payment, interest, and a budget home once compensation is due

An unpaid compensation order is a continuation of the wrong. Good design therefore names who pays, from what budget or reserve, within what time, with what interest or indexation if delayed, and what enforcement follows if the administration still does not comply.

### 7. Allow full reparation where justice requires, while staying proportionate about proof and causation

The archive should prefer systems that can compensate direct loss honestly and define the boundaries of indirect, future, dignitary, or diffuse loss openly. The key is not maximal generosity. It is a visible rule on what counts and why.

### 8. Preserve strict-liability or special-sacrifice logic where public burdens are concentrated unfairly

In some settings it is unjust to demand that the claimant prove fault by the state in the ordinary way. Dangerous infrastructure, emergency destruction, compulsory sacrifice for public safety, or comparable burdens may warrant compensation because it would be manifestly unfair for a small number of people to carry a public burden alone.

### 9. Publish aggregate compensation data so recurring harms become governance intelligence

Compensation systems should record claim type, acceptance and rejection rates, delay, payment time, recurring agencies or risk sources, and use of settlement versus adjudication. Otherwise the state pays to repeat the same mistakes in secrecy.

### 10. Keep recourse against individual officials typed and separate from the claimant’s repair

The person harmed should not have to wait for disciplinary or recourse proceedings against officials before receiving reparation. Official accountability may matter, but it is a different lane from the claimant’s remedy.

## Guardrails

- Do not assume annulment or rehearing is enough when the person has suffered concrete loss.
- Do not force every compensation claim into ordinary civil litigation if a typed administrative or claims-board route would be more proportionate.
- Do not make prior findings of unlawfulness practically irrelevant to later compensation claims.
- Do not blur legal entitlement into discretionary ex gratia payment.
- Do not let treasury, agency, or budget mechanics hollow out final compensation orders.
- Do not design public-liability rules so narrowly that concentrated public burdens are simply socialized onto unlucky individuals.

## Failure modes

- **quash-only rights**: the claimant proves unlawfulness but receives no realistic material repair.
- **second-lawsuit tax**: a person must relitigate from scratch to turn a successful review into compensation.
- **mercy-state compensation**: government presents discretionary grace as if it were legal remedy.
- **unpaid-judgment administration**: compensation is awarded but not executed promptly.
- **fault-proof trap**: liability rules are framed so narrowly that harms from dangerous or exceptional public action are practically unrecoverable.
- **claims-board theater**: an administrative settlement lane exists but has no clocks, reasons, review, or payment discipline.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can tell you **that unlawful or harmful administration may be challenged** but cannot also show **when compensation or other reparation is available, which liability theories apply by field, whether prior illegality findings matter in later compensation, what low-friction claims or settlement lane exists, who pays valid claims and how quickly, and how recurring compensation patterns feed prevention**, then it still has **review rhetoric without a public-liability constitution**.
