# 705 — Mode packets for lane-typed government fields: normal, degraded, emergency, recovery, handback clocks, and no permanent government by exception

## One-line thesis

Once the archive has lane packets, interface packets, and a field control plane, it should also publish one **mode packet** for the field — naming normal, degraded, emergency, and recovery operation, the triggers for entering each mode, what powers and duties change, what rights and service floors remain non-negotiable, and what handback clock returns the stack to ordinary routing — so a stressed system does not quietly adopt emergency pathways as its permanent constitution.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a usable ladder for ideal-government-by-scope work.

`699` gives the reusable scope spine. `700` gives the minimum design packet. `701` gives the rivalry matrix. `702` forces mixed fields to be split into constitutional lanes before scope choice. `703` makes the seams between lanes explicit. `704` adds the field-level control plane.

What the archive still lacked was a compact doctrine for **operating mode**.

That gap matters because distributed government often looks most constitutional in calm weather and least constitutional under pressure. A field can have honest lane packets, decent handoffs, and a visible control plane — then quietly abandon all three the moment demand spikes, infrastructure breaks, a hazard hits, staff collapse, or fiscal stress forces triage. The public route changes, standards loosen, ordinary review clocks stall, and exception queues become the real intake path. Months later the emergency arrangement is still doing the work, but nobody has admitted that the field is now being governed under a different constitution.

The archive therefore needs one smaller object after the control-plane packet:

**a mode packet that says how the field changes state without changing title by habit.**

This is not a general theory of emergency powers. The archive already has strong work on emergency homes (`495`, `622`), continuity and fallback (`408`, `420`, `646`), public communication (`626`), and stack repair or handback (`605`, `631`, `642`, `643`).

What is needed here is the shorter field-level rule that later ideal-government notes can cite when they need to say:

- what counts as ordinary operation;
- what counts as degraded but still rights-bearing operation;
- when true emergency routing is lawful;
- what recovery mode is for once acute command has passed;
- and how the stack visibly returns to normal before the exception lane becomes the real government.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate **scope** from **mode**

Scope says which government or lane owns a function. Mode says **how that already-assigned field is operating right now**.

The archive should keep those questions separate. A municipality does not become a regional government merely because the field is in emergency mode. A metro or national lane does not gain permanent title merely because it temporarily leads during a crisis. A control plane does not become the sovereign owner of every lane merely because it convenes more often under stress.

Without this separation, temporary operating shifts get misread as constitutional settlement.

### 2. Prefer one small finite mode set

Most fields do not need a bespoke vocabulary of a dozen situational states. The archive should usually prefer four declared modes:

- **normal** — ordinary routing, ordinary service standards, ordinary review clocks;
- **degraded** — the field is functioning but below ordinary capacity or performance, with fallback rules now active;
- **emergency** — acute command, extraordinary prioritization, or cross-lane override powers are temporarily active because ordinary routing cannot safely hold;
- **recovery** — acute command has ended, but restoration, backlog burn-down, repair sequencing, and controlled handback are still underway.

A field may add another mode where genuinely necessary, but the burden should be on clarity, not customization.

### 3. Every mode should have one visible entry trigger and one lawful declaring lane

A mode should not begin because everybody can feel that conditions have worsened. The packet should name:

- the observable threshold for entry,
- who may declare the mode,
- whether consultation or notice is required,
- how the declaration is recorded,
- and who can challenge or review it.

Typical triggers include backlog beyond a rights floor, infrastructure loss, staffing collapse, hazard declaration, repeated handoff failure, or critical capacity exhaustion across more than one lane. What matters is not the exact trigger class but that the field does not change mode by folklore.

### 4. Rights floors and service floors must be typed by mode, not abandoned by mood

A field may have to change throughput, prioritization, timing, documentation detail, or sequencing under stress. But the archive should force the note to say what **does not** disappear.

A normal mode packet should therefore name, by mode:

- the minimum rights floor that still binds,
- the minimum service floor that still binds,
- what forms of triage are allowed,
- what reasons and notice still have to be given,
- and what complaints, review, or safeguarding routes remain live even when ordinary throughput fails.

Otherwise “degraded mode” becomes polite language for lawless rationing.

### 5. Mode changes should alter routes explicitly, not only backstage priorities

When a field changes mode, the people inside it need to know:

- whether the ordinary front door still works,
- whether an emergency intake route is now primary,
- whether some requests are paused or merged,
- whether clocks have changed,
- whether particular handoffs are suspended,
- and who is currently responsible for cross-lane prioritization.

The mode packet should therefore publish one visible public route change statement, not only a backstage operating instruction.

### 6. Degraded mode is a constitutional state of its own, not just a softer emergency

Many real public failures are not full emergencies. They are prolonged degraded states: staffing shortages, IT outages, supply interruption, damaged estates, funding delay, or unresolved surge pressure.

The archive should therefore treat **degraded mode** as a first-class design object. It should say:

- what fallback path now governs,
- what work is paused or narrowed,
- how exceptions are queued,
- what additional reporting the control plane needs,
- and what threshold forces a decision either upward into emergency mode or downward into restored normal service.

Without a declared degraded mode, fields lurch between denial and pseudo-emergency.

### 7. Emergency mode should be narrow, clocked, and review-heavy

The archive should mistrust emergency routing that arrives with no expiration discipline.

An emergency mode packet should therefore name:

- the extraordinary powers or compressed approvals that activate,
- the lanes that may be bypassed and the lanes that may not,
- the logging and disclosure duty for exceptional acts,
- the renewal clock,
- the review lane,
- and the public communication duty while the mode remains active.

Emergency mode exists to preserve lawful governability under acute stress, not to provide a convenient faster constitution.

### 8. Recovery mode should be about restoration and backlog honesty, not celebratory narrative

A field often leaves acute emergency command before it has actually returned to ordinary life. Assets remain damaged, temporary facilities persist, backlogs have widened, staff are exhausted, and exceptional routing still shapes access.

The archive should therefore treat **recovery mode** as a distinct packet. It should usually name:

- the backlog classes created by the emergency,
- the restoration sequence for ordinary routes,
- the residual temporary powers that remain,
- the capital or repair dependencies still blocking normal service,
- the review rhythm for ending recovery mode,
- and the criteria for handback to normal.

Otherwise governments narrate “back to normal” while operating through a lingering exception constitution.

### 9. Handback should be a declared event with its own clock

The archive should not allow modes to end by collective shrug. A field should publish:

- who declares handback,
- what evidence shows that handback is honest,
- what temporary powers expire immediately,
- which residual tasks move into ordinary lanes,
- what remains on the exception queue after handback,
- and what post-event review or after-action work is still owed.

This is the field-level version of the archive’s wider handback and reactivation discipline: temporary routes should leave cleanly or admit that they are no longer temporary.

### 10. Keep one mode history in the same public stack memory as every other scope event

Mode change should update the same visible constitutional memory surfaces the archive already uses. At minimum, the field should be able to show:

- current mode,
- declaration date and authority,
- operative service and rights floors,
- current public route map,
- active exceptions,
- next review date,
- and handback status.

Otherwise the live field will be governed in one mode while the public surfaces still describe another.

## Compact mode packet

A normal-length mode object should usually fit in one table-like block:

| Field | Modes | Entry trigger | Declaring lane | What changes | Non-negotiable floor | Public route | Handback clock |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Housing** | normal / degraded / emergency / recovery | emergency shelter saturation, unlawful temporary-accommodation backlog, severe weather mass displacement | municipal lead with metro or national escalation where threshold is crossed | intake triage tightens, overflow placement rules activate, capital and safeguarding exceptions queue centrally | anti-street-homelessness floor, safeguarding, written reason for displacement triage, live complaints route | ordinary local intake unless emergency shelter route is formally activated and published | weekly review in emergency; fortnightly recovery review until ordinary placement and appeals clocks are restored |
| **Transport** | normal / degraded / emergency / recovery | network outage, corridor closure, operator failure, infrastructure hazard | metropolitan or regional transport lead; national declaration if strategic corridors fail | service hierarchy shifts, temporary timetable and traffic-priority rules activate, emergency communications cadence increases | life-safety duties, accessibility minimum, public disruption notices, visible complaint and refund route | normal operator and city channels until one emergency travel route is declared and mirrored everywhere | daily emergency review; monthly recovery review until temporary routings and emergency contracts retire |
| **Care** | normal / degraded / emergency / recovery | unsafe discharge pressure, staffing collapse, infectious surge, failed provider continuity | regional care lead with municipal and national reporting duties | discharge routing compresses, fallback providers activate, safeguarding queue gets priority, non-urgent assessments may sequence later | safeguarding, continuity for highest-risk users, reasoned prioritization, live redress route | usual local entry plus declared urgent-care route if fallback scheduling is active | twice-weekly emergency review; fortnightly recovery review until backlog and temporary placements are cleared |

The archive should treat this as the normal length unless the field is unusually hard.

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse a temporary operating mode with a permanent rescoping of the field.
- Do not let degraded mode become an unreviewed holding pattern.
- Do not let emergency mode bypass logging, disclosure, review, or public route clarity.
- Do not declare recovery complete while exception queues, temporary powers, or backlog classes still govern real access.
- Do not let handback happen invisibly.
- Do not allow repeated mode activation to substitute for the deeper constitutional repair the field actually needs.

## Failure modes

- **exception permanence** — a temporary routing arrangement becomes the field’s real operating constitution by elapsed time.
- **degraded denial** — the field is visibly failing but no degraded mode is declared, so rights and fallback rules become opaque.
- **emergency laundering** — extraordinary powers or bypasses are used for convenience rather than acute necessity.
- **recovery theater** — officials declare normality while backlog, triage, and temporary authority still shape who gets served.
- **handback blur** — nobody can say when the field returned to ordinary routing, which powers expired, or what residue remained.
- **repeat activation without repair** — the field cycles through emergency declarations because the underlying structural defect is never fixed.

## Practical tests

A field is using a mode packet honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can the field distinguish scope ownership from operating mode without smuggling permanent rescoping in through crisis language?
2. Is there a small declared mode set rather than ad hoc institutional mood labels?
3. Does each mode have a visible trigger, lawful declaring lane, review route, and renewal or review clock?
4. Are rights floors, service floors, and triage limits named by mode rather than abandoned when pressure rises?
5. Can the public see what route changed, what clocks changed, and what emergency or degraded intake path is now in force?
6. Is degraded mode treated as a real constitutional state rather than an unnamed pre-emergency fog?
7. Does recovery mode name backlog, restoration sequence, and residual temporary powers instead of pretending the emergency simply ended?
8. Is handback a declared event with evidence, expiry of temporary powers, and an after-action or repair route?
9. Does the live stack memory show the current mode and the history of recent mode shifts?
10. If the field keeps returning to degraded or emergency mode, is that generating structural repair rather than normalizing exception rule?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a later note presents a distributed ideal government for a whole field but cannot say **what the field’s normal, degraded, emergency, and recovery modes are; who declares them; what rights and service floors still bind; what public route changes; and how handback returns the stack to ordinary routing**, then it still has **lanes, seams, and a control plane without an operating-mode constitution**, and its design remains one level short of being honestly governable under stress.
