# 821 — Dispute-resolution-chain templates for cross-boundary scope forms: negotiation ladders, mediation, expert determination, jurisdiction maps, continuity orders, and no heavy power by hostage dispute

## One-line thesis

Once the archive has said **which cross-boundary institutional form is honest**, **what legitimacy chain belongs to it**, **what fiscal chain belongs to it**, **what administrative spine belongs to it**, **what oversight chain belongs to it**, **what participation chain belongs to it**, **what information chain belongs to it**, **what boundary-and-membership chain belongs to it**, and **what decision-rule chain belongs to it**, it should also be able to say **what dispute-resolution chain belongs to it**: thin connectors should rely on recorded negotiation and visible member-side escalation, shared-service backbones should use negotiation-first ladders with mediation, technical determination, continuity protection, and public-law endpoints, bounded functional authorities should publish jurisdiction maps and interim continuity orders, and general-purpose territorial tiers should rely on ordinary justice and constitutional routes rather than permanent bargaining by hostage dispute.

## Why this matters

`790` gives the archive a short answer key for what ideal governments at each scope should generally be. `810` says how to assemble threshold proof before saying that a metropolitan, pooling, regional-platform, fiscal-floor, or legitimacy-thickness line is really crossed. `812` says which institutional form is honest once that burden is real. `813` says who should sit in the room and which decisions require which legitimacy key. `814` says what money constitution belongs to the form. `815` says where the operator, employer, records home, and continuity spine should live. `816` says what scrutiny and redress chain belongs to the form. `817` says what public-input chain belongs to it. `818` says where the shared facts of the body should live. `819` says how territory and membership should be entered, changed, exited, and unwound. `820` says what vote, quorum, ratification, deadlock, and emergency grammar belongs to the body. `822` says who owns the assets, carries the liabilities, and takes the handback if those conflicts are really balance-sheet conflicts rather than pure vote or venue questions.

What the recent scope stack still lacked was one compact answer to the next practical question:

**once those institutions disagree, who hears what kind of dispute, how fast, under which law, with which interim continuity rule, and with what public endpoint?**

That gap matters because many cross-boundary failures are failures of **conflict routing honesty**.

One failure is **hostage dispute governance**: a member or host can block continuity, records release, shared-service performance, or cost reallocation because the constitution never said what route exists between negotiation failure and final court judgment.

A second failure is **jurisdiction blur**: user complaints, inter-member cost fights, technical-performance disputes, tariff objections, boundary conflicts, and competence challenges are all thrown into one vague bucket called “dispute resolution”, even though they need different ladders.

A third failure is **forum shopping by strength**: the strongest member, host, ministry, or operator picks whichever venue best preserves leverage because nobody can state the lawful sequence among negotiation, mediation, expert determination, regulator, ombuds, court, or constitutional review.

A fourth failure is **continuity blackout**: an essential service, network, or pooled back-office function is allowed to wobble while the parties argue because there is no declared interim order, caretaker rule, or stay discipline.

The older `492` note already says how a wider polity should route competence conflicts across levels of government in general constitutional terms. This note is narrower and more operational. It gives the archive one compact rule for how an already identified cross-boundary body should route its own disputes once the form, composition, finance, administration, oversight, map, and vote grammar are already known.

Official material supports a more disciplined ladder. The Council of Europe’s Charter gives local authorities a right of recourse to a judicial remedy in order to secure the free exercise of their powers and respect for local self-government. The EGTC regulation shows that serious cross-border bodies should not rely on vague custom alone: it gives third parties judicial recourse, sets jurisdiction rules for disputes involving an EGTC, and expects the convention and statutes to specify core constitutional machinery. OECD work on contracts across levels of government treats contracts as governance frameworks that define mutual obligations and enforcement mechanisms rather than aspirational memoranda. OECD work on inter-municipal shared services likewise stresses that agreements should specify rights, obligations, financing, supervision, dispute-resolution, and termination rules, and its annexed model agreements illustrate negotiation-first dispute ladders with administrative-law endpoints. The archive should compress those lessons into one rule: **treat dispute routing as constitutional infrastructure, not as what the strongest actor improvises after relations sour.**

## Pattern pack

### 1. Use this note after form, map, and vote questions are separated

This note does not decide whether a metropolitan, county-pooling, regional-platform, fiscal-floor, or legitimacy-thickness threshold has been crossed.

Use `810` for that.

This note does not decide whether the honest answer is a thin connector, shared-service backbone, bounded functional authority, or general-purpose territorial tier.

Use `812` for that.

This note does not decide where scrutiny, complaint handling, ombuds, audit, or final judicial remedy generally belong.

Use `816` for that.

This note does not decide what vote, quorum, unanimity reserve, ratification class, deadlock route, or emergency exception belongs to the body.

Use `820` for that.

If the live question is instead a broader constitutional conflict over powers, duties, or fiscal supremacy among tiers, use `492`. If the live question is not primarily about venue or escalation but about who owns the assets, who bears the residual liabilities, or what handback map must survive the conflict, use `822` alongside this note. If the live question is instead whether the conflict exists because procurement, concession, franchise, PPP, or managed-service instruments have exceeded the honest commercial perimeter of the body, use `823` alongside this note so the archive does not mistake a contracting-boundary failure for a mere dispute-routing failure. If the live question is instead whether the underlying conflict is really about who may permit, inspect, direct, suspend, or sanction in the body’s name, use `824` alongside this note so the archive does not mistake a coercive-boundary failure for a mere venue problem.

This note applies after those questions are separated and the remaining live issue is:

- what types of disputes the body should distinguish,
- which route each class should first enter,
- when negotiation must give way to mediation, technical determination, regulator, ombuds, tribunal, or court,
- what stays or continuity orders exist while the dispute runs,
- and how outcomes feed back into the public constitutional map rather than disappearing into private compromise.

### 2. Distinguish four recurrent dispute-resolution chains before inventing hybrids

The archive should usually begin with four recognizable chains.

| Form | Default dispute-resolution chain | What it may legitimately carry | What it must not hide |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Thin connector** | recorded negotiation plus member-side escalation | agenda, alignment, and voluntary co-ordination disputes among principals | fake binding arbitration over a body that still lacks real authority |
| **Shared-service backbone** | negotiation-first ladder plus mediation, technical determination, continuity protection, and administrative or court endpoint | service-level, cost-allocation, records-transfer, host-performance, and withdrawal disputes | service hostage tactics or informal settlement by operator leverage |
| **Bounded functional authority** | published jurisdiction map plus user complaint, technical-review, regulator or ombuds lane, and ordinary court route with interim continuity orders | tariff, access, safety, procurement, performance, and inter-member disputes inside a real system | forum blur that lets the board or host absorb every dispute class |
| **General-purpose territorial tier** | ordinary administrative justice, sectoral regulators where relevant, constitutional review for competence questions, and legislated continuity rules | public-law disputes that belong to a real territorial government | permanent bargaining constitutions or bespoke veto diplomacy in place of law |

The archive should not begin with bespoke grievance poetry until one of these four chains has first been ruled out honestly.

### 3. Separate five dispute classes before choosing the route

Later archive work should usually classify the dispute before it argues about venue.

1. **user-and-case disputes** — service denial, case routing, access, tariff application, or maladministration affecting users;
2. **member-and-burden disputes** — cost allocation, contribution formulas, records duties, staff transfer, reserve use, or host obligations among participating governments;
3. **technical-and-performance disputes** — service-standard interpretation, operational metrics, asset condition, engineering questions, or contract-performance arguments;
4. **constitutional-perimeter disputes** — competence, remit, boundary, accession, withdrawal, amendment, or dissolution conflicts;
5. **continuity-and-emergency disputes** — whether an interim order, temporary continuation, or caretaker act is lawful while the primary conflict remains unresolved.

The archive should not let one generic clause pretend to cover all five classes equally well.

### 4. Thin connectors should use recorded negotiation and visible non-binding escalation

A thin connector is honest when it remains a co-ordination venue rather than a shadow sovereign.

Its dispute-resolution chain should therefore stay light.

That usually means:

- disputes are first logged as disagreements among principals rather than as quasi-adjudication inside the connector itself,
- negotiation occurs on the record with visible dissent,
- unresolved binding consequences go back to the member bodies, ministers, or councils that actually hold authority,
- and any eventual legal dispute is brought through the principal that owns the duty rather than through connector theater.

What the archive should reject here is the move where a connector with no real mandate pretends it can privately arbitrate binding territorial or fiscal outcomes for everyone else.

### 5. Shared-service backbones need negotiation-first ladders, technical determination, and continuity protection

A shared-service backbone exists to pool machinery while member governments retain political title to the duty.

Its dispute-resolution chain should therefore usually include several visible rungs.

The first rung is **structured inter-member negotiation** with a short clock and a written issue statement.

The second rung is **mediation or facilitated settlement** where relationship repair matters or the disagreement is about burden sharing, service expectations, or transition sequencing.

The third rung is **technical or expert determination** where the real issue is service-standard compliance, asset condition, data integrity, accounting treatment, or another bounded operational question that is poorly handled by pure political bargaining.

The fourth rung is a **public-law endpoint** — regulator, ombuds, tribunal, administrative court, or ordinary court depending on the legal design — where one party claims the arrangement has breached law, rights, or lawful authority.

Across all four rungs, the archive should usually require a continuity rule that keeps essential service, records access, payroll, safety, or live cases from collapsing while the fight is being resolved.

What is not honest here is treating indispensable pooled machinery as though it can be shut off to gain bargaining leverage.

### 6. Bounded functional authorities need published jurisdiction maps and interim continuity orders

A bounded functional authority becomes the honest answer when one real cross-boundary system requires continuous operation, asset stewardship, or binding technical decisions.

Its dispute-resolution chain should therefore be heavier than a backbone’s.

Typical honest ingredients include:

- a published map of which disputes go to internal reconsideration, technical review, ombuds, regulator, tribunal, court, or constitutional venue,
- explicit separation between user grievance routes and intergovernmental or member disputes,
- expert determination for narrow operational or engineering issues where legal finality still remains available,
- interim continuity orders or stays that protect users, safety, or network integrity while the primary dispute runs,
- and publication or registry discipline so outcomes feed back into the body’s constitutional memory.

The archive should be especially suspicious when a bounded authority has real tariff, corridor, land, or safety power but no declared route for urgent interim protection.

### 7. General-purpose territorial tiers should rely on ordinary justice rather than permanent bargaining constitutions

Once the archive has concluded that the honest answer is a general-purpose territorial tier, it should usually stop searching for bespoke dispute shortcuts as the primary route.

At that point the dispute-resolution chain should normally rely on:

- ordinary administrative complaint and review,
- sectoral regulators or specialized tribunals where the field calls for them,
- ombuds and legality routes for maladministration,
- constitutional or supreme-court review where competence or fundamental constitutional questions arise,
- and legislated interim-relief and continuity rules rather than improvised hostage bargaining among subunits.

Negotiation among governments may still be useful. It should not become a shadow substitute for public-law dispute routes once the body is a real tier.

### 8. Require explicit clocks, stay rules, and publication of outcome class

Every serious cross-boundary body should be able to answer five practical questions.

First, **how quickly must the parties move from intake to negotiation?**

Second, **what converts failed negotiation into mediation, technical determination, regulator review, or court?**

Third, **what stays, preservation orders, or continuity commands protect users, records, assets, and safety while the dispute is live?**

Fourth, **what outcome classes exist — settlement, mediated agreement, expert determination, regulator order, court judgment, constitutional ruling, or handback command?**

Fifth, **how does the outcome feed back into charters, standing orders, service agreements, or public guidance so the same dispute does not recur as pure memory loss?**

The archive should reject dispute clauses that can describe escalation only after service has already degraded or whose settlement path disappears into uninspectable side letters.

### 9. Require dispute-resolution fields in applied verdict packets

When `811` is used for a real polity, proposal, or case, the packet should carry a short dispute-resolution holding whenever conflict routing is live.

Typical fields include:

- dispute classes distinguished,
- first-entry venue,
- mediation or expert-determination rung,
- public-law or court endpoint,
- interim continuity or stay rule,
- publication or precedent route,
- and next review clock for whether the current ladder still matches the gravity of the body.

That keeps applied judgments from scattering conflict routes, stay powers, and continuity rules across side notes.

## Failure modes

Later archive work should reuse a small dispute-routing failure glossary rather than inventing fresh prose each time.

- **hostage dispute governance** — one member, host, or operator can degrade continuity to improve bargaining leverage.
- **jurisdiction blur** — user, technical, fiscal, and constitutional disputes all disappear into one vague clause.
- **forum shopping by strength** — the strongest actor picks whichever venue best preserves leverage because the ladder is unclear.
- **continuity blackout** — essential service, records, or safety lapse while the parties argue because no interim order exists.
- **private peace without public memory** — the parties settle, but the outcome never updates the public constitutional map, guidance, or charter text.

## Compression rule for the archive

If later archive work cannot answer **what kind of dispute this is, where it enters, how it escalates, what stays live while it runs, and where final lawful resolution sits** in one short paragraph, then the body’s conflict constitution is still too vague to count as honestly designed.
