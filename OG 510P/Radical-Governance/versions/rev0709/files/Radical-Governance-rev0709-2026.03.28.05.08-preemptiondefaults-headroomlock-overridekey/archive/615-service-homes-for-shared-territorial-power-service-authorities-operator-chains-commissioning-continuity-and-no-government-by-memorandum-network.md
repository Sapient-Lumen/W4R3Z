# 615 — Service homes for shared territorial power: service authorities, operator chains, commissioning, continuity, and no government by memorandum network

## One-line thesis

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, it needs an honest **service home** as well as a democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, participatory, and knowledge one: a visible split among authority, operator, and regulator; a named territorial service map showing what stays local and what becomes shared; lawful commissioning, franchising, or direct-operation routes where system services are real; service standards, continuity duties, and fallback rules that survive contracts and politics; and clear public entry and complaint handoffs — so the territory stops being governed through memoranda, host-city favors, improvised consortia, and operator clubs.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a substantial constitutional-upgrade ladder for shared territorial power. It can say when co-operation becomes government, what democratic form should follow, what fiscal base is needed, what operating core is needed, what accountability and public-law basis are required, how the tier should be wired into the wider intergovernmental order, how residents should participate directly, and what common knowledge spine the territory needs.

What it still lacked was the **service analogue**. A metropolitan or regional tier may have an assembly, budget, staff, legal personality, participation routes, and a respectable observatory, yet still remain half-built if nobody can plainly say who actually plans, commissions, regulates, delivers, and keeps essential shared services running across the territory.

That gap matters because many of the reasons for creating shared territorial government are operational rather than merely symbolic. The metro or region is expected to make transport work as one system, align waste or water across boundaries, co-ordinate emergency communications, pool specialist services, integrate access to social or administrative services, or manage networks whose failure spills across many municipalities at once. If the governing tier has no honest service home, real power migrates to host municipalities, incumbent operators, concessionaires, or informal mayoral bargains.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine for the **service home of shared territorial government**: when a real metropolitan or regional tier must stop living through memoranda and borrowed operators and acquire a visible territorial service constitution of its own.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate the authority, the operator, and the regulator before anything else

A real shared territorial tier should be able to state which body sets strategy and service obligations, which body or bodies actually operate services or assets, and which body regulates, inspects, licenses, or adjudicates. The archive should reject arrangements in which the same organisation quietly writes the service rules, chooses the operator, runs the service, grades its own performance, and hears complaints about itself.

### 2. Publish a territorial service map that says what stays local and what becomes shared

Not every service should be metropolitan or regional just because the polity exists. The archive should therefore prefer a visible service map naming:

- services that remain wholly local,
- services delivered locally under shared standards,
- services commissioned or co-ordinated at metro or regional level,
- services directly operated by the shared tier or its entities,
- and services that remain state or national even when delivered in the territory.

Without that map, the shared tier drifts into service authority by accumulation.

### 3. Use one lead service authority where the system must actually behave as one system

Some cross-boundary services cannot work honestly if every municipality retains a veto or runs its own disconnected operating logic. The archive should therefore prefer one visible lead authority where the territory needs one network logic, one timetable logic, one access standard, one emergency doctrine, one strategic procurement route, or one capital-maintenance sequence.

This does not mean one monopoly operator for everything. It means one place where the system bargain is made and can be defended publicly.

### 4. Give the tier lawful delivery instruments, not only aspirations

A shared tier that is meant to run real services needs lawful delivery routes. Depending on the service, that may mean direct operation, municipal shared service centres, authorities with arm’s-length operators, concessions, franchises, framework contracts, or tightly bounded co-operative delivery.

The archive should reject “service strategies” that name goals but leave the tier unable to commission, franchise, procure, enforce service obligations, take over a failing route, or preserve continuity when a contractor or partner exits.

### 5. Keep front doors near people even when the operating system is shared

Shared territorial government often exists because the back end should be integrated while public access still needs local presence. The archive should therefore prefer designs in which service authority and system planning may be metropolitan or regional, but people can still enter through near-to-person channels: municipal counters, assisted-digital points, local stations or depots, common helplines, or one-stop service desks.

The point is not to make every service hyper-local. The point is to prevent shared territorial service design from turning into distance by design.

### 6. Put contractors, concessionaires, and member-government operators inside one territorial service constitution

A metro or region may deliver through many bodies. That is not itself a defect. The defect arises when each operator or contractor carries a different service promise, different continuity assumptions, different complaint path, and different data/reporting rhythm.

The archive should therefore prefer one territorial service constitution for any shared service system: common service obligations where appropriate, common reporting and incident rules, continuity duties, performance disclosure, interoperability expectations, and explicit fallback responsibility if one actor fails.

### 7. Treat continuity and failure takeover as part of the service home

A service home is dishonest if it works only while everyone cooperates and every operator stays solvent, friendly, and competent. The archive should therefore treat continuity planning, substitution rights, emergency operating doctrine, reserve capacity, transition packs, and failure-takeover rules as part of the service constitution rather than procurement aftercare.

The question is simple: if the host municipality, incumbent operator, or concessionaire fails, who still owes the territory the service?

### 8. Dock the service home into the other homes instead of letting it float free

An honest service home depends on the rest of the constitutional ladder. The delivery chain should line up with:

- the **democratic home**, so representatives can ask who is delivering what and why,
- the **fiscal home**, so service obligations match revenue and subsidy rules,
- the **administrative home**, so there is actual commissioning and contract-management capacity,
- the **accountability home**, so complaints and audits do not vanish into operator gaps,
- the **public-law home**, so core service obligations are not purely contractual,
- the **participatory home**, so users can influence service priorities,
- and the **knowledge home**, so service standards and route or facility choices rest on visible territorial evidence.

### 9. Upgrade from shared-service cooperation to real service government when the work becomes strategic, recurrent, and distributive

Municipal cooperation is often enough for bounded back-office or specialist services. But once the shared tier recurrently allocates service levels across the territory, decides long-term network sequencing, compels system integration, or arbitrates who gets priority access, the archive should stop describing the arrangement as mere co-operation.

At that point, service authority has become one of the real powers of territorial government and should be constitutionalized honestly.

### 10. Publish the chain ordinary people actually need

Most people do not need the institutional theory. They need to know who to contact, who is responsible, which standard applies, and where unresolved matters go next. The archive should therefore prefer public service maps, clear complaint-routing, named fallback authorities, and visible handoffs among local counters, shared authorities, and operators.

The shared tier should not require residents to reverse-engineer the constitution during a service failure.

## Service-home matrix

| Question | Thin or dishonest arrangement | Honest service home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Who owns the service promise?** | strategy language with no binding owner | a named authority with service obligations and fallback duties |
| **Who actually delivers?** | host municipality, legacy operator, or contractor by habit | declared operator chain with public roles and interfaces |
| **How are system choices made?** | recurring bargains among mayors or providers | one lead territorial authority for genuinely shared-system decisions |
| **How does the tier act?** | memoranda and persuasion only | lawful commissioning, franchising, direct-operation, or shared-service instruments |
| **Where do people enter?** | scattered municipal or operator front doors with no common handoff | near-to-person entry plus a visible territorial routing spine |
| **What happens on failure?** | everyone blames everyone else | reserve, takeover, substitution, and continuity rules are declared in advance |

## Failure modes

- **government by memorandum network**: the shared tier makes real territorial service choices but still governs mainly through informal agreements and goodwill.
- **host-city service capture**: one municipality’s departments or operators effectively become the metro or regional state without an honest mandate.
- **operator sovereignty**: the real choices sit with incumbent providers because the shared tier cannot commission, franchise, or step in.
- **front-door scatter**: users cannot tell where to go, which standard applies, or who owes them a response.
- **contract without constitution**: vital service obligations live only in procurement or concession documents and disappear on expiry or renegotiation.
- **continuity orphaning**: no actor clearly owns backup operation or failure takeover when a provider collapses or exits.
- **postcode integration**: service access and standards depend on which municipality one entered through rather than on the common territorial service promise.
- **strategy without instrument**: a shared tier publishes mobility, utility, or inclusion plans but lacks the legal and administrative tools to deliver them.
- **service accountability ricochet**: residents are bounced among municipal offices, shared authorities, contractors, and regulators with no clear remedy path.

## Practical tests

A shared territorial body has an honest service home when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can it state clearly which services remain local, which are shared, and which remain state or national?
2. For each shared service, can it distinguish the authority, the operator, and any regulator or inspector?
3. Does at least one named territorial authority own the system bargain where the service must behave as one network or one access standard?
4. Does the shared tier have lawful delivery instruments — such as commissioning, franchising, direct operation, or bounded shared-service agreements — rather than strategy language alone?
5. Can ordinary users still find accessible front doors and clear handoffs even when the back end is shared?
6. Are contractors, concessions, and member-government operators kept inside one visible territorial service constitution with common reporting and continuity expectations?
7. Are continuity, failure takeover, and substitution duties explicit before a provider fails?
8. Do the service arrangements visibly dock into the tier’s budget, scrutiny, complaint, participation, and evidence systems?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a metropolitan or regional tier now governs real service systems, but cannot plainly say **who owns the service promise, who operates it, how the tier can lawfully commission or step in, where people enter, and who keeps the service alive when a partner fails**, then it still lacks **a service home for shared territorial power** and is drifting into **government by memorandum network**.
