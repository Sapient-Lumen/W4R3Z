# 588 — Delegated state tasks, local discretion, agent status, funding, and no mixed-accountability blur

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should use **delegated state tasks** only for bounded functions that remain owned by a higher tier but require territorial execution through local or regional governments, and it should write those delegations as explicit public compacts that name the task, the discretion band, the instruction and supervision route, the funding rule, and the remedy path, so lower tiers do not become underfunded agents of the center while still being blamed as if they were governing in their own right.

## Why this matters

The archive already distinguishes **own powers**, **deconcentration**, **function transfer**, **unfunded mandates**, and **administrative supervision**. What it still lacked was one compact doctrine for the awkward middle category: tasks that are not genuinely local self-government, but are also not simply carried by a line-ministry field office.

This category matters because many multilevel systems quietly run on it. Municipalities may issue identity documents, administer social assistance, register events, inspect premises, or carry out environmental, electoral, civil-protection, or public-health steps on behalf of the state. Regions may implement national programmes, licensing regimes, or sectoral schemes while remaining constrained by national law and central instructions.

Done honestly, delegation can bring the state closer to people, use existing local administrative reach, and preserve a single national entitlement or compliance regime without building a separate field bureaucracy everywhere. Done badly, it produces the opposite of constitutional clarity: residents cannot tell whether the municipality is deciding or merely executing; the center controls through circulars, software, and reimbursement conditions while denying it has recentralized; and local politicians are punished for failures in tasks they cannot actually redesign.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine for **delegated state tasks**. Ideal government should prefer true self-government for genuinely local politics, true deconcentration for functions that must remain in the center’s own chain of command, and carefully bounded delegation only where a higher-tier task genuinely benefits from lower-tier execution without pretending that agency work is the same thing as autonomous local rule.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish own competences, delegated tasks, and deconcentrated administration in law, not folklore

A polity should be able to say of every recurring public task whether it is:

- an **own competence** of a local or regional authority,
- a **delegated task** carried out for a higher tier,
- or a **deconcentrated state function** executed by the center’s own territorial administration.

If those categories are left implicit, later conflicts become ungovernable: who may instruct whom, who pays when demand rises, who answers appeals, whether expediency review is lawful, and whether voters are choosing policy or only the quality of execution.

### 2. Prefer own powers for local politics and use delegation only for bounded higher-tier tasks

The archive should resist the temptation to solve every coordination problem with delegation. If the substance of the work is setting local priorities, balancing local trade-offs, or exercising general place-shaping judgment, it probably belongs in **own local or regional competence**.

Delegation is better suited to tasks that remain substantively a higher-tier responsibility but gain from lower-tier execution because proximity improves access, fact-gathering, case handling, or practical reach. A common pattern is a national entitlement or regulatory regime that needs territorial intake and routine administration without fragmenting the underlying rule.

### 3. Write a delegation charter for every recurring delegated task

A delegated task should not live only in a vague statute clause, ministry circular, or inherited habit. The archive should prefer a visible delegation instrument — statutory, regulatory, or intergovernmental where constitutionally proper — that states:

- the exact task being delegated,
- the higher-tier authority that owns the task,
- the lower-tier authority that executes it,
- whether the task is mandatory or optional,
- the decision points where local discretion exists,
- the standards or instructions that bind execution,
- the supervision and escalation route,
- the funding and reimbursement rule,
- the data and records rule,
- the complaint and appeal path,
- and the review, amendment, or withdrawal conditions.

If these things are missing, the arrangement is probably not a disciplined delegation but a fog bank of mixed accountability.

### 4. Keep room for local adaptation wherever the common rule does not require uniformity

A delegated task should not automatically reduce the lower tier to a mechanical relay. Where the common higher-tier purpose can tolerate local variation, the lower tier should have visible discretion to adapt outreach, workflow, sequencing, access channels, language support, local coordination, or other execution details to local conditions.

That discretion band matters because local authorities often know what residents actually need, where administrative friction sits, and how to keep a common entitlement reachable in real territory rather than only in law.

### 5. Publish the instruction chain and the authority map

Residents and frontline staff should be able to answer simple questions:

- Is this decision made locally or on behalf of a higher tier?
- Which rules are fixed nationally or regionally, and which steps can be adapted locally?
- Who can issue binding instructions?
- Who hears complaints and appeals?
- Who is politically answerable for policy design, and who is operationally answerable for service execution?

The archive should therefore prefer a plain-language authority map for each delegated task rather than leaving people to infer constitutional structure from letterhead, software branding, or office location.

### 6. Make the money lane explicit and fully costed

Delegation without an honest finance lane is usually fiscal theater. If a higher tier wants a lower tier to carry out a delegated task, the settlement should specify:

- how demand is estimated,
- what costs count,
- whether funding is formula-based, reimbursed, or case-based,
- how mid-year demand shocks are handled,
- which capital, staffing, software, translation, or inspection costs are covered,
- and what happens when the higher-tier rule changes.

The archive should assume that underfunded delegation is a common path to disguised recentralization: the higher tier keeps policy control, the lower tier absorbs queue anger and budget strain, and everyone pretends the arrangement is cooperative.

### 7. Separate legality review of own powers from expediency control of delegated tasks

Where a local authority acts in its **own sphere**, higher-tier supervision should ordinarily stay legality-bound and proportionate. Where that same authority executes a **delegated task**, a higher tier may properly exercise a broader form of direction or expediency review because the task remains legally its own.

But the line must stay visible. The archive should therefore reject systems where broader supervisory powers over delegated tasks quietly bleed into general tutelage over a local authority’s own political choices.

### 8. Keep prior approval and substitute action exceptional and typed

Delegated tasks may justify stronger tools than own competences, but stronger does not mean ambient. If a higher tier retains prior approval, substitute action, or direct instruction powers for a delegated task, those powers should be:

- specifically grounded in law,
- tied to named task classes,
- limited by time and procedure,
- reviewable,
- and published in a way that local authorities and residents can actually understand.

Otherwise, delegation becomes a route by which a central ministry acquires practical command over local administration without ever admitting that it has done so.

### 9. Pair supervision with advice, capability support, and operational help

If the center delegates a task because local reach is valuable, it should not relate to local government only as an inspector. A mature delegation regime should include:

- advisory channels,
- standard operating guidance,
- training,
- shared tooling where justified,
- legal and technical help desks,
- and escalation routes for unusual cases.

Delegation should not mean “carry this risk for us and guess the rest”.

### 10. Consult local authorities before changing delegated tasks that affect them directly

Delegation arrangements mutate easily: new forms, new software, narrower deadlines, new eligibility rules, new audits, new proof requirements, or new reporting burdens. The archive should therefore prefer prior consultation with affected local or regional authorities whenever a higher tier materially changes the delegated task architecture.

Consultation should cover not only legal text but operational consequences: staffing effects, records burdens, public-facing communication, transitional case handling, and the real cost of compliance.

### 11. Give lower tiers a visible route to refuse, escalate, or trigger redesign when delegation becomes impossible

A serious delegation regime needs an honesty valve. If a lower tier lacks the minimum viable capability to execute a delegated task safely and lawfully, or if higher-tier redesign has made the task unfunded, contradictory, or technically impossible, there should be a route to:

- notify the delegating authority,
- trigger support or temporary substitution,
- request redesign,
- or seek legal clarification.

Without that route, local authorities are forced either to fail silently or to pretend capacity exists when it does not.

### 12. Review delegated tasks periodically and decide whether they should remain delegated at all

Delegation should not be immortal by habit. Some tasks should eventually move into:

- true local or regional self-government,
- deconcentrated state administration,
- a shared intermunicipal service backbone,
- or a newly transferred competence with its own finance and accountability settlement.

The archive should therefore prefer review clocks that ask whether the reason for delegation still holds, whether the discretion band is honest, and whether the accountability story remains legible to residents.

## Guardrails

- Do not use delegation to avoid deciding whether a task really belongs to the center or to self-government.
- Do not treat delegated work as free local capacity that can absorb every new form, queue, and reporting burden.
- Do not let higher-tier control over delegated tasks quietly bleed into political tutelage over own local powers.
- Do not leave residents guessing whether their municipality is deciding policy or merely acting as an agent.
- Do not change delegated-task rules through software, circulars, or funding conditions without visible legal and operational explanation.

## Failure modes

- **mixed-accountability blur**: voters blame local government for a policy it cannot redesign.
- **agency without honesty**: the center says a task is local while still controlling rules, finance, and supervision.
- **delegation by circular**: binding operational change arrives through guidance, portals, or forms rather than clear law.
- **underfunded agency**: the lower tier becomes the shock absorber for a higher-tier entitlement or compliance regime.
- **supervision spillover**: stronger controls justified for delegated tasks metastasize into routine control over own competences.
- **local discretion fiction**: the law promises adaptation to local conditions but the real workflow leaves no meaningful room to adapt.

## Practical tests

A delegated-task regime is being used honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is the task clearly labeled as delegated rather than left ambiguous between own competence and state field administration?
2. Is there a visible delegation instrument that names ownership, executor, discretion, supervision, finance, records, and remedy?
3. Does the lower tier retain meaningful local adaptation where uniformity is not actually required?
4. Are the funding and reimbursement rules explicit enough to prevent disguised unfunded mandates?
5. Is the stronger supervision associated with delegation prevented from bleeding into general tutelage over the lower tier’s own sphere?
6. Can residents tell who designed the policy, who executed it, and where to challenge it?
7. Is there a review path if the delegated arrangement becomes impossible, misleading, or institutionally obsolete?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity says a municipality or region is “responsible” for a task but cannot show the **delegation charter, discretion band, money lane, instruction route, and appeal map**, then it has not actually clarified the function. It has produced **agency by blur**.
