# 584 — Common floors, local headroom, explicit preemption rules, and no silent override by upper-tier convenience

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat **preemption and override as explicit constitutional instruments rather than as ambient higher-tier privilege**: upper levels may set rights floors, solidarity guarantees, interoperability rules, and protections against genuine cross-border harms, but they should leave subnational governments real room to go further or adapt locally unless a law clearly names the conflict, states the protected interest, consults affected governments, provides transition and finance where duties are displaced, and subjects any ceiling preemption or field occupation to review rather than letting uniformity spread by habit.

## Why this matters

The archive now has stronger rules for **which functions belong at which level** (`579`), **when territorial asymmetry is justified** (`580`), **why function and finance must be assigned together** (`581`), **how capability backstops should work** (`582`), and **how rescue of failing subnational governments should remain lawful and temporary** (`583`). What it still lacked was one compact doctrine for a quieter but equally important constitutional question: **when may a higher tier override, displace, narrow, or freeze lower-tier lawmaking and administration once powers have been distributed?**

That gap matters because many constitutions praise subsidiarity in the abstract while letting preemption proliferate in practice. A national parliament sets a legitimate rights floor, then silently turns it into a complete ceiling. A ministry issues “guidance” that local governments cannot realistically refuse. A fiscal transfer becomes a disguised command lane. A sector law occupies the field so broadly that municipalities keep only ceremonial responsibility. A platform, procurement framework, or state IT system hard-codes one model and quietly erases lawful local discretion.

The result is a recurring pathology: **silent override by upper-tier convenience**. Uniformity expands not because the constitution openly decided that a matter truly requires national or federal control, but because higher tiers find standardisation easier than governing the real diversity of places.

The archive needs a narrower rule. **A serious multi-level constitution should distinguish common floors from total occupation, explicit preemption from soft coercion, and necessary system protection from prestige centralization.** Otherwise the assignment of powers is mostly decorative.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish floors, ceilings, and field occupation

The archive should force constitutions and ordinary statutes to say which kind of higher-tier intervention they are using.

- **floor preemption** sets a minimum below which no lower tier may fall, such as equal-rights guarantees, minimum service obligations, baseline labour or environmental protections, or interoperability requirements;
- **ceiling preemption** prevents lower tiers from going further than the higher-tier rule;
- **field occupation** reserves an entire subject matter to the higher tier, leaving little or no subnational room.

These are not the same move. Floor-setting is often compatible with genuine self-government. Ceiling preemption and field occupation are much stronger constitutional acts and should face a higher burden of justification.

### 2. Presume local and regional headroom above common floors

If the archive is serious about subsidiarity and place-based governance, the default should be **common floor, local headroom**. Where a higher tier sets a rights floor, safety baseline, disclosure duty, accessibility minimum, or social guarantee, lower tiers should ordinarily remain free to adapt delivery, add stricter protections, or pursue place-specific ambition unless doing so would create unlawful discrimination, impossible fragmentation, or serious cross-border harm.

That presumption matters because diversity is often the point of multi-level government. Municipalities may need to exceed national building-energy minimums, stronger tenant protections may be appropriate in one housing market and not another, and regions may need stricter watershed or transport coordination rules than a generic national baseline anticipates. A constitution that always turns minimums into maximums quickly hollows out subnational politics.

### 3. Require an explicit legal statement of preemption rather than ambient override

The archive should reject vague displacement. If a higher tier intends to override or occupy subnational authority, the law should say so expressly and identify:

- the lower-tier power being displaced,
- whether the move is a floor, a ceiling, or a full reservation of the field,
- the interest being protected,
- the geographic and policy scope,
- the duration and review rule,
- and the institutions responsible for interpretation and enforcement.

This discipline matters because silent override often hides inside broad framework laws, guidance documents, conditional circulars, or technical standards that are treated as politically irresistible even when the legal basis is thin.

### 4. Use stronger justification for ceilings and total occupation than for floors

A higher tier may often justify setting minimum rights and system standards. It should face a much steeper burden before forbidding lower tiers from going further.

The archive should therefore prefer a justification test for ceiling preemption or field occupation such as:

- the issue presents a genuine cross-border spillover or network-integrity risk,
- fragmented rules would materially impair equal civil status, market legibility, or national macro-stabilization,
- the need cannot be met through a floor-plus-interoperability design,
- and the higher-tier solution is proportionate to the interest being protected.

Uniformity should not win merely because it is administratively neat.

### 5. Consult subnational governments before preempting them

Because preemption changes the actual scope of self-government, it should trigger structured consultation rather than being presented as a completed fact. The archive should therefore prefer laws and constitutional practice that require affected municipalities, metros, regions, indigenous governments, or their representative associations to be consulted in due time and in a usable form before powers are narrowed or reclaimed.

Consultation should cover not only the legal text but also implementation consequences: what discretion remains, what finance or systems change, which records or staff shift, and what transition costs arise. A higher tier that will not explain the practical effects of preemption is often not governing honestly.

### 6. When duties are displaced, reassign finance, liabilities, and operational burden as well

The archive should keep **preemption** tied to the doctrine of **mandate-finance congruence**. If a higher tier occupies a field or removes a subnational tool, it should also state what happens to the corresponding duties, revenues, liabilities, and delivery risks.

Otherwise the polity gets a common bad pattern: local governments keep blame for outcomes they no longer have power to shape, or they remain nominally responsible for delivery while the higher tier dictates the critical variables. Real override should therefore come with real fiscal and operational reallocation.

### 7. Preserve discretion in delegated tasks and delivery adaptation where possible

Even where the higher tier lawfully controls a matter, the archive should still preserve room for **local adaptation in implementation**, especially for delegated tasks. A nation may define benefit eligibility, registry forms, curriculum standards, or pollution ceilings while leaving municipalities and regions discretion over outreach, staffing, sequencing, one-stop access, language support, local co-ordination, and service design.

This matters because preemption should not collapse into micro-management. A system can need common legal effect while still benefiting from different front doors, local data-cleaning practice, catchment-specific logistics, or neighbourhood-facing service channels.

### 8. Do not allow hidden preemption through money, procurement, software, or data architecture

The archive should name a crucial modern failure mode: preemption can occur without an obvious statute.

A higher tier may effectively erase subnational choice by:

- attaching operationally irresistible conditions to grants,
- bundling one model into mandatory shared procurement,
- forcing one software workflow that embeds one legal interpretation,
- centralizing a registry or platform in ways that leave no lawful discretion at the edge,
- or publishing “voluntary” model rules that subordinate governments cannot safely refuse.

These tools may sometimes be justified. But they should be treated as **override-capable instruments** and subjected to the same visibility, legality, proportionality, and review expectations as direct statutory preemption.

### 9. Reserve emergency override for genuine urgency and put it on a short clock

Some circumstances justify temporary upward override: acute epidemic control, grid protection, wartime logistics, emergency civil defense, or immediate constitutional-rights protection where delay would cause serious harm. But emergency preemption should be treated like crisis federalism, not like an ordinary convenience tool.

The archive should therefore insist that emergency override:

- names the triggering condition,
- states what lower-tier powers are paused or narrowed,
- expires automatically unless renewed through a lawful process,
- preserves judicial review,
- and carries a visible path back to ordinary scope allocation.

Temporary necessity should not be allowed to create permanent field occupation by precedent.

### 10. Use interoperability and mutual recognition before full uniformity where possible

The archive should prefer a narrow-waist style solution whenever it can honestly work. Often the real need is not identical local law everywhere, but **mutual legibility** across systems: common data fields, compatible licenses, portable rights evidence, reciprocal recognition rules, or common measurement and reporting standards.

Where interoperability solves the problem, total preemption is usually excessive. It is often enough to require lower tiers to publish in a standard format, honour a shared identifier, recognise certain documents, or meet common outcome floors while leaving substantial room for local policy form.

### 11. Protect constitutional enclaves where higher-tier override should be especially constrained

Some lower-tier domains deserve a stronger anti-preemption presumption because they are central to the meaning of local or plurinational self-government. The archive should be especially cautious about upper-tier override of:

- local democratic procedure and meeting openness,
- local public-space stewardship and place-making,
- municipal service design where rights floors are still met,
- treaty- or constitution-grounded indigenous self-government,
- and territorially specific arrangements created through explicit asymmetry settlements.

This does not make those domains absolute. It means the burden of override should be visible and unusually high.

### 12. Build judicial and constitutional review around the type of preemption claimed

A good system should let courts, constitutional tribunals, or equivalent review bodies ask structured questions:

- Is the higher-tier law explicit enough to count as preemption at all?
- Is it a floor, a ceiling, or field occupation?
- Is the stated interest constitutionally weighty enough for the type of override claimed?
- Were consultation and transition obligations met?
- Is the intervention proportionate, or could a floor-plus-headroom design have worked?
- Has an emergency or temporary override outlived its lawful rationale?

Without this discipline, override disputes become pure political muscle contests dressed up as legal interpretation.

### 13. Review ceiling preemption periodically and reopen headroom when the case weakens

Conditions change. A sector that once required tight national uniformity may later be governable through interoperable minimum standards. Capacity may grow at metropolitan or regional level. A digital registry may reduce transaction costs enough that stricter local rules no longer threaten legibility.

The archive should therefore prefer review clocks for strong preemption instruments, especially ceiling preemption and field occupation. The question should not only be “was this override lawful when adopted?” but also “**does the justification still hold, or has headroom become constitutionally affordable again?**”

## Guardrails

- Do not let a minimum national or federal standard silently become a complete ceiling without saying so openly.
- Do not use grants, platforms, procurement frameworks, or guidance documents to achieve practical preemption while denying that override has occurred.
- Do not occupy an entire field when interoperability, mutual recognition, or a rights floor would solve the real problem.
- Do not reclaim lower-tier powers without reassigning finance, liabilities, and delivery responsibility as well.
- Do not treat emergency override as a route to permanent ordinary central control.

## Failure modes

- **floor-to-ceiling drift**: a legitimate minimum standard quietly becomes the maximum permitted local ambition.
- **field occupation by vagueness**: a broad framework law is interpreted as wiping out lower-tier authority even though the displacement was never made explicit.
- **soft preemption by money**: fiscal dependence turns nominal self-government into one-way compliance.
- **platform preemption**: software, registries, or digital workflows erase lawful local discretion without constitutional debate.
- **prestige uniformity**: higher tiers centralize for symbolic command rather than because the problem actually requires it.
- **blame without power**: lower tiers remain politically answerable for outcomes after the crucial governing tools have been removed.

## Practical tests

A polity is handling preemption honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can it tell you whether the higher-tier rule is a floor, a ceiling, or a reservation of the field?
2. Is preemption stated explicitly in law rather than inferred from political pressure or administrative habit?
3. Does the system presume local or regional headroom above common floors unless a stronger case is made?
4. Were affected subnational governments consulted in time and in a usable way before their authority was narrowed?
5. When powers were displaced, were finance, liabilities, and delivery burdens reassigned visibly as well?
6. Are grants, procurement frameworks, and digital platforms treated as override-capable instruments rather than as neutral technicalities?
7. Do emergency override lanes expire and return power through a visible review path?
8. Is there an independent forum able to test whether full uniformity was really necessary?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **which higher tier may set standards or override lower-tier action**, but cannot also show **whether the instrument is a floor, a ceiling, or field occupation; what consultation occurred; what headroom remains; what finance and duties shifted; and how the override is reviewed or sunset**, then it still has **preemption power without a constitutional doctrine of local self-government**.
