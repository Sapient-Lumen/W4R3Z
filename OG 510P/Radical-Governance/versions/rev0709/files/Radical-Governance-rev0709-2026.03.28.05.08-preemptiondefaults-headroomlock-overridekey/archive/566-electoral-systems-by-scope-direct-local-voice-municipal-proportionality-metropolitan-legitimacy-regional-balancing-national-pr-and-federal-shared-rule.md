# 566 — Electoral systems by scope: direct local voice, municipal proportionality, metropolitan legitimacy, regional balancing, national PR, and federal shared rule

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should not force one electoral formula onto every scale: use direct and low-barrier democracy where the unit is genuinely small and stewardship-heavy, prefer proportional or semi-proportional council elections for municipalities and metropolitan governments that must aggregate durable diversity, fit executive election rules to the actual executive model, reserve territorial second chambers for real shared rule rather than decorative bicameralism, and keep continental and global bodies grounded mainly in treaty delegation plus bounded participatory and parliamentary complements rather than pretending every problem is solved by copying a national ballot upward.

## Why this matters

The archive already has notes on **representation by scope**, **political competition**, **legislatures**, **districting**, **electoral administration**, and **ideal constitutional kits by scope**. What it still lacked was a compact answer to a more surgical constitutional question: **which electoral systems belong at which scale, and why?**

That gap matters because institutions frequently fail not only from bad boundaries or partisan refereeing, but from using the wrong vote-to-seat logic for the job. A village can be suffocated by imported party-list machinery. A diverse city can be trapped by winner-take-all localism. A metropolitan government can wield binding transport, land-use, housing, and utility powers while still being indirectly composed through a congress of mayors. A federation can create a second chamber that is either too weak to protect shared rule or so overpowered that ordinary majorities cannot govern. And a country can directly elect a powerful president on one logic while electing a fragmented legislature on another, then act surprised when deadlock becomes the constitution’s main operating system.

The right archive stance is therefore **scale-matched electoral design**. Electoral systems are not moral badges. They are translation devices between public conflict and governed power. The ideal system at each level is the one that makes the relevant public visible, produces accountable government without counterfeit majorities, and matches the executive and legislative structure actually in use.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Keep the smallest scale genuinely local: use assemblies, recallable delegates, or very low-barrier ward contests where people can actually know the stakes

Blocks, hamlets, villages, estates, and neighborhood institutions usually govern upkeep, small grants, stewardship, local norms of use, and very short feedback loops. At this scale, the archive should prefer direct assemblies, consensus where practical, recallable delegates, or very simple low-cost contests over heavy party-system machinery. The point is not anti-politics. It is fit. When the governed unit is small enough for face-to-face accountability, the constitutional question is usually how to preserve voice and inclusion without letting one clique freeze everyone else out.

Where such bodies allocate scarce money or represent internally diverse populations, some ballot discipline may still be necessary. But the archive should resist importing nationalized electoral complexity merely because it looks modern. The smallest scale should maximize legibility, rotation, and corrective voice rather than staging a miniature parliament for problems of litter, repairs, safety, and mutual obligations.

### 2. Make municipalities the first serious electoral scale: councils should usually be chosen by rules that reflect real urban diversity instead of manufacturing false unanimity

Municipalities are the first level that typically governs a wide portfolio of ordinary life: land use, local streets, sanitation, libraries, parks, permits, primary service access, and often schooling, safety, or housing administration. They therefore need councils that can aggregate plural interests without pretending the city is socially uniform.

The archive should generally prefer proportional or semi-proportional systems for municipal councils in medium and large jurisdictions, especially where ethnic, class, linguistic, or neighborhood cleavages are durable. Open-list or flexible-list PR, STV, and well-designed mixed systems can all work. The key is to avoid turning a city of many publics into a winner-take-all machine that lets 35 or 40 per cent of the vote govern as if it were a permanent civic consensus.

Very small towns may still justify simpler majoritarian or at-large arrangements when the social field is genuinely small and competition remains low-cost and open. But the design burden should be on proving simplicity is still fair, not on assuming that municipal democracy must always imitate a small club.

### 3. Fit the local executive rule to the local executive model: do not mix a plebiscitary mayor with a legislature that cannot govern alongside one

Municipal constitutional design is often broken by false modularity. Designers pick a council electoral system in one room, a mayoral ballot in another, and only later notice that the combined structure breeds personalism, paralysis, or permanent shadow bargaining.

If the mayor is largely ceremonial or mostly chosen through and accountable to the council, then the council election does most of the constitutional work. If the mayor is directly elected and politically powerful, the archive should demand clear executive-legislative fit: realistic powers, workable budgeting rules, override and scrutiny routes, and electoral incentives that do not guarantee collision between an isolated mayor and a fragmented chamber. The question is not whether direct election is democratic in the abstract. The question is whether the whole municipal constitution can still steer, bargain, and correct.

### 4. Metropolitan governments need direct legitimacy when their decisions bind the whole urban system

Where a metropolitan or county-wide institution can tax, zone, assign housing burdens, run regional transit, coordinate utilities, or shape emergency systems, it should not be composed only indirectly through incumbent local officeholders. The archive should prefer directly legitimated metropolitan assemblies or councils, elected by systems that reflect the real plurality of the region while still preserving area-wide governability.

At this scale, proportional or mixed systems are often the best default because metropolitan politics usually combines territorial diversity with region-wide party or coalition formation. Electoral design should also prevent the largest core municipality from simply swallowing the rest while avoiding malapportioned protection for every edge municipality. The aim is neither pure population rule nor municipal veto confederacy; it is a publicly visible regional demos for shared systems.

### 5. Regional and provincial systems should balance territory and pluralism without pretending every subunit is a sovereign people

Regional government often sits between metropolitan and national politics. It may coordinate health systems, watershed planning, intercity transport, major education platforms, or federal-subnational shared rule. Electoral systems at this level therefore need to preserve both social pluralism and territorial legibility.

The archive should usually prefer proportional or mixed systems for regional lower chambers, potentially with modest territorial correctives, rather than strong winner-take-all formulas. Where internal territorial diversity is constitutionally salient, some seat-allocation or district design may ensure peripheral areas remain visible. But the archive should reject heavy malapportionment that lets one thinly populated zone dominate a much larger public. Territorial balancing is a corrective, not a license to manufacture minority rule.

### 6. National lower chambers should usually be proportional enough to reflect the actual country, especially where pluralism is deep and coalition government is normal

At national scale, the archive should generally lean toward proportional representation or mixed-member proportional design for the main legislative chamber, especially in socially divided, multi-party, or federal polities. The national legislature is where law, budget, rights, and long-horizon bargaining must occur in public view. False majorities at that level can distort not just a service portfolio but the whole constitutional order.

This does not mean every country needs the same formula, threshold, district magnitude, or list type. It does mean the archive should treat strong disproportionality as something that requires justification, not as the invisible default. Where governability is the concern, better tools usually include threshold design, constructive no-confidence rules, committee structure, cabinet-formation rules, and clear coalition discipline — not simply discarding proportionality and hoping the constitution will become simpler by misdescribing the electorate.

### 7. Territorial second chambers are justified by shared rule, not by nostalgia for aristocratic furniture or duplicate review

A second chamber can make constitutional sense where constituent units must visibly co-govern the union. That logic is strongest in federations and some deeply regionalized states. The archive should therefore prefer upper chambers that are explicitly tied to territorial representation and to the domains where constituent-unit consent or review is constitutionally warranted.

The archive should reject duplicate bicameralism in which the upper house is elected almost the same way as the lower one, at nearly the same time, with nearly the same political logic, but still receives a large veto over ordinary law. When there is a territorial chamber, its composition, powers, and dispute-resolution mechanisms should be explained by shared rule. If that case cannot be made, unicameralism or a weaker review chamber is usually cleaner.

### 8. Direct democracy works best as a bounded complement, with the smallest scales usually able to use more of it than the largest scales

Initiatives, referendums, recall, and participatory budgeting can improve accountability and agenda control, but they do not abolish the need for representative institutions. The archive should therefore treat direct democracy as a bounded complement whose appropriate scope varies by level.

At local scale, direct democratic mechanisms are often easier to understand, easier to deliberate on, and closer to the consequences. At metropolitan and regional scale, they may still work on specific questions if the issue is clearly framed and the legal effect is visible. At national scale, they should be used carefully, with clear subject-matter limits and honest implementation pathways. At continental and global scale, the archive should be extremely cautious: direct voting slogans outrun constitutional reality much faster than actual shared authority does.

### 9. Electoral-system choice should track party-system needs and ballot simplicity without quietly excluding independents or minorities

Different scales need different kinds of political organization. Micro-local and some municipal politics should leave room for independents, civic lists, neighborhood groupings, and non-party coalitions. Larger regional and national systems usually need durable party competition to structure accountability and bargaining. Electoral design should support that without hardening cartel privilege.

This means ballot access, thresholds, district magnitude, and list design should be judged together. A system that is formally proportional can still be exclusionary if thresholds are too high or districts too small. A system that allows independent candidacy can still be fake if campaign finance and signature rules make entry impossible. The ideal is not endless fragmentation or forced bipolarity; it is contestable structure appropriate to scale.

### 10. Continental and global bodies usually need delegated or indirect democratic legitimacy, plus bounded parliamentary and participatory complements, not fantasy world-state ballots

Some continental bodies will properly include directly elected parliamentary elements, especially where lawmaking authority is real and bounded. But most transnational and global institutions still derive authority primarily through treaties, member-state representation, and mandate-limited problem solving. The archive should therefore resist the reflex to imagine that every legitimate higher-scale institution must copy a domestic lower house.

A better design is narrower and more honest: treaty accountability through national constitutional systems, parliamentary oversight where the legal order supports it, transparent appointments, public reporting, and participatory or deliberative complements for affected publics. The point is not to freeze global democracy at zero. It is to avoid pretending that one undifferentiated planetary ballot would solve legitimacy for institutions whose real powers, publics, and enforcement capacities remain highly uneven.

## Guardrails

- Do not use winner-take-all municipal systems as the invisible default in socially diverse cities.
- Do not create powerful metropolitan institutions that are democratically indirect when their decisions bind people directly.
- Do not combine a separately elected executive with a legislature chosen on incompatible incentives and then call the resulting deadlock a civic virtue.
- Do not justify second chambers unless they perform genuine shared-rule or bounded review functions.
- Do not use direct democracy as a bypass around rights, minorities, or ordinary implementation discipline.
- Do not treat threshold design, district magnitude, and ballot access as technical footnotes when they decide who is politically visible.
- Do not imitate national electoral forms upward into continental or global settings whose legitimacy still runs mainly through treaties and delegated authority.

## Failure modes

- **tiny-parliament syndrome**: a genuinely micro-local body is overloaded with party machinery, formalism, and zero-sum factionalism it does not need.
- **false-city-majority**: a city council elected on a strongly majoritarian basis governs as if a minority vote share were a permanent urban consensus.
- **metro-without-demos**: a binding metropolitan authority is really just a meeting of incumbents from lower tiers.
- **dual-mandate collision**: a powerful directly elected executive faces a chamber and party system designed for different incentives, producing chronic blockage or personalist workarounds.
- **territorial over-veto 2.0**: an upper chamber justified in the language of federalism blocks ordinary democratic government far beyond the domains that protect shared rule.
- **plebiscitary escape hatch**: initiatives, referendums, or recall are used to avoid ordinary legislative responsibility, minority protection, or implementation honesty.
- **threshold laundering**: a system marketed as proportional quietly excludes smaller parties, dispersed minorities, or independent entrants through magnitudes, thresholds, finance, or petition rules.
- **world-parliament theater**: designers promise global democratic salvation through a ballot for institutions that still lack the legal basis, demos, and enforceable mandate to carry it.

## Practical tests

A scale-matched electoral architecture passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does each level use an electoral or participatory form that matches the actual scope of the public it governs?
2. Are councils or assemblies at municipal, metropolitan, regional, and national scales representative enough that durable diversity is not translated into counterfeit unanimity?
3. Are executive-election rules fitted to the real executive model rather than chosen in isolation?
4. Where a second chamber exists, are its composition and powers justified by shared rule or bounded review rather than duplication?
5. Are direct-democracy tools legally bounded, implementable, and scaled to questions a public can actually deliberate on?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can explain **how ballots are cast** but not **why this vote-to-seat rule fits this scale, this executive model, this territorial structure, and this public**, then it still has **elections without an electoral constitution**.
