# 573 — Administrative procedure, permits, and licenses by scope: community witnessing, municipal front doors, regional technical review, national procedure codes, and no government by permit maze

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat **administrative procedure, permits, and licenses** as the **constitutional authorization layer** of government: keep lived fact-finding, pre-application help, and ordinary place-based intake close to communities and municipalities; pool specialist review and cross-boundary technical assessment regionally where risks or spillovers justify it; reserve national or constitutional-state authority for the core procedural guarantees, common clocks, appeal architecture, and recognition rules that make the whole system legible; and use continental and global layers for interoperability, evidence exchange, and mutual recognition rather than for a fantasy of one world permit desk.

## Why this matters

The archive now has stronger notes on **regulators and inspections**, **land administration**, **administrative geography**, **entity registries**, **public records**, and **quality infrastructure**. What it still lacked was the authorization layer that turns those backbones into actual governing decisions: the permit application, the business license, the building clearance, the environmental approval, the occupancy certificate, the operating condition, the renewal, the suspension, the revocation, and the appeal.

That gap matters because many people experience the state less through constitutional theory than through an application window, an online portal, a municipal clerk, a planning office, an environmental authority, or a professional-licensing counter. When that authorization layer is well designed, the state is intelligible: people can tell what permission is required, why it exists, which office owns it, what evidence must be supplied, how long the decision should take, what happens if the authority asks for more, how to challenge an error, and which scale of government can lawfully attach conditions. When it is badly designed, the result is **government by permit maze**: duplicated paperwork, repeated requests for evidence the state already holds, contradictory conditions from different offices, local bottlenecks, unexplained delay, fee farming, and power that feels negotiable rather than lawful.

An ideal governance archive therefore needs one compact note on how permits, licenses, and administrative procedure should be distributed by scope. The right answer is not to centralize every authorization at the top, and not to let each locality improvise its own miniature sovereign procedure code. It is to keep local intake and place knowledge close to the ground, pool specialist review where fragmentation would produce incompetence, preserve strong national procedural floors, and keep cross-border layers narrow enough to help with exchange and recognition without inventing a universal permission bureaucracy.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat permits and licenses as bounded ex ante risk tools, not as the default answer to uncertainty

A permit or license should exist because a government has identified a real reason to control an activity before it begins: serious safety risk, environmental harm, irreversible land-use consequence, scarce public resource allocation, professional trust, network integrity, or another named public interest that cannot be handled well enough through disclosure, ordinary inspection, insurance, or ex post sanction alone.

The archive should therefore prefer authorization regimes that begin with a public harm model and a necessity test. If officials cannot explain why the permission must exist before action occurs, the polity should assume that the activity should move to a lighter-touch regime, a registration model, or ordinary rule enforcement instead.

### 2. Keep lived fact-finding and pre-application help close to communities, but do not confuse local knowledge with sovereign procedural autonomy

At the smallest scales, residents, workers, neighbours, and local civic bodies often know first whether a proposed use is real, whether a site is occupied, whether a building condition is dangerous, whether a local operator exists beyond paper, or whether a permit file hides obvious mismatch with lived reality. Good systems therefore leave room for witnessing, objection, correction, and pre-application guidance near the people affected.

But the archive should reject the idea that each neighbourhood or tiny local unit should invent its own forms, filing rules, appeal clocks, or legal standards for what makes an application complete. Local knowledge is an input into lawful administration, not a substitute for lawful administration.

### 3. Municipalities should be the ordinary front door for place-based permitting and licensing

Most authorization regimes touch place: building, occupancy, street use, trading, food premises, taxis, local events, local business activity, environmental nuisances, signage, excavation, and the everyday interface between a person or firm and the public realm. Municipal or county-scale government is usually where the physical site, local plan, utility condition, address reality, and practical enforcement route are visible.

The archive should therefore prefer municipalities as the ordinary intake and service layer for place-based permits and licenses: one recognisable public front door, clear filing help, completeness checks, practical notices, accessible status updates, and routine coordination with the local inspectors who will later verify compliance. But municipalities should not become islands of procedure that force applicants to relearn the state every time they cross a boundary.

### 4. Regional, provincial, state, or intermunicipal layers should pool specialist review and cross-boundary coordination

Many permit decisions depend on technical capacity or spillover analysis that small municipalities cannot sustain alone: major environmental clearances, watershed impacts, shared air basins, regional transport interfaces, hazardous installations, high-complexity building systems, utility corridors, or projects whose effects cross municipal borders. If every small locality must carry complete technical review capacity, quality becomes thin and uneven.

The archive should therefore prefer higher subnational pooling for specialist review boards, environmental and engineering expertise, intermunicipal case coordination, and certain appellate or reconsideration functions. The design aim is not to strip localities of voice. It is to prevent place-based permissions from depending on whichever municipality happens to have the thinnest bench or the strongest local patronage machine.

### 5. National or constitutional-state authority should own the procedural floor

Some parts of the authorization layer are too important to leave to local improvisation: who may demand a permit at all, what counts as a complete application, minimum notice and hearing rights, access to the file, stated reasons, ordinary decision clocks, fee discipline, conflict rules, recusal rules, service standards, review routes, record retention, and the legal effect of approvals, conditions, expiry, suspension, transfer, and revocation.

The archive should therefore prefer a national or constitutional-state **procedure code floor**. In federations, constituent units may legitimately control large parts of administrative law, but there still needs to be a law-owning level that makes the basic grammar of public decision-making legible. A polity should not make people discover their rights to reasons, deadlines, appeal, or file access by municipality-by-municipality folklore.

### 6. Ask once where possible, route evidence from authoritative sources, and never make digital convenience a new exclusion system

A well-designed permit system should not force applicants to submit the same corporate extract, identity evidence, parcel record, cadastral plan, tax certificate, or prior approval repeatedly when the state already has lawful access to an authoritative source. Single-entry points, once-only evidence reuse, and interoperable back-office exchange can reduce burden and error.

But the archive should also prefer systems that preserve human help, assisted-digital channels, and offline routes when digital access is weak or when the stakes are high. A single digital front door is not a constitutional achievement if it becomes a single digital trap.

### 7. Make clocks, statuses, conditions, and reasons explicit enough that silence cannot masquerade as law

A permit system becomes arbitrary when nobody can tell whether an application is incomplete, queued, under technical review, paused for additional evidence, approved with conditions, denied on the merits, deemed withdrawn, expired, or awaiting appeal. It becomes worse when authorities can simply remain silent and force applicants to infer meaning from non-response.

The archive should therefore prefer authorization systems with explicit state models: receipt, completeness, active review, request-for-information, decision due date, approval, approval with conditions, refusal, suspension, revocation, renewal, transfer, and appeal. Decision clocks, pause rules, and the legal meaning of missed deadlines should be stated in law or publicly binding procedure. High-risk authorizations generally require an explicit decision; low-risk or purely criteria-bound cases may sometimes justify deemed approval or automatic issuance, but only if the rule is clear in advance and the record remains reviewable.

### 8. Keep fee logic and permit inventories honest so authorization cannot become a hidden tax system

Permits and licenses often become politically tempting revenue instruments, especially where municipalities are fiscally weak. This distorts the regime: too many permissions, too many renewals, too many minor conditions, and too much discretionary friction because the bureaucracy has learned to monetize dependence.

The archive should therefore prefer publicly listed permit inventories, legal bases, criteria, fees, service standards, and expiry rules, with periodic pruning of obsolete permissions. Authorization fees may recover administrative costs or fund justified supervision, but they should not quietly become general-revenue substitutes or cartel shields for incumbents.

### 9. Continental and global layers should support recognition, exchange, and narrow common rails rather than impersonate a world licensing ministry

Some permissions cross borders or depend on cross-border trust: customs and trade authorizations, professional recognition, transport and safety certificates, product approvals, procurement qualifications, and certain digital or corporate proofs. These are real governance problems, and they benefit from common data standards, mutual-recognition arrangements, evidence exchange, and problem-solving channels.

The archive should therefore prefer continental and global roles that stay narrow: interoperable procedures, common evidence vocabularies, treaty-bound recognition frameworks, and disciplines against unnecessary barriers. What it should reject is the fantasy that there should be one remote supranational permit desk deciding every local, national, or sectoral authorization question.

## Guardrails

- Do not require permits or licenses where ordinary rules, inspection, disclosure, or registration would protect the public interest just as well.
- Do not make applicants resubmit evidence the state already lawfully possesses from an authoritative register.
- Do not let every municipality invent its own basic procedure code, status language, or evidence grammar.
- Do not pool technical review upward and then erase local notice, local facts, or local accountability.
- Do not make silence, portal opacity, or unexplained “pending” states function as substitute decisions.
- Do not let permit fees, renewals, or conditions become routine revenue extraction.
- Do not make a digital portal the only practical route for people who need permission, remedy, or help.
- Do not let cross-border recognition systems silently override democratically chosen local safety, labour, accessibility, or environmental protections that remain lawful.

## Failure modes

- **government by permit maze**: public authority is experienced as repeated applications, duplicate evidence requests, unclear statuses, and office-hopping rather than as intelligible law.
- **folklore procedure**: applicants survive by brokers, local memory, or personal networks because the actual rules are under-specified or not published clearly.
- **digital-only trap**: online portals exist, but they shift burden onto users, exclude people needing help, and still require paper or in-person duplication.
- **technical thinness**: small jurisdictions hold decisive power over complex authorizations without access to competent technical review.
- **fee farming**: governments multiply permissions or renewals because the regime has become a revenue source.
- **evidence ping-pong**: agencies bounce applicants between registries, municipalities, and higher authorities for documents the state could have routed internally.
- **cross-boundary paralysis**: a project or activity touching several jurisdictions stalls because no lawful coordination lane exists.
- **silent refusal by delay**: the state avoids accountable denial by never reaching a visible merits decision.

## Practical tests

An authorization architecture is scale-matched and lawful when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can the polity explain why each major permit or license exists as an ex ante risk tool rather than as administrative habit?
2. Do residents and applicants have a recognisable local front door for place-based permissions, help, and factual correction?
3. Are specialist review and cross-boundary effects pooled above tiny jurisdictions when local fragmentation would obviously degrade competence?
4. Is there a clear law-owning level that sets the procedural floor for notice, hearing, reasons, file access, clocks, fees, and appeals?
5. Can applicants reuse authoritative evidence instead of repeatedly resubmitting the same documents to adjacent public bodies?
6. Are statuses, deadlines, conditions, expiry effects, and appeal routes explicit enough that silence cannot impersonate law?
7. Do continental or global layers help with exchange and recognition without turning into a remote universal permit bureaucracy?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can tell you **which office accepts the application** but cannot tell you **why the permission exists, which scale of government owns the procedural rules, what the status clock means, whether the state could have fetched the evidence itself, and how a refusal becomes contestable**, then it still has **authorizations without a constitutional procedure layer**.
