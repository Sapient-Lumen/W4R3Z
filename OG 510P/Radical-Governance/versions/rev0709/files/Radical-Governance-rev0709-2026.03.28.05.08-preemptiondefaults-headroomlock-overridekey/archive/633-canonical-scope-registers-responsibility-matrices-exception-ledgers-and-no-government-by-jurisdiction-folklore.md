# 633 — Canonical scope registers, responsibility matrices, exception ledgers, and no government by jurisdiction folklore

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should keep one **canonical public scope register** that shows, for each major function and lane of authority, the default legal home, territorial footprint, finance path, real delivery chain, exceptions, temporary deviations, review route, and next review date, so the stack is governed as an inspectable object rather than inferred from scattered statutes, grants, habits, and insider folklore.

## Why this matters

The archive now says much more clearly **which scope should usually hold which class of problem** (`579`, `602`, `603`), **how misfit should be diagnosed and repaired** (`604`, `605`), **when shared territorial arrangements have become real government and need constitutional upgrade** (`606`-`627`), and **how ideal-scope claims should be tested, presumed, moved, handed back, and reopened** (`628`-`632`).

What it still lacked was the simplest operating artifact underneath all those doctrines: **where is the current stack written down in one place?**

That gap matters because multilevel systems often live on documents that do not line up with each other. A constitution names broad competences. Sector laws split them differently. Grants attach soft commands. Digital platforms encode hidden defaults. Crisis memoranda suspend ordinary boundaries. Delegations and special measures accumulate. Citizens, officials, journalists, auditors, and even ministers then start answering the question “who actually owns this?” from memory, custom, or political convenience.

The archive should therefore prefer a **canonical scope register**. If a polity cannot point to one current public map of responsibility and its exceptions, it is still governing the stack partly by folklore.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Keep one canonical register for the live scope stack

A serious multilevel constitution should have one public register that states the current allocation of major functions across scopes. This is not a replacement for the constitution, statutes, or treaties. It is the **maintained index** that lets people see how those sources currently compose into a working stack.

The register should be authoritative enough to orient the public, legislatures, administrations, auditors, and courts, but subordinate enough that it never pretends to create powers by spreadsheet alone.

### 2. Register lanes, not just fields

Many fields are shared. Education, housing, transport, health, policing, and environment rarely sit at only one level. The register should therefore not stop at saying “transport is metropolitan” or “health is regional.” It should split each field into lanes such as:

- rulemaking,
- finance,
- planning,
- delivery,
- data and records,
- monitoring,
- inspection and enforcement,
- complaints and review,
- emergency override,
- and residual or fallback responsibility.

Without lane-splitting, a register becomes too coarse to be honest.

### 3. For each lane, record the legal home and the operational chain

Every entry should say both **who owns the lane in law** and **who actually carries it in practice**. At minimum, each entry should name:

- the ordinary legal home,
- whether the power is own, shared, delegated, contracted, or emergency-held,
- the current operational actor or actors,
- the public-facing front door,
- the review or remedy route,
- and the authority source that anchors the entry.

The archive should reject registers that show only paper authority or only de facto workflow. Honest multilevel government needs both columns at once.

### 4. Keep the territorial footprint visible for each function

The same function can live on different maps at once: municipality for intake, metro for systems planning, region for hospitals, nation for equalization, treaty body for standards. The register should therefore show the **territorial footprint** attached to each lane and the relationship among those maps.

This matters because many scope failures are really map failures. The public should not have to reverse-engineer whether the relevant geography is the electoral boundary, the service catchment, the tax base, the basin, the commuting shed, or the emergency zone.

### 5. Show the finance path next to the function, not in another universe

A scope register is incomplete if it says who is responsible but not who pays. Each entry should therefore carry a compact finance field that identifies the relevant own-source base, shared revenue, formula transfer, earmark, user charge, borrowing lane, or emergency backstop.

Otherwise the stack remains formally legible but fiscally misleading.

### 6. Separate defaults from exceptions, delegations, and temporary deviations

The register should distinguish:

- the default allocation,
- durable asymmetry,
- delegated execution,
- contracted operation,
- special measures or distress supervision,
- emergency override,
- and temporary pilots or transitional arrangements.

These should not be folded into one generic “responsible authority” box. A polity needs to be able to see whether a level holds a function by ordinary right, by borrowed mandate, by crisis lease, or by unresolved residue.

### 7. Give each entry a review clock and trigger links

The register should not be static. Each entry should show:

- the last review date,
- the next scheduled review date,
- relevant trigger classes from the scope-audit registry,
- and any open packet or pending redesign attached to the entry.

This is how the current note docks into `632`. The nominal map and the trigger system should point at each other.

### 8. Version the register and keep public diffs

Because scope drift often hides in small edits, the register should be versioned. Every material change should leave behind:

- the prior entry,
- the new entry,
- the reason for change,
- the legal instrument used,
- the consultation or consent path,
- and the effective date.

The archive should prefer one public diff spine over a world where institutional rewiring is visible only to insiders who already know which ministry circular changed what.

### 9. Publish both a human-readable map and a machine-readable one

The public needs a readable guide, but administrations, researchers, and civic monitors also need structured data. The register should therefore exist in both:

- a human-readable narrative/table form,
- and a machine-readable schema that can be joined to budget, plan, service, complaint, and territorial datasets.

This keeps the stack inspectable without forcing every user into legal archaeology.

### 10. Let the register admit uncertainty instead of inventing clarity

Some fields will be contested, transitional, or genuinely concurrent. The register should have explicit statuses for:

- settled,
- shared but lane-split,
- delegated,
- temporary override,
- under review,
- and disputed.

The archive should reject fake precision. When the stack is unsettled, the honest move is to mark the entry as unsettled and show the governing route for resolution.

### 11. Use the register as the docking spine for the rest of the constitution

A scope register becomes valuable when it links outward to:

- constitutions, statutes, charters, and treaties,
- budget and equalization rules,
- plan hierarchies and territorial maps,
- service and regulatory bodies,
- complaints and judicial review routes,
- active exceptions or special measures,
- and any open rescoping or handback packets.

This is how the register avoids becoming another dead spreadsheet.

### 12. Treat missing entries and stale entries as constitutional defects

If a major function lacks an entry, or if the entry has not been refreshed after visible legal or operational change, that should count as a governance defect in its own right. Silence should not be neutral.

A polity that changes who really governs without updating the public map is not merely disorganized. It is obscuring the constitutional stack.

## Register entry matrix

| Field | Minimum content | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| function and lane | field plus lane split such as rulemaking, finance, delivery, review | keeps “shared responsibility” from collapsing into fog |
| legal home | own, shared, delegated, contracted, emergency-held, or supervised | shows what kind of authority is being exercised |
| operational holder | actual actor running the lane | catches paper/practice divergence |
| territorial footprint | boundary or functional geography used for that lane | exposes map mismatch early |
| finance path | revenue, transfer, charge, borrowing, or backstop basis | prevents mandate/finance blur |
| front door and remedy | citizen entry point plus complaint/appeal/review route | keeps the stack navigable |
| exception status | asymmetry, pilot, distress, override, or under-review marker | stops temporary residue impersonating the norm |
| review clock | last review, next review, open trigger or packet | keeps the map alive rather than ceremonial |

## Guardrails

- Do not let the register claim to create powers that only law can create.
- Do not collapse shared fields into one undifferentiated ownership label.
- Do not hide delegations, distress measures, emergency overrides, or contracted operation inside the default map.
- Do not separate responsibility mapping from finance, remedy, or territory.
- Do not publish a register once and then leave it to decay.
- Do not treat ambiguity as solved merely because a box has been filled.

## Failure modes

- **jurisdiction folklore**: people rely on memory, status, or insider custom because no current public map exists.
- **paper-only map**: the register mirrors statutes but ignores the actor who really controls delivery or discretion.
- **workflow-only map**: the register lists current operators but hides the legal basis and review route.
- **shared-field blur**: a whole sector is assigned to several levels without lane typing.
- **exception laundering**: delegations, crisis holds, or distress supervision quietly become the apparent default.
- **stale stack**: major reforms happen, but the public map is never updated.
- **clarity theater**: the register presents false certainty where competences are actually contested or under review.

## Practical tests

A polity is keeping an honest scope register when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is there one public current map of major functions across scopes rather than a scavenger hunt through scattered legal texts?
2. Are shared fields split into lanes rather than assigned in one blurry block?
3. Does each entry show both the legal home and the actual operational holder?
4. Is the territorial footprint visible for the relevant lane?
5. Is the finance path shown next to the function?
6. Are delegations, contracts, pilots, distress measures, and emergency overrides marked separately from the default allocation?
7. Does each entry name the public front door and remedy route?
8. Does the register show review dates and link to open trigger or rescoping packets?
9. Are changes versioned with public diffs and reasons?
10. Can the register admit disputed or transitional status instead of inventing fake clarity?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can explain **what its multilevel doctrine says in theory** but cannot also show **one current, public, lane-split register of who legally owns, operationally carries, finances, reviews, and temporarily deviates from each major function**, then it still has **scope theory without an inspectable constitutional stack**.
