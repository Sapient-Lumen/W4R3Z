# 808 — Calibration packs, moderation loops, and precedent banks for scope assessments: score anchors, difficult-case conferences, documented departures, and no benchmark by reviewer mood

## One-line thesis

The archive should pair its benchmark, mission, evidence-register, and mission-integrity notes with one compact **review-calibration grammar**: **every serious scope assessment should keep a small calibration pack with lane-specific score anchors, worked examples, threshold splits, precedent notes, moderation checkpoints, and documented departures, so that different review teams can reach comparably disciplined judgments without collapsing everything into one blended score or one charismatic reviewer’s mood**.

## Why this matters

The archive can now say **what the ideal scope stack is** (`790`), **how to benchmark it** (`791`), **how to stress it** (`792`), **how to maintain it** (`793`), **how to move toward it** (`794`), **what proof must stay public** (`795`), **how persistent failure can justify intervention** (`796`), **who may challenge those judgments** (`797`), **which peer class counts before scoring begins** (`798`), **what must happen to temporary constitutional objects** (`799`), **how a serious mission should run** (`800`), **how its findings should be stated** (`801`), **how those findings become owned repair work** (`802`), **how claimed repair is later verified** (`803`), **how one real path through the stack can be walked** (`804`), **how the evidence entering the review should be sampled** (`805`), **how relied-on exhibits stay typed and reusable** (`806`), and **how mission composition stays independent enough to count as judging rather than self-review** (`807`).

What the archive still lacked was one compact rule for a quieter but crucial problem:

**how do two honest review teams avoid scoring the same constitutional reality very differently merely because they learned the tool differently, favor different kinds of evidence, or carry different intuitions about thresholds?**

That gap matters because serious review systems often fail not through corruption or negligence, but through ungoverned variation. One team treats a partial metropolitan authority as “substantially fit” because it exists at all; another treats the same arrangement as structurally unfit because taxation and accountability are missing. One mission reads a contradictory evidence packet as a caution against scoring; another converts the same contradiction into a harsh deficiency. One reviewer thinks an emergency host arrangement deserves temporary tolerance; another grades it as permanent constitutional failure. Official review practice points toward the missing discipline. GAO’s 2024 Yellow Book requires audit organizations to design, implement, and document a system of quality management that supports personnel’s **consistent understanding** and the consistent operation of quality responses. OECD’s peer-review handbooks repeatedly frame assessor familiarity with key documents, cross-checking against other assessments, and maintenance of the same approach as prerequisites for proper, fair, efficient, and equitable reviews. WHO’s review and roster practices use team-member orientation and training to make the tool intelligible before evaluators begin, and WHO regulatory-system evaluation guidance explicitly ties training and roster management to consistency in quality. The archive should compress that lesson into one note: **serious scope review needs calibration packs and moderation loops, not just honest teams and a benchmark card**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate independence from calibration

`807` asks whether the right people are judging.

This note asks whether those people are **judging in a comparable way**.

A mission can be independent and still be erratic. It can be conflict-screened and still drift between overly generous and overly punitive interpretations. The archive should therefore treat mission integrity and review calibration as two separate disciplines.

### 2. Start each recurring review line with a small calibration pack

A serious review line should keep one compact pack containing at least:

- the current scoring or verdict lanes,
- lane-specific anchor examples,
- threshold split notes for hard boundary cases,
- common evidence minima,
- known contradiction patterns,
- and a short precedent bank of comparable prior cases.

This is not a giant manual. It is the minimum kit that keeps a review line from re-inventing its standards every mission.

### 3. Use lane-specific anchors, not one mood for the whole constitution

The archive already rejects one blended constitutional score.

Calibration should follow the same rule. Each lane should have its own worked anchors. For example:

- what counts as **merely nominal** metropolitan governance,
- what counts as **partial but real** fiscal congruence,
- what counts as **credible but incomplete** handback planning,
- or what counts as **adequate evidence** for a route-walk failure.

Without lane-specific anchors, teams smuggle a general impression of the polity into every sub-judgment.

### 4. Run an opening moderation session before fieldwork hardens intuitions

Before scoring begins, the review team should usually walk through a few worked examples and difficult thresholds together.

The point is not to predetermine the verdict. It is to surface hidden differences in how reviewers interpret:

- the score bands,
- the evidence minimum,
- the role of contradiction,
- and the difference between transitional tolerance and structural acceptance.

A calibration note written after scoring is much less useful than one used before and during the mission.

### 5. Keep a precedent bank, but bind it to comparator discipline

Precedent is necessary and dangerous.

The archive should therefore keep a compact precedent bank that says:

- what earlier case is being invoked,
- why it is comparable under `798`,
- what lane it illuminates,
- and what feature makes the precedent informative rather than prestigious.

This prevents both amnesia and postcard benchmarking.

### 6. Record documented departures instead of pretending precedent is either binding or irrelevant

A mature review system should not force new teams to mimic prior cases mechanically.

But it should force them to say when they depart and why.

For each material departure from prior anchor or precedent, the calibration record should say:

- what earlier anchor or case was considered,
- why it was not followed,
- whether the difference comes from evidence, comparator class, tool revision, or normative refinement,
- and whether the departure should itself become a new anchor.

That is how the archive gets learning without reviewer free play.

### 7. Treat contradiction handling as part of calibration, not just of evidence

`806` keeps the contradiction visible in the evidence chain.

This note adds the missing review-discipline layer: teams should agree in advance how contradiction affects scoring. For example:

- when contradiction lowers confidence but not severity,
- when it blocks a verdict entirely,
- when it triggers more sampling or route work,
- and when it justifies a documented split view.

Without that, different teams punish contradiction differently and the archive mistakes that variance for substantive insight.

### 8. Use hard-case conferences for threshold items

Some judgments predictably sit near boundaries:

- bridge arrangement or durable institution,
- partial municipal competence or empty shell,
- oversight with teeth or oversight by memo,
- closure proof or improved narrative,
- independent review or prestigious self-marking.

The archive should prefer a short hard-case conference for such items, with the threshold question stated plainly and the competing anchor cases named.

### 9. Carry calibration through drafting, not only scoring

Reviewer drift often re-enters during prose drafting.

A team may agree on the score but describe it in language that sounds harsher or softer than the scale implies. The archive should therefore run a drafting moderation pass asking:

- does the prose match the lane score,
- does the confidence language match the support set,
- and does the severity clock match the stated deficiency rather than the drafter’s rhetorical style?

### 10. Monitor team drift across time, not only within a single mission

Calibration is not a one-mission ritual.

The archive should prefer some compact periodic check on whether later teams:

- cluster at easier or harsher thresholds than earlier teams,
- use contradiction residue differently,
- change the minimum evidence expected for similar findings,
- or quietly widen the meaning of temporary exceptions.

That drift log is the difference between a living standard and seasonal reviewer culture.

### 11. Recalibrate openly when the tool changes

Sometimes the archive should change its own standards.

When that happens, the review line should not pretend continuity. It should issue a short recalibration note saying:

- what changed,
- which old anchors are no longer controlling,
- whether historical scores remain comparable,
- and what transition rule applies.

Otherwise teams compare unlike verdicts across revisions.

### 12. Publish a public-safe calibration note

`795` should often be able to publish a very small outward explanation of the review line’s calibration discipline, such as:

- the existence of score anchors,
- the use of comparator-matched precedents,
- the existence of moderation checkpoints,
- and whether any material departures from earlier precedent occurred.

This gives outsiders more than “the team applied the methodology consistently” without forcing publication of the whole internal pack.

### 13. Keep calibration tight enough to live inside this archive

The archive should prefer:

- short anchor notes rather than thick manuals,
- compact precedent summaries rather than large case bundles,
- stable cross-references to earlier notes rather than duplicated theory,
- and named departures rather than endless commentary.

This note is therefore not a license to build a giant assessor bureaucracy. It is a rule for preserving comparability without exploding the archive.

## Minimum fields for a calibration pack

| Field | What it tells later actors | Minimum form | Typical failure if missing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Lane** | which constitutional question is being calibrated | one lane label | one general mood spreads across all judgments |
| **Anchor examples** | what clearly fits each band or category | short worked examples | reviewers improvise thresholds mission by mission |
| **Evidence minimum** | what support is ordinarily needed | one short rule | confidence and severity drift apart |
| **Contradiction rule** | how contrary evidence changes the verdict | one short rule | similar contradiction produces different outcomes |
| **Precedent references** | which earlier comparable cases matter | short case list | every team behaves as if history began with them |
| **Departure log** | why the team diverged from anchor or precedent | optional short note | learning and inconsistency become indistinguishable |
| **Recalibration date** | when the pack was last refreshed | short date | outdated anchors silently govern new tools |

## Minimal review-calibration grammar

| Review problem | Calibration response | What it prevents | Next use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **same tool, different reviewer instincts** | opening moderation plus worked anchors | score drift by personality | mission method under `800` |
| **prestige precedent from the wrong peer class** | comparator check before citing prior case | postcard benchmarking and admiration bias | comparator discipline under `798` |
| **contradictory evidence near a threshold** | hard-case conference plus contradiction rule | one team scoring through doubt while another freezes | evidence and finding grammar under `806` / `801` |

## Use rule for the archive

When later notes need to say not just **how a serious scope review was run** but **how similar cases stay judged in a similar way across missions**, the archive should usually do these things in order:

1. **use `791` to declare the benchmark lanes and tripwires that matter**,
2. **use `798` to define the right comparator class before precedent is invoked**,
3. **use `800` to convene the mission and schedule one opening moderation checkpoint**,
4. **use `807` to ensure the people taking part in that moderation count as sufficiently independent to judge at all**,
5. **use `806` to keep the worked examples, anchor exhibits, and departure notes tied to stable evidence objects**,
6. **use this note to assign score anchors, precedent references, contradiction rules, and documented departures**,
7. **use `801` so the final findings and confidence labels stay inside the calibrated meanings rather than reviewer tone**,
8. **use `793` when the calibration pack itself needs a periodic refresh or when a tool change breaks comparability across time**,
9. **use `795` to publish the compact public-safe calibration explanation rather than merely asserting methodological consistency**.

The archive should not let constitutional judgments depend on who happened to be in the room that week.

## Guardrails

- Do not let one precedent count unless its comparator class under `798` is explicit.
- Do not treat a charismatic lead reviewer as a substitute for anchors and moderation.
- Do not let contradiction handling vary invisibly from team to team.
- Do not change thresholds mid-mission without a documented recalibration note.
- Do not let drafting rhetoric outrun the actual lane score and confidence class.
- Do not retain huge precedent bodies where short case notes and citations will do.

## Failure modes

- **reviewer mood constitutionalism** — the same facts score differently depending on who is holding the pen.
- **anchor drift** — score bands remain named but their practical meaning quietly shifts.
- **precedent cosplay** — teams cite famous cases with no comparator fit.
- **contradiction roulette** — contrary evidence changes the result only when some reviewers happen to dislike ambiguity.
- **drafting inflation** — a moderate finding is written as a crisis, or a severe finding as a mild caution.
- **tool revision laundering** — standards change but historical comparability is falsely preserved.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Could two teams point to the same lane-specific anchors before they begin scoring?
2. Is there a visible rule for when contradictory evidence lowers confidence, blocks scoring, or triggers more work?
3. Are prior cases cited only after comparator fit has been declared?
4. Can the team explain why it departed from a prior anchor without pretending the precedent never existed?
5. Does the prose in the final finding register match the actual lane score and severity clock?
6. Is there a visible refresh point for the calibration pack when the tool or archive standard changes?
7. Would an outsider learn more than “the methodology was applied consistently” from the public proof pack?
