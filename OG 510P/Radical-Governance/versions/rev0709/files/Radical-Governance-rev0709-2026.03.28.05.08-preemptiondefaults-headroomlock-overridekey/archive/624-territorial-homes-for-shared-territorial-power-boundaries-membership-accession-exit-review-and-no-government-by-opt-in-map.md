# 624 — Territorial homes for shared territorial power: boundaries, membership, accession, exit, review, and no government by opt-in map

## One-line thesis

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, it should stop living on voluntary membership residue, project geography, or host-city convenience and instead acquire an explicit territorial home: a publicly declared boundary, named member units, clear rules for accession, exit, annexation or detachment, periodic but not perpetual review, and one authoritative map that matches the powers, money, plans, and obligations the territory actually carries.

## Why this matters

The archive already says when co-operation becomes government, and it now says what democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, participatory, knowledge, service, planning, capital, regulatory, contract, workforce, digital, emergency, and redress homes that government should have. What it still lacked was the **territorial analogue**.

That omission matters because many live metropolitan or regional arrangements still govern through unstable geography. A transport compact has one membership. A growth board has another. A resilience platform uses a third map. A funding programme covers a fourth area. The public hears the word “metropolitan” or “regional,” but the real constitution is a shifting collage of participating municipalities, corridor exceptions, opt-in districts, and ministerial carve-outs. No one can tell where the territory begins and ends, who belongs, who pays, who must comply, or how the map can change.

Sometimes this is defensible because the arrangement is still only narrow co-operation. But once the body is making recurring territorial bargains on mobility, land, housing, resilience, major infrastructure, redistribution, or shared services at system scale, that fluidity becomes a constitutional defect rather than a mere organisational quirk. A government that cannot say where it governs is inviting blame-shifting, selective exit, strategic holdout, and opaque overreach.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine for the moment when a shared tier should stop inheriting its geography from convenience and acquire an honest territorial map of its own.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from functional territory, but do not stop at statistical description

Commuting zones, labour markets, watersheds, corridors, service sheds, hazard footprints, and growth systems often explain why a shared body appears in the first place. That functional picture matters because municipal boundaries rarely capture the whole geography of shared problems.

But a functional area alone is not yet a governing territory. It may reveal where a polity **should** coordinate, yet it does not by itself decide the authoritative boundary for public law, budget obligations, planning weight, service duties, or representation. The archive should therefore treat functional geography as the starting point for design, not the end of it.

### 2. Keep flexible participation below the constitutional threshold

Below the threshold of real government, opt-in geography can be acceptable. Municipalities may collaborate on one plant, one procurement pool, one dispatch center, one shared data service, or one corridor project without freezing a general-purpose territorial map. This is the zone for bounded co-operation, pilot geography, and reversible partnership.

The archive should therefore avoid the opposite mistake of forcing every collaborative arrangement into a permanent territorial shell. Flexibility is honest when the function is narrow, the powers are limited, and exit is still thinkable without constitutional damage.

### 3. Fix the map once the body becomes the real home of repeated territorial bargaining

Once a metropolitan or regional body starts making repeated cross-boundary bargains that shape the territory as a whole, the polity should prefer a declared and authoritative map. Transport pricing, housing allocation logic, strategic land release, major capital sequencing, resilience investment, water-security planning, common operating standards, redistributive grant formulas, and system-wide emergency obligations should not float on a partnership list that changes with each negotiation cycle.

At that point the boundary is no longer just an implementation detail. It is part of the constitution of the shared tier.

### 4. Prefer one named territorial membership set over sector-by-sector membership drift

Real shared government can still use special-purpose districts or sub-territorial service zones where the function genuinely demands it. But the archive should reject the habit in which the same supposed metropolitan or regional tier has one membership for planning, another for transport, another for resilience, another for finance, and another for participation, with no clear rule tying them together.

A tier that governs territorially should have one primary membership set and one authoritative public map. Sectoral variation should be the justified exception, not the invisible norm.

### 5. Build boundary criteria that combine function, legibility, and governability

An honest territorial home should not be drawn by aesthetics, party advantage, or attachment to inherited lines alone. Boundary criteria should combine at least three things:

- **functional fit**: does the map capture the real shared system reasonably well,
- **civic legibility**: can residents and institutions understand whether they are inside or outside,
- **governability**: is the territory administrable, financeable, and representable without producing permanent deadlock or unworkable fragmentation.

This means the archive should resist both extremes: a purely technocratic map that ignores political life, and a purely inherited map that ignores the territory the institutions are actually governing.

### 6. Publish accession, annexation, detachment, and exit rules before conflict arrives

Territorial honesty requires more than a fixed boundary on day one. It also requires visible change rules. The chartering law or equivalent hard-law basis should say:

- how a neighboring municipality or district may join,
- when annexation or inclusion can be initiated without local invitation,
- what tests justify detachment or exit,
- what happens to assets, debts, staff, records, and ongoing contracts if the map changes,
- and which public consultation, legislative, and review routes apply.

A territory without visible change rules invites government by brinkmanship. Every later map dispute becomes an improvised constitutional crisis.

### 7. Make boundary review periodic, evidence-based, and hard to game

A real territorial home should not be frozen forever. Functional geographies move. Commuting shifts. Urbanisation sprawls. Shrinking territories contract. New infrastructure remakes catchments. Cross-border labour markets emerge. A clean design therefore needs some review path.

But review should be periodic rather than continuous, criteria-bound rather than discretionary, and evidence-led rather than hostage to each annual bargaining round. The archive should prefer review clocks, published datasets, and threshold tests over permanently open-ended map politics.

### 8. Distinguish exit from opt-out

Exit from a territorial tier is a constitutional event. Opt-out from one programme is an implementation choice. The archive should keep those distinct.

If a body is genuine metropolitan or regional government, member units should not be able to avoid common burdens simply by refusing participation in one sector while continuing to benefit from the whole territorial settlement. Programme flexibility can exist, but it should not dissolve the integrity of the shared tier. Otherwise the map is fixed only for claims on others and fluid for obligations to others.

### 9. Use subzones and overlays sparingly, and keep them subordinate to the primary map

Some territories need internal subzones: fare zones, flood zones, service districts, school catchments, environmental overlays, or phased growth areas. These can be valid design tools. But they should remain subordinate to the primary territorial constitution.

The archive should reject the pattern where the main territorial map becomes unreadable because too many overlays quietly take over the real work of governing. Overlays are implementation instruments, not substitutes for a named territorial home.

### 10. Treat cross-border and polycentric territories as reasons for stronger mapping discipline, not for map nihilism

Some metropolitan and regional systems are especially hard to draw. They may cross regional lines, state lines, or national borders. They may be polycentric rather than dominated by one core city. They may have strong rural-urban interdependence rather than one obvious agglomeration.

These cases do not eliminate the need for a territorial home. They increase it. Where one map cannot carry all functions, the polity should at least publish the primary territorial constitution plus any bounded secondary geographies, and explain why they differ. Difficulty is not an excuse for geographic fog.

### 11. Dock the territorial map to the rest of the constitutional kit

The boundary of a shared tier should line up with the other constitutional homes already described in the archive. The electorate, fiscal base, plan geography, capital pipeline, observatory basemap, service obligations, emergency footprint, regulatory jurisdiction, complaint routes, and intergovernmental standing should all refer back to the same primary territory unless there is a stated exception.

The archive should therefore treat unexplained map divergence as a warning sign of deeper institutional dishonesty.

### 12. Preserve local consultation and lawful process for boundary change, but do not let holdout veto impersonate constitutional principle

Boundary changes affect local communities directly and require consultation, explanation, and lawful process. That must remain a hard rule.

But the archive should also resist the opposite dogma that every affected incumbent must hold a practical veto over any territorial correction, even where the existing map is already producing large-scale governance failure. Consultation is a democratic safeguard. It is not a guarantee that every inherited line is permanently right.

## Threshold matrix

| Signal | Still honest co-operation geography | Territorial home now required |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Map use** | one bounded project or service area | shared tier is treated as the governing area for repeated strategic bargains |
| **Membership** | participants vary by narrow task | one primary membership set is needed for law, money, planning, and accountability |
| **Boundary change** | reversible participation changes | accession, annexation, detachment, or exit now alter public obligations and rights |
| **Citizen relation** | residents are affected indirectly through one service | residents need to know whether they live inside a territory with shared rules, burdens, and representation |
| **Institutional fit** | geography can stay flexible without constitutional confusion | divergent maps across planning, finance, services, and elections now produce institutional dishonesty |
| **Review need** | adaptation can happen informally | periodic evidence-based boundary review needs a declared legal route |

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse a statistical functional area with a complete constitutional map.
- Do not force every narrow co-operation arrangement into permanent territorial government.
- Do not let real shared government continue on opt-in geography once law, finance, planning, or service obligations have become territorial.
- Do not allow sector-by-sector membership drift to become the hidden constitution of a territory.
- Do not treat consultation as optional when changing territorial boundaries.
- Do not let consultation harden into a rule that every incumbent can veto any needed territorial correction.
- Do not let subzones, overlays, and corridor exceptions erase the primary map.
- Do not allow entry, exit, annexation, or detachment fights to be improvised after debts, assets, and obligations are already entangled.

## Failure modes

- **government by opt-in map**: a supposed territorial tier still governs through voluntary membership residue and changing partnership geography.
- **map drift**: planning, finance, services, and representation each use different unstated territorial extents.
- **holdout cartography**: one or two incumbents can block correction of a clearly misfit governing area while still benefiting from the shared system.
- **sectoral border shopping**: actors choose the map that benefits them function by function, dissolving responsibility.
- **overlay takeover**: special districts and internal zones become the real constitution while the primary territory turns ceremonial.
- **annexation by ambiguity**: a body gradually acts beyond its understood area because no authoritative map or boundary-change rule was ever published.
- **exit without settlement**: membership changes occur without clear treatment of assets, staff, debt, records, contracts, or representation.

## Practical tests

A polity is drawing the territorial question honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can it identify the primary territorial map of each shared metropolitan or regional tier it has constitutionalized?
2. Can it explain how that map relates to the functional geography that justified the tier in the first place?
3. If maps differ across sectors, are those differences exceptional, published, and justified rather than accidental?
4. Are accession, exit, annexation, detachment, and review rules written before the next conflict?
5. Do the electorate, budget, plan, observatory, service obligations, and complaint routes all refer back to the same primary territory unless a stated exception applies?
6. Are affected communities consulted in due time and by a lawful process when boundaries change?
7. Is there a periodic evidence-based review route that can correct clear misfit without keeping the map permanently negotiable?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared body now makes real territorial bargains, but no one can say with confidence **where the territory actually is, who belongs to it, how the map changes, and which burdens and rights travel with that map**, then the polity has not yet built an honest metropolitan or regional government. It is still governing by **opt-in map**.
