# 766 — Lobbying-transparency and revolving-door necessity tests for ideal governments: registers, meeting logs, gift rules, cooling-off, advice lanes, and no policy by invisible influence

## One-line thesis

Ideal governments should treat lobbying and influence as **a legitimate democratic input that becomes dangerous when it is invisible, unequal, or privately entangled with officeholding**: they should disclose who seeks to influence whom, on whose behalf, about what, through which channels, under which gift and hospitality rules, and with what pre- and post-public-employment constraints, so policy is not quietly written by invisible influence.

## Why this matters

The archive already says a great deal about **public integrity by scope** (`499`), **anti-corruption bodies** (`564`), **public appointments** (`760`), **political finance** (`764`), and **access to information** (`765`). What it still lacked as one compact operating rule was a cleaner answer to a different but adjacent question:

**how should an ideal polity make policy influence visible before private contact hardens into public decision?**

Democracies should not criminalize advocacy, expertise, petition, representation, or organized interests as such. Public officials need outside information, and affected people need ways to press their case. The danger is not that influence exists. The danger is that influence becomes selectively legible, professionally opaque, or structurally unequal: a public meeting is minuted while an unofficial dinner is not; a campaign donor becomes a policy adviser without visibility; a former minister returns as a lobbyist before cooling-off has even begun; a trade association speaks for multiple undisclosed clients; or a consultancy reaches the same officials through strategy, law, media, procurement, and lobbying without any public trail that these contacts belong to the same influence campaign.

Ideal-government design therefore needs a doctrine of **lobbying-transparency necessity**. The live design question is not whether governments should hear from affected interests. It is whether the polity can see enough of the influence market to judge who is being heard, on whose behalf, under what constraints, and with what revolving-door risk.

## Necessity table

| Necessity test | What counts as proof | Honest design consequence | Default answer if proof is weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Need to make organized influence visible** | policy choices are shaped through repeated professional advocacy, not only open hearings or ordinary constituent contact | lobbying register and official-side contact disclosure | ad hoc voluntary transparency |
| **Need to know whose interests are being represented** | intermediaries, coalitions, firms, or trade bodies stand between decision-makers and ultimate beneficiaries | disclose clients, controlling interests, represented funders, and relevant support | speaker names only |
| **Need to prevent contact opacity around consequential decisions** | ministers, legislators, senior officials, regulators, or mayors take decisions after private meetings, calls, dinners, events, or platform messaging | meeting logs, subject-matter disclosure, and record duties for designated public office holders | reliance on occasional diary releases |
| **Need to manage revolving-door incentives** | officials can favour private actors while in office or carry insider access directly into advocacy afterwards | pre- and post-public-employment rules, cooling-off, switch-side restrictions, advice lanes, and monitoring | general ethics rhetoric only |
| **Need for credible compliance rather than performative registration** | hidden influence can survive through under-reporting, front groups, or stale data | auditing, sanctions, de-registration powers, and searchable public data | self-certification without checking |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat lobbying as legitimate participation, but refuse invisible policymaking

The archive should not confuse lobbying with corruption by definition. Lobbying can improve policy when it brings expertise, affected-party knowledge, and adversarial challenge into view. But legitimacy depends on visibility, fair access, and integrity rules. Ideal governments should therefore regulate **opacity and undue influence**, not the mere existence of representation.

### 2. Define covered influence activity broadly enough to catch the real market for access

A narrow definition limited to professional lobbyists making formal appointments with ministers will miss much of the real influence landscape. Coverage should be broad enough to catch relevant communications intended to influence legislation, regulation, procurement design where appropriate, grants, licences, strategies, and major executive decisions.

### 3. Combine actor-side registration with official-side meeting disclosure

Registers alone can become incomplete and meeting logs alone can become fragmented. Ideal systems should combine both sides where stakes justify it: those who professionally seek influence disclose who they are, whom they represent, and the subject matter; designated public office holders disclose relevant meetings, contacts, gifts where reportable, and other structured interactions.

### 4. Publish the fields that let outsiders reconstruct an influence campaign

Useful disclosure usually includes the identity of the actor, client or represented interest, subject matter, targeted institution or office holder, date, relevant file or decision if known, and where appropriate the communication channel. A register that names actors but hides objectives, targets, or represented interests leaves the public guessing about the actual policy campaign.

### 5. Regulate gifts, hospitality, sponsored travel, and advisory side-doors as part of the same integrity system

Influence does not travel only through formal meetings. Gifts, hospitality, externally funded travel, advisory boards, secondments, strategic retreats, and temporary task forces may all become channels of obligation or soft capture. Ideal governments should type these as part of the same influence-control architecture rather than treat them as unrelated etiquette problems.

### 6. Build risk-based revolving-door rules instead of relying on vague loyalty expectations

The movement of people between public and private roles is not inherently corrupt, but it can create strong incentives for favour trading, insider advantage, switch-side representation, and regulatory capture. Rules should therefore be risk-based and role-specific: cooling-off periods, restrictions on lobbying former institutions, bans on using confidential information, limits on representing parties in files previously handled, and pre-public-employment screens for entrants from regulated sectors where they would immediately supervise former employers or clients.

### 7. Provide advice lanes and disclosure duties before violations occur

Officials and prospective employers often need clarity before taking action. Ideal systems should therefore include ethics advice, advance opinions where lawful, notification duties for planned outside roles, and clear documentation rules. This reduces both inadvertent breach and the temptation to leave everything until scandal.

### 8. Scale the rules honestly across levels of government

National ministers and legislators usually need the strongest rules, but local and metropolitan governments also face intense development, procurement, franchise, and land-use influence pressures. Small governments may not need large standalone registries, yet they still need simple meeting-log, recusal, gift, and cooling-off disciplines, possibly through shared platforms or common state-level infrastructure.

### 9. Keep the data public, searchable, and linkable to the surrounding decision record

Influence disclosure is most useful when it can be connected to consultations, bills, delegated legislation, procurement notices, subsidy decisions, campaign-finance data, and official diaries. Searchable structured data matters more than a pile of scanned forms.

### 10. Enforce proportionately but for real

Late filing, inaccurate reporting, unregistered lobbying where required, unlawful switch-side advocacy, and improper gifts need a visible escalation ladder: correction, publication of breach, fines, suspension or loss of access where lawful, referral for further sanction, and public reporting on compliance. Without enforcement, the system teaches actors how much opacity they can get away with.

## Guardrails

- Do not define lobbying so narrowly that only a small professional subset is visible while influence migrates elsewhere.
- Do not rely on actor registration without also improving official-side contact disclosure for high-risk offices.
- Do not treat gifts, hospitality, advisory roles, and sponsored access as outside the lobbying problem.
- Do not write revolving-door rules so vaguely that they cannot guide or sanction anyone.
- Do not publish unusable disclosure fragments that cannot be searched, compared, or linked to actual decisions.

## Failure modes

- **policy by invisible influence**: consequential policy is shaped through contacts and intermediaries the public cannot see.
- **front-group laundering**: represented interests hide behind coalitions, consultants, or civil-society branding without funder visibility.
- **register without targets**: the public knows who registered, but not whom they contacted or what they sought.
- **revolving-door signalling**: officials align present decisions with hoped-for future employment or access.
- **compliance theatre**: forms are filed, but no one audits, cross-checks, or sanctions them.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can tell you **which public decisions were made** but cannot also show **who sought to influence them, on whose behalf, through which contacts, under what meeting-log and gift rules, with what revolving-door constraints, and under what audit or sanction regime**, then it still has **policy participation without a constitutional rule against invisible influence**.
