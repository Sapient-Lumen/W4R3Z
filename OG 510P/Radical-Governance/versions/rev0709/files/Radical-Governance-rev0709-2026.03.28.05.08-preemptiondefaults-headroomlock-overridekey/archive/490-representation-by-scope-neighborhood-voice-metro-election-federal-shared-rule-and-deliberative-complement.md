# 490 — Representation by scope: neighborhood voice, metro election, federal shared rule, and deliberative complement

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should not clone one representation formula at every scale: it should use close, participatory voice where place knowledge is richest, directly elected councils for city and metro systems, population-based representation for common citizenship, territorial shared-rule chambers where constituent units need a federal voice, and representative deliberative mini-publics as a complement for hard cross-cutting questions.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a better answer to **what each level should govern** and **how decentralization should be financed**. It still needs a compact answer to **how each level should be represented**. That is a distinct design problem.

A block, a city, a metropolitan region, a federation, and a global institution do not need the same democratic machinery in miniature. Small scopes can sustain thicker participation and sharper local visibility. Larger scopes need aggregation, durable parties, administrative continuity, and fair ways to represent both persons and places. Federations in particular must reconcile two democratic facts at once: citizens are political equals, and constituent units are not merely administrative departments.

The ideal response is therefore mixed constitutional design rather than electoral monoculture. The question is not which one mechanism is morally pure. The question is which mechanism best fits the scale, problem class, and correction path.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Do not repeat one electoral form at every scale

The archive should reject the instinct to copy-paste one governance form downward and upward. Single-winner mayoral logics, national party-list logics, neighborhood assembly logics, and treaty-body negotiation logics solve different coordination problems. Treating them as interchangeable produces fake symmetry instead of good government.

### 2. Keep micro-local institutions close, legible, and participation-heavy

At the block, ward, village, tribal, and neighborhood scale, the ideal institution is usually not a miniature parliament. It is some combination of:

- assembly,
- ward or neighborhood council,
- petition and recallable delegate channels,
- participatory budgeting,
- and direct problem-solving bodies for shared spaces and small commons.

This is the scale where the archive should prefer thick voice over ideological spectacle, because participants can often see the consequences of decisions directly.

### 3. Require direct democratic legitimacy for municipalities and metros

Cities and metropolitan systems should not be governed mainly through informal bargaining among indirectly delegated officeholders when they exercise real planning, transport, housing, policing, water, or emergency powers. If a metro body can tax, regulate, or bind the region, it should carry direct democratic legitimacy strong enough to match the scale of its authority.

### 4. Use population-based representation where equal citizenship is at stake

At large territorial scales, especially the national lower house, the archive should usually prefer representation formulas that reflect citizens as political equals rather than treating territory alone as the basis of power. Exact electoral design can vary, but the guiding discipline is clear: equal citizenship should be represented as persons, not dissolved into a patchwork of inherited geography.

### 5. Use federal shared-rule chambers where places must co-govern the union

Where a country is genuinely federal or strongly regionalized, a second chamber can justify itself when it gives constituent units a visible role in central decision-making. The ideal second chamber is not duplication for its own sake. It is a shared-rule device for questions that materially affect the constitutional balance, territorial finance, or powers of the constituent units.

### 6. Limit territorial veto power to the domains that justify it

A chamber that represents provinces, states, regions, or Länder should not automatically block every ordinary law merely because it exists. Its strongest role should usually attach to:

- competence allocation,
- intergovernmental finance,
- constitutional amendment,
- territorial integrity and boundary change,
- and legislation that substantially alters the powers or burdens of constituent units.

This keeps federal shared rule real without converting the entire polity into permanent territorial deadlock.

### 7. Institutionalize deliberative mini-publics as complements, not alibis

Randomly selected, representative deliberative bodies can improve legitimacy and problem-solving for long-horizon, values-laden, or technically dense questions. They work best when they are clearly linked to public decision-making, are representative enough to be trusted, and do not pretend to replace elected accountability. The archive should treat them as a complement for difficult agenda-setting, value clarification, and recommendation formation, not as a theatrical substitute for governing.

### 8. Fit executive design to legislative reality

Electoral design should not be chosen in isolation from executive form, party system, judicial review, and fiscal constitution. A directly elected executive with no durable legislative basis, or a fragmented legislature without coalition discipline, can turn formal representation into chronic governability crisis. The ideal archive should therefore evaluate representation systems as part of a whole institutional stack.

### 9. Make the global layer representative by function, not by pretending to be a world state

The global layer should rarely imitate a domestic nation-state. Its legitimacy usually comes from treaty consent, state representation, narrow mandates, transparency, and problem-specific participation by affected communities and experts. Where broader public voice is needed, advisory or deliberative mechanisms can supplement treaty machinery, but the archive should resist the fantasy that one undifferentiated global parliament would solve every planetary problem.

## Guardrails

- Do not appoint metropolitan rulers to govern millions without direct public legitimacy.
- Do not confuse hyper-local participation with an adequate design for macro redistribution or rights protection.
- Do not let federal upper houses become duplicate lower houses with different furniture.
- Do not use citizens’ assemblies as a way to outsource hard decisions while elected actors dodge ownership.
- Do not discuss electoral systems without also discussing executive-legislative fit.

## Failure modes

- **scale-cloning**: one institutional form is copied across every level whether or not it fits.
- **metro technocracy**: functional urban systems are governed by bargaining cartels rather than electorally legible authority.
- **territorial over-veto**: an upper chamber blocks ordinary democratic government far beyond the domains that justify shared-rule protection.
- **assembly theater**: deliberative forums create legitimacy optics without any binding uptake path.
- **representation mismatch**: institutions represent people where places should matter, or places where equal citizenship should dominate.

## Practical tests

A representation architecture passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does each scale use a representation form suited to its problem class rather than cloned from another scale?
2. Do city and metro bodies with real coercive powers carry direct democratic legitimacy?
3. Is equal citizenship represented clearly at the scale where common rights and redistribution are made?
4. If there is a territorial second chamber, is its strongest authority limited to domains that genuinely affect constituent-unit self-government?
5. Are deliberative mini-publics linked to actual decision routes rather than used as decorative consultation?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity uses the same representational logic for **the block, the metro, the federation, and the planet**, it is probably not honoring democracy at every scale; it is imposing **institutional monoculture on different problems**.
