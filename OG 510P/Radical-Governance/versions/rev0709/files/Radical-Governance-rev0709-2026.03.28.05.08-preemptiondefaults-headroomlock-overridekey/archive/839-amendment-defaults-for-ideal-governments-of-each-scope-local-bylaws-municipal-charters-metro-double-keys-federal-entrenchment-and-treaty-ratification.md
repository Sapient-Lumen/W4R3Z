# 839 — Amendment defaults for ideal governments of each scope: local bylaws, municipal charters, metro double keys, federal entrenchment, and treaty ratification

## One-line thesis

The archive should usually prefer different amendment grammars by scale: **micro-local rules should change publicly and cheaply; municipalities through charter or organic-law procedures with real community consultation for boundary or status change; metropolitan and regional governments through double-key amendment where shared systems or constituent bargains are real; national constitutions through heightened but repairable procedures with territorial participation for settlement-class changes; and continental or global regimes through explicit treaty amendment and ratification rather than executive side deals, secretariat drift, or permanent provisionalism**.

## Why this matters

The archive already knows a great deal about constitutional change, especially in `498`, `565`, `595`, `630`, `668`, `729`, and `819`. But those notes answer the problem from several angles at once: bargain change, territorial reorganization, treaty amendment, ratification, and cross-boundary membership.

What the archive still lacked was one compact answer-key note for the narrower recurring question: **how should each scale of government be allowed to change itself once it already exists?** Without that compression layer, later archive work risks two opposite mistakes. One is **silent redesign**, where powers, boundaries, rights, or fiscal burdens drift by ordinary law, side letter, budget blackmail, or emergency carry-over. The other is **constitutional petrification**, where even obviously needed repair becomes impossible because every adjustment is treated like founding-day sacred text.

The archive therefore needs one short note on **amendment defaults by scope**. The live question is not whether constitutions should ever change. It is **what kind of change grammar honestly fits each scale, and what kind does not**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Sort amendment by settlement thickness before sorting by institutional vanity

A serious polity should not use one amendment lane for everything.

The archive should usually distinguish at least five classes:

- **operational or procedural change** inside an already-settled institution;
- **charter or organic-law change** that materially alters how one tier governs itself;
- **boundary or status change** that alters who belongs, what territory counts, or what kind of authority the unit is;
- **division-of-powers or rights-floor change** that alters the vertical bargain of the stack;
- and **treaty or compact amendment** above the state.

Once these classes are mixed together, either trivial changes become impossible or foundational change becomes too easy.

### 2. Micro-local bodies should change their own rules publicly, cheaply, and without sovereign theater

Neighborhood, ward, village, block, and other steward-scale bodies should usually have the lightest amendment grammar.

The honest default here is:

- public amendment of standing orders, meeting rules, committee arrangements, and tiny stewardship practices;
- visible notice and minutes so change is legible to the people who actually use the body;
- simple local votes rather than heavy constitutional ritual;
- and quick correction if a rule proves exclusionary, captured, or unworkable.

But micro-local bodies should **not** be free to amend themselves into miniature sovereigns. They should not create lasting debt powers, heavy coercive powers, exclusionary franchise rules, or shadow constitutions by local enthusiasm alone. The cheap amendment lane here fits **procedure and stewardship**, not the creation of a new state.

### 3. Municipal government should have a real charter lane, with community consultation for map or status change

Municipal government is the first tier that normally deserves a true charter or organic-law amendment grammar.

The archive should usually prefer a structure in which:

- ordinary internal administrative adjustment can happen through bylaw or ordinance;
- charter-class changes go through a higher lane than ordinary municipal legislation;
- boundary, amalgamation, split, incorporation, or status changes require prior consultation of the affected community and visible transition planning;
- and the municipality cannot be silently rewritten by national ministry circular, budget choke point, or emergency residue.

This means the city should not need national constitutional drama to adjust a committee structure. But it also means the city should not wake up to a new map, new status, or hollowed-out charter because another tier treated municipal government as administrative furniture.

### 4. Metropolitan government should usually change through a double key once shared urban power is real

When a metropolitan body governs real shared systems, amendment should not live entirely with one host city, one minister, or one conference of incumbents.

The archive should usually prefer some version of **double consent** for settlement-class metropolitan change:

- a whole-territory legitimacy key, such as the metropolitan assembly, electorate, or formally constituted metro chamber;
- and a member-unit or constituent key where the metropolitan form is built from municipalities or counties that remain constitutionally meaningful principals.

Not every metro change needs the heaviest lock. Technical annexes, service schedules, or operating rules can travel by lighter lanes if the founding instrument clearly authorizes that. But changes to territory, core powers, finance, or direct legitimacy should not happen by side memorandum or host-government dominance.

### 5. Regional, provincial, or state government should have entrenched amendment wherever shared rule or territorial self-government is real

Once a region or constituent unit is part of the constitutional bargain, its amendment grammar should be thicker than municipal self-organization.

The archive should usually expect:

- a protected role for the regional legislature, electorate, or both in regional constitutional change;
- stronger locks for changes that affect the division of powers, fiscal autonomy, language settlement, institutional identity, or participation in shared rule;
- and explicit federal or national procedures when subnational change also alters the wider bargain.

The archive should distrust any design in which a central majority can substantially rewrite regional self-government through ordinary national law while still claiming that territorial autonomy remains constitutionally real.

### 6. National constitutions should be harder to amend than ordinary law, but not so rigid that repair requires rupture

At national level, amendment should normally be demanding, but not mythically impossible.

The archive should usually prefer combinations such as:

- legislative supermajorities;
- double-decision procedures, time delays, or intervening elections for settlement-class change;
- territorial participation where federal balance is affected;
- and referendum only for constitutionally defined classes of amendment, not as a free-floating cleansing ritual for any executive project.

The key design target is **repairable entrenchment**: strong enough to block convenience amendment, weak enough to allow lawful repair before crisis hardens into rupture, coup logic, or revolutionary bypass.

### 7. Continental and global regimes should amend only by explicit treaty text and ratification, with lighter lanes only where the treaty itself authorizes them

Above the state, the archive should usually reject both ambient rigidity and ambient drift.

The honest default is:

- explicit treaty amendment or protocol routes for settlement-class change;
- participation rights for all parties in proposals to amend a multilateral treaty;
- ratification according to each member’s own constitutional process where the constitutive instrument requires it;
- and clearly delimited lighter lanes for annexes, schedules, or technical updates only when the treaty itself provides for them.

This means continental and global bodies should not quietly rewrite their own mandate through conference conclusions, secretariat FAQs, interpretive inflation, or permanent “provisional” practices that never return to formal consent.

### 8. Keep amendment legible by scale: light below, double-key in the middle, entrenched at the constitutional bargain, explicit above

A serious multi-level order should be able to explain its change grammar in plain language.

People should be able to tell:

- which local rules can be changed cheaply and publicly;
- which municipal matters require charter-class treatment or community consultation;
- which metropolitan or regional changes require both territory-wide and constituent consent;
- which national amendments need supermajorities, federal participation, or selective referendum;
- and which upper-tier changes require treaty text and ratification instead of ambient practice.

If nobody can say that simply, the polity probably has **change power without an honest amendment map**.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat every operational tweak as settlement-class amendment.
- Do not let municipalities or regions be remapped or hollowed out without prior consultation and transition law.
- Do not let metropolitan or federal bargains be rewritten by the wrong organ acting alone.
- Do not use referendums as free-floating shortcuts around constitutionally assigned institutions.
- Do not let treaty bodies widen themselves by guidance, emergency residue, donor leverage, or secretariat habit.

## Failure modes

- **micro-sovereign bylaw creep**: tiny bodies use cheap amendment lanes to claim powers they were never meant to hold.
- **map surgery by ministry**: municipal boundaries or status change without genuine local consultation or lawful continuity planning.
- **metro capture amendment**: one host government or incumbent cartel rewrites a shared-city settlement alone.
- **silent recentralization**: regional or federal autonomy is emptied by ordinary national law without openly amending the bargain.
- **treaty drift**: continental or global institutions enlarge themselves through practice, interpretation, or provisionalism rather than express amendment and ratification.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity cannot say **which changes are cheap, which are charter-class, which require double consent, which alter the constitutional bargain, and which need treaty amendment and ratification**, then it does not yet have an honest amendment constitution; it has **change by drift, panic, or veto folklore**.
