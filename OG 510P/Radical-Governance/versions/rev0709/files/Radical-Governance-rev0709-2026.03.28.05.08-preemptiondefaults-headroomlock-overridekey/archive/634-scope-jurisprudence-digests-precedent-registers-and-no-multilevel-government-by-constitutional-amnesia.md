# 634 — Scope jurisprudence digests, precedent registers, and no multilevel government by constitutional amnesia

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should preserve competence, self-government, and rescoping rulings in a public **scope-jurisprudence digest** linked to the canonical scope register, so settlements of who may govern what become accessible constitutional memory rather than specialist folklore, serial relitigation, or unpublished administrative custom.

## Why this matters

The archive already says more clearly **how scope should be assigned** (`602`-`605`, `628`-`633`), **how competence conflicts should be routed** (`492`), and **how local and regional self-government should reach judicial remedy** (`593`). What it still lacked was the memory layer that sits after those rulings arrive.

That gap matters because multilevel systems often lose doctrinal memory in a very ordinary way. A court settles a competence fight. A ministry loses a supervision case. A regional or local authority wins consultation relief. An emergency hold is cut back. A transfer condition is struck down or re-read. Yet months later the live guidance, grant terms, digital workflows, and public explanations still reflect the pre-ruling world. The result is not only legal untidiness. It is **constitutional amnesia**: the polity technically had an umpire, but the settlement never became durable operating memory.

The archive should therefore prefer a compact **scope-jurisprudence digest** docked to the canonical scope register. If the register says where authority currently lives, the digest should say **which rulings, opinions, and settlements got it there**, what exactly they changed, how strongly they bind, and whether later law, later cases, or later territorial reform have narrowed or superseded them.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Keep one public digest beside the canonical scope register

A serious multilevel polity should maintain one public digest of landmark and operationally important rulings about:

- competence allocation,
- local and regional self-government,
- supervision limits,
- consultation and consent failures,
- fiscal congruence and transfer conditions,
- emergency override,
- boundary and territorial change,
- and handback or restoration of ordinary control.

This digest is not a substitute for full judgments, statutes, or official gazettes. It is the maintained bridge between raw legal material and the live scope register.

### 2. Record holdings at lane level, not only at sector level

A ruling rarely says simply “transport is regional” or “housing is local.” More often it distinguishes financing from delivery, rulemaking from implementation, consultation from veto, or ordinary supervision from delegated-task control.

The digest should therefore record the **affected lane or lanes** for each decision. Otherwise case memory will be too blunt to keep the register honest.

### 3. Give every entry a minimal constitutional-memory packet

Each digest entry should include, at minimum:

- the deciding body,
- decision date and operative date,
- issue type,
- affected function and lane,
- short holding,
- remedy or order,
- whether interim relief was granted,
- the affected territories or authorities,
- the source link,
- the register fields that changed,
- and the current status: binding, persuasive, provisional, superseded, narrowed, or under legislative response.

Without a compact packet, jurisprudence survives only in specialist recollection.

### 4. Update the scope register automatically when a ruling lands

The digest should not sit beside the register as a decorative annex. When a ruling changes the live position, the canonical scope register should be updated in a visible way, with:

- the prior entry,
- the post-ruling entry,
- the date of change,
- the rule or judgment that caused it,
- and any open repair tasks still needed in legislation, finance, guidance, contracts, or software.

This is how the archive avoids the split between “law on paper” and “government in practice.”

### 5. Publish important rulings in accessible, up-to-date, and searchable form

Judicial settlements cannot serve as public constitutional memory if they are buried behind subscription walls, scattered across courts, or delayed until long after officials have improvised around them.

The archive should therefore prefer publication regimes that make important judicial decisions and their essential effects easy to find, promptly available, and kept current enough to matter for operational government.

### 6. Preserve coherence without pretending every ruling is timeless or universal

A digest should help coherence, but it should not freeze the constitution into false mechanical certainty. The archive should therefore distinguish among:

- holdings that bind generally,
- holdings that bind only within a jurisdiction or court hierarchy,
- fact-specific decisions with narrow operational effect,
- and persuasive but non-binding materials such as advisory opinions or review reports.

This helps the polity remember what was actually settled without turning all guidance into fake precedent.

### 7. Keep interim orders and temporary settlements visible

Scope government often changes first through interim relief: a stay on a boundary change, a pause on a transfer condition, a suspension of a supervisory act, a temporary continuation order for services, or a time-boxed restoration plan.

Those provisional outcomes should be visible in the digest and in the register’s exception fields. Otherwise temporary judicial states either vanish from memory or harden into undeclared permanent arrangements.

### 8. Keep losses, rejections, and failed claims in the memory layer too

A digest that preserves only celebrated wins becomes advocacy, not constitutional memory. The archive should prefer recording rejected claims, failed standing theories, refused interim relief, and decisions that upheld a contested higher-tier act.

The point is not to flatter any level of government. It is to stop the stack from being reconstructed only from the cases each actor likes to cite.

### 9. Put stale-precedent checks on the same review clock as the live stack

Some holdings remain central for decades. Others are overtaken by constitutional amendment, territorial reform, new legislation, later cases, or changes in the actual architecture of government.

The digest should therefore support visible status review. When the underlying map, law, or function set changes, old entries should be marked as historic, narrowed, superseded, or still controlling. Constitutional memory should be durable, not fossilised.

### 10. Treat downstream follow-through as part of the ruling, not optional aftercare

A competence judgment is not fully implemented when the opinion is published. It is implemented when the related grant rules, manuals, platform defaults, workflow gates, delegation templates, budget assumptions, and public-facing explanations have been brought into line.

The digest should therefore point to concrete downstream repair tasks and closure status. Otherwise the legal center moves while the administrative periphery keeps governing from stale precedent.

## Guardrails

- Do not let landmark competence or self-government rulings remain buried in specialist case-law memory.
- Do not update the digest without also checking the live scope register for consequential change.
- Do not pretend provisional relief, stayed acts, or under-review arrangements are settled doctrine.
- Do not collapse binding judgments, persuasive reports, and political declarations into one undifferentiated “authority” label.
- Do not preserve only favorable cases and call the result a constitutional memory layer.
- Do not let publication lag, broken search, or inaccessible formats turn public law back into insider knowledge.

## Failure modes

- **constitutional amnesia**: the polity wins or loses major scope cases but the operating stack keeps reflecting the old story.
- **case-law folklore**: only specialists know which ruling actually controls because no public digest exists.
- **register drift after judgment**: the canonical scope map is not revised after a legally material decision.
- **precedent laundering**: a narrow or provisional ruling is narrated as a broad permanent settlement.
- **victory-only memory**: rejected claims and adverse holdings disappear, leaving a partisan doctrine trail.
- **fossilised doctrine**: historic holdings remain presented as current even after later law or map changes.
- **publication without integration**: full judgments are online, but no one can tell what they changed in the live stack.

## Practical tests

A polity is keeping honest scope jurisprudence memory when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is there one public digest of important competence, self-government, and rescoping rulings?
2. Does each entry say which function and lane were actually affected?
3. Are interim orders, final holdings, and current status distinguished clearly?
4. Does the digest link directly to the source decision or official record?
5. Does each legally material ruling trigger a visible check on the canonical scope register?
6. Are rejected claims and adverse rulings kept in the memory layer as well as wins?
7. Can users tell whether a holding is binding, persuasive, provisional, narrowed, or superseded?
8. Are important rulings available in accessible and reasonably up-to-date public form?
9. Do follow-through tasks in guidance, grants, software, contracts, and finance stay visible until closed?
10. When the territorial map or legal framework changes, are old holdings reviewed for continuing force?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can point to **courts, charters, and competence rulings** but cannot also show **one maintained public memory layer that says what those decisions changed in the live scope stack, with status, source, and follow-through**, then it still has **formal adjudication without durable constitutional memory**.
