# 611 — Public-law homes for shared territorial power: legal personality, named competences, rulemaking, and no government by interlocal contract

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should allow municipalities and regions to cooperate by agreement for bounded shared tasks, but once a metropolitan or regional body is doing real territorial government it should have an honest public-law home: legal personality, a named territorial basis, competences prescribed by constitution or statute, a proportionate rulemaking lane, clear relations with member governments and the state, and no government by interlocal contract.

## Why this matters

The archive now contains a threshold doctrine for when co-operation becomes government, plus companion notes on the democratic, fiscal, administrative, and accountability homes of shared territorial power. What it still lacked was the **public-law analogue**: what legal form should follow once a joint territorial body has crossed the line from co-ordination into real government?

That gap matters because live systems often try to govern a territory through memoranda, contribution agreements, host-government workarounds, or consortium charters that were never designed to carry real public power. They set fares, growth priorities, capital plans, infrastructure sequencing, or spatial-development bargains for a whole urban or regional territory, yet their legal basis remains closer to a club contract than to a constitutional actor. The result is a familiar pathology: everyone can feel that a real government exists, but nobody can state plainly **what public-law body it is**, what powers it truly owns, what rules it may make, what limits bind it, or how its powers can be reviewed and amended.

A tighter doctrine is therefore needed. Once shared territorial power becomes real, the polity should stop governing by legal residue and give that power an honest legal home.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Keep interlocal agreement as a tool, not as a permanent constitutional substitute

Agreements among municipalities, regions, or state field offices are useful. They let governments pool staff, build shared facilities, operate joint services, and coordinate bounded spillover problems without forced merger.

But the archive should reject the habit of letting the agreement itself become the whole constitution of a territory. Contracts are good at allocating tasks among signatories. They are weak substitutes for public law once the body starts exercising durable territorial power over residents, capital, plans, and rules.

### 2. Give real shared territorial government legal personality of its own

A body that hires staff, holds assets, enters procurement, borrows, sues or is sued, owns records, or carries continuing duties should not depend entirely on one host municipality or a rotating association secretariat for its legal existence. The archive should therefore prefer separate legal personality once the body becomes a durable territorial institution rather than a short-lived co-ordination vehicle.

Legal personality is not institutional grandeur for its own sake. It is the minimum condition for saying where the body legally begins and ends.

### 3. Prescribe the body’s basic powers and responsibilities in constitution or statute, not only in side agreements

The more a shared territorial body governs, the less acceptable it is for its core powers to live only in memoranda, ministerial letters, or informal leader deals. The archive should therefore prefer a hard-law basis that names:

- the territory or criteria by which the territory is defined,
- the body’s organs and who composes them,
- the competences it owns directly,
- the tasks it performs on delegation,
- the finance and charging powers attached to it,
- the review and amendment route,
- and the defaults for conflicts with municipal, regional, or national law.

This is the legal minimum for turning a shadow constitution into an honest one.

### 4. Separate own competences from delegated tasks and from contractual services

Many live arrangements blur three different things: powers the territorial body owns in its own right, tasks carried out on behalf of another authority, and routine services delivered under contract. The archive should reject this blur.

A serious legal home should distinguish them visibly. **Own competences** are the body’s to govern within law. **Delegated tasks** remain tied to the delegating authority and should carry explicit funding, standards, and supervision rules. **Contracted services** should stay bounded and should not be allowed to impersonate constitutional power. Without this separation, no resident can tell who is really responsible for what.

### 5. Match the legal form to the breadth of the territorial vocation

A narrow shared service may only need a legally constituted joint authority or public corporation with a tightly bounded charter. A co-operative region may need a stronger association with legal personality but limited own competences. A body that repeatedly makes land-use, transport, housing, resilience, utility, or territorial-investment bargains across a whole metro or region may need the legal status of a real subnational government.

The archive should therefore reject one-size-fits-all legal form. The legal home should expand only as far as the actual territorial vocation requires — but once the work is real government, the legal form should stop pretending otherwise.

### 6. Give the body a proportionate rulemaking lane once it governs real territory

Real territorial government often needs more than the ability to spend and coordinate. It may need to adopt binding plans, ordinances, technical regulations, tariff schedules, service rules, land-use instruments, or other normative acts within a clearly bounded field.

The archive should therefore prefer a visible rulemaking lane once the shared body’s work requires binding territorial choices. The lane does not need to mimic a national legislature. But it should be clear which norms the body may make, under what procedure, with what publicity and review, and how those norms sit within the wider hierarchy of law.

### 7. Do not let soft planning or funding leverage become hidden lawmaking

One common evasion is to deny a body formal rulemaking authority while allowing it to govern through “soft” metropolitan plans, grant conditions, investment sequencing, or mandatory conformity expectations that everyone in practice must follow. The archive should treat this as a warning sign.

If a body’s plans or funding conditions function as territory-shaping law, then the legal settlement should say so honestly and provide the matching deliberation, publication, challenge, and amendment machinery. The polity should not hide real norm-setting inside informal leverage.

### 8. Keep member governments and the state in the picture, but make the conflict rules explicit

An honest public-law home does not mean metropolitan or regional supremacy in all things. Municipalities, regions, and national governments will still coexist, share fields, and sometimes overlap. What matters is that the stack states how those relations work.

The archive should therefore prefer explicit rules on consultation, concurrence where needed, paramountcy or hierarchy where unavoidable, delegation boundaries, supervisory legality review, and judicial remedy. A territorial body should not have to govern by guessing whether its statute, its compact, or a ministry circular wins.

### 9. Make amendment, boundary change, and exit rules lawful instead of improvisational

Because metropolitan and regional territories evolve, the legal home should also say how the body changes. How are borders adjusted? How are new municipalities brought in? How are functions added or removed? What consultation is required? What happens to staff, debt, assets, records, and pending proceedings if the legal form changes?

The archive should reject arrangements where the territory’s real constitution can be rewritten by executive bargain alone. A public-law home should make institutional change visible and rule-bound.

### 10. Use contracts and memoranda below the constitutional layer, not above it

Even a well-constituted territorial government will still use contracts, service agreements, and operating protocols. The archive does not reject those tools. It rejects their misuse.

Contracts should operationalize a legal settlement, not replace it. Memoranda can help co-ordinate, but they should sit below the constitutional and statutory layer rather than carrying powers that properly belong in public law.

## Public-law-home matrix

| Condition | Agreement-led co-operation fits | Hybrid public-law body fits | Full territorial public-law home fits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Typical task** | bounded shared service or technical pool | recurring co-ordination plus some standing territorial programmes | real metropolitan or regional government across several sectors |
| **Legal basis** | interlocal agreement or compact | statute-backed joint authority / legal entity | constitution or statute establishing a territorial government |
| **Legal personality** | may be borrowed or minimal | distinct legal entity is usually preferable | own legal personality is required |
| **Competences** | narrow, derivative, reversible | mixed own and delegated competences with explicit boundaries | named own competences plus defined delegated lanes |
| **Rulemaking** | little or none beyond internal rules | bounded regulatory / planning instruments | visible ordinance, regulation, or binding-plan lane within law |
| **Main risk if misused** | contract starts carrying hidden public power | unclear split between own and delegated authority | overbuild if the territorial vocation remains narrow |

## Failure modes

- **government by interlocal contract**: a territory is really governed through agreements never meant to carry constitutional weight.
- **host-body dependency**: the shared tier exists only through one municipality’s legal shell, records, payroll, or procurement powers.
- **phantom competence**: everyone assumes the body can act because it has money or prestige, but no clear public-law basis exists.
- **delegation blur**: own powers, delegated tasks, and purchased services are mixed together until responsibility becomes unreadable.
- **soft-law hard effects**: nominally non-binding plans or funding conditions operate as de facto law without matching procedure or review.
- **ghost rulemaking**: the body shapes binding outcomes but lacks an explicit rulemaking lane and therefore governs through workaround and ambiguity.
- **conflict fog**: nobody can tell when municipal law, regional law, national law, or the shared body’s acts prevail.
- **rewrite by summit**: major institutional change occurs through leader bargain or ministerial improvisation rather than lawful amendment.

## Practical tests

A shared territorial body has an honest public-law home when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does the body have legal personality proportionate to its continuing duties, assets, liabilities, and records?
2. Are its basic powers and responsibilities prescribed by constitution or statute rather than only by agreement?
3. Is the territorial basis of the body named clearly enough that residents can tell whether they are inside it and why?
4. Are own competences, delegated tasks, and contracted services kept distinct?
5. If the body makes binding territorial choices, is there a visible and reviewable rulemaking lane for them?
6. Are its relations with member governments and the state governed by explicit conflict, supervision, and remedy rules?
7. Are amendment, boundary change, and function transfer handled by lawful procedures rather than executive improvisation?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared territorial body now governs a real place but nobody can plainly say **what legal person it is, what competences it owns, what rules it may make, and under which public-law charter it acts**, then it still lacks **a public-law home for shared territorial power** and is drifting into **government by interlocal contract**.
