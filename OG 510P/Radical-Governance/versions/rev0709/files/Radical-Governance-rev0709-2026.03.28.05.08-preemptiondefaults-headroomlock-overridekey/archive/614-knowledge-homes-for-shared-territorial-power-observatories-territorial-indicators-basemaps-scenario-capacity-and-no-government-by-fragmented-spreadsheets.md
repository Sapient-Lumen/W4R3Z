# 614 — Knowledge homes for shared territorial power: observatories, territorial indicators, basemaps, scenario capacity, and no government by fragmented spreadsheets

## One-line thesis

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, it needs an honest **knowledge home** as well as a democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, and participatory one: a territorially coherent observatory, common basemaps and boundary files, a published indicator set at the scale actually being governed, lawful data-sharing and evaluation capacity, open methods and release discipline, and visible routes from evidence into plans and budgets — so the shared tier stops governing through municipal spreadsheet fragments, ministry leftovers, vendor dashboards, and dueling numbers.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a substantial constitutional-upgrade ladder for shared territorial power. It can say when co-operation has become real government, what democratic form should follow, what fiscal base is needed, what operating core is needed, what accountability and public-law basis are required, how the tier should be wired into the wider intergovernmental order, and how residents should be able to participate directly.

What it still lacked was the **knowledge analogue**. A metropolitan or regional tier may have an assembly, budget, staff, legal personality, and participation routes, yet still remain practically half-built if it cannot say with authority what territory it measures, what indicators it uses, what basemap and address geography define the unit of decision, how member-government and operator data are combined, and where the public can inspect the common factual spine behind consequential territorial bargains.

That gap matters because much of the work done by real shared territorial government is inherently evidence-hungry and cross-boundary: housing supply, transport demand, hospital catchments, flood risk, economic development, climate resilience, waste flows, utility loads, logistics corridors, and land-use change. If the governing scale is metropolitan or regional but the evidence base remains trapped inside municipal silos or ministry averages, the polity gets the worst of both worlds: it centralises choice without building a shared factual commons.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine for the **knowledge home of shared territorial government**: when a real metropolitan or regional tier must stop borrowing fragmented data from elsewhere and build its own visible territorial evidence spine.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat the territorial evidence spine as constitutional infrastructure, not analytics aftercare

A real metropolitan or regional tier should be able to say what territory it governs, what geography it measures, what baseline indicators it publishes, and which evidence routes support planning, budgeting, and review. The archive should therefore treat the observatory and indicator layer as part of the constitutional kit of shared territorial government, not as a discretionary dashboard project that can be postponed indefinitely.

### 2. Start from the governed territory, not from inherited reporting silos

A knowledge home begins by matching evidence to the real territorial unit of action. If the polity governs a functional urban area, basin, corridor, hospital catchment, labour market, or resilience zone, then the core indicators and mapping files should be able to see that territory.

The archive should reject arrangements in which the shared tier makes consequential metropolitan or regional choices while still relying mainly on municipal reporting units that do not line up with the territory being governed, or on national averages too coarse to illuminate real cross-boundary trade-offs.

### 3. Give the tier a common basemap, boundary file, and place-reference discipline

Shared government cannot think clearly about territory if every actor uses different boundary assumptions, zoning layers, route definitions, catchment lines, or place identifiers. The archive should therefore prefer one intelligible territorial reference pack for the shared tier: authoritative boundary files, shared basemaps, common identifiers where lawful, and explicit crosswalks to municipal, regional, and national units.

This is not a call for one giant central database. It is a call for one visible territorial frame of reference, so disputes are about policy rather than about which map was quietly used.

### 4. Publish a bounded common indicator set with methods, metadata, and release discipline

A knowledge home should not be an unstructured heap of charts. It needs a published set of core territorial indicators tied to the work of the shared tier: mobility, housing, land use, infrastructure condition, environmental risk, finance, service access, demographic change, and other genuinely relevant measures.

The archive should prefer indicators that are:

- published with definitions, methods, and metadata,
- updated on a visible release rhythm,
- comparable across places and over time,
- disaggregated where territorial justice requires it,
- and linked to the plans, budgets, or performance questions they are supposed to inform.

### 5. Build a lawful observatory rather than an extractive data lake

A metropolitan or regional knowledge home should be able to combine administrative data, official statistics, geospatial data, operational data, and evaluation findings from several institutions. But the archive should strongly resist the habit of treating this as justification for one indiscriminate warehouse of everything.

The preferred design is an **observatory**: a visible, governed, and lawful evidence function with clear stewardship, defined acquisition routes, privacy and security rules, publication rules, and interfaces to member governments and operators. The goal is shared understanding, not territorial surveillance by accumulation.

### 6. Keep official statistics, service operations, and political messaging distinct even when they meet at the same tier

The shared tier may host an observatory or analytic unit, but it should not be allowed to quietly collapse measurement, operations, and political communications into one voice. The archive should preserve visible distinctions among:

- official or quasi-official territorial statistics,
- operational monitoring for live services and infrastructure,
- policy evaluation and foresight,
- and political argument by elected or executive actors.

Without those distinctions, the shared tier drifts toward metropolitan government by curated slide deck.

### 7. Give the tier scenario, evaluation, and foresight capacity rather than only rear-view mirrors

Real shared territorial government should not only count what has already happened. It should also be able to test scenarios, compare options, model spillovers, and evaluate whether plans or investments changed the territory in the way claimed.

The archive should therefore prefer a minimum scenario-and-evaluation capacity in the knowledge home: the ability to compare route options, housing allocations, basin interventions, siting choices, investment sequences, and resilience strategies using a common territorial evidence base rather than political intuition alone.

### 8. Make publication and public inspection part of the design, not a discretionary extra

The public should be able to find the shared tier’s indicator set, territorial definitions, methods, core map layers, release calendar, and major evidence products without insider access. Where privacy, safety, or security require limits, the tier should still publish enough metadata and methodological explanation to make contestation possible.

The archive should reject government by invisible workbook, consultant PDF pile, or vendor-controlled dashboard whose methods cannot be inspected.

### 9. Dock the knowledge home into budgeting, planning, scrutiny, and participation

Evidence is most honest when people can see where it goes next. Indicators should appear in plan framing, budget memoranda, major capital proposals, territorial strategies, scrutiny hearings, and participatory processes. Evaluation results should visibly feed review, not disappear after publication.

The archive should reject a knowledge home that floats above politics as neutral décor. The point is not technocracy. The point is that territorial politics should argue over a visible common factual spine rather than over private number sets.

### 10. Keep room for challenge, correction, and multiple knowledge sources without losing a common territorial frame

A real knowledge home should be open to correction from universities, civil society, professional bodies, local knowledge holders, operators, journalists, and national statistical or mapping institutions. The archive should therefore prefer designs with challenge routes, revision notes, methodological transparency, and cross-source comparison.

But challenge should happen against a common frame. The answer to bad official territorial evidence is not every municipality, ministry, contractor, or lobbyist publishing a different map and calling it democracy.

## Knowledge-home matrix

| Question | Thin or dishonest arrangement | Honest knowledge home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What territory is being measured?** | implicit or shifting boundaries | explicit territorial definition with published boundary files and crosswalks |
| **Where do shared facts live?** | scattered municipal sheets, ministry fragments, consultant decks | a visible observatory or equivalent territorial evidence function |
| **What indicators guide choice?** | ad hoc metrics chosen case by case | published core indicator set with methods and metadata |
| **How are data combined?** | improvised extraction and opaque vendor pipelines | lawful data-sharing, stewardship rules, and interoperable evidence routes |
| **Can people inspect the evidence?** | private workbooks, PDFs, or dashboards with no methods | public release discipline, metadata, and inspectable methods |
| **Does evidence change decisions?** | reports float free of budgets and plans | indicators and evaluations dock into planning, budgeting, scrutiny, and participation |

## Failure modes

- **government by fragmented spreadsheets**: the shared tier governs real territory but still relies on mismatched local files and one-off merges.
- **map shopping**: actors switch territorial definitions or basemaps to suit the argument of the day.
- **dashboard theatre**: polished indicators exist, but methods, metadata, and uptake paths are missing.
- **vendor epistemic capture**: essential territorial knowledge lives inside proprietary models or dashboards the public sector cannot inspect.
- **metro-by-average**: consequential territorial decisions rely mainly on national or regional averages that hide intra-territorial variation.
- **observatory without law**: data are pooled and reused without clear stewardship, privacy, security, or publication rules.
- **rear-view governance**: the tier can report historical conditions but lacks scenario, foresight, or evaluation capacity for live choices.
- **knowledge without docking**: evidence products are published but do not visibly inform plans, budgets, hearings, or review.
- **dueling numbers federalism**: each component government or operator brings its own facts because the shared tier never built a common factual spine.

## Practical tests

A shared territorial body has an honest knowledge home when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can it state clearly what territory it measures and publish the corresponding boundary files, basemaps, or crosswalks?
2. Does it maintain a visible observatory or equivalent evidence function for the shared territory rather than depending on ad hoc file gathering?
3. Is there a published core indicator set with definitions, metadata, and an intelligible release rhythm?
4. Are administrative, operational, geospatial, and statistical data combined through lawful and documented stewardship arrangements rather than opaque extraction?
5. Can the public inspect enough of the methods, metadata, and core outputs to contest claims intelligently?
6. Does the tier have at least minimum scenario, evaluation, or foresight capacity for major territorial choices?
7. Are evidence products visibly docked into planning, budgeting, scrutiny, and participation rather than left as decorative analytics?
8. Are challenge and correction routes open without letting every actor dissolve the common territorial frame into number pluralism?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a metropolitan or regional tier now governs real territory, but cannot plainly say **what map it uses, what indicators it treats as common facts, how member and operator data are lawfully combined, where methods and releases are published, and how that evidence changes plans and budgets**, then it still lacks **a knowledge home for shared territorial power** and is drifting into **government by fragmented spreadsheets**.
