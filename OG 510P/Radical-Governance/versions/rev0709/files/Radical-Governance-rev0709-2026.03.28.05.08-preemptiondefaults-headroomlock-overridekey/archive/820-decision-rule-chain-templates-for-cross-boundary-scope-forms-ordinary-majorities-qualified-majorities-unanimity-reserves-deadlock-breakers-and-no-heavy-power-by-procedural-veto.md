# 820 — Decision-rule-chain templates for cross-boundary scope forms: ordinary majorities, qualified majorities, unanimity reserves, deadlock breakers, and no heavy power by procedural veto

## One-line thesis

Once the archive has said **which cross-boundary institutional form is honest**, **what legitimacy chain belongs to it**, **what fiscal chain belongs to it**, **what administrative spine belongs to it**, **what oversight chain belongs to it**, **what participation chain belongs to it**, **what information chain belongs to it**, and **what boundary-and-membership chain belongs to it**, it should also be able to say **what decision-rule chain belongs to it**: thin connectors should seek consensus for advisory work and require visible member ratification for anything binding, shared-service backbones should use ordinary-majority operating rules with reserved supermajority or member-ratification gates for portfolio, burden, and liability shifts, bounded functional authorities should use public standing orders with ordinary-majority defaults plus qualified-majority or double-key gates for tariffs, debt, land, remit, and boundary changes, and general-purpose territorial tiers should govern by ordinary democratic majorities with narrowly cabined supermajority reserves and explicit deadlock routes rather than by permanent veto geography.

## Why this matters

`790` gives the archive a short answer key for what ideal governments at each scope should generally be. `810` says how to assemble threshold proof before saying that a metropolitan, pooling, regional-platform, or legitimacy-thickness line is really crossed. `812` says which institutional form is honest once that burden is real. `813` says who should sit in the room and which decisions require which legitimacy key. `814` says what money constitution belongs to the form. `815` says where the operator, employer, records home, and continuity spine should live. `816` says what scrutiny and redress chain belongs to the form. `817` says what public-input chain belongs to it. `818` says where the shared facts of the body should actually live. `819` says who belongs, what territory counts, and how accession, amendment, exit, and dissolution should work.

What the recent scope stack still lacked was one compact answer to the next question:

**once the room, map, money, and bureaucracy are known, how should consequential decisions actually clear?**

That gap matters because many cross-boundary failures are failures of **procedural constitution** rather than abstract institutional form.

One failure is **unanimity creep**: a connector or backbone keeps a unanimity rule far past the point where one member can block routine continuity, user protection, or clearly budgeted service maintenance.

Another failure is **ratification blur**: a body claims it may act internally even though the decision really changes burden-sharing, tariff exposure, debt, membership, or territorial claim and should have climbed to a higher gate.

A third failure is **quorum games**: controversial acts are not defeated in public but are instead delayed, starved of attendance, or pushed through in meetings whose attendance floor no longer matches the gravity of the act.

A fourth failure is **emergency exception creep**: what began as a narrow continuity power becomes a standing license for executives or chairs to bypass the ordinary rule chain.

Official materials support a more disciplined ladder. The EGTC regulation requires the convention itself to be concluded unanimously, requires the statutes to specify organ competences and decision-making procedures, and treats amendments, liability, and budget questions as matters that belong in explicit constitutional text. World Bank metropolitan-governance work notes that some inter-municipal councils are coordinating bodies whose decisions still require ratification by each member local government. OECD work on inter-municipal co-operation in the Western Balkans shows that local assemblies may approve co-operation agreements by majority vote in open session and that agreements should specify rights, obligations, financial resources, supervision, dispute resolution, and termination. OECD work on shared municipal services in Lithuania likewise argues that the legal framework should specify joint decision-making bodies, founding-charter content, withdrawal, and dispute-resolution rules rather than leaving the practical governance grammar vague. The Council of Europe’s accountability recommendation adds the public-law discipline underneath all of this: accountability frameworks must identify who is accountable, for what, and through which procedures, and decision makers must be able to report on, explain, and accept consequences for their decisions. The archive should compress those lessons into one rule: **do not let the gravity of a body outrun the clarity of its voting, quorum, ratification, and deadlock grammar**. citeturn283897view3turn283897view1turn652686view0turn441700view0turn283897view4

## Pattern pack

### 1. Use this note after form and legitimacy are separated

This note does not decide whether a metropolitan, county-pooling, regional-platform, fiscal-floor, or legitimacy-thickness threshold has been crossed.

Use `810` for that.

This note does not decide whether the honest institutional answer is a thin connector, shared-service backbone, bounded functional authority, or general-purpose territorial tier.

Use `812` for that.

This note does not decide who sits in the room or which decisions need a second legitimacy key in the first place.

Use `813` for that.

This note does not decide what revenue constitution belongs to the body, where the operating spine lives, where scrutiny and remedy sit, what public-input chain belongs to the body, where the shared facts live, or who belongs to the body and how its territory may change.

Use `814`, `815`, `816`, `817`, `818`, and `819` for those questions.

This note applies after those questions are separated and the remaining live issue is:

- which decisions may pass by ordinary internal vote,
- which decisions require a qualified majority,
- which decisions must clear member ratification or another second key,
- when unanimity is honest and when it becomes veto feudalism,
- what quorum and notice rules are proportionate to the gravity of the act,
- and what deadlock or emergency route exists when a body cannot lawfully or practically wait.

### 2. Distinguish four recurrent decision-rule chains before inventing hybrids

The archive should usually begin with four recognizable chains.

| Form | Default decision-rule chain | What may pass by ordinary internal vote | What should usually trigger a higher gate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Thin connector** | consensus-seeking coordination plus member ratification for anything binding | agendas, studies, draft strategies, common statements, voluntary work plans | binding obligations, burdens on members, public claims of territorial mandate |
| **Shared-service backbone** | ordinary-majority operating board with reserved ratification or supermajority classes | service standards within charter, routine procurement, ordinary staffing and continuity acts | portfolio expansion, major liabilities, burden reallocation, entry/exit consequences, termination |
| **Bounded functional authority** | public standing orders with ordinary-majority default plus qualified-majority and double-key reserves | routine operating decisions within remit and budget, ordinary asset maintenance, implementation choices | tariffs, major debt, land leverage, boundary or remit change, coercive rule moves, long emergency extension |
| **General-purpose territorial tier** | ordinary democratic majority with narrowly specified supermajority reserves and deadlock routes | ordinary legislation, budgets, oversight, routine appointments, regular policy choice | constitutional redesign, territorial restructuring, exceptional debt or lock-in, entrenched-rule change |

The archive should not begin with bespoke procedural poetry until one of these four chains has first been ruled out honestly.

### 3. Classify the decision before choosing the threshold

Later archive work should usually sort a disputed act into one of five classes before it argues about majority thresholds.

1. **routine operating acts** — ordinary execution within settled remit, budget, and service standards;
2. **plan-and-budget acts** — annual or medium-term programming, ordinary capital prioritisation, staffing envelopes, or service-level packages;
3. **liability-and-lock-in acts** — debt, guarantees, long concessions, tariff frameworks, major procurement lock-in, or reserve depletion;
4. **constitutional-perimeter acts** — remit expansion, boundary change, membership change, land-power extension, amendment of founding rules, or dissolution;
5. **continuity-and-emergency acts** — temporary measures needed to prevent service collapse or acute public harm.

The archive should not argue about majority size in the abstract. First identify the act class. Then match the gate.

### 4. Thin connectors should default to consensus-seeking and visible non-binding procedure

A thin connector is honest when it remains a co-ordination venue rather than a shadow sovereign.

Its decision-rule chain should therefore stay light:

- seek consensus where the output is advisory, diagnostic, or agenda-setting;
- record dissents rather than pretending agreement where it does not exist;
- require visible member ratification before any act creates binding duty, financial commitment, or territorial claim;
- and refuse to treat silence, absence, or private follow-up as a lawful substitute for consent.

This is where `780` still matters: no connector should drift into **government by non-reply**.

Unanimity may be honest for entering the arrangement or adopting the founding convention. It is usually not honest as the standing rule for every work-plan update, continuity fix, or ordinary recommendation once the body is already operating.

### 5. Shared-service backbones need an operating majority and a reserved-consequence schedule

A shared-service backbone exists to pool machinery while the member governments retain the political title to the duty.

Its decision-rule chain should therefore normally split into two lanes.

The **operating lane** may pass by ordinary majority of the governing board or assembly for matters such as:

- routine procurement within approved envelopes,
- ordinary staffing, scheduling, and workflow design,
- service telemetry, maintenance, and process improvement,
- and ordinary incident response inside the already authorised service catalogue.

The **reserved-consequence lane** should instead require supermajority, member ratification, or both for matters such as:

- adding or removing major services,
- shifting cost-allocation formulas,
- taking on major liabilities or long lock-in contracts,
- changing withdrawal consequences,
- approving mergers, splits, or host transfer,
- or changing user entitlements across member places.

The archive should be suspicious of shared-service bodies that keep either everything at unanimity or almost nothing above ordinary majority. Both extremes usually hide a failure to classify acts honestly.

### 6. Bounded functional authorities should use ordinary-majority default plus qualified-majority and double-key reserves

A bounded functional authority is not merely a project club. It is the steward of one real cross-boundary system.

Its decision-rule chain should therefore normally include:

- published standing orders,
- clear quorum requirements,
- ordinary-majority default for routine decisions within settled remit,
- qualified-majority requirements for major debt, tariff frameworks, long concessions, land or corridor leverage, or binding rule changes inside the remit,
- and a second legitimacy key for the heaviest decisions where `813` says that board vote alone is not enough.

This is the point in the ladder where the archive should become especially strict about **act typing**.

A fare adjustment within a published tariff framework is not the same constitutional act as creating a new compulsory levy. A maintenance bond inside an approved capital plan is not the same act as a system-expanding debt programme. A service-area refinement is not the same act as a boundary or membership redesign. Decision-rule inflation and decision-rule minimalism both come from collapsing those classes.

### 7. General-purpose territorial tiers should govern by ordinary majority unless the constitution clearly says otherwise

A real territorial tier should not be forced to govern as a permanent hostage negotiation among inherited subunits.

Its decision-rule chain should therefore normally use:

- ordinary democratic majority for ordinary legislation, budgets, oversight, appointments, and policy direction;
- expressly limited supermajority reserves for constitutional or quasi-constitutional acts such as territorial redesign, entrenched standing orders, or exceptional debt lock-in;
- and public procedures for reconsideration, referral, or judicial challenge rather than informal veto custom.

The archive should be very reluctant to bless permanent municipal vetoes inside a body it is otherwise calling a genuine territorial government. That is usually a sign that the body is still constitutionally a compact among principals rather than an honest tier in its own right.

### 8. Require explicit deadlock, quorum-failure, and emergency rules

Every serious cross-boundary body should have a visible answer to three practical questions.

First, **what happens if quorum fails repeatedly?**

Second, **what happens if a reserved-class decision cannot clear the first vote but service continuity, legal compliance, or user protection still require action?**

Third, **what may the executive do before the body reconvenes, and for how long?**

The archive should usually require:

- re-vote clocks or special reconvening rules,
- mediation, conciliation, or referral routes where member conflict is the real issue,
- temporary caretaker powers that are narrow, logged, and time-limited,
- public notice and recorded reasons for emergency use,
- and automatic return of the matter to the ordinary body once the acute condition passes.

The archive should reject emergency clauses that have no renewal clock and deadlock clauses that amount to indefinite suspension of shared duty. If the unresolved issue is no longer what vote or reserve class applies but which venue hears the conflict, when mediation or expert determination should begin, or what continuity order survives while the body argues, the archive should hand that question to `821` rather than overloading the decision-rule note into a full dispute-routing constitution.

### 9. Put decision-rule fields into applied verdict packets

When `811` is used for a real polity, proposal, or case, the packet should carry a short decision-rule holding whenever the internal rule grammar is live.

Typical fields include:

- ordinary-vote rule,
- quorum rule,
- reserved supermajority classes,
- second-key classes,
- unanimity reserves,
- emergency exception,
- deadlock route,
- and next review clock for whether the current rule chain still matches the gravity of the body.

That keeps applied judgments from scattering voting thresholds, ratification rules, and emergency shortcuts across side notes.

## Failure modes

Later archive work should reuse a small decision-rule failure glossary rather than inventing fresh prose each time.

- **unanimity creep** — unanimity is left in place for ordinary operating acts long after it has become a service-hostage rule.
- **ratification blur** — a body claims internal authority for acts that really change burden, liability, remit, or map and should have climbed to a higher gate.
- **quorum games** — controversial acts are won or lost by attendance sabotage rather than by recorded argument and vote.
- **procedural veto geography** — inherited member units keep permanent vetoes inside what is supposedly a real territorial tier.
- **emergency exception creep** — temporary continuity powers become a standing bypass around the ordinary decision chain.
- **deadlock without handoff** — the constitution can describe refusal but not lawful continuity, mediation, or handback when refusal persists.

## Compression rule for the archive

If later archive work cannot answer **what passes by ordinary vote, what needs a higher gate, what counts as quorum, and what happens under deadlock or emergency** in one short paragraph, then the body’s internal decision constitution is still too vague to count as honestly designed.
