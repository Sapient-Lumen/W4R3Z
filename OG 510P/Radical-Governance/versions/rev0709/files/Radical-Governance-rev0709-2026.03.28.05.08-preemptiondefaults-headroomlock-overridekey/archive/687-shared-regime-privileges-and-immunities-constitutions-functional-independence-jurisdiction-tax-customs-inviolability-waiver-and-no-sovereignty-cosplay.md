# 687 — Shared-regime privileges-and-immunities constitutions: functional independence, jurisdiction, tax, customs, inviolability, waiver, and no sovereignty cosplay

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed privileges-and-immunities authority map** that separates juridical personality, inviolability, immunity from legal process, archives and communications protection, fiscal and customs facilities, visa and entry support, category-specific treatment for representatives and officials, waiver authority, and abuse-control lanes — so institutional independence is protected without decaying into sovereignty cosplay, perk inflation, or unreviewable impunity.

## Why this matters

The archive now explains how shared regimes admit participants, differentiate commitments, make decisions, keep records, run sessions, staff secretariats, buy goods, and administer internal justice (`667`–`686`). That is enough to describe a large part of a regime’s constitutional life. It is still not enough to explain **how the regime remains legally operable inside host states without becoming hostage to every tax rule, search power, service fee, visa choke point, or local-court proceeding**.

That gap matters because many shared regimes are neither ordinary private associations nor sovereign states.

They need the legal capacity to contract, own property, hire staff, hold funds, receive delegations, maintain archives, and communicate securely. They also need protection against host-state or forum-state pressures that can disable their independence at exactly the moment a dispute becomes politically uncomfortable. But the answer cannot be to speak as if every international or intergovernmental body were a miniature sovereign entitled to every diplomatic luxury forever.

Without a typed privileges-and-immunities constitution, six familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **status inflation**: functional protections granted for institutional independence are redescribed as prestige rewards, diplomatic rank theater, or personal perk bundles.

The second is **category blur**: the regime cannot clearly distinguish what applies to the organization itself, to member representatives, to officials, to experts on mission, to contractors, or to family members.

The third is **forum confusion**: immunity from legal process, inviolability of premises, protection of archives, tax treatment, customs facilities, and entry support collapse into one vague idea of immunity.

The fourth is **waiver opacity**: outsiders cannot tell who may waive immunity, on what standard, through what instrument, or with what record.

The fifth is **host-state dependency**: visas, premises access, utilities, policing coordination, or import arrangements are managed informally, making practical independence depend on goodwill instead of a visible constitutional packet.

The sixth is **impunity theater**: the regime invokes immunity to block outside process while failing to show how misconduct, abuse, or private-law disputes are handled through internal or substitute lanes.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **shared-regime privileges-and-immunities discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from function, not prestige

A serious regime should say explicitly that privileges and immunities exist to protect the independent performance of the institution’s functions, not to flatter officeholders or mimic state sovereignty.

This is the archive’s first anti-theater rule for international insulation: call every privilege by the task it protects.

### 2. Publish one category map instead of one undifferentiated immunity word

The regime should separate at minimum:

- the organization as juridical person,
- member or party representatives,
- elected or appointed officers,
- ordinary officials and staff,
- experts on mission,
- contractors and vendors,
- and dependants or household members where a host package extends to them.

Not every category should receive the same treatment. They do need to travel through one visible map.

### 3. Separate legal capacity from immunity

The organization should have a typed legal-personality lane: power to contract, own and dispose of property, operate accounts, and institute legal proceedings where the regime itself needs to act.

That capacity should not be confused with immunity from being sued or otherwise subjected to local process. A regime that can contract but cannot be sued at all must separately explain its claims and remedy lane.

### 4. Type the protections by object

A serious regime should distinguish:

- inviolability of premises,
- inviolability or protection of archives and official records,
- official communications protections,
- immunity from legal process for the organization,
- functional immunities for representatives or officials,
- exemption from specified direct taxes and customs duties,
- currency, transfer, or banking facilities where needed,
- and entry, residence, or visa facilitation for accredited participants.

This is how the archive prevents the word immunity from swallowing many different constitutional objects.

### 5. Keep representative and staff protections function-specific

Representatives need enough protection to participate independently in the regime’s work. Officials need enough protection to perform their duties without host-state retaliation or harassment.

But the regime should still distinguish between diplomatic-style treatment for a narrow class of officeholders and ordinary functional immunity for the larger staff body. Otherwise the archive slides from independence into rank inflation.

### 6. Publish a waiver constitution

A serious regime should say:

- who may waive immunity for the organization,
- who may waive it for particular officials or experts,
- what written form a waiver takes,
- what public or reviewable record is kept,
- and what standard governs the choice.

The default rule should be functional: immunity may be waived where it would impede the course of justice and can be waived without prejudice to the interests of the regime.

### 7. Bind privileges to host-state implementation packets

Privileges that exist only in a treaty clause but not in daily operations are decorative.

The regime should therefore maintain one host-implementation packet covering identity cards, badges, entry and visa support, customs handling, tax treatment, law-enforcement contact points, emergency contact rules, official-vehicle treatment where applicable, and premises-access protocols. This is the operational half of the constitutional map.

### 8. Separate inviolability from secrecy and non-cooperation

Protection of premises, archives, and official correspondence should not become a general excuse for missing records, blanket refusal, or evidence black-hole government.

The archive should prefer a rule that protected materials still travel through named internal retrieval, authorization, and disclosure lanes, with redaction, confidentiality, and lawful public-access rules typed in advance.

### 9. Exclude contractors and donors from ambient privilege borrowing

Contractors, grantees, consultants, and donors should not inherit immunity or host-state facilities by proximity unless a governing instrument expressly gives them a defined status for a defined function.

This prevents the regime’s legal shield from turning into a private-services moat.

### 10. Pair immunity with accountability and remedy

Any regime that invokes immunity should also be able to point immediately to:

- internal ethics and discipline lanes,
- staff justice and whistleblower protection,
- contract and private-law dispute routes,
- cooperation rules for criminal or serious misconduct referrals,
- and waiver authority.

Privileges and immunities are constitutionally defensible only when they sit inside a broader accountability architecture.

## Working matrix

| Object | Honest rule | Why | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| legal personality | type contracting, property, banking, and legal-capacity powers separately | lets the regime function as an institution | confusing capacity with blanket immunity |
| categories | distinguish organization, representatives, officials, experts, vendors, and households | prevents category blur and status inflation | one undifferentiated badge logic |
| protections | separate process immunity, archives, premises, communications, tax/customs, and entry support | keeps each privilege reviewable by purpose | treating immunity as one magical word |
| waiver | publish authority, standard, instrument, and record | makes immunity bounded rather than mystical | private or improvised waiver practice |
| host implementation | maintain one operational packet for IDs, visas, customs, policing, and access | turns formal status into usable independence | landlord-grace governance |
| accountability | pair immunity with internal justice, claims, and cooperation lanes | prevents impunity theater | immunity without remedy |

## Guardrails

- Do not describe privileges and immunities as prestige goods or diplomatic vanity packages.
- Do not give every category the same treatment by default.
- Do not collapse legal capacity, process immunity, inviolability, fiscal treatment, and entry support into one vague formula.
- Do not let waiver practice remain oral, personalized, or politically private.
- Do not let archives protection become a pretext for missing-record government.
- Do not let contractors, donors, or adjacent private actors borrow institutional immunities by atmosphere.
- Do not invoke immunity without being able to point to review, discipline, claims, or cooperation lanes.

## Failure modes

- **status inflation**: functional protection is redescribed as rank or prestige.
- **category blur**: representatives, staff, experts, and vendors drift into one badge class.
- **forum confusion**: legal process, tax, archives, and premises rules are all mislabeled as generic immunity.
- **waiver opacity**: no one can tell who can waive, or how.
- **host-state dependency**: practical operability depends on informal indulgence rather than a typed packet.
- **impunity theater**: immunity is visible; accountability is not.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it publish one authority map separating legal personality, immunity, inviolability, fiscal facilities, and entry support?
2. Does it distinguish the status of the organization, representatives, officials, experts, and contractors?
3. Are premises, archives, communications, tax/customs, and process protections typed separately rather than collapsed into one slogan?
4. Does it identify the waiver authority, waiver standard, and waiver instrument?
5. Does it maintain one host-implementation packet for IDs, visas, customs, policing contacts, and access protocols?
6. Are contractors and donors excluded from ambient privilege borrowing unless explicitly covered?
7. Can the regime explain how protected records still move through internal retrieval and disclosure lanes?
8. Can it point immediately to discipline, staff justice, private-law claims, or cooperation routes that keep immunity from becoming impunity?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime needs **independence inside host-state territory** but cannot say **what legal capacities it has, what protections attach to which category, what may be waived by whom, how host-state implementation works, and what accountability lanes remain visible when immunity is invoked**, it still has **public authority without an honest privileges-and-immunities constitution**.
