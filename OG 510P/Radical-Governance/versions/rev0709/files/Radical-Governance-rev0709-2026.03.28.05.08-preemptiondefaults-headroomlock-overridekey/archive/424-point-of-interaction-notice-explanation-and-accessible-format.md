# 424 — Point-of-interaction notice, explanation, and accessible format

## One-line thesis

Any public AI touchpoint that can affect a person should announce itself early, explain itself meaningfully when it matters, and do so in formats the affected person can actually use.

## Why this matters

A recurring public-sector failure is **late or unusable transparency**:

- people only discover automation after an adverse outcome,
- notices are buried in general terms and conditions,
- explanations arrive too late to help a challenge,
- the language is too technical to be meaningful,
- disabled users or vulnerable groups receive disclosures in formats they cannot use.

Official practice is converging on a tighter rule. The EU AI Act requires direct-interaction systems to inform people that they are interacting with AI unless that is obvious, requires certain deployers to inform people when high-risk AI is being used in decisions about them, and gives affected persons a right to clear and meaningful explanations of the role of high-risk AI in the decision process. The Act also states that these notices should be provided in accessible formats for persons with disabilities. Canada’s current automated-decision regime likewise continues to move toward stronger transparency and explanation requirements.

The archive should therefore distinguish between **front-door notice** and **decision-stage explanation**, and insist that both are accessible, plain-language, and connected to remedy.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Give front-door notice before or during the interaction

A person should not have to reverse-engineer that an AI system is present. At the first meaningful point of contact, disclose:

- that AI is being used,
- whether the system is giving information, triaging, recommending, or helping decide,
- whether a human will review the output,
- how to reach a human or alternative channel.

This notice belongs where people actually encounter the system: the form, portal, phone flow, message, interview screen, or letter.

### 2. Separate general notice from decision-specific explanation

A generic notice is not the same as an explanation of a particular result. The archive should require two layers:

- a **general use notice** explaining the presence and role of the system,
- a **decision-specific explanation** when a consequential result is produced or relied upon.

The second layer should explain:

- what role the system played,
- what the main decision factors were,
- what a human reviewer did or did not do,
- what recourse is available next.

### 3. Publish in plain language and accessible formats

A notice that is formally available but practically unusable is not a public safeguard. Notices and explanations should:

- use plain-language terms,
- avoid vendor jargon and abstract ML claims,
- be available in accessible digital and non-digital formats,
- account for disability, age, and other vulnerability contexts.

The design goal is not disclosure theater. It is actual comprehension under ordinary service conditions.

### 4. Trigger stronger explanation when stakes rise

The depth of explanation should scale with consequence. Where the system can materially affect a person’s rights, benefits, obligations, liberty, or essential service access, the explanation should be stronger and more specific than for low-stakes informational tools.

That means the archive should reject a one-size-fits-all explanation template.

### 5. Link every notice to a live recourse path

A meaningful explanation without a way to act on it is mostly decorative. Every notice or explanation should point to:

- a review path,
- the time window to seek it,
- the evidence or records that may be requested,
- a reachable human support channel.

Transparency should reduce the cost of challenge, not merely narrate the existence of power.

### 6. Keep dated versions of notices and explanation templates

Investigators and affected people should be able to see what the service actually told users at the time. Preserve historical versions of:

- interaction notices,
- decision letters,
- explanation templates,
- accessibility variants,
- public help pages.

Without version history, later audits cannot tell whether the system was transparent when it mattered.

### 7. Test notice quality with real users, not only lawyers and product owners

The service should test whether users can answer basic questions after seeing the notice:

- Did they understand AI was involved?
- Did they understand the role it played?
- Did they know how to challenge or opt for another route?
- Could disabled users access the same information without delay or dependency?

A notice passes when users can navigate consequence, not when counsel signs off on wording.

## Guardrails

- Keep the first notice short, prominent, and unavoidable.
- Do not force people to hunt through privacy policies to discover automation.
- Provide fuller documentation behind the short notice where stakes warrant it.
- Ensure accessible-format availability is operationally real, not a help-desk fiction.
- Review notice wording after complaints, appeals, and user-testing failures.

## Failure modes

- **surprise automation**: the person learns about AI only after an adverse result.
- **notice burial**: the disclosure exists but is hidden in generic legal text.
- **explanation mismatch**: the person gets a broad system description instead of the reasons for their own result.
- **inaccessible disclosure**: the relevant information exists but not in a usable format.
- **remedy disconnect**: the notice explains the system but not what the person can do next.

## Practical tests

A notice-and-explanation regime passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Are people told at the point of interaction that AI is involved when it is not obvious?
2. Are people told when consequential high-risk AI is used in decisions about them?
3. Can affected people obtain a clear and meaningful explanation of the role of the system and the main elements of the decision?
4. Are notices and explanations available in accessible formats without special pleading?
5. Does each notice connect directly to review, correction, or appeal routes?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a person cannot tell **that AI was used, what role it played, and what they can do next**, the service is not yet transparent enough for public consequence.
