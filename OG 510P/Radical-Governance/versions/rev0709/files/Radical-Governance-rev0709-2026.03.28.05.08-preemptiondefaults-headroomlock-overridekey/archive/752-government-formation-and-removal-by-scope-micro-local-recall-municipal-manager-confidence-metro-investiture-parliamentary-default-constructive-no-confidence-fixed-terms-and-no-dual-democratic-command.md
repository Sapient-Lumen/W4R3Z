# 752 — Government formation and removal by scope: micro-local recall, municipal manager confidence, metro investiture, parliamentary default, constructive no confidence, fixed terms, and no dual democratic command

## One-line thesis

Ideal governments should choose formation and removal rules that create **one legible confidence chain** at each scope — recallable stewards for micro-local bodies, elected-council plus removable manager or mayoral executive in municipalities, explicit investiture and termination rules for metropolitan or regional governments with real power, parliamentary or assembly-confidence default for most large multi-party states, and fixed-term presidential exceptions only where the system can safely absorb dual mandates without chronic collision.

## Why this matters

The archive already has notes on representation by scope (`490`), executive design by scope (`493`), legislatures by scope (`548`), electoral systems by scope (`566`), the canonical multi-level matrix (`660`), the collapsed-stack rules for microstates (`661`), the scope spine for ideal governments (`699`), the ideal-governments synthesis (`749`), and the morphology and regional-necessity tests (`750`, `751`).

What it still lacked was one compact doctrine for a more operational constitutional question:

**once an election or appointment has occurred, how does a government actually take office, how can it be removed, and how is deadlock repaired without constitutional improvisation?**

That missing layer matters because many governments fail not at the ballot alone but in the handoff from representation to rule. Some systems elect assemblies but cannot reliably produce executives. Some elect both legislature and executive with rival democratic claims and then pretend the constitution has no responsibility for the collision. Some local governments copy strong-mayor or committee arrangements from elsewhere without deciding who can dismiss whom, who keeps services running during conflict, or how a failed governing coalition is replaced without administrative drift. Some metropolitan bodies exercise real public power while surviving on permanent bargaining among incumbents, as if government formation were a private club ritual rather than a public constitutional act.

Ideal-government design therefore needs a doctrine of **formation honesty**. Every scope should say who forms the government, what majority or mandate is required, how failure is repaired, what caretaker limits apply between governments, and how removal happens without turning every crisis into a constitutional blank space.

## Scope table

| Scope | Default formation logic | Default removal logic | Why this fit is usually best | What it must not become |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Micro-local** | open assembly, rotation, or election of a chair / steward team | recall, term-end rotation, or simple majority replacement | keeps authority intimate, light, and reversible | a tiny entrenched presidency |
| **Municipal** | council forms government directly, appoints manager, or works with a separately elected but law-bound mayor | council dismissal of manager, recall or impeachment for mayor where lawful, continuity clerk or administrator | joins democratic direction to professional administration | personal rule or foggy committee drift |
| **Metropolitan / regional with real power** | explicit investiture, public coalition agreement, or directly elected executive under a clear chamber relation | stated no-confidence, recall, term-end, or narrow impeachment rules plus service continuity | prevents rule by permanent summit bargaining | binding government without a formation constitution |
| **National / federal union** | parliamentary confidence as default in multi-party settings; fixed-term presidency only by strong justification | constructive no confidence, confidence vote, dissolution rules, impeachment, and caretaker limits | gives one visible route from election to governable cabinet or executive | dual democratic command without correction path |
| **Continental / plurilateral / global** | treaty-designated appointment or election under mandate-bound rules | fixed term, member recall under treaty rules, or for-cause removal with succession protocol | suits narrow-waist bodies whose legitimacy comes from mandate clarity | secretariat rule by indefinite drift |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from confidence chains, not regime labels

The archive should not ask first whether a system is “parliamentary”, “presidential”, “managerial”, or “mayoral”. It should ask a simpler design question:

**where is the decisive confidence relationship, and can the public tell how it works?**

At each scope, someone or some body must be able to say who currently governs, on what basis, and by what route that basis can be renewed or withdrawn.

### 2. Keep micro-local government easy to rotate and easy to replace

At neighborhood, village, ward, block, or similarly intimate scale, the formation problem is usually modest. The archive should generally prefer:

- direct selection in an open assembly or simple local election,
- short terms,
- rotation where practical,
- recall or replacement by a clear local majority,
- and narrow caretaker expectations when offices fall vacant.

This level needs legitimacy through proximity and reversibility, not a complex separation-of-powers drama.

### 3. At municipal scale, keep democratic direction and professional administration visibly distinct

The municipality is often the first place where formation rules must protect both democracy and continuous service delivery.

The archive should usually prefer one of two clean forms:

- a **council-manager** form, where the elected council forms the political leadership and appoints a professional manager who serves at the council’s confidence; or
- a **mayor-council** form, where a separately elected mayor has an explicit mandate but remains bounded by law, budget authorization, scrutiny, and removal routes.

What matters is not the label alone. It is whether:

- the executive center is identifiable,
- the administration can keep running through political conflict,
- and removal rules are lawful and legible rather than improvised through scandal politics or budget sabotage.

### 4. Do not let metropolitan or regional governments exercise real power without investiture grammar

A metropolitan or regional body that can tax, plan, allocate housing obligations, run transport trunks, or bind land and infrastructure sequencing is not merely a forum. It is a government.

Once that threshold is crossed, the archive should require explicit formation rules:

- who nominates the executive,
- who invests or confirms it,
- what coalition or majority is needed,
- how resignation or loss of support is recorded,
- what caretaker powers remain after breakdown,
- and who maintains services while a successor is formed.

Without that grammar, the polity risks rule by perpetual summit bargaining among incumbent mayors, ministers, or delegates.

### 5. Prefer parliamentary or assembly-confidence government as the default for large multi-party states

For most large, plural, coalition-prone national polities, the archive should treat parliamentary or assembly-confidence government as the default starting point.

The reason is not nostalgia. It is constitutional repairability. Where party systems are fragmented and coalition formation is normal, a system that lets the executive emerge from and remain answerable to a legislative majority usually gives a clearer path for government formation, replacement, and mid-term correction than a system that elects two rival democratic command centers and then hopes partisan virtue will do the rest.

This default is especially strong where the polity is federal, plurinational, highly proportional, or socially divided and therefore likely to require public coalition bargaining.

### 6. Treat fixed-term presidential or semi-presidential government as an exception that needs a positive case

The archive should not treat presidential or semi-presidential design as inherently illegitimate. But it should require a stronger justification before adopting it as the ideal answer.

A fixed-term executive with a direct personal mandate can be workable. Yet it also risks:

- dual democratic command,
- deadlock between executive and legislature,
- personalization of government formation,
- difficult mid-term correction,
- and pressure to stretch impeachment, emergency powers, or constitutional courts into routine conflict managers.

The archive should therefore ask whether the polity truly benefits from a separately elected executive strongly enough to justify those risks.

### 7. Use constructive no-confidence or successor-first rules where negative majorities are easy to assemble

Where parliamentary systems face fragmented party systems or recurrent anti-incumbent coalitionism, the archive should often prefer **constructive no-confidence** or other successor-first mechanisms.

The point is not to make governments immortal. It is to ensure that a majority seeking to remove a government can also produce a replacement rather than merely opening a vacancy and hoping that constitutional improvisation will finish the job.

This is especially useful where ordinary no-confidence votes would otherwise reward performative toppling without replacement capacity.

### 8. Bound dissolution and caretaker powers so repair does not become executive leverage

Dissolution can be a legitimate deadlock-breaker. But it should not become a routine threat that lets incumbents govern parliament by fear of election.

The archive should therefore prefer:

- visible grounds or procedures for dissolution,
- timing limits near recent elections or near the natural end of term,
- caretaker restrictions on appointments, contracts, and major policy change,
- and continuity rules that keep ordinary services, salaries, and rights-bearing procedures alive during interregna.

Government formation rules should repair impasse without letting the outgoing executive smuggle through a last-minute constitutional advantage.

### 9. Separate ceremonial headship from governing headship wherever that reduces conflict

Many systems benefit from distinguishing the symbolic or arbitral head of state from the operational head of government.

The archive should generally prefer that separation where it helps:

- preserve continuity during coalition bargaining,
- anchor impartial ceremonial functions,
- and avoid collapsing every constitutional crisis into the personal contest for one office.

This is less important at municipal or micro-local scale and more important in national systems where government formation may be prolonged.

### 10. Treat removal as a constitutional lane, not as an invitation to sabotage continuity

Every level should say what happens if the government falls.

That means naming:

- the interim officeholder,
- the powers that remain live,
- the decisions that are frozen,
- the deadline or process for successor formation,
- and the treatment of budgets, cases, permits, and emergency operations during the gap.

A removal rule that topples a government but leaves the public unsure who now answers the phone, signs the payroll, or keeps legal deadlines running is only half a constitution.

## Guardrails

- Do not copy national regime labels downward into local government without asking who keeps services running.
- Do not allow metropolitan or regional executives with binding power to survive on unwritten summit custom.
- Do not adopt dual-mandate national systems unless the polity can state clearly why the gain outweighs the repair cost.
- Do not let dissolution become a standing intimidation tool against parliamentary scrutiny.
- Do not treat caretaker periods as a quiet chance for irreversible appointments or contracts.

## Failure modes

- **dual democratic command**: executive and legislature both claim direct supremacy with no stable correction path.
- **negative-majority parliamentarism**: assemblies can always remove a government but cannot form a successor.
- **metro formation fog**: real urban government exists, but nobody can identify the investiture and removal rules.
- **managerial shadow rule**: professional administration becomes the real government because political formation rules are too weak or chaotic.
- **caretaker opportunism**: outgoing governments use the interregnum to lock in deals, appointments, or emergency residue.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can describe **who votes** but cannot also say **how a government is formed, how it falls, who carries the state in the gap, and how deadlock is repaired without rival democratic command**, then it still has **electoral design without a government-formation constitution**.
