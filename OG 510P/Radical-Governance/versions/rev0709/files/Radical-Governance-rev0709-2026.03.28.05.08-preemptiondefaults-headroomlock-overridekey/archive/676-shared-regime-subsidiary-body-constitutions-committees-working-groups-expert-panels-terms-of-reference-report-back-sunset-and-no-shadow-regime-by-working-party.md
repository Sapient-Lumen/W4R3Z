# 676 — Shared-regime subsidiary-body constitutions: committees, working groups, expert panels, terms of reference, report-back, sunset, and no shadow regime by working party

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should create **subsidiary bodies through explicit delegation instruments** that name mandate, composition, officer rules, procedure inheritance, publication and records rules, report-back duty, review or sunset, and non-delegable matters — so committees and working parties do not become permanent shadow constitutions.

## Why this matters

The archive now has notes on entry and exit (`669` and `670`), representation (`671`), decision rules (`672`), officer-and-secretariat power (`673`), records (`674`), and initiation (`675`).

But shared regimes rarely govern only through their plenary or top council.

Most of the actual sorting, drafting, technical review, pre-negotiation, and implementation follow-up happens inside:

- committees,
- working groups,
- subsidiary councils,
- expert panels,
- rapporteur groups,
- and ad hoc task forces.

Without a typed subsidiary-body constitution, three familiar pathologies emerge.

The first is **shadow-regime government by working party**: the parent body still exists formally, but the real settlement happens in a smaller or less visible body that lacks a clear mandate.

The second is **delegation drift**: a body created to study, report, or draft quietly starts deciding, interpreting, or allocating resources on its own.

The third is **zombie committee persistence**: bodies created for a temporary question survive indefinitely because no review, renewal, or sunset lane ever forces a constitutional decision about whether they are still needed.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **subsidiary-body discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish body types rather than calling everything a committee

Not all subordinate bodies do the same constitutional work.

The archive should require regimes to separate at least:

- standing committees,
- open-ended working groups,
- limited-membership committees,
- expert advisory panels,
- technical drafting groups,
- investigation or review panels,
- and temporary task forces.

A body that studies should not be mistaken for a body that decides. A body that advises should not quietly become a body that binds.

### 2. Create each body through one explicit constitutive act

A serious regime should not rely on folklore to explain why a subsidiary body exists.

The constitutive act should name:

- the legal basis,
- the parent body,
- the body type,
- the mandate,
- the duration,
- and the instrument by which the body may be amended, renewed, or dissolved.

### 3. State the mandate narrowly enough to separate study, drafting, implementation support, and decision

“Consider the matter” is usually too vague.

The body’s terms of reference should specify whether it may:

- gather evidence,
- convene consultations,
- prepare draft text,
- recommend options,
- oversee implementation,
- adopt procedural conclusions,
- or make legally or politically binding decisions.

If the parent body wants to delegate a real decision power, it should say so expressly and also say what remains non-delegable.

### 4. Publish composition and participation rules fit to the body’s job

Subsidiary bodies often create exclusion by design drift.

The regime should therefore say:

- whether membership is open-ended or limited,
- what representation or expertise criteria apply,
- how chairs and officers are chosen,
- whether observers may attend,
- and what balance principles apply across regions, development levels, gender, or stakeholder class where relevant.

### 5. Inherit parent procedure by default unless a visible variation is justified

A subsidiary body should not become a hidden constitutional island.

The archive should prefer a rule that the parent body’s procedure, publicity, records, language, and conflict rules apply as far as practicable unless the constitutive act states the variation and why it is needed.

### 6. Separate advisory output from adopted settlement

Committees and expert groups may recommend or draft. That does not itself make their paper the regime’s law.

The regime should preserve a visible boundary between:

- committee reports,
- technical recommendations,
- draft negotiating texts,
- interim findings,
- and parent-body adopted decisions.

### 7. Bind every subsidiary body to report-back and ratification logic

A body that works below the plenary should still answer upward.

The constitutive package should say:

- when it reports,
- to whom,
- in what format,
- whether its output requires endorsement or approval,
- and what happens if the parent body takes no action.

No body should accumulate practical supremacy simply because its reports are always “taken note of” without real constitutional disposition.

### 8. Review need, mandate creep, and body survival on a clock

A body established once should not survive forever by procedural inertia.

The archive should therefore prefer review or sunset rules that ask:

- is the body still needed,
- is its mandate still accurate,
- has its membership become distorted,
- has a temporary function become permanent,
- and should the body be renewed, upgraded, merged, narrowed, or dissolved?

### 9. Apply the same records, publicity, and conflict rules to the real worksite

If the real settlement happens in a smaller room, that room deserves constitutional discipline too.

Subsidiary bodies should therefore have visible rules for:

- agendas and document symbols,
- meeting records,
- publicity status,
- conflict-of-interest or independence disclosures where expert advice matters,
- and custody of working papers and final reports.

### 10. Publish one delegation map across the regime

A serious regime should maintain one visible map showing:

- each subsidiary body,
- its parent,
- its mandate,
- its membership rule,
- its chairing and secretariat support,
- whether it may decide or only recommend,
- what it reports upward,
- and when it next faces review or sunset.

If users must guess whether the real constitution lives in the plenary, the bureau, an expert panel, or a working party that never formally expires, the regime’s delegation map has failed.

## Working matrix

| Subsidiary-body question | Honest rule | Why | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| why the body exists | one constitutive act with legal basis and mandate | prevents folklore delegation | body existence inferred from repeated meetings |
| what the body may do | typed powers: study, draft, advise, oversee, or decide | keeps delegation from drifting | “consider the matter” as a blank cheque |
| who may sit in it | explicit open or limited membership and balance rules | prevents hidden exclusion | committee composition by insider convenience |
| what procedure applies | parent rules inherited unless variation is stated | keeps the regime legible across levels | secret micro-constitutions for sub-bodies |
| what comes back upward | report-back and endorsement or ratification lane | preserves parent-body responsibility | committee outputs operating as law by habit |
| when it ends or is reviewed | review clock or sunset | prevents zombie committee persistence | permanent temporary bodies |

## Guardrails

- Do not let a working group become the real legislature by practice alone.
- Do not create a body without one visible constitutive act and terms of reference.
- Do not delegate binding authority by implication where the parent body still claims ownership.
- Do not vary openness, records, or procedure in a subsidiary body without saying so publicly.
- Do not let temporary bodies survive indefinitely without review.
- Do not treat expert advice as equivalent to member-authorized decision.

## Failure modes

- **shadow-regime government**: a subordinate body becomes the real constitutional center without visible authorization.
- **delegation drift**: an advisory or drafting body quietly starts deciding substantive questions.
- **zombie committee persistence**: temporary structures outlive their problem because nobody must reauthorize them.
- **membership capture**: a limited body becomes the gate through which all settlement must pass.
- **ratification theater**: the parent body formally approves what it no longer meaningfully controls.
- **documentation asymmetry**: the main body is visible while the real worksite stays procedurally dark.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does each subsidiary body have one explicit constitutive basis and parent?
2. Does the regime distinguish advisory, drafting, oversight, and decision functions?
3. Are membership and participation rules visible and fit to the body’s job?
4. Does parent-body procedure apply by default unless variation is justified?
5. Are committee and expert outputs clearly separated from adopted acts?
6. Is there a report-back and endorsement or ratification lane?
7. Does each body face review, renewal, merger, or sunset on a clock?
8. Is there one public delegation map of all live subsidiary bodies?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can name **its plenary bodies and formal decisions** but cannot say **which committees, expert groups, and working parties actually do the sorting, drafting, and interim governance work under what mandate and for how long**, it still has **visible institutions without an honest delegation constitution**.
