# 818 — Information-chain templates for cross-boundary scope forms: shared facts, observatories, record homes, official-statistics links, publication calendars, and no heavy power by private dashboard

## One-line thesis

Once the archive has said **which cross-boundary institutional form is honest**, **what legitimacy chain belongs to it**, **what fiscal chain belongs to it**, **what administrative spine belongs to it**, **what oversight chain belongs to it**, and **what participation chain belongs to it**, it should also be able to say **what information chain belongs to it**: thin connectors should use open shared-indicator rooms and light observatories, shared-service backbones should keep principal-readable service telemetry plus declared case and record routes, bounded functional authorities should keep authoritative operational data homes with method notes and publication calendars, and general-purpose territorial tiers should carry a fuller public-knowledge constitution rather than governing through host-held or vendor-held dashboards.

## Why this matters

`497` already gives the archive a scale-matched public-knowledge stack: local records, metropolitan observatories, independent statistics, audit, and open science. `716` already gives lane-typed government fields a reusable information packet naming canonical case homes, record homes, sharing rules, publication lanes, retention, and recovery. `790` gives the archive a short answer key for what ideal governments at each scope should generally be. `810` says how to assemble threshold proof before saying a metropolitan, county-pooling, regional-platform, fiscal-floor, or legitimacy-thickness line is really crossed. `812` says which institutional form is honest once the burden is real. `813` says who should sit in the room and which decisions require which legitimacy key. `814` says what money constitution belongs to that form. `815` says where the operator, employer, records home, and continuity spine should live. `816` says what scrutiny and redress chain belongs to the form. `817` says what public-input chain belongs to the form.

What the recent scope stack still lacked was one compact answer to the next question:

**where do the shared facts of the body honestly live?**

That gap matters because many constitutional failures are failures of **dashboard sovereignty**.

One failure is **private-dashboard government**: the body makes real choices through indicators, forecasts, rankings, or incident views that live mainly inside a vendor environment, consultant workbook, or host-agency console that the public cannot inspect and even the nominal principals cannot fully interrogate.

Another failure is **host-held fact capture**: a connector or backbone is said to be joint, but one municipality, ministry, or operator quietly controls the common data model, the release timing, the metadata, and the only operational picture that everyone else must trust.

A third failure is **observatory cosplay**: a body publishes maps or dashboards, but nobody can say which indicators are authoritative, which methods and classifications were used, which data are only provisional, how revisions are handled, or how the public can challenge a bad metric before it hardens into allocation or sanction.

A fourth failure is **record-metric confusion**: the live case home, the durable public record, the statistical baseline, and the performance dashboard are treated as though they were one thing. They are not. Once those titles blur, rights and accountability blur with them.

Official material supports a more disciplined information ladder. The United Nations’ Fundamental Principles of Official Statistics treat official statistics as an indispensable element in a democratic information system and insist on professional, impartial, transparent production. The OECD’s Local Data Portal and local-statistics work show the value of comparable subnational indicators, published methodologies, and cross-place comparison rather than governance by anecdote or incomparable local spreadsheets. UN-Habitat’s urban-observatory model treats urban data monitoring, collection, and analysis as a durable institutional function rather than as an ad hoc project dashboard. The World Bank’s Government Analytics work stresses that governments are already data-rich, that administrative data and external assessments should be combined thoughtfully, and that managers need usable, problem-solving analytics rather than disconnected data exhaust. The archive should compress those lessons into one rule: **match information thickness to real public power, and stop letting any body govern mainly through facts that it cannot publicly describe, methodically defend, or hand back intact.**

## Pattern pack

### 1. Use the information-chain note after form, legitimacy, fiscal, administrative, oversight, and participation questions are separated

This note does not decide whether a threshold is crossed.

Use `810` for that.

This note does not decide whether the honest form is a thin connector, shared-service backbone, bounded authority, or general-purpose territorial tier.

Use `812` for that.

This note does not decide who sits in the room or which decisions require ordinary board power, ratification, or a second key.

Use `813` for that.

This note does not decide what revenue base, equalization rule, reserve, or capital lane belongs to the body.

Use `814` for that.

This note does not decide who employs the staff, runs the operations, owns the systems, or carries continuity.

Use `815` for that.

This note does not decide who scrutinizes, audits, hears complaints, supervises legality, or provides final judicial remedy.

Use `816` for that.

This note does not decide which public-input methods and response duties belong to the body.

Use `817` for that.

This note applies after those questions are separated and the remaining live issue is:

- where common indicators and classifications live,
- who owns the operational picture,
- how case homes, record homes, observatories, and dashboards relate,
- what publication and metadata discipline the body owes,
- and when an arrangement has become too information-heavy to keep pretending it is only a light connector or borrowed service wrapper.

### 2. Distinguish four recurrent information chains before inventing hybrids

The archive should usually begin with four recognizable information chains.

| Form | Default information chain | What it may legitimately carry | What it must not hide |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Thin connector** | common indicator room, open issue maps, shared glossary, and light observatory support | non-binding comparisons, planning baselines, shared diagnostics, horizon scanning | undisclosed authoritative rankings, hidden trigger dashboards, or sole custody of cross-boundary facts |
| **Shared-service backbone** | principal-readable service telemetry plus declared case and record routing rules | queue data, service-quality measures, workload dashboards, accessibility metrics, operational learning | host-only or vendor-only performance truth that principals and users cannot inspect |
| **Bounded functional authority** | authoritative operational data home with method notes, revision rules, and public release rhythm | asset state, network performance, incident data, capital sequencing metrics, model outputs tied to one named system | governing by proprietary black box or unpublished operational metrics |
| **General-purpose territorial tier** | fuller public-knowledge constitution with observatory, records and publication lanes, open-data discipline, and official-statistics links where appropriate | territorial indicators, budget and service metrics, policy baselines, public reporting, cross-sector evidence integration | rule by hidden dashboards, private benchmarking, or politically timed metric release |

The archive should not begin with bespoke data poetry until one of these four chains has first been ruled out honestly.

### 3. Separate five information titles even when one institution houses several of them

Later archive work should usually ask five separate questions:

- who owns the **indicator and classification baseline**,
- who owns the **live operational picture**,
- where the **canonical case home and canonical record home** sit,
- who controls the **publication and metadata lane**,
- and how the body connects to **official-statistics, registry, or external-assessment lanes** beyond itself.

One legal order may house several of these titles together. The archive should still separate them explicitly. Silence is how dashboards, record repositories, and statistical baselines collapse into one foggy object that nobody can challenge cleanly.

### 4. Thin connectors should open the evidence room without pretending to own a sovereign metric state

A thin connector is honest when its information chain is limited to things such as:

- shared issue maps,
- common definitions,
- non-binding indicator sets,
- comparative dashboards for planning and mutual learning,
- and light observatory support for a cross-boundary problem that is visible but not yet fully constitutionalized.

The archive should normally require visible answers to questions such as:

- who supplied the data,
- which indicators are merely diagnostic versus decision-triggering,
- what metadata and caveats travel with them,
- how updates and corrections are published,
- and which principal bodies must act if the connector’s evidence points toward a harder decision.

A connector becomes information-chain dishonest once it keeps the only operative burden map, land-allocation ranking, emergency trigger dashboard, or fiscal-risk picture on which other governments are expected to act without equal visibility and challenge rights.

If the connector is becoming the real place where common facts harden into obligations, the archive may also have misclassified the form and should revisit `812` and `813`.

### 5. Shared-service backbones should expose service telemetry to the principals and the public without creating host-held fact monopolies

A shared-service backbone exists because several governments retain political title while pooling machinery.

Its information chain should therefore start from one premise:

**pooled operations may centralize telemetry, but they must not privatize truth.**

That usually means the archive should require visible answers to questions such as:

- which service measures are seen by every principal,
- where case-state truth resides,
- where the durable record resides,
- how user-facing status surfaces relate to the system of record,
- who may alter classifications, thresholds, or queue rules,
- and how performance information is published without erasing privacy, security, or lawful confidentiality.

A backbone becomes constitutionally dishonest when one host, vendor, or lead municipality becomes the only place that can see the queues, reclassify service demand, reconcile the logs, or interpret the metrics that justify priorities for everyone else.

### 6. Bounded functional authorities should own an authoritative operational data home rather than governing through contractor black boxes

A bounded functional authority becomes the honest answer when one named system needs continuous operation, asset stewardship, or capital sequencing.

Its information chain should therefore be heavier than a backbone’s.

Typical honest ingredients include:

- a named operational data home,
- versioned methods and classifications,
- model governance for forecasts or dispatch logic,
- declared asset and incident registers,
- publication calendars or regular release rhythms for public-facing indicators,
- and explicit interfaces to auditors, regulators, emergency partners, and official-statistics lanes where needed.

The archive should reject the pattern where a transit authority, basin authority, grid operator, or similar body makes real governing choices through vendor-held optimization models, unpublished incident data, or consultant-maintained dashboards that the body itself cannot fully audit or explain.

### 7. General-purpose territorial tiers should carry a fuller public-knowledge constitution

A general-purpose territorial tier makes recurring decisions across sectors, burdens, budgets, land, and long horizons.

Its information chain should therefore be treated as a durable constitutional layer.

That can include:

- a territorial observatory or analytics function,
- lawful records and publication lanes,
- clear release and revision discipline for major indicators,
- open-data or data-sharing discipline where appropriate,
- stable links to national official-statistics baselines,
- and visible interfaces between administrative records, budget evidence, service metrics, and public reporting.

The archive does not need to assume that every territorial tier runs a separate full statistical office. It does need to assume that no serious tier should govern through hidden data rooms, selectively shared dashboards, or improvisational metrics that cannot be compared, reproduced, or publicly corrected.

### 8. Require method notes, metadata, and revision discipline once indicators matter for burden, sanction, or capital sequencing

An information chain is not honest if the archive cannot say how the indicators are made.

Later case work should usually require some visible method object proportionate to the stakes, such as:

- a glossary,
- an indicator note,
- a metadata page,
- a revision log,
- a publication calendar,
- or a model card for operational forecasting or prioritization.

A body that governs through numbers without publishing their method discipline is not yet running a real information chain. It is running metric theater.

### 9. Keep official-statistics links and external assessments distinct from the body’s own operational self-description

A cross-boundary body may publish its own dashboards and still depend on wider baselines.

The archive should therefore keep distinct:

- the body’s operational telemetry,
- the broader official-statistics baselines used for comparison,
- and external assessments or lived-path verification used to challenge both.

This keeps a body from grading itself solely through self-generated metrics while still allowing it to run serious internal telemetry.

### 10. Publish information-chain upgrade triggers when lighter forms start carrying heavier informational reality

An information chain is not permanent merely because it was once appropriate.

Later archive work should say what would force an upgrade, such as:

- a connector becoming the only place where cross-boundary indicators are consolidated and interpreted,
- a backbone’s host retaining sole access to operational truth,
- a bounded authority depending on proprietary models or vendor-held logs for core public decisions,
- or a territorial tier making multi-sector allocations through unpublished dashboards or ad hoc extracts.

When those triggers appear, the archive should stop saying only that the form or bureaucracy has matured. It should also say that the **information chain** has matured and now requires thicker observatory, record, publication, metadata, or official-statistics architecture.

### 11. Name downshift tests as well

The archive should also guard against permanent over-thickening of information machinery.

If a temporary observatory has returned to simple open indicators, if a crisis dashboard has reverted to ordinary reporting, or if a temporary data room can now be folded into a stable public publication lane, then heavier information governance may no longer be necessary.

Permanent analytics empires should not survive merely because they once solved a harder problem.

### 12. Require one short information-chain holding in applied verdicts

When `811` is used for a live case, the archive should normally be able to add one short holding such as:

- **honest current form is a shared-service backbone; the pooled center may run common service telemetry, but municipalities retain equal access to the metrics, each keeps a declared record route, and no host-only dashboard may determine shared priorities**;
- **honest current form is a bounded transit authority; the authority now needs its own operational data home, published method notes for reliability and incident measures, and a release rhythm that does not depend on contractor discretion**;
- **honest current form is now a metropolitan tier; planning, land, housing, mobility, and fiscal indicators should move into a visible metropolitan observatory with stable publication and correction discipline rather than remain scattered across core-city and state dashboards**.

That keeps applied judgments from stopping at the words **authority**, **metro**, or **backbone** without saying where the shared facts actually live.

## Guardrails

- Do not let a connector’s dashboard become the undeclared sovereign over other governments’ facts.
- Do not let a shared-service backbone hide queue truth, service metrics, or case-state logic inside one host or vendor environment.
- Do not let a bounded authority govern a critical system through proprietary black boxes it cannot inspect or explain.
- Do not let a territorial tier time or frame metric releases as pure political messaging while still calling them common facts.
- Do not confuse the live case home, the durable public record, the statistical baseline, and the public dashboard.
- Do not centralize every dataset merely because common methods are needed.
- Do not let privacy or security become a blanket excuse for method opacity where public challenge still requires intelligible explanation.
- Do not keep temporary crisis dashboards as permanent shadow constitutions after the exceptional need has narrowed.

## Failure modes

- **private-dashboard government** — real choices are made through vendor-held or consultant-held metrics that the public body cannot fully explain or hand back.
- **host-held fact capture** — one principal quietly owns the classifications, release timing, and operational truth for everyone else.
- **observatory cosplay** — maps and indicators are published, but no clear metadata, revision rule, or challenge path exists.
- **record-metric confusion** — the case home, record home, and performance dashboard are treated as though they were the same object.
- **metric release politics** — numbers that steer burdens or legitimacy appear only when politically convenient.
- **black-box operations** — a bounded authority depends on hidden model logic or inaccessible logs for core public decisions.
- **analytics overhang after downshift** — emergency or transitional data machinery survives after the constitutional need has narrowed.

## Compression rule for the archive

If the archive cannot answer the question **what information chain belongs to this cross-boundary form and this decision?** in one sentence naming the **indicator owner, operational picture, case or record home, publication discipline, and anti-cosplay limit**, then the knowledge design is still too vague to count as settled.
