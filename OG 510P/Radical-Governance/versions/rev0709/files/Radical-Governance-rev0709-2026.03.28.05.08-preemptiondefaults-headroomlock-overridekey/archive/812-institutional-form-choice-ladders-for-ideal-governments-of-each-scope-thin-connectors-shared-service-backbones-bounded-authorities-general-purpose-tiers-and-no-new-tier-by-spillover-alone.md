# 812 — Institutional-form choice ladders for ideal governments of each scope: thin connectors, shared-service backbones, bounded authorities, general-purpose tiers, and no new tier by spillover alone

## One-line thesis

When the archive has already established that a cross-boundary problem is real, it should choose institutional form through one compact **choice ladder**: use a **thin connector** when alignment and information-sharing are enough; a **shared-service backbone** when capacity pooling is the real need; a **bounded functional authority** when one system needs continuous cross-boundary operations or asset stewardship; and a **general-purpose elected tier** only when recurring distributive, land-use, borrowing, coercive, or multi-sector balancing powers make thinner forms constitutionally dishonest.

## Why this matters

`790` now gives the archive a short answer-key for what ideal governments at each scope usually are. `810` now says how to assemble threshold claims before the archive concludes that a metropolitan, county-pooling, regional-platform, fiscal-floor, or legitimacy-thickness line has actually been crossed. `811` now says how a live applied judgment should travel once the answer-key holding, threshold holding, benchmark class, repair state, and publication class are known. `813` now says how the archive should choose the legitimacy chain and decision gates once the honest form is already known. `814` now says how the archive should choose the fiscal chain once form and legitimacy are already known but the revenue, cost-sharing, equalization, reserve, or capital design is still open.

What the archive still lacked was a compact answer to the next question:

**once the need is real, what institutional form should carry it?**

That gap matters because real constitutional work often fails exactly there.

One failure is **tier-thickening panic**: once spillovers or shared systems are visible, the archive jumps too fast to a new elected tier even when a bounded service backbone or narrow functional authority would be enough.

Another failure is **connector theater**: a problem that plainly involves binding priorities, stable pooled finance, or recurring distributive choices is left inside summits, memoranda, or rotating co-ordination forums that cannot honestly own the power they are already exercising.

A third failure is **category blur**: a hosted shared-service center quietly becomes the real governor of a field; a special-purpose authority accumulates general-purpose reach; or a regional platform with decisive budget, land-use, or solidarity work is still described as if it were only technical co-operation.

Official practice supports a more disciplined ladder. The Council of Europe’s Charter on local self-government grounds power in the level closest to citizens while requiring that transferred tasks be matched with resources. The OECD Recommendation on Effective Public Investment Across Levels of Government says governments should co-ordinate at the relevant geographic scale and explicitly lists contracts, dialogue platforms, partnerships, joint authorities, and mergers as different modes rather than as one constitutional blob. OECD work on shared municipal services treats pooling as a serious answer where accessibility, affordability, quality, and capacity are the real issue. OECD work on metropolitan governance shows that metropolitan arrangements come in types rather than one mandatory form. World Bank metropolitan-governance guidance similarly stresses that arrangements evolve, may coexist at different scales, and should be tailored while still protecting participation, responsiveness, and accountability. The archive should compress those lessons into one rule: **choose the thinnest form that can honestly carry the work, but thicken fast once thinner forms start hiding real public power.**

## Pattern pack

### 1. Use the ladder after trigger work, not instead of it

This note does not decide whether a metropolitan, county-pooling, regional-platform, or legitimacy threshold has been crossed.

Use `810` for that.

This note applies after the archive can already say one of the following:

- a shared urban system is real,
- sparse territories need pooled backbone capacity,
- one bounded cross-boundary system needs common operation,
- a regional platform is performing serious territorial work,
- or a case has already drifted into informal upper-tier power.

The ladder therefore answers the **form-choice** question, not the **threshold-crossing** question. Once that answer is known, `813` should carry the separate question of composition, ratification, and second-key design, `814` should carry the separate question of what fiscal chain honestly belongs to that form, `815` should carry the separate question of what operator, records-home, employer, and continuity chain honestly belongs to it, `819` should carry the separate question of who belongs to that body, what territory counts, how accession or amendment should work, and when exit or dissolution is honest, and `824` should carry the separate question of whether that form may merely route and harmonize, may pool permitting and inspection support, or may hold its own openly bounded coercive powers.

### 2. Distinguish four recurrent forms before inventing hybrids

The archive should usually begin with four recognizable forms.

| Form | What it is for | What it may legitimately carry | What it must not hide |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Thin connector** | alignment, signalling, common planning, joint learning | meetings, shared data, joint strategies, non-binding co-ordination, common standards proposals | undeclared binding allocations or shadow rule |
| **Shared-service backbone** | pooled administrative or delivery capacity across several governments | common staff, procurement, IT, case processing, specialist service support, hosted operations under declared principals | disguised transfer of political title or accountability |
| **Bounded functional authority** | one cross-boundary system that needs continuous operation or asset stewardship | transit trunks, basin systems, grids, ports, corridor management, other narrow-purpose system governance | drift into general-purpose government |
| **General-purpose elected tier** | recurring public choices that cut across sectors and territories and require democratic ownership | land-use balancing, housing obligations, taxation or borrowing, distributive allocations, binding multi-sector strategy, coercive rules where law allows | summitry pretending to be democracy or a decorative shell |

The archive should not jump straight to bespoke hybrids unless one of these four forms has first been ruled out honestly.

### 3. Choose a thin connector when the real need is alignment, not command

A thin connector is the right answer when the problem is mostly one of:

- information-sharing,
- common diagnosis,
- synchronized planning,
- relationship-building,
- voluntary project preparation,
- or early-stage agenda-setting.

This form is especially useful where trust is low, the functional need is visible but still immature, or the immediate gain from acting together is modest and practical.

But a thin connector becomes dishonest once it starts doing things such as:

- setting binding quotas or territorial obligations,
- allocating material burdens among municipalities,
- managing debt or long-term pooled finance,
- deciding disputes with no external review,
- or becoming the real place where hard trade-offs are made.

At that point the archive should stop treating the form as merely connective.

### 4. Choose a shared-service backbone when the real need is pooled capacity with title preserved

A shared-service backbone is often the honest answer where several local governments retain the political title to a function, but cannot each carry the administrative or specialist machinery efficiently on their own.

Typical signs include:

- low-density or shrinking territories,
- specialist staffing shortages,
- back-office duplication,
- uneven quality in licensing, payroll, IT, procurement, inspection support, call handling, or care administration,
- and strong gains from joint processing, joint procurement, common digital infrastructure, or pooled professional teams.

In this form, the archive should require clarity on:

- who remains the legal principal,
- which decisions stay political versus operational,
- how costs are shared,
- who may enter and exit,
- how host-of-record duties are bounded,
- and how audit, records, complaints, and continuity work across the pooled arrangement.

A shared-service backbone is therefore **pooling without hidden sovereignty transfer**.

### 5. Choose a bounded functional authority when one system needs continuous cross-boundary governance

A bounded functional authority becomes the honest answer when one named system cannot be run credibly through loose co-ordination or dispersed host arrangements alone.

Typical cases include:

- metropolitan transport trunks,
- watershed or basin management,
- grid and system-balancing functions,
- ports and airports,
- corridor or network infrastructure,
- tertiary referral systems,
- or another narrow field where asset stewardship, real-time operations, common technical standards, and continuity matter.

This form should have:

- a hard public charter,
- a named remit boundary,
- stable finance appropriate to the function,
- clear board or executive responsibility,
- records and review routes,
- and explicit upgrade or slimming tests.

The archive should prefer this form over a general-purpose tier when the function is genuinely bounded and cross-sector distributive politics are still limited.

### 6. Choose a general-purpose elected tier when the work has become political in the full constitutional sense

A general-purpose elected tier is warranted when the arrangement must repeatedly make choices that are:

- territorial and distributive,
- multi-sector rather than single-purpose,
- binding across member governments,
- fiscally consequential through taxation, major transfers, or borrowing,
- materially coercive through planning, siting, obligation-setting, or enforcement,
- or legitimacy-heavy enough that indirect brokerage no longer counts as an honest democratic chain.

This is the point at which metropolitan government, county-style pooling with public-legible authority, or a real regional tier becomes the honest answer.

The archive should treat the following as especially strong signals for general-purpose thickening:

- binding land-use and housing allocation across municipalities,
- major pooled capital budgets and debt,
- routine burden-sharing fights,
- recurrent redistribution or solidarity decisions,
- multi-field emergency command that never returns cleanly to lower levels,
- and arrangements whose real public controversy now concerns values and burdens, not just technical operations.

### 7. Keep the “as thin as possible, as thick as necessary” rule explicit

The ladder should not be used as a one-way ratchet toward thicker institutions.

The default discipline is:

- do not create a new elected tier merely because co-ordination is annoying,
- do not create a functional authority merely because incumbents dislike bargaining,
- do not preserve tiny stand-alone delivery where pooled capacity would be plainly better,
- but do not leave real public power inside forms too weak or opaque to own it.

The archive should therefore say the form-choice rule explicitly in later case work: **choose the lightest form that can carry the job without hiding binding power, fiscal exposure, or democratic consequence.**

### 8. Allow mixed stacks and scale variation by function

One place may honestly use more than one form at once.

A metro area may need:

- a thin connector for tourism branding,
- a shared-service backbone for digital procurement,
- a bounded authority for transit,
- and a general-purpose metropolitan tier for land-use, housing balance, and pooled capital.

A sparse territory may need:

- micro-local or municipal voice for place priorities,
- a shared-service county-style backbone for administration and emergency coverage,
- and a regional platform for hospitals, corridors, or watersheds.

The archive should not force one perfect map to do every job.

### 9. Name escalation triggers from thinner to thicker forms

Later notes should say **what would force an upgrade**.

Examples:

- a connector begins making binding allocations or becomes the real veto point,
- a shared-service backbone accumulates policy discretion over priorities among member governments,
- a bounded authority starts controlling several major fields at once,
- or a supposedly technical body becomes the actual site of distributive conflict and public contest.

Once those triggers appear, the archive should stop treating the earlier form as stable and should issue a clearer form holding under this ladder.

### 10. Name downshift and burial tests as well

Because the archive is trying not to bloat itself or the constitution it studies, every thicker form should also carry a slimming question.

Examples:

- has a crisis workaround expired,
- has one authority’s portfolio narrowed back to a single bounded service,
- has pooled delivery become unnecessary after merger or capability equalization,
- or has a connector become redundant because the work has been constitutionalized elsewhere?

This keeps emergency thickening, pilot machinery, and inherited shells from hardening into permanent architecture without a fresh case.

### 11. Require one short institutional-form holding in applied judgments

When the archive uses `811` for a real case, it should normally be able to say one short sentence such as:

- **threshold crossed; current honest form is a bounded transport-and-spatial authority, not yet a full metro tier**;
- **sparse-territory capacity problem; current honest form is pooled shared-service backbone with municipal political title preserved**;
- **regional platform matured beyond consortium form; honest answer is now a real regional tier with direct democratic ownership**.

This keeps applied verdicts from blurring the difference between **a real problem** and **the right institutional answer to that problem**. It also keeps them from stopping too early: once the answer says *what form*, `813` should say *who owns it and what decisions require which legitimacy key*, `814` should say *what money constitution belongs to it*, and `815` should say *where the real operating spine belongs*.

### 12. Keep form choice separate from prestige imitation

The archive should not choose forms because famous places use them.

Metro mayors’ summits, county maps, regional parliaments, basin boards, and development agencies all travel badly when copied without asking:

- what real work exists,
- what legal title is moving,
- what finance and liability follow,
- what public controversy is being decided,
- and whether the citizen can still tell who is governing.

Form choice should therefore remain tied to vocation, burden, finance, and legitimacy — not to postcard borrowing.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat spillover alone as automatic proof of a new elected tier.
- Do not treat shared services as a free pass to hide who still owns the legal duty.
- Do not let a bounded authority quietly accumulate general-purpose powers without upgrade tests.
- Do not leave real distributive, borrowing, or land-use power inside connectors or technical boards.
- Do not force one territorial line to carry every function when mixed forms are more honest.
- Do not preserve a thicker form after the condition that justified it has plainly vanished.

## Failure modes

- **connector theater** — a forum or compact is described as light co-ordination even though it already allocates burdens or sets binding priorities.
- **pooling amnesia** — a shared-service backbone is described as mere administration while it quietly decides substantive priorities with no public-legible chain.
- **authority drift** — a narrow functional body becomes a shadow general-purpose government.
- **prestige mimicry** — a place copies a metro, county, or regional model mainly because admired jurisdictions use it.
- **one-map absolutism** — every function is forced into the same territorial shell even though the work lives on different footprints.
- **permanent workaround constitution** — temporary hosts, crisis boards, or pilots survive after their justification has expired.

## Practical tests

An institutional-form holding is honest when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is the threshold or functional need already clear enough that form choice is the live question?
2. Does the chosen form match the real problem: alignment, pooled capacity, bounded system operation, or general-purpose political balancing?
3. Is the legal title to duties and liabilities visible rather than hidden inside hosting or brokerage?
4. Are taxation, borrowing, coercion, distributive choice, or binding land-use powers owned by a form thick enough to carry them honestly?
5. If the chosen form is thin, is there a declared trigger for upgrading once it starts hiding real power?
6. If the chosen form is thick, is there a declared slimming or burial test if the justification disappears?
7. Can a citizen still tell who decides, who pays, who reviews, and where challenge routes live?
8. Is the holding short enough that later notes can cite the chosen form without reopening the whole design argument?

## Compression rule for the archive

If the archive can say **the threshold is crossed** but still cannot say whether the honest answer is a **thin connector, shared-service backbone, bounded functional authority, or general-purpose elected tier**, then it has **a trigger doctrine without a compact form-choice doctrine**.
