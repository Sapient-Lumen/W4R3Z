# 578 — Open data and public-information reuse by scope: community scrutiny, municipal publishing, regional aggregation, national standards, and no government by PDF fog

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat **open data and public-information reuse** as the **constitutional public-reuse layer** of government: keep community scrutiny, correction demand, and context close to the people and places described by the data; require municipalities and frontline bodies to publish routine local information in genuinely reusable ways rather than as decorative PDF dumps; pool regional aggregation, comparison, and cross-boundary publication where fragmented local release would make the public blind to shared systems; reserve national or constitutional-state authority for the legal framework on reuse, licensing, metadata, machine-readable publication, privacy and confidentiality boundaries, high-value dataset rules, and catalog standards; and keep continental or global roles narrow enough to support interoperable metadata, cross-border discovery, and common high-value publication patterns without becoming one world data ministry.

## Why this matters

The archive now has tighter notes on **public records and official publication**, **official statistics**, **base registries**, **trust services**, **administrative geography**, **service delivery**, and **access to information and data protection**. What it still lacked was the layer that answers a simpler but increasingly decisive question: once the state has lawful records and non-personal information it can disclose, **how should it publish that information so the public can actually find it, compare it, reuse it, and build civic oversight or useful services on top of it?**

That gap matters because governments often perform transparency in the most inert format available. They publish scanned tables, fragmented spreadsheets, portal screenshots, unsearchable annual reports, or narrow agency pages with no stable metadata, no open formats, no bulk access, and no durable catalog logic. The result is a polity that can claim disclosure while still governing by **PDF fog**: the information technically exists, but reuse is so brittle, partial, or labor-intensive that only specialists can make sense of it.

An ideal archive therefore needs one compact note on the public-reuse layer. This layer is not identical to freedom-of-information law, not identical to records law, and not identical to digital-public-infrastructure doctrine. It is about **proactive publication, reusable formats, metadata, catalog discipline, lawful reuse terms, dynamic-data APIs where speed matters, and the scale match between local truth, regional comparability, national standards, and narrow cross-border discovery rails**. Open data should expand public scrutiny and practical utility without pretending that every document should be public or that privacy can be solved by wishful thinking.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish open reusable publication from both records custody and request-driven access

A polity should not confuse three different things: keeping authentic records, answering access-to-information requests, and proactively publishing reusable public information. Records law protects authenticity, retention, and evidentiary continuity. Access law protects the right to ask for information that is not already disclosed. Open-data policy governs the proactive release of non-personal or lawfully de-identified information in forms suited to reuse.

The archive should therefore treat open data as a **separate but connected constitutional layer**. A government that has strong archives but no reusable publication remains hard to inspect. A government that answers requests but never proactively publishes common datasets makes civic oversight too slow and unequal. And a government that publishes datasets without authentic source ownership or lawful confidentiality boundaries turns openness into a sloppy data dump.

### 2. Keep community scrutiny and error surfacing close to the lived reality the data describes

At the smallest scales, people, journalists, civic groups, researchers, and local businesses often notice first when public information is missing, stale, misleading, or impossible to compare. Community scrutiny is therefore valuable for identifying publication gaps, demanding correction, and explaining what local context the raw numbers miss.

But the archive should reject the idea that every neighborhood body should run its own durable open-data platform. Micro-local actors are usually better at **problem discovery and contextual challenge** than at long-term catalog stewardship. Their role is to pressure the system toward truthful publication, not to become miniature sovereign data portals.

### 3. Municipalities and frontline public bodies should publish routine local facts in genuinely reusable forms

A large share of public information is generated close to the ground: budgets, procurement notices and awards, permits, inspections, service requests, street and curb rules, transit operations, environmental readings, local laws, facility inventories, election administration facts, and the practical datasets that shape daily life. Municipalities and comparable frontline bodies should therefore publish routine local information proactively where law allows.

The archive should insist, however, that publication be **actually reusable**. That means stable links, machine-readable formats where feasible, adequate metadata, update discipline, clear field meanings, and lawful reuse terms. A city that publishes key operational information only as styled PDFs, image tables, or rotating web fragments has disclosed without really publishing.

### 4. Regional, metropolitan, state, provincial, or intermunicipal layers should aggregate cross-boundary data where comparison and systems-level visibility matter

Many public systems do not stop at municipal boundaries: commuting, transit, housing markets, watersheds, hospital catchments, waste systems, air quality, labor markets, and procurement ecosystems all cross jurisdictions. If every municipality publishes only its own slice without any comparable aggregation, the public sees fragments where the real system is shared.

The archive should therefore prefer regional publication layers where scale adds interpretability: metropolitan dashboards, basin-wide environmental series, corridor data, regional service comparisons, pooled procurement publication, and other cross-boundary releases that let people inspect a functional geography rather than a political map. The point is not to confiscate local truth from its source. It is to make shared systems legible above local fragmentation.

### 5. National or constitutional-state government should own the reuse framework, metadata rules, catalog architecture, and high-value publication standards

Some parts of open-data governance are too basic to rights protection, market fairness, and interoperability to be left to local improvisation: the legal basis for reuse, charging rules, open-license patterns where used, machine-readable publication expectations, metadata requirements, catalog architecture, persistent identifiers, API and bulk-download norms, confidentiality and anonymization boundaries, and the treatment of high-value or dynamic datasets.

The archive should therefore prefer a national or constitutional-state layer that defines **what it means to publish public information for reuse rather than for mere display**. This level should maintain or require coherent catalog infrastructure, minimum metadata standards, inventory discipline, and rules for when dynamic data deserve APIs, when bulk download matters, and when privacy, security, or confidentiality clearly block public release.

### 6. Publish dynamic and high-value datasets through stable APIs and bulk download where speed and scale matter

Not all public data need real-time interfaces. But some categories lose much of their public value if they are delayed, manually scraped, or trapped behind ornamental web pages: mobility data, environmental observations, meteorology, geospatial updates, company data, some statistical releases, and other high-value or frequently updated datasets.

The archive should therefore prefer a stronger publication standard for **dynamic and high-value data**: stable APIs, clear technical documentation, lifecycle maintenance, and bulk download where relevant. Governments should not use “portal presence” as a substitute for operational accessibility. If the public can see that the data exist but cannot realistically retrieve or reuse them at scale, then the reuse layer is still performative.

### 7. Metadata, catalogs, and inventories are constitutional infrastructure, not clerical garnish

A dataset that cannot be found, interpreted, or related to its source is only nominally open. The archive should therefore treat metadata, inventories, and catalogs as first-class governance components. Good publication requires titles that mean something, descriptions of scope and limitations, update cadence, provenance, field definitions, coverage notes, licensing or reuse terms, contact points, and persistent identifiers that survive redesigns.

This matters especially in multi-level systems. A national catalog should not replace local publication, but it should make local and regional publication discoverable through a common frame. Without that catalog logic, open-data policy becomes a scattering of agency websites rather than a governed public-information surface.

### 8. Do not let openness erase privacy, confidentiality, safety, or the difference between person-level data and public facts

The archive should be explicit that open-data doctrine is not a command to publish everything. Personal data, protected confidential information, sensitive security details, and information whose disclosure would create clear and disproportionate harm require lawful restraint, aggregation, de-identification, or refusal. Reuse frameworks should work with privacy, confidentiality, and access law rather than pretending those problems disappear once a portal exists.

But confidentiality should not become a blanket excuse for withholding obviously publishable information. The archive should therefore reject both reckless data dumping and theatrical secrecy. Good open-data governance names the lawful boundary, documents the reason, and still publishes what can be safely and meaningfully released.

### 9. Cross-border and global roles should stay narrow: semantic alignment, discovery, and common publication patterns

There is real value in international metadata models, common vocabularies, and shared expectations for high-value datasets. These help people find comparable information across portals, jurisdictions, and borders without forcing one sovereign repository for everything.

The archive should therefore prefer continental and global roles that remain narrow: interoperability standards, metadata profiles, common dataset categories, cross-border discovery portals, and shared publication guidance. What it should reject is the fantasy that one supranational platform should absorb every domestic dataset or overwrite domestic decisions about what may lawfully be published.

### 10. Open-data publication should be tied back to source authority, correction routes, and update discipline

Reusable publication cannot float free from the institutions that own the underlying truth. A data portal should preserve lineage to the source body, indicate update timing, and offer a route for challenge or correction. Otherwise the state creates one more layer of detached copies, stale extracts, and misleading dashboards.

This links the public-reuse layer back to the archive’s work on **base registries**, **official statistics**, **public records**, **administrative procedure**, and **service delivery**. A polity should let the public reuse information broadly, but it should not let a derivative portal quietly become the only visible version of the truth.

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse records custody or request-driven disclosure with proactive reusable publication.
- Do not let municipalities satisfy transparency duties with scanned tables, styled PDFs, or web fragments when machine-readable release is feasible.
- Do not centralize local datasets so aggressively that local source ownership, context, and correction routes disappear.
- Do not publish dynamic or high-value datasets only through human-oriented webpages when APIs or bulk download are the obvious scale match.
- Do not let metadata, schema notes, update cadence, and provenance vanish behind “download data” buttons.
- Do not use open-data rhetoric to justify reckless publication of personal, confidential, or clearly dangerous information.
- Do not let privacy or confidentiality become a blanket excuse for withholding obviously publishable non-personal information.
- Do not let catalog portals become disconnected copy layers with no visible lineage to the source authority.
- Do not treat international discovery rails or metadata standards as authority to build one sovereign world data portal.

## Failure modes

- **government by PDF fog**: the information is technically public but practically unusable for comparison, audit, or reuse.
- **portal theatre**: officials can point to a portal, but links are unstable, metadata are thin, and bulk reuse is unrealistic.
- **catalog without custody**: datasets are discoverable, but no one can tell which body owns the underlying truth or how errors get corrected.
- **local transparency lottery**: some municipalities publish reusable data while others publish only decorative fragments, making oversight postcode-dependent.
- **regional blindness**: shared systems remain invisible because publication stops at municipal borders.
- **API tokenism**: an API exists, but it is undocumented, unstable, rate-limited into uselessness, or missing bulk access where scale demands it.
- **confidentiality theater**: obvious public-interest datasets are withheld under vague claims that no one can meaningfully challenge.
- **data dump irresponsibility**: large raw releases occur without context, update discipline, or privacy review, creating harm and distrust.
- **copy-state drift**: portal extracts and dashboards quietly become more visible than the source systems they depend on.

## Practical tests

An open-data and public-information-reuse architecture is scale-matched and rights-compatible when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is reusable publication treated as a distinct discipline rather than being confused with archives, gazettes, or access-to-information request handling?
2. Can communities, journalists, researchers, and local civic actors identify missing, stale, or misleading datasets and route corrections toward the source owner?
3. Do municipalities and frontline public bodies publish routine local information proactively in reusable formats with adequate metadata where law allows?
4. Are regional or metropolitan publication layers used where shared systems would otherwise remain invisible across fragmented local releases?
5. Is there a clear law-owning level that sets the reuse framework, charging rules, metadata expectations, catalog architecture, privacy boundaries, and high-value-dataset standards?
6. Are dynamic or high-value datasets made available through stable APIs and, where relevant, bulk download rather than only through human-facing webpages?
7. Do catalogs and inventories preserve provenance, update cadence, field meaning, and contact points rather than acting as unlabeled piles of files?
8. Are privacy, confidentiality, security, and de-identification handled as real release conditions rather than as either an afterthought or an all-purpose veto?
9. Do cross-border discovery and metadata standards stay narrow enough to improve comparability without pretending to absorb domestic publication sovereignty?
10. Can a user or reviewer trace a published dataset back to its source authority, understand its limits, and find a correction path when the data look wrong?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say it is transparent but still publishes core public information mainly as **scanned reports, decorative dashboards, unstable downloads, or portal fragments without usable metadata, APIs, bulk access, provenance, or correction routes**, then it still has **public disclosure without a constitutional public-reuse layer**.
