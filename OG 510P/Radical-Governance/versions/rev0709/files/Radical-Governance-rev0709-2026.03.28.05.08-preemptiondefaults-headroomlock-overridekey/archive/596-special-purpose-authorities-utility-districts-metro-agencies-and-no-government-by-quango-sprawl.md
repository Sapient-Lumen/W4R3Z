# 596 — Special-purpose authorities, utility districts, metro agencies, and no government by quango sprawl

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should use special-purpose authorities only as tightly bounded tools for real network, basin, corridor, or technical-governance problems, so shared systems can be run at the right scale without letting single-purpose bodies metastasize into a shadow constitution of opaque boards, off-budget debt, and blame-proof government.

## Why this matters

The archive already has notes on capability congruence, intermunicipal consortia, concurrent competences, deconcentrated administration, delegated tasks, metropolitan governance, territorial reorganization, home rule, ordinance power, and judicial remedy. What it still lacked was one compact doctrine for the **single-purpose institutional layer**: the transport authorities, water and sewer districts, port authorities, housing corporations, drainage districts, development agencies, school districts, utility boards, and similar public bodies that sit beside ordinary territorial government.

That gap matters because special-purpose bodies solve real problems and create real constitutional hazards. Sometimes a service or asset system simply does not fit the municipal map. A transit network, watershed, port complex, airport system, electricity grid, sewer basin, or metropolitan housing platform may need a body whose territory and technical focus are narrower or wider than any one general-purpose local government. But once governments discover this instrument, they often overuse it. One district becomes ten. One transport agency becomes a transport authority, toll body, fare corporation, parking district, and land-development entity. Elected local governments retain the blame, but the power, debt, procurement, and contracts migrate into appointed boards and hard-to-read statutes.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine for **special-purpose bodies without quango sprawl**. Ideal government should allow them where hard network, basin, corridor, or technical conditions justify them. But it should also force a visible choice: either the function is narrow enough to stay inside a bounded authority with clear democratic anchoring, or the bundle of functions has become broad enough that the polity should create, empower, or rely on a genuine general-purpose territorial government instead of governing by proliferating agencies.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat special-purpose bodies as exception tools, not as the default architecture of place-governance

A special-purpose authority should exist because one task or system genuinely needs a distinct territorial scale, technical regime, or financing structure. It should not be the lazy default whenever higher tiers want to avoid strengthening a municipality, county, metro, or region.

The archive should therefore treat special-purpose bodies as typed exceptions inside a broader territorial constitution, not as the ordinary way public authority is arranged.

### 2. Separate four different forms: general-purpose self-government, sectoral authority, operating utility, and representative association

The archive should keep at least four forms visibly distinct:

- **general-purpose territorial government**, which carries a broad democratic mandate over place;
- **special-purpose authority or district**, which exists to govern one bounded field or system;
- **public operator or enterprise**, which may run infrastructure or service operations without being the primary constitutional policy-setter;
- **representative or cooperative association**, which coordinates or advocates but should not silently become a taxing or coercive government.

Blurring these forms invites constitutional drift. A public operator should not quietly become the law-owning authority. A special-purpose district should not masquerade as a metropolitan government. An association of municipalities should not accumulate hard powers that bypass local electorates.

### 3. Use special-purpose authorities where the problem has a hard functional geography

Single-purpose bodies are most defensible where the relevant geography is stubbornly non-municipal: a watershed, drainage basin, commuter rail shed, port complex, airport zone, trunk utility network, flood-control system, waste-disposal catchment, or metropolitan corridor.

The archive should prefer special-purpose authorities where at least one of the following is plainly true:

- the infrastructure has strong network effects or indivisible fixed assets;
- the spillovers are too concentrated or too technical for loose voluntary coordination alone;
- creditor, safety, or continuity requirements justify ring-fenced governance;
- the territory of the function differs materially from ordinary municipal or regional boundaries.

Where those conditions do not hold, the default should usually be ordinary territorial government, cooperation, or delegation instead.

### 4. Give each authority a narrow charter and forbid mandate creep by implication

Every special-purpose body should have a charter that names:

- its exact field of action;
- the territory it covers;
- the powers it may exercise;
- the powers it does **not** possess;
- the finance instruments it may use;
- the review, audit, and dissolution route.

The archive should reject the common drift in which a body created for one purpose gradually acquires land-use leverage, policing, development finance, procurement platforms, or data powers merely because they are useful to the original mission. If the real bundle has become multi-sector and territorial, the polity should say so and redesign the institution honestly.

### 5. Match democratic anchoring to the severity of the body’s powers

The stronger the powers, the stronger the democratic anchor should be. A body that merely coordinates technical planning or owns a ring-fenced asset can often be governed by elected representatives delegated from the participating governments. A body that sets compulsory charges, issues significant debt, makes binding regional rules, condemns land, or shapes metropolitan development in several sectors needs stronger democratic legitimacy and transparency.

The archive should therefore prefer a visible ladder:

- **advisory/technical bodies** may use weaker democratic anchoring;
- **single-purpose governing authorities** should usually be controlled by elected officials from constituent governments or, where powers are broad enough, directly elected;
- **bodies with multi-sector territorial power** should trigger the question whether a genuine metro or regional government is the more honest institution.

### 6. Keep finance legible: no shadow taxation, hidden guarantees, or off-map debt

Special-purpose bodies are often created partly to finance infrastructure or services. That can be legitimate. It can also become a hiding place for public debt, quasi-taxes, complex fee structures, and guarantees that no ordinary voter can reconstruct.

The archive should therefore require every authority to publish:

- its revenue sources and charging logic;
- whether charges are voluntary, compulsory, tariff-based, or tax-like;
- who ultimately guarantees its debt;
- how liabilities interact with member governments;
- whether the authority can pledge future revenues, assets, or land values.

A polity should not be able to claim local fiscal restraint while quietly shifting borrowing and pricing power into authority law.

### 7. Preserve a singular public accountability map even when service delivery is split

Residents should not need an institutional anthropology degree to know who decides, who operates, who pays, and where complaints go. Even when a service is carried by a special-purpose body, the polity should keep one publicly intelligible accountability map naming:

- the elected governments that appoint or oversee the board;
- the authority’s statutory duties;
- the operator, if different from the authority;
- the complaint and appeal route;
- the audit and open-records route;
- the emergency and continuity chain.

The archive should reject systems where ordinary territorial governments remain the citizen-facing blame sink while the real decisions vanish into a stack of semi-visible boards.

### 8. Do not use special-purpose bodies to evade home rule, consultation, or territorial politics

A government should not route controversial land use, transport pricing, school closure, policing technology, or utility disconnection choices into a special-purpose authority merely because elected territorial bodies would have to debate them in public.

The archive should therefore treat agency creation, function transfer, and mandate expansion through special-purpose bodies as constitutionally significant. If the move materially narrows local headroom, bypasses consultation, or reshapes territorial politics, it should trigger the same seriousness as any other multilevel override or competence transfer.

### 9. Review the authority periodically and keep absorption or conversion on the table

A body that was justified twenty years ago may no longer be the right institution after territorial reform, capability growth, digitization, or the creation of a stronger metro or regional government. Special-purpose bodies should therefore face periodic review asking:

- does the problem still require a separate authority?
- could the function now sit inside a general-purpose territorial government?
- should the authority be narrowed, merged, federated, or dissolved?
- has mandate creep turned a single-purpose body into undeclared general government?

The archive should prefer sunset or review clocks over immortal agency accumulation.

### 10. Where many single-purpose bodies accumulate in one territory, ask whether the polity actually needs a general-purpose metro or regional tier

One transport authority or one basin district may be sensible. But when a territory is governed by a transport body, housing corporation, planning board, sewer district, development agency, resilience authority, and land-value capture entity at once, the design question changes. The problem may no longer be sectoral coordination. It may be that the polity lacks an honest general-purpose metropolitan or regional government.

The archive should therefore treat **special-purpose proliferation** as a constitutional signal, not just an administrative curiosity. Sometimes the cure for quango sprawl is not one more agency but a real democratic tier.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat special-purpose authorities as a free substitute for strengthening ordinary territorial government.
- Do not blur special-purpose governance, operating enterprises, and representative associations.
- Do not let single-purpose charters quietly accumulate multi-sector territorial power.
- Do not allow major charges, debts, takings, or coercive effects to sit inside weakly anchored boards.
- Do not hide fiscal risk or member-government guarantees behind authority law.
- Do not let authority proliferation erase a singular citizen-facing accountability map.
- Do not use special-purpose bodies to evade local consultation, home rule, or visible override rules.
- Do not make authorities immortal by habit when territorial conditions have changed.

## Failure modes

- **quango sprawl**: governments solve every coordination problem by creating another board, district, or authority until the territorial constitution becomes unreadable.
- **shadow metro government**: a stack of single-purpose bodies performs metropolitan governance without a democratic metropolitan settlement.
- **operator-sovereign blur**: a utility or operating company quietly becomes the real policy-setter.
- **ring-fenced opacity**: debt, tariffs, guarantees, and capital commitments migrate into authority law and disappear from ordinary public understanding.
- **appointment fog**: the board exercises major public power but its democratic chain is too weak or obscure to sustain legitimacy.
- **override by districting**: higher tiers use authorities to bypass home rule, consultation, or ordinary territorial politics.
- **immortal agency accumulation**: bodies survive long after their original rationale has weakened, creating duplicative power and overlapping mandates.

## Practical tests

A polity is handling special-purpose bodies honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is each special-purpose authority justified by a real functional geography, network logic, basin logic, or continuity requirement rather than by institutional convenience alone?
2. Are general-purpose governments, special-purpose authorities, operating utilities, and representative associations kept legally distinct?
3. Does every authority have a narrow charter with explicit powers, exclusions, finance rules, review routes, and dissolution logic?
4. Is the democratic anchoring of the body proportionate to the severity of its powers?
5. Can residents tell who appoints the board, who pays, who operates, who audits, and where complaints or appeals go?
6. Are debts, guarantees, charges, and member-government liabilities visible rather than hidden in off-map finance?
7. Does authority creation or mandate expansion trigger serious consultation and multilevel review when it narrows territorial self-government?
8. Is there a periodic test for whether the function should remain separate or move into a genuine general-purpose territorial tier?
9. Where many authorities accumulate in one territory, does the polity ask whether it actually needs an honest metropolitan or regional government?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can explain **which municipality or region exists**, but cannot also explain **which special-purpose bodies govern the same place, why they exist, how they are financed, who appoints them, which powers they hold, and when they should be folded back into democratic territorial government**, then that polity has not built clear multilevel government. It has built **a shadow constitution of boards, districts, and authorities**.
