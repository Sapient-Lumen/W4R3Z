# 547 — Budgeting, treasury, and public accounts: credible ceilings, legislative authority, one treasury, and no government by fund fog

## One-line thesis

An ideal polity should turn public money into a lawful cycle of strategy, appropriation, controlled execution, and audited account: set clear fiscal objectives and medium-term ceilings, present one comprehensive budget rather than a maze of side funds, give the legislature real time and analytic capacity to approve totals and appropriations, execute through a unified treasury, chart of accounts, commitment controls, and cash planning, publish in-year reports and year-end financial statements, disclose fiscal risks honestly, and route audit findings back into correction so the state cannot govern through supplementary fog, arrears, or off-budget improvisation.

## Why this matters

The archive already says a great deal about taxation, equalization, public investment, integrity, public enterprises, delegated public functions, and fiscal federalism. What it still lacked was one compact note on the **budget constitution** itself: the machinery that turns public priorities into authorized spending, turns authorizations into controlled payments, and turns execution back into visible accounts that can be challenged.

That gap matters because many governments fail less from a shortage of announced policy than from a broken money cycle. A legislature approves promises it cannot really see. Ministries spend through side vehicles, extra-budgetary funds, quasi-fiscal mandates, or emergency carry-over habits. Cash is rationed in-year without honest political ownership. Suppliers and public workers absorb the shock through arrears, hidden commitments, or service failure. Audits arrive too late to shape correction. Citizens then experience the state as simultaneously overpromising and unavailable.

An ideal government therefore needs a doctrine of **lawful budget governance**. The right answer is not only “balance the books,” and it is not only “fund public goods.” It is a public finance operating system that makes trade-offs explicit, limits credible, execution controlled, and accounts intelligible enough that money cannot disappear into institutional fog.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from a credible fiscal frame and medium-term ceilings, not an annual wish list

A serious budget should begin with explicit fiscal objectives, realistic macro assumptions, and a medium-term view of what the state can sustain. Annual appropriations still matter, but they should sit inside a multi-year frame that exposes future obligations, policy trade-offs, and fiscal space honestly enough that the budget is not rebuilt from scratch every year as political theater.

That frame should be simple enough for outsiders to follow: what the government is trying to protect, what aggregate limits or objectives govern the plan, which new commitments are being made, and what that implies for later years. Medium-term budgeting is valuable not because forecasts are perfect, but because pretending the future does not exist is worse.

### 2. Present one comprehensive budget and treat off-budget vehicles as exceptions to be justified

The budget should aim to show the real fiscal state, not merely the easiest slice to table in parliament. Extra-budgetary funds, earmarked accounts, emergency windows, special accounts, and quasi-fiscal entities may sometimes be defensible, but they should face a high burden of justification and clear reporting obligations. A government should not move activity out of the core budget merely because it wants fewer questions.

An outsider should be able to tell, from the main budget documentation and supporting fiscal reports, where the money is, what is authorized, what is financed by taxes or borrowing, and what material exposures sit outside ordinary appropriations. Budget comprehensiveness is one of the main ways democratic finance stays democratic.

### 3. Give the legislature real time, analytic support, and authority over the totals before the details

Budget authorization should not be a ceremonial sprint. Legislatures need enough time, committee capacity, access to information, and analytic support to review the executive’s proposal before the fiscal year begins. A good system lets representatives debate the aggregate fiscal frame and major trade-offs before they descend into amendment-by-amendment theatre.

This also means protecting the legislature against information asymmetry. Parliamentary budget offices, independent fiscal institutions, audit support, and committee hearing powers can all help. The goal is not to make legislatures run the treasury. It is to prevent “executive monopoly over numbers” from becoming the real constitution of public finance.

### 4. Execute through one treasury, one chart of accounts, commitment control, and cash planning

Once the budget is approved, execution should not fragment into bank-account folklore and administrative improvisation. A well-designed treasury should consolidate cash visibility, manage payments through a controlled account structure, and connect budget classification, accounting, and reporting through a coherent chart of accounts. Fragmented government banking arrangements and disconnected coding structures make even honest governments hard to understand and weak governments easy to exploit.

Commitment control matters as much as cash control. Ministries should not be able to incur obligations casually and discover later that no appropriation or cash plan can support them. Good systems separate appropriation, commitment, verification, payment, and accounting clearly enough that arrears, cash rationing, and blame-shifting cannot masquerade as orderly management.

### 5. Use in-year reporting to surface correction needs early rather than hiding them until year-end

A budget is not governed only on budget day and audit day. Governments should publish in-year reports that compare approved appropriations, supplementary changes, commitments where feasible, cash releases, and actual expenditure and revenue in forms that can be understood by legislatures, watchdogs, journalists, and subnational partners. Where the budget is drifting, the drift should be visible early.

Correction should also be lawful. Supplementary budgets, virements, reserve use, and emergency reallocations may sometimes be necessary, but they should follow published rules, thresholds, and disclosure duties. The executive should not be able to rewrite the enacted budget silently through administrative churn.

### 6. Publish year-end financial statements and make accounts answerable through independent audit

The budget cycle should culminate in public accounts that reconcile what was authorized, what was spent, what assets and liabilities changed, and what obligations remain outstanding. Year-end reporting is where political promises meet accounting reality. If that bridge is weak, the budget becomes a speech act rather than a governing instrument.

Independent external audit and legislative follow-up are therefore part of budget design, not aftercare. Audit findings should travel into hearings, management responses, implementation tracking, and future budget decisions. A polity that produces audits no one must answer for is performing oversight rather than doing it.

### 7. Disclose fiscal risks from guarantees, PPPs, SOEs, subnationals, pensions, and disasters in one place

Many of the most consequential public-finance decisions happen outside the annual appropriation bill. Guarantees, state-owned enterprises, public-private partnerships, litigation, natural disasters, financial-sector exposures, subnational distress, pension promises, and contingent liabilities can all destabilize the public balance sheet while ordinary budget debates stay narrow and complacent.

An ideal system should therefore publish a regular fiscal-risk view that pulls these exposures into one intelligible surface. The point is not prophetic certainty. It is to stop governments from claiming surprise each time the off-budget world arrives to collect.

### 8. Treat performance, spending review, and citizen budgets as complements to legality, not substitutes for it

Modern budget practice should care about value, outcomes, equity, and public intelligibility. Performance information, spending reviews, citizen budgets, and open-data releases can all improve democratic control. But they should sit on top of, not instead of, legal authorization, reliable execution controls, and auditable accounts.

A government that publishes attractive dashboards while basic appropriations, commitments, liabilities, and audit follow-up remain unclear is not transparent in any meaningful constitutional sense. The archive should prefer performance-with-accounts over performance theater.

## Failure modes

- **fund fog**: money is dispersed across special accounts, earmarks, extra-budgetary funds, and state entities so no ordinary reader can see the whole.
- **supplementary government**: the enacted budget matters less than the pile of in-year rewrites, reserves, and reclassifications.
- **cash rationing by stealth**: political actors avoid revising the budget honestly and instead let delayed releases, arrears, or payment freezes do the real cutting.
- **commit first, explain later**: ministries incur obligations without secure appropriation, cash coverage, or real-time visibility.
- **off-budget populism**: guarantees, PPPs, SOEs, or directed lending carry promises the core budget refuses to price.
- **audit afterlife**: accounts and audit reports appear, but nothing in incentives, hearings, or future appropriations changes.
- **dashboard constitutionalism**: attractive performance or open-data surfaces hide weak accounting, weak legal authority, or weak control.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Can an outsider reconcile the approved budget, all in-year changes, execution reports, year-end accounts, and audit findings without guessing where major public money went?
2. Does the legislature have enough time, information, and analytic support to approve the fiscal frame and appropriations before the fiscal year starts?
3. Can any ministry or public body create material obligations without appropriation, commitment control, and treasury visibility?
4. Are fiscal risks from guarantees, PPPs, SOEs, litigation, pensions, and subnational exposures surfaced in one regular public view?
5. When audit findings recur, does something in law, management, appropriations, or executive authority actually change?
