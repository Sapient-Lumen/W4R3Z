# 696 — Shared-regime evaluation-and-learning constitutions: workplans, independence, management response, recommendation tracking, after-action review, and no regime by report shelf

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed evaluation-and-learning constitution** that separates workplan setting, commissioning, evaluator independence, evaluation from audit or monitoring, management response, recommendation tracking, public release, and structured learning loops such as after-action review — so institutional learning does not decay into report shelfware, recommendation theater, or “lessons learned” with no decision route.

## Why this matters

The archive now explains interpretation and jurisprudence memory (`679`–`680`), budget and audit-follow-through (`683`), administrative justice (`686`), field continuity (`690`), statistics-and-indicator discipline (`694`), and enterprise risk and internal control (`695`).

That is enough to explain **how a regime measures performance, watches risk, and operates controls**. It is still not enough to explain **how the organization decides what should be independently evaluated, how that work is planned and resourced, how evaluator independence is protected, how recommendations are answered, how follow-through is tracked, how public release and management response work, and how faster learning devices like after-action reviews differ from formal evaluation**.

That gap matters because shared regimes now produce many adjacent artifacts — monitoring dashboards, audits, inspections, reviews, inquiries, research papers, lessons-learned notes, simulation reports, and strategic reviews — that all sound evidence-based but do very different constitutional jobs.

Without a typed evaluation-and-learning constitution, eight familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **report shelf government**: evaluations are commissioned, published, and thanked, but their recommendations are not routed into management response, budgets, or change decisions.

The second is **method blur**: evaluation, audit, performance monitoring, inspection, and lessons learned are used as interchangeable labels, so nobody knows what independence, method, or evidence standard actually applied.

The third is **workplan opacity**: important topics never enter the evaluation programme because there is no visible selection logic, governing-body oversight, or resourcing rule.

The fourth is **independence capture**: the office being examined quietly shapes the terms of reference, team, publication timing, or final framing.

The fifth is **response theater**: management “welcomes” findings, but no recommendation owner, action, timeline, or status discipline follows.

The sixth is **recommendation graveyard**: actions are marked in progress forever because there is no aging logic, closure test, or re-escalation path.

The seventh is **learning amnesia**: lessons from emergencies, pilots, and reforms are collected but not converted into training, updated guidance, or changed controls.

The eighth is **public blackout**: evaluation products, management responses, and follow-up reports stay buried even when the regime claims to value transparency and learning.

Shared regimes need a tighter learning grammar.

## Pattern pack

### 1) Evaluation charter and coverage map

Publish a visible charter that states:

- what counts as evaluation in the regime;
- what does not count as evaluation;
- what units or activities fall within scope;
- which function is the custodian of the evaluation system;
- how evaluation serves accountability, learning, and decision support.

### 2) Biennial or annual workplan lane

Maintain a workplan that identifies:

- priority topics;
- commissioning authority;
- intended timing;
- indicative budget and resourcing;
- why each topic was selected;
- how the workplan links to strategic priorities, principal risks, and major organizational changes.

### 3) Independence and role separation

Separate clearly:

- governing-body oversight;
- evaluation commissioners;
- evaluation managers;
- evaluation teams;
- reviewed entities;
- any advisory or reference groups.

The subject of the evaluation should not control the judgment of the evaluation.

### 4) Method boundary map

Distinguish evaluation from adjacent instruments, including:

- monitoring and routine performance reporting;
- internal and external audit;
- compliance inspection;
- investigation;
- research;
- lessons-learned studies;
- after-action and intra-action reviews.

Each tool can be valuable, but they should not borrow one another’s legitimacy by label confusion.

### 5) Terms of reference and publication discipline

Require consequential evaluations to carry:

- scope and questions;
- intended users;
- method summary;
- independence safeguards;
- conflict checks where needed;
- publication expectation or lawful confidentiality explanation;
- location of the eventual report, brief, and annexes if used.

### 6) Management response and action plan lane

For each evaluation, require management to state for every material recommendation:

- accepted, partially accepted, or not accepted;
- accountable owner;
- planned action;
- target date;
- resource implication if material;
- explanation where a recommendation is rejected or modified.

### 7) Recommendation tracking and aging discipline

Maintain one follow-up register that distinguishes:

- not started;
- in progress;
- completed subject to verification;
- closed after verification;
- superseded, merged, or no longer applicable;
- overdue or stalled.

Perpetual “in progress” is a governance smell.

### 8) Learning conversion lane

Require significant evaluations and learning reviews to specify how findings travel into:

- policy or guidance updates;
- training and onboarding;
- revised controls or delegations;
- future workplans;
- budget or staffing proposals;
- change-management packets.

Learning should leave operational residue.

### 9) Fast-cycle learning lane

Maintain a separate lane for after-action reviews, intra-action reviews, retrospectives, or lessons-learned exercises that states:

- when the fast-cycle tool should be used instead of or before formal evaluation;
- who participates;
- whether the purpose is immediate correction, longer-term learning, or both;
- how outputs are recorded and routed;
- when escalation into formal evaluation is warranted.

### 10) Governing-body visibility and public release

Require the regime to show:

- evaluation annual reporting;
- governing-body or oversight-committee review;
- publication of reports, management responses, and follow-up outputs where disclosure rules allow;
- trends in implementation and recurring themes;
- resourcing and capacity limits that affect evaluation coverage.

## Working matrix

| Learning lane | Core constitutional question | Minimum visible packet |
|---|---|---|
| Evaluation scope | what counts as evaluation here? | charter, custodian, boundary map |
| Workplan | what will be evaluated, when, and why? | approved workplan, topic logic, budget, timing |
| Independence | who may assess and who may respond? | role map, ToR safeguards, publication rule |
| Management response | how are recommendations answered? | acceptance status, owner, action, timeline |
| Follow-up | how do recommendations live or die over time? | tracking register, aging logic, closure test |
| Learning conversion | how do findings change practice? | update route, training, control or policy changes |
| Fast-cycle learning | what is not a full evaluation but still must be routed? | AAR/IAR trigger, output form, escalation path |

## Guardrails

- Do not label every review, dashboard, or audit an “evaluation”.
- Do not let the evaluated office quietly own the terms, team, timing, and publication of its own assessment.
- Do not publish recommendations without a required management response.
- Do not let follow-up registers remain permanently “open” with no aging, verification, or re-escalation.
- Do not treat lessons learned as completed until they have changed guidance, training, controls, or plans.
- Do not claim transparency if reports and management responses are effectively undiscoverable.

## Failure modes

- **Report shelfware** — the report exists, but no action route follows.
- **Method blur** — monitoring, audit, review, and evaluation are conflated.
- **Captured independence** — the evaluated entity shapes the judgment lane.
- **Recommendation graveyard** — open actions accumulate without closure discipline.
- **Learning amnesia** — after-action insights never become changed operating practice.
- **Publication fog** — reports are nominally public but operationally buried.

## Practical tests

1. Can the regime show a current evaluation charter and approved workplan?
2. Does it distinguish evaluation from monitoring, audit, inspection, investigation, and after-action review?
3. Can a user identify who commissioned, managed, conducted, and responded to a given evaluation?
4. Are management responses required for material recommendations and publicly discoverable where disclosure rules allow?
5. Is there one recommendation-tracking register with overdue logic and closure verification?
6. Can the organization point to a recent finding that changed guidance, training, controls, or budget?
7. Does the workplan visibly consider strategic priorities, principal risks, or major reforms rather than topic popularity alone?
8. Are after-action or intra-action reviews routed into immediate corrective action and, where necessary, later formal evaluation?
9. Does the governing body or oversight committee see implementation trends and capacity gaps, not just finished reports?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can show **reports, dashboards, and occasional “lessons learned” language** but not **what counts as evaluation, who keeps that function independent, how workplans are chosen, how management must respond, how recommendations are tracked to closure, and how fast-cycle learning is converted into operational change**, then it still has **evidence rhetoric without an honest evaluation-and-learning constitution**.
