# 405 — Civic participation ops and decision legibility

## One-line thesis

Participation should be run as an **operational system with visible commitments**, not as a symbolic consultation layer detached from decisions.

## The problem

Governments often launch participation tools that collect input but fail to show:

- what decision is actually open,
- who decides,
- what timeline governs response,
- what happened to the input,
- what citizens can do if the process stalls or is ignored.

This produces participation theater rather than durable democratic capacity.

## Design rule

Every participation process should have a public **decision docket** with:

- the decision to be made,
- the authority responsible,
- the phase of the process,
- the target groups to reach,
- the rules for how input will shape outcomes,
- the response date,
- the public status page after close.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Decision docket

No participation process without a docket.

A docket should state:

- the exact decision scope,
- what is negotiable and what is fixed,
- what resources are attached,
- where final authority sits,
- when a public response will be published.

### 2. Commitment clock

Publish a countdown to response.

After participation closes, institutions should have to post:

- what they heard,
- what they changed,
- what they rejected,
- why,
- what remains undecided,
- when implementation updates will appear.

### 3. Multi-channel inclusion map

Digital participation is not enough by itself. Build a channel mix:

- online platform,
- SMS / low-bandwidth option,
- in-person sessions,
- accessible formats,
- community intermediaries,
- multilingual support.

### 4. Transparent moderation and AI use notice

If AI is used to cluster, summarize, rank, moderate, or translate public input, disclose:

- where AI intervenes,
- what outputs are machine-assisted,
- what human checks exist,
- how participants can challenge a misclassification or summary error.

### 5. Participation procurement checklist

When choosing a platform, test for:

- accessibility,
- privacy discipline,
- transparency / explainability,
- interoperability,
- sustainability / maintenance,
- open source or inspectable code where feasible,
- procurement terms that avoid lock-in.

### 6. Process-to-budget link

Where participation concerns spending or prioritization, the public should see:

- the available budget,
- the eligibility rules,
- cost estimates,
- implementation status,
- deviations from the voted or prioritized plan.

### 7. Status board and aftercare

The status board should survive the consultation window. Keep visible:

- decisions made,
- implementation stage,
- bottlenecks,
- reasons for delay,
- reopened issues,
- evaluation findings.

## Anti-theater tests

A process fails if:

- the institution cannot say what exact decision is open,
- participants never see a public response,
- the platform is accessible only to already-engaged elites,
- the process depends on a vendor that cannot export data or preserve public records,
- staff have no operational owner responsible for follow-through.

## Operational owner

Every recurring participation program needs an accountable operating team responsible for:

- recruitment and outreach,
- moderation and safety,
- synthesis quality,
- records retention,
- public response drafting,
- implementation tracking,
- lessons-learned review.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a participation design cannot answer **who decides, by when, under what rule, and what happens next**, it is not a governance mechanism yet.
