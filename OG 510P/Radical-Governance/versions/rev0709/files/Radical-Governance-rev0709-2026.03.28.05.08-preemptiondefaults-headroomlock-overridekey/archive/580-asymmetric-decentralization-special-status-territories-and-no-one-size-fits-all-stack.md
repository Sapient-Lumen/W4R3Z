# 580 — Asymmetric decentralization, special-status territories, and no one-size-fits-all stack

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat **asymmetry** as a disciplined constitutional tool rather than as a taboo or a patronage trick: keep a clear default stack for ordinary places, but allow differentiated powers, institutions, and finance for metropolitan areas, capital districts, islands, remote regions, indigenous or plurinational territories, and other genuinely exceptional geographies when functional scale, capacity, history, identity, remoteness, or constitutional settlement justify it; pair that flexibility with equal-rights floors, transparent criteria, published finance rules, review clocks, and routes into and out of special status; and reject both one-size-fits-all map worship and ad hoc dealmaking that turns territorial difference into permanent favoritism.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a strong **scope-allocation grammar**, a note on **adaptive boundaries**, a note on **ideal constitutional kits by scope**, and a note on **indigenous self-government**. What it still lacked was a compact doctrine for the next question: **when should governments at the same nominal level be allowed to differ from one another on purpose?**

That gap matters because many real polities do not fit a clean ladder. A giant capital city is not just another municipality. A remote archipelago cannot be governed exactly like a compact inland province. An indigenous territory may need self-government protections that ordinary municipal law cannot supply. A high-capacity metropolitan region may need powers that a small rural district should not carry. And a borderland or outlying island may need special mobility, customs, language, or fiscal rules that would make little sense elsewhere.

If constitutional design denies those differences entirely, it often creates brittle uniformity that mis-scopes power and service delivery. But if it embraces asymmetry carelessly, it invites opaque bargaining, elite carve-outs, and permanent resentment from places told that someone else’s special deal is now the constitution. The archive therefore needs a middle rule: **special status should be possible, but it must be principled, visible, and reviewable.**

## Pattern pack

### 1. Keep a general default stack, then justify deviations explicitly

The archive should resist the temptation to design every place as exceptional from the start. Most people still benefit from a legible default settlement: neighborhood voice, municipal general government, metropolitan or regional coordination where the system is real, national guarantees, and narrow-waist continental or global layers where cross-border problems require them.

Asymmetry should therefore begin as a rebuttable deviation from a published default, not as constitutional improvisation. The burden is on the polity to say **why this place needs different powers, money, or institutional form**, and why ordinary rules are inadequate.

### 2. Use asymmetry for real differences in function, capacity, geography, history, or constitutional settlement — not as a reward for political leverage

Special status is most defensible when it responds to a concrete and durable difference:

- a metropolitan economy whose transport, housing, land, and infrastructure systems obviously exceed ordinary municipal scale,
- a remote or insular territory with unusual delivery costs and connectivity constraints,
- an indigenous, plurinational, or treaty-based settlement that requires self-government beyond standard local law,
- a capital district with national institutions and security burdens unlike those of an ordinary city,
- or a region with demonstrated administrative capacity that can responsibly carry additional powers.

The archive should reject asymmetry justified mainly by bargaining power, ruling-party alliance, or historical accident that nobody can now explain.

### 3. Metropolitan asymmetry is often the most practical case: large city-regions may need powers that ordinary municipalities do not

OECD work now treats asymmetric decentralisation as increasingly common in urban areas and notes that capital districts and city-regions often receive differentiated powers. That matches the archive’s wider judgment that metropolitan systems are real even when constitutions pretend otherwise.

Where a city-region carries trunk transit, strategic land use, housing allocation, air-quality burdens, logistics corridors, or large shared utilities, the archive should prefer an honest special metropolitan settlement over either of two evasions: pretending the metro is merely one municipality among many, or treating every metro problem as a reason for national micromanagement.

The key is not to glorify the big city. It is to match the institutional kit to the actual urban system.

### 4. Remote, insular, and peripheral territories may need stronger autonomy in some domains and stronger solidarity in others

Not every asymmetry should mean “more powers.” Sometimes it means different delivery rules, adjusted funding formulas, tailored transport and communications obligations, or protected language and cultural institutions. Islands, mountain regions, frontier zones, and sparsely populated territories often face unusual scale penalties that a symmetrical formula hides badly.

The archive should therefore treat remoteness asymmetry as a combined governance-and-solidarity question. A remote territory may need greater discretion over local adaptation and stronger guaranteed support for connectivity, health access, education reach, and essential services. The answer is not uniformity. It is **differentiated design without differentiated citizenship**.

### 5. Indigenous and plurinational asymmetry is constitutional, not decorative

Some asymmetries are not just functional tweaks. They arise from prior peoples, treaty relationships, constitutional recognition, or the right of self-government in internal matters. The archive already has a dedicated note on this. Here the additional rule is simple: such asymmetry should not be treated as an administrative favor that can be casually withdrawn when central politics turns hostile.

The archive should therefore distinguish **constitutional asymmetry grounded in self-government or treaty settlement** from ordinary managerial asymmetry. The former deserves stronger entrenchment, more demanding consent rules for change, and clearer protection against unilateral clawback.

### 6. Match fiscal asymmetry to responsibility asymmetry, or the special status is mostly theater

Differentiated powers without differentiated finance produce the same old failure in a more ornate form. A capital district with national burdens, a remote territory with high service costs, or a metropolitan region with system-wide obligations cannot govern honestly if its revenue tools and transfer rules remain identical to those of places without the same responsibilities.

The archive should therefore insist that asymmetric powers come with equally visible fiscal design: adjusted tax authority where appropriate, published transfer formulas, equalization treatment that reflects real costs and capacities, and borrowing rules that fit the institutional role of the territory. No special status should depend mainly on discretionary annual pleading to the center.

### 7. Put every special-status regime on a legal basis with criteria, powers, finance, review, and exit or conversion rules

A good asymmetric settlement should be legible enough that an outsider can answer five questions quickly:

1. what is special,
2. why it is special,
3. what powers and duties differ,
4. how the money works,
5. and how the arrangement is reviewed, revised, entrenched, expanded, reduced, or exited.

Without that discipline, asymmetry degrades into constitutional folklore. The archive should prefer charters, statutes, or constitutional provisions that specify scope, finance, oversight, and review clocks over vague “special arrangements” that survive only through elite memory.

### 8. Protect equal-rights floors and common rule-of-law guarantees across all asymmetric settlements

A polity may vary territorial powers substantially without allowing basic rights, due process, political competition, or common citizenship to disintegrate. The archive should therefore distinguish **permitted territorial diversity** from **forbidden territorial inequality**.

Special status should not be used to excuse abusive policing, weaker electoral integrity, poorer access to remedy, inferior civil status documentation, or abandonment of minimum social floors. A remote island may need different transport law. An indigenous territory may need stronger self-government. A capital district may need special land-use and security rules. None of that justifies a lower rank of citizenship.

### 9. Use review clocks and monitored learning so asymmetry remains adaptive rather than mythic

OECD work increasingly treats asymmetric decentralisation as compatible with experimentation and learning-by-doing, especially for metropolitan governance. That is useful, but only if experimentation stays visible.

The archive should therefore prefer periodic review for asymmetric settlements, especially those created as pilots or capacity-based differentiations. Review should ask whether the asymmetry improved fit, accountability, service quality, legitimacy, and fiscal honesty — and whether it should now be deepened, normalized, copied, narrowed, or retired.

### 10. Reject both symmetry fetish and asymmetry patronage

The archive’s final rule is two-sided. **Symmetry fetish** forces unlike places into the same constitutional box because uniformity feels neat from the center. **Asymmetry patronage** hands out tailored deals mainly to buy peace, reward allies, or quiet a politically important territory without publishing a coherent rule.

Ideal territorial design refuses both mistakes. It uses asymmetry where needed, but only under a discipline strong enough that difference remains constitutional rather than feudal.

## Guardrails

- Do not assume every municipality, region, or territory needs the same powers just because they share a nominal tier.
- Do not hand out special status mainly as a reward for bargaining power or governing-party alliance.
- Do not give a special territory extra responsibilities without matching finance, staff, and oversight.
- Do not use asymmetry to weaken common rights floors, electoral fairness, remedy, or legal identity.
- Do not let pilot asymmetries run forever without review.
- Do not let treaty- or self-government-based asymmetry be casually clawed back as if it were ordinary administrative delegation.

## Failure modes

- **symmetry fetish**: unlike territories are forced into one template that fits none of them well.
- **special-deal constitutionalism**: asymmetry emerges through opaque bargaining rather than principled criteria.
- **metro denial by uniformity**: giant city-regions are treated as if they were ordinary municipalities.
- **remote-place abandonment**: peripheral areas receive the same formal rules but none of the solidarity needed to make those rules real.
- **identity asymmetry without entrenchment**: treaty or self-government arrangements depend on ordinary political goodwill rather than durable protection.
- **pilot forever**: temporary asymmetry becomes permanent without ever being judged honestly.

## Practical tests

An asymmetric territorial settlement passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is there a clear default constitutional stack from which the asymmetry is explicitly distinguished?
2. Can the polity explain the special status in terms of real function, geography, capacity, history, identity, or constitutional settlement rather than raw leverage?
3. Are differentiated powers matched by differentiated finance, staffing, and oversight?
4. Do common rights floors, remedy routes, and equal citizenship still hold?
5. Is there a visible route for review, entrenchment, revision, normalization, or exit?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can describe **its special territories, capital districts, autonomous regions, or city-region exceptions** but cannot show **why they differ, what powers and money differ, what common floors remain non-negotiable, and how the arrangement is reviewed or changed**, then it does not yet have **principled asymmetry**; it has **territorial exception politics with better stationery**.
