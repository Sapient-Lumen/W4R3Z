# 729 — Ratification packets for ideal-government scope shifts: proof weights, consent ladders, double legitimacy, and no function move by wrong organ

## One-line thesis

Once the archive has a default routing table (`726`), an override grammar (`727`), and a minimum sideways-authority packet (`728`), it should also keep one compact **ratification packet** for materially moving a function across scopes, especially where protected local or regional self-government, delegated tasks, boundary effects, or treaty-bound compact obligations are in play: classify the move by constitutional depth, scale the proof burden accordingly, require assent from the organs and publics whose protected bargain is being touched, and refuse any rescoping by executive convenience, grant coercion, software default, or after-the-fact folklore.

## Why this matters

The archive can now answer three increasingly practical questions about ideal governments by scope.

It can say **what the stack is** (`725`). It can say **where functions should usually live** (`726`). It can say **when to override that default downward, upward, or sideways** (`727`). And it can now say **what minimum constitutional kit a bounded in-between authority needs** once a function is honestly routed to a basin, corridor, grid, port, referral platform, or similar body (`728`).

What the archive still lacked was the next operational object: **when a sponsor says a function should move, pool, split, or be reclassified across scopes, who actually has to agree, and how strong does the case have to be?**

That gap matters because multilevel systems often fail not at the level of theory, but at the level of ratification. The routing logic may be sound, yet the move is pushed through by the wrong organ. A ministry uses grant conditions to do what law did not openly authorise. A municipal club creates a de facto metropolitan government without a metropolitan public lane. A nation pushes equal-rights duties downward without carrying the fiscal guarantee and review spine that made those duties honest at national scale. A regional platform hardens into real territorial government without territorial assent. A “temporary” cross-border compact quietly becomes ordinary government because nobody asked again who had to consent to permanence.

The archive therefore needs one short answer that later notes can cite instead of re-litigating the whole constitutional question every time: **a ratification packet for scope shifts**. The packet should not decide whether the move is wise. It should say what must be proven, whose assent is needed, what must remain challengeable, and when the proposal must climb from ordinary legislation into a stronger consent lane.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Classify the move before debating the merits

A polity should not start with slogans like decentralise, federalise, regionalise, or pool. It should first classify **what kind of scope move is being proposed**.

At minimum, the archive should distinguish:

- **implementation tuning inside a fixed competence**;
- **delegated or pooled delivery that preserves legal title and principal public lane**;
- **ordinary scope reassignment among existing tiers inside the same protected constitutional bargain**;
- **constitutional-bargain change** that alters protected local or regional self-government, the guarantor tier, the representative chain, the tax base, or the review route;
- and **territorial-status or cross-border compact change** that affects boundaries, electorate, legal personality, treaty obligations, or membership.

If the move is misclassified at the start, the ratification lane will usually be wrong as well.

### 2. Scale the proof burden with the constitutional depth of the move

The archive should keep a simple burden ladder.

Low-burden moves may justify themselves by ordinary administrative evidence: duplication, timing, workflow integrity, or service continuity. Medium-burden moves should also prove why the new lead scope is more territorially honest, fiscally viable, and reviewable than the old one. High-burden moves should additionally prove why the protected bargain really must change, why lighter repairs are insufficient, and why the new arrangement will not quietly widen again by drift.

The point is not mathematical precision. The point is to block a recurring failure mode: **major scope change defended with minor-administration evidence**.

### 3. Keep one minimum proof kernel for every material scope shift

However the burden ladder varies by system, every material move should still carry one visible proof kernel.

That kernel should say:

1. what function or lane is moving;
2. what scope currently leads it;
3. what scope will lead it after the move;
4. why the current placement is misfitting or dishonest;
5. why lower-depth repairs are insufficient;
6. what fiscal lane, review lane, and participation lane will exist after the move;
7. whether the move is temporary, pilot-bound, emergency-bound, or permanent;
8. and what handback, rereview, or upgrade trigger applies.

If the sponsor cannot state those elements plainly, the polity is not yet looking at an honest scope-shift proposal.

### 4. Require assent from the present public lane, not only from the sponsor

A sponsor should not be able to move a function merely by claiming system efficiency. The current constitutional home of the function should have a visible place in the ratification chain.

That does not always mean veto. But it usually means the present public lane must receive:

- formal notice,
- the full proof packet,
- a response window,
- and a recorded opportunity to object, condition, negotiate, or seek review.

This matters because many improper scope shifts rely on one political trick: the organ proposing the move behaves as though proposal and ratification are the same act.

### 5. When the move creates a new shared territorial public, require double legitimacy rather than incumbent club consent alone

Some scope shifts do more than reassign a function. They create a new shared public sphere: a metropolitan transport-and-land bargain, a regional hospital and tertiary-skills platform, a basin authority with distributive consequences, or a cross-border compact with repeated rulemaking effects.

Where that happens, the archive should prefer **double legitimacy**. A move should not be ratified only by one aggregated population majority or only by incumbent governments bargaining among themselves. The ratification lane should visibly include both:

- the **people or electorate affected as a whole**, and
- the **constituent governments or territories whose protected position is being altered**.

Different systems will operationalise that differently: territorial chambers, double majorities, constituent-unit assent, municipal consent rules, or equivalent public mechanisms. But the archive should be clear about the principle: **new shared government should not be born solely from executive club agreement**.

### 6. Moves downward from guarantor tiers need a floor-preservation certificate, not only a subsidiarity argument

Downward moves are often attractive in theory. They promise intimacy, tailoring, and faster correction. But when the function being moved carries equal citizenship, rights floors, large risk pooling, or anti-exclusion guarantees, a simple subsidiarity argument is not enough.

The archive should therefore require a distinct **floor-preservation certificate** whenever a national or equivalent guarantor function moves downward in any material way. The sponsor should have to show:

- which rights or equality floors remain non-negotiable;
- how funding and equalization still protect them;
- how review and appeal remain reachable;
- and what backstop allows the guarantor tier to intervene if the floor breaks.

Otherwise decentralisation becomes a polite word for dumping obligations onto unequal territories.

### 7. Moves upward need anti-convenience proof and a handback or settlement rule

Higher-tier ratification should not be granted merely because a stronger tier already has staff, software, money, or political momentum. A move upward should have to prove that the gain is not just administrative convenience for the center, but a genuine response to spillovers, solidarity, interoperability, security, or system footprint.

And where the move is not explicitly permanent, the ratification packet should also state:

- the review clock,
- the settlement or handback criteria,
- the burden for renewal,
- and the route back down if the exceptional reason fades.

No polity should let upward ratification drift into permanent title by elapsed time.

### 8. Sideways moves into bounded functional authorities need footprint proof plus exclusion proof

The new sideways-authority packet (`728`) explains how to build an in-between body once the route is chosen. The missing ratification discipline is simpler: **why this body, why this footprint, and why no broader one?**

A sideways move should therefore prove not only that a basin, corridor, grid, airport zone, trauma network, port region, or referral platform is real, but also:

- which powers stay outside the new body;
- which principal democratic or ministerial lane remains above it;
- what would trigger democratic or territorial upgrade;
- and what would trigger narrowing, dissolution, or handback.

Without that exclusion proof, sideways ratification becomes a common laundering device for building real government without owning the constitutional name.

### 9. Boundary, electorate, and status effects should trigger community assent, not only sector assent

Some scope shifts are presented as technical service reforms even though they alter who belongs to which public, which taxes can be levied, which body may make binding rules, or who votes for whom. Where those effects are real, the ratification lane should widen beyond sector stakeholders.

The archive should therefore say that any move materially affecting:

- boundaries,
- municipal or regional status,
- electoral chains,
- standing tax bases,
- or the general public-law identity of a territory

should trigger a community or territorial assent lane proportionate to the depth of the change. Consultation alone may suffice for some cases. Others will require stronger constituent or referendum logic. What should not happen is pretending that a public-status change was only a service-management memo.

### 10. Emergency scope shifts need a short ratification clock or they are not honest

Emergency command can justify immediate temporary concentration. It cannot justify indefinite constitutional ambiguity.

The archive should therefore require every emergency scope move to carry a short ratification clock. Within that clock, the polity must either:

- ratify the temporary move under explicit terms,
- narrow it,
- replace it with a lawful redesign packet,
- or hand it back.

If none of that happens, the move should be treated as constitutionally suspect rather than as normalised by passage of time.

### 11. Ratification should name the losing claims as well as the winning one

A good ratification record should not preserve only the victorious story. It should also say:

- which rival scopes were considered;
- what objections were raised by affected tiers or publics;
- what conditions were attached;
- and what unresolved risks remain.

This is not merely archival hygiene. It prevents later actors from pretending the shift was obvious, uncontested, or more constitutionally complete than it really was.

### 12. No ratification without a continuity pack

A move that wins on theory but breaks finance, records, notice, cases, staff, assets, appeals, or procurement is not yet a ratified constitutional success. The ratification packet should therefore dock to a continuity pack showing how the operating constitution survives the move.

If continuity cannot yet be shown, the honest answer is not ratify now and repair later. The honest answer is that the proposal is still incomplete.

## Ratification ladder

| Move class | Typical proof weight | Minimum ratification expectation | What the archive should block |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| implementation tuning inside fixed competence | low | ordinary legal lane plus ordinary accountability | calling workflow cleanup a scope shift |
| delegated or pooled delivery with title preserved | medium | public law plus assent from principal lane and visible reserved powers | outsourcing or shared service masquerading as constitutional transfer |
| ordinary reassignment among existing tiers | medium to high | legislation, affected-tier consultation, named fiscal and review continuity | scope moves by grant, memo, or platform default |
| protected-bargain shift affecting self-government, guarantor duties, or representative chain | high | higher-law route plus territorial or constituent assent as required | silent recentralization or obligation dumping |
| boundary, status, compact, or membership change | very high | explicit public ratification with community or constituent consent logic | new public order by sectoral workaround |

## Guardrails

- Do not let the sponsor classify its own move without challenge.
- Do not let ordinary administrative evidence carry constitutional-bargain change by itself.
- Do not treat consultation after the deal is done as real assent.
- Do not let grant conditions, host-city contracts, intergovernmental memoranda, or software defaults substitute for the correct ratification lane.
- Do not move guarantor duties downward without an explicit floor-preservation certificate.
- Do not ratify a sideways authority without naming both its exclusions and its upgrade-or-exit triggers.
- Do not let emergency scope shifts survive beyond the short ratification clock without a fresh lawful act.
- Do not ratify a move whose operating continuity pack is still missing.

## Failure modes

- **scope shift by wrong organ**: the body proposing the move behaves as though proposal, assent, and implementation are the same act.
- **consent theater**: affected tiers or communities are consulted after the constitutional decision is already closed.
- **minor-evidence constitutionalism**: a major reallocation is defended only with efficiency, management, or software-integrity arguments.
- **downward dumping**: national or guarantor obligations are pushed down without equalization, rights floors, or review continuity.
- **sideways laundering**: a bounded authority is ratified without exclusions and slowly becomes general territorial government by residue.
- **emergency permanence**: a temporary upward move becomes the live constitution because nobody returned to ratification.
- **continuity blindness**: the move is declared lawful in the abstract but breaks records, appeals, budgets, staff, or assets in practice.

## Practical tests

A scope-ratification regime is honest when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Has the move been classified by constitutional depth before the merits are debated?
2. Is the proof burden proportionate to that depth rather than fixed at one generic standard?
3. Does the packet name the present lead scope, proposed lead scope, misfit diagnosis, continuity plan, and review or handback logic?
4. Does the current public lane have a real place in the assent chain rather than learning of the move after ratification?
5. Where a new shared territorial public is being created, does the ratification lane reflect double legitimacy rather than only incumbent bargaining?
6. If the move goes downward from a guarantor tier, is there an explicit floor-preservation certificate?
7. If the move goes upward or is temporary, are settlement, renewal, and handback rules visible?
8. If the move goes sideways, does the packet prove both footprint and exclusions?
9. Where boundaries, electorate, tax base, or legal status are affected, is there a community or constituent assent lane proportionate to the change?
10. Is ratification blocked until the operating continuity pack is credible enough to preserve money, staff, records, cases, assets, and remedies?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can explain **why a function ought to move** but cannot also say **how deep the move is, what proof burden applies, who must consent, what guarantees survive, and what continuity pack keeps the move real rather than theatrical**, then it does not yet have an honest scope shift. It has only **a routing preference in search of lawful ratification**.
