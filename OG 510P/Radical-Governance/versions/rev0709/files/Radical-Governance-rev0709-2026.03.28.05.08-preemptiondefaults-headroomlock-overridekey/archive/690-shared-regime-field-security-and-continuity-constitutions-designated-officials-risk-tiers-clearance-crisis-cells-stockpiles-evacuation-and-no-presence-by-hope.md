# 690 — Shared-regime field security-and-continuity constitutions: designated officials, risk tiers, clearance, crisis cells, stockpiles, evacuation, and no presence by hope

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed field security-and-continuity constitution** that separates risk assessment, travel and deployment clearance, duty-station roles, premises security, crisis management cells, incident logging, logistics and stockpiles, evacuation or hibernation choices, and continuity of records and core services — so territorial presence is sustained by visible duty-of-care and continuity architecture rather than by courage theater, local improvisation, or hope.

## Why this matters

Once a regime can explain what kinds of territorial presence it has (`689`), it still has to answer a harder operational question: **how do those sites remain safe, reachable, and governable when conditions deteriorate, violence rises, disaster hits, communications fail, or mobility is suddenly constrained?**

That question cannot be left to generic bravery or to private heroics by field staff.

Shared regimes often operate in unequal environments. Some sites are in stable capitals with predictable utilities and formal host-state support. Others sit in fragile settings, conflict zones, epidemic contexts, or places where roads, labs, internet, customs, and medical evacuation capacity are all uncertain. The archive already knows that serious governance requires named review, named authority, and named records. Field presence needs the same discipline.

Without a typed field security-and-continuity constitution, six familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **presence by hope**: staff are deployed because the mission matters, but the regime cannot show what risk assessment, clearance, or fallback plan authorized the exposure.

The second is **heroics as policy**: local personnel carry the real security and continuity burden while headquarters congratulates resilience instead of funding it.

The third is **premises-only security theater**: guards, gates, and badges exist, but travel, communications, evacuation, medical support, and records continuity are not typed.

The fourth is **crisis-command fog**: when an incident occurs, no one can tell who convenes the crisis cell, who has authority to shelter, relocate, suspend travel, or close the office, and who keeps the record.

The fifth is **logistics fragility**: the organization talks about preparedness but has no real stock, surge, or last-mile supply chain plan.

The sixth is **continuity amnesia**: when a site closes or scales down suddenly, records, approvals, finance, and beneficiary-facing service lanes are treated as afterthoughts.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **shared-regime field security-and-continuity discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate duty of care from mission zeal

A serious regime should say explicitly that mission importance does not cancel duty of care. The question is not whether the cause is urgent. The question is whether the regime can show a visible basis for exposing people, assets, records, and services to a given operating environment.

This is the archive's first anti-heroics rule: importance of mission is not a substitute for security governance.

### 2. Type the field roles at duty-station level

Each duty station or field footprint should have a visible map of roles for:

- the accountable senior official for security or continuity decisions,
- the office head,
- security or safety focal points,
- crisis-management or emergency decision cell,
- operations and logistics leads,
- records and IT continuity leads,
- and regional or headquarters backstop roles.

A site without a role map is already governing by confusion.

### 3. Distinguish risk assessment from travel or deployment clearance

The regime should separate:

- background risk assessment,
- standing operating conditions,
- individual travel or deployment authorization,
- and emergency movement decisions once conditions change.

That prevents one old area rating from masquerading as a live deployment decision.

### 4. Publish a field decision ladder for posture changes

A serious regime should maintain a visible ladder covering at minimum:

- normal operations,
- heightened precautions,
- restricted travel,
- remote or reduced operations,
- hibernation or shelter-in-place,
- temporary relocation,
- evacuation,
- and controlled re-entry.

Each posture should name who can trigger it, what evidence threshold applies, and what minimum service or records duties survive.

### 5. Separate premises security from mobility, communications, and medical support

A guarded gate is not a full security constitution.

The regime should separately type:

- building and perimeter controls,
- visitor and badge lanes,
- travel clearance and movement tracking,
- communications redundancy,
- medical referral and evacuation support,
- and family or dependant treatment where relevant.

This is how the archive prevents physical-premises controls from impersonating full duty of care.

### 6. Require one crisis cell with named logs and authority boundaries

When incidents escalate, the regime should convene one named crisis cell or equivalent command structure. That body should keep an incident log, track decisions and authorizations, record who was contacted, and maintain a visible boundary between operational command, public communications, staff welfare, and substantive program decisions.

No crisis cell should become a free-floating shadow cabinet.

### 7. Pair field security with logistics reality

Preparedness claims should point to actual operational support: supply routes, stockpiles where justified, emergency procurement lanes, communications kits, alternative worksites, vehicle rules, and regional or global surge support.

If the regime cannot sustain life-safety and minimum operations when regular supply chains fail, its field presence is still aspirational.

### 8. Give locally recruited personnel equal constitutional visibility

A serious regime should not treat internationally mobile staff as the only people who count in the security constitution. Locally recruited personnel often bear the longest-term risk, the weakest mobility options, and the hardest continuity burden.

Their security support, communications, movement restrictions, welfare measures, and post-incident support should be visible in the same constitutional packet, not hidden in local custom.

### 9. Type records, finance, and service continuity before the crisis hits

Every duty station should know, in advance:

- what records must remain reachable,
- which systems need offline or remote fallback,
- who can approve urgent payments or payroll,
- how beneficiary-facing or state-facing services degrade,
- and what handoff occurs if the office goes dark.

That is how the archive prevents continuity from being rediscovered during the outage.

### 10. Exercise, review, and debrief the whole field constitution

Security and continuity rules should be practiced through simulations, training, incident reviews, and explicit learning loops. After action, the regime should update postures, stock levels, focal-point lists, and continuity packets rather than treating the event as a one-off story.

## Working matrix

| Object | Honest rule | Why | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| duty-station roles | name accountable officials, crisis cell, focal points, and backstops | makes emergency authority visible | everyone assuming someone else is in charge |
| risk and clearance | separate standing assessment from live travel or deployment approval | keeps exposure decisions current | one stale rating governing all travel |
| posture ladder | type restriction, hibernation, relocation, evacuation, and re-entry | prevents ad hoc crisis improvisation | binary open-or-shut thinking |
| premises and movement | separate building controls from travel, communications, and medical support | captures real duty of care | guard-post theater |
| logistics and stock | maintain supply, surge, and last-mile support lanes | turns preparedness into capability | preparedness claims with no supplies |
| continuity | pretype records, payroll, approvals, and degraded services | keeps governance alive during disruption | rediscovering continuity mid-crisis |

## Guardrails

- Do not let mission urgency replace risk assessment or duty-of-care reasoning.
- Do not confuse a guarded building with a full field security constitution.
- Do not treat one old area rating as a permanent deployment authorization.
- Do not let crisis cells become undocumented power centers.
- Do not exclude locally recruited personnel from the visible support architecture.
- Do not discuss preparedness without naming logistics, communications, and stock or surge support.
- Do not postpone records, payroll, or service continuity planning until the office is already failing.

## Failure modes

- **presence by hope**: deployment proceeds without a visible risk and continuity basis.
- **heroics as policy**: local staff are expected to absorb structural risk privately.
- **premises-only security theater**: gates and guards stand in for a real field constitution.
- **crisis-command fog**: nobody can name who decides what when conditions deteriorate.
- **logistics fragility**: stock, transport, and surge claims collapse under real stress.
- **continuity amnesia**: the office closes but records, payments, and essential operations have no typed fallback.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does each duty station or field footprint have a named security-and-continuity role map?
2. Are risk assessment, deployment clearance, and emergency movement decisions separated?
3. Does the regime maintain a posture ladder from normal operations through evacuation and re-entry?
4. Are premises controls, travel rules, communications fallback, and medical support typed separately?
5. Is there one named crisis cell or equivalent with an incident log and decision record?
6. Can the regime point to real logistics, stockpile, or surge arrangements rather than generic preparedness language?
7. Are locally recruited personnel visibly covered by the support and continuity architecture?
8. Are records, payroll, approvals, and beneficiary-facing services covered by predesigned fallback rules?
9. Are exercises, simulations, or after-action reviews used to update the packet?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can show **where it works** but not **how it assesses field risk, authorizes movement, runs crisis command, sustains logistics, protects local personnel, and keeps records and essential services alive when a site degrades**, then it still has **territorial presence without an honest field security-and-continuity constitution**.
