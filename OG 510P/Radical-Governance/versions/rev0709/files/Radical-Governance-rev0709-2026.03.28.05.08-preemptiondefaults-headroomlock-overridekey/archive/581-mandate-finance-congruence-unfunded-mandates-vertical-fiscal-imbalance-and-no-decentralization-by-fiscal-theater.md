# 581 — Mandate-finance congruence, unfunded mandates, vertical fiscal imbalance, and no decentralization by fiscal theater

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat **mandate-finance congruence** as constitutional law rather than budget etiquette: no level of government should be assigned a durable public function unless the same settlement also names the revenue basket, transfer formula, equalization rule, borrowing limits, and capability backstop that make the function actually dischargeable; delegated tasks must come with commensurate resources, local self-government must include at least some real own-source fiscal room, and upward rescue must be visible and reviewable rather than leaving decentralization to degrade into blame-shifting.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a strong grammar for **which powers belong at which level** (`579`), a compact doctrine for **what an ideal constitutional kit looks like by scope** (`565`), and notes on **revenue, equalization, budgeting, and debt** (`517`, `489`, `547`, `567`). What it still lacked was one compact note for the most common practical failure in multi-level governance: **a function is assigned, but the money architecture is not**.

That failure matters because constitutions and statutes often pretend that assigning a duty is the same thing as governing it. A nation announces local climate action, but gives cities neither authority nor money. A region delegates service delivery downward but keeps the tax handle and the procurement specialists. A metropolitan body is told to coordinate transport and housing while surviving on annual bargaining and voluntary contributions. A small municipality is made formally responsible for legal duties that only a larger pool, shared service, or stronger transfer system could honestly sustain.

The result is a familiar pathology: **fiscal theater**. Higher levels praise subsidiarity while keeping fiscal power, lower levels are blamed for failures they were never equipped to prevent, and citizens cannot tell whether a broken service reflects bad local government, bad intergovernmental finance, or deliberate constitutional evasion.

A better rule is simple: **no mandate without its finance stack**. If a function truly belongs at a given level, the same settlement must specify how that level is funded to perform it, how shortfalls are corrected, how asymmetries are handled, and what visible backstop applies when capacity collapses.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat function assignment and finance assignment as one constitutional act

The archive should reject the habit of writing beautiful competence lists while leaving the revenue and transfer question for later budget politics. A real assignment should always answer, at minimum:

- what part of the function is mandatory versus discretionary,
- which revenue sources or transfer channels support it,
- whether those funds are general-purpose, formula-based, reimbursable, or conditional,
- what equalization or solidarity rule protects poorer places,
- what borrowing is allowed for capital-heavy versions of the function,
- and what happens when the designated level cannot actually perform.

If those answers are absent, the assignment is usually not real.

### 2. Use a revenue-basket rule rather than one magical funding source

A serious subnational or shared government rarely survives well on a single revenue source. The archive should prefer a **basket** that can include:

- visible own-source revenues tied to place or service,
- shared taxes where the base is too large or too mobile for one local jurisdiction,
- formula grants for delegated or equalized functions,
- user charges where they are lawful and non-exclusionary,
- and prudently governed borrowing for long-lived capital assets.

The key is not just diversification for its own sake. The key is matching the funding mix to the function’s logic. Everyday local discretion needs some own-source room. Equal citizenship floors need predictable transfers and equalization. Large infrastructure needs capital finance, not annual pleading. High-volatility or highly mobile bases should not be the main support of ordinary frontline obligations.

### 3. Make delegated functions reimbursement-visible and reviewable

Some tasks are not core self-government functions; they are duties delegated downward by a higher law-owning level. The archive should therefore distinguish **self-governing functions** from **delegated execution** and insist that delegated work come with one of three explicit finance paths:

1. full reimbursement,
2. formula transfer anchored to workload and need,
3. or a clearly stated local co-finance share that is small enough to be honest and contestable.

Anything else invites a shell game in which the center claims policy credit while municipalities or regions absorb the fiscal pain.

### 4. Treat vertical fiscal imbalance as a warning light, not a normal resting state

Some degree of vertical fiscal imbalance is ordinary in multi-level systems because nations usually own stronger redistributive and macro-stabilizing taxes. But large or persistent dependence becomes dangerous when lower levels have major obligations and little meaningful control over the resources needed to discharge them.

The archive should therefore treat **vertical fiscal imbalance** as acceptable only when paired with:

- predictable transfers,
- formula transparency,
- real subnational planning horizons,
- equalization that protects poor places,
- and enough own-source discretion that local elections still matter.

When the balance tips too far toward dependency, decentralization ceases to be self-government and becomes administrative subcontracting with political blame attached.

### 5. Keep at least some visible own-source fiscal room at the level where local democracy is supposed to matter

A municipality or region that cannot raise or vary any meaningful revenue is not fully self-governing, even if it holds elections. The archive should therefore continue to prefer at least some own-source fiscal room for levels that claim democratic local or regional mandate. That does not mean every level needs broad tax sovereignty. It means that political responsibility should not be entirely severed from fiscal choice.

The right own-source room depends on the place and the constitution. Often it will mean property-related taxation, fees, or surcharges on relatively stable bases. In some systems it may include shared taxes with rate discretion inside a bounded range. The design point is modest but important: local voters should be able to see that at least part of local service quality depends on local fiscal decisions, not only on central favor.

### 6. Equalization should protect citizenship, not erase local effort

Equalization exists because inherited tax bases vary far more than equal citizenship should. The archive should therefore continue to treat equalization as a constitutional floor, not as charity. But equalization should also not be designed as if all local fiscal effort were irrelevant.

A better rule is to equalize for structural capacity gaps and expenditure need while still preserving some reward for competent administration, lawful collection, and visible local effort. That balance prevents two bad stories at once: the rich-place story that every transfer is confiscation, and the poor-place story that the constitution promises self-government while funding it only where land values already happen to be high.

### 7. Use function-by-function finance matching, not one undifferentiated transfer fog

The archive should resist systems where most subnational money arrives through opaque negotiation or through a pile of badly differentiated grants. A better design sorts functions into a few recognisable finance lanes:

- **local place services**: own-source revenue plus equalization,
- **delegated national or regional administration**: reimbursement or workload formulas,
- **shared metropolitan and basin systems**: base-sharing or joint formulas,
- **capital-intensive infrastructure**: capital grants plus prudential borrowing,
- **emergency or shock response**: extraordinary stabilization lanes with handback rules.

This does not require hundreds of special funds. It requires fewer and clearer lanes.

### 8. Pair finance rules with capability backstops

Money alone does not make a function governable. Some duties require technical staff, legal support, procurement competence, or interoperable systems that small jurisdictions cannot sustain alone. The archive should therefore pair mandate-finance congruence with **capability congruence**.

A sound constitution names what happens if the designated level lacks capacity:

- shared service centers,
- regional technical corps,
- temporary trusteeship or co-production,
- automatic escalation to a larger pool for specified functions,
- and explicit restoration criteria if the function is later returned.

This matters because unfunded mandates and unstaffed mandates often travel together.

### 9. Make asymmetry in finance as explicit as asymmetry in powers

The archive now has a note on principled territorial asymmetry (`580`). The same honesty is needed in fiscal design. If metros, islands, capital districts, remote territories, or indigenous jurisdictions have differentiated powers or constraints, their finance model should also be differentiated on paper rather than improvised each budget season.

That can mean altered transfer weights, different revenue handles, special infrastructure banks, remoteness adjustments, treaty-based fiscal compacts, or explicit equalization modifiers. The key is that difference must be rule-bound and publishable, not handled as a permanent side deal.

### 10. Use visible rescue and handback rules rather than silent fiscal swallowing

Sometimes higher levels do need to rescue a failing lower-level function or budget. The archive should not romanticize collapse. But rescue should never be allowed to masquerade as proof that the lower level never needed real powers or money in the first place.

A serious rescue design names:

- the trigger for intervention,
- the scope of temporary control,
- the financing bridge,
- the performance or governance conditions for restoration,
- and the date or review clock for handback.

Otherwise emergency support quietly becomes permanent recentralization.

## Scope table

| Finance problem | Preferred constitutional answer | Typical instruments | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Locally discretionary everyday services** | local own-source room plus equalization | property-related taxes, local fees, bounded surcharges, formula equalization | grant dependency sold as autonomy |
| **Delegated execution of higher-level policy** | mandated reimbursement or workload-based formula transfer | per-case formulas, service standards funding, ring-fenced reimbursement | downloading duties without cash |
| **Cross-border metropolitan or basin systems** | shared-base or joint formula finance | tax-base sharing, regional transport levies, corridor formulas, basin funds | voluntary-only contributions for binding systems |
| **Large long-lived infrastructure** | capital grants plus prudential borrowing and asset-matched repayment | golden-rule borrowing, development-bank finance, pooled bond access | funding capital entirely from unstable annual operating grants |
| **Equal citizenship across unequal territories** | explicit equalization and risk-pooling | equalization formulas, solidarity funds, insurance pools | postcode public services tied to inherited wealth |
| **Temporary incapacity or crisis** | visible backstop and restoration path | emergency transfers, temporary administration, monitored handback | permanent recentralization by stealth |

## Guardrails

- Do not assign a function to a level unless the same act or settlement names its finance path.
- Do not call a level self-governing if it has elections but no meaningful fiscal room.
- Do not delegate national priorities downward through mandates that arrive without commensurate money, staff, or tools.
- Do not rely on annual bargaining to finance recurring obligations that citizens treat as rights-bearing services.
- Do not let equalization substitute for all own-source responsibility, or own-source rhetoric substitute for equal citizenship.
- Do not permit metropolitan systems to depend entirely on voluntary municipal payments when the function is functionally indivisible.
- Do not use rescue finance to erase the distinction between temporary backstop and permanent competence transfer.
- Do not hide differentiated territorial finance in one-off deals, side letters, or capital-project favoritism.

## Failure modes

- **decentralization by blame-shifting**: higher levels assign duties downward and retain the money upward.
- **vertical fiscal dependency trap**: local elections matter less because almost all serious finance arrives by grant discretion.
- **unfunded rights floors**: equal-rights promises exist on paper but are not resourced where they must be delivered.
- **metro by begging bowl**: city-region systems exist in reality but survive on unstable negotiations among municipalities.
- **capital starvation**: long-lived infrastructure is financed from short-term operating scraps, then allowed to decay.
- **silent recentralization through rescue**: emergency takeover becomes permanent because no restoration rule was written.
- **asymmetric favoritism**: differentiated territories receive opaque fiscal deals without visible criteria or review.

## Practical tests

A mandate-finance settlement is constitutionally serious when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. For every major assigned function, is there a visible revenue or transfer path attached to the assignment itself?
2. Can the polity distinguish self-governing functions from delegated tasks, and show how delegated work is reimbursed or formula-funded?
3. Do lower levels with democratic mandates have at least some meaningful own-source fiscal room?
4. Are equalization and solidarity rules strong enough that citizenship does not depend on local inherited tax base?
5. Where vertical fiscal imbalance is high, are transfer formulas, planning horizons, and accountability rules strong enough that dependency does not become theater?
6. For capital-heavy duties, are borrowing access and prudential limits visible and matched to asset life rather than improvised?
7. When capacity fails, is there a named backstop and a route back out of temporary intervention?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **which level is responsible** but cannot also say **who pays, by what formula or tax handle, with what equalization floor, under what borrowing rules, and through which visible backstop when the assigned level cannot cope**, then it still has **jurisdictional assignment without mandate-finance congruence**.
