# 607 — Democratic homes for shared territorial power: indirect councils, double legitimacy, direct election, and no metropolitan rule by proxy

## One-line thesis

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, the honest constitutional question is not whether it needs democracy but **which democratic home fits the power**: keep indirect representation for narrow and bounded co-ordination, use double-legitimacy designs where both people and component governments must visibly count, and prefer directly elected territorial government once the body can bind residents through compulsory finance, major debt, cross-sector territorial bargaining, or durable system-shaping authority.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a stronger line on scope, constitutional kits, diagnosis, repair ladders, and the threshold at which co-operation becomes real government. What it still lacked was a compact doctrine for the **form of democratic anchoring** that should follow that threshold judgment.

That gap matters because many live systems fail in one of two opposite ways. Some leave major metropolitan or regional power inside boards made up only of delegated incumbents, as if indirect presence alone answered every legitimacy question. Others jump too quickly to a new directly elected tier without clarifying the relation between the larger territory and its component municipalities or counties, producing resentment, duplication, or local-democracy erosion. The archive therefore needs a narrower decision rule: when is delegated representation enough, when is a mixed or double-legitimacy form cleaner, and when is direct election the honest answer?

This is not mainly a debate about institutional aesthetics. It is about matching **representation form** to the severity and type of territorial power. Once a shared body can tax, borrow, redistribute, sequence investment, or make recurring cross-sector bargains that residents cannot practically escape, representation by proxy becomes harder to defend. But where a body remains a genuinely bounded coordination instrument, a lighter democratic form may still be appropriate.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from the power actually exercised, not from a default institutional romance

The archive should resist two reflexes: assuming that every shared body must be directly elected because democracy is good, and assuming that delegated boards are always sufficient because the component governments are themselves elected. The right question is narrower and harder: **what kind of public power is this institution actually exercising over a territory?**

Representation form should follow the reality of the mandate: the breadth of functions, the binding force of decisions, the character of finance, the durability of commitments, the extent of distributive bargaining, and the degree to which ordinary residents are governed directly or only through their own local governments.

### 2. Keep indirect representation for narrow, bounded, and clearly derivative co-ordination

An indirectly constituted body can be honest when it remains recognisably derivative of its member governments. This is the natural lane for regional assemblies of municipal delegates, mayoral conferences, statutory joint boards, and similar arrangements whose main job is to align planning, pool service capacity, manage a narrow shared asset, or coordinate implementation in a limited field.

In that lane, legitimacy comes from the fact that the real principals remain visible: the municipalities, counties, or regions that created the body, fund it, and can politically answer for it. The institution should therefore stay clearly bounded, reversible, and explainable. It should not quietly accumulate more severe powers while preserving the same thin democratic story.

### 3. Use double-legitimacy designs when both population and component governments need visible standing

Some territorial bodies govern a space that is more than a loose consortium but still politically composed of meaningful member governments. In those cases, the archive should prefer a **double-legitimacy** design rather than pretending there is only one democratic subject.

That can take different forms: a directly elected metropolitan or regional assembly with a second chamber or consent rule for component governments; a single chamber elected on a territorial basis but with seat-allocation or voting rules that protect smaller components; or a mixed body where part of the forum is territorially elected and part is delegated from member governments. The institutional detail can vary. The constitutional point is that both logics of legitimacy should be visible when both are genuinely at stake.

### 4. Prefer direct election once the body exercises compulsory, redistributive, debt-bearing, or cross-sector territorial power

Direct election becomes the presumptive answer once a shared territorial body can do one or more of the following on a durable basis:

- impose compulsory charges or tax-like levies,
- issue major debt or bind future revenue,
- redistribute resources or burdens among component places,
- make repeated trade-offs across land use, transport, housing, infrastructure, resilience, or similar fields,
- or operate as the real executive center of a functional urban or regional territory.

At that point the body is no longer merely helping elected governments cooperate. It is itself governing a shared public. Residents should then have a route to authorize, judge, and replace the leadership of that territorial government as such.

### 5. Preserve component local democracy rather than treating metropolitan upgrade as a winner-take-all replacement

A stronger territorial democratic home does not require flattening the lower layer beneath it. The archive should continue to reject metropolitan or regional reform that recentralizes everything upward or strips municipalities of their own political space merely because a wider tier is needed.

An honest metropolitan or regional constitution should therefore specify the continuing role of component governments: what remains local, what becomes shared, how conflicts are settled, which floors are common, and where local headroom survives. A new democratic home for shared territorial power should solve proxy rule without dissolving local self-government into decorative neighborhood administration.

### 6. Give the larger territorial body a real public forum, not just an executive cartel

Whatever the electoral form, the territorial body should not be all executive and no assembly. The archive should prefer a visible forum that can deliberate in public, hold hearings, question the executive, approve budgets, scrutinize plans, and leave an intelligible record.

This matters especially in metropolitan governance, where political life often collapses into summit bargaining among mayors, ministers, agencies, and operators. A democratic home that lacks a real forum easily turns back into rule by compact table even if its founding statute uses constitutional language.

### 7. Match citizen-participation design to the territorial scale, but do not use participation to excuse thin representation

Citizen participation can improve legitimacy, trust, and information quality at metropolitan and regional scale. Deliberative mini-publics, structured consultation, participatory planning, and stakeholder forums are all useful complements. But they are not substitutes for an honest representative chain once the institution carries real territorial governing power.

The archive should therefore reject a familiar dodge: replacing elections or visible delegated representation with advisory participation theater while the consequential bargains remain inside boards, cabinets, or intergovernmental compacts.

### 8. Treat indirect-to-direct transition as a reviewable ladder, not a metaphysical leap

Some territories will move from loose co-ordination to indirect statutory co-operation, then to mixed or double-legitimacy forms, and finally to directly elected metropolitan or regional government. The archive should allow that ladder. But every step should publish the threshold for the next one.

A polity should be able to say in advance: if this body acquires compulsory levies, major debt, wider executive powers, or recurrent cross-sector bargaining authority, its representative form must be reconsidered. This keeps institutional growth honest and prevents proxy government from hardening through inertia.

## Democratic-home matrix

| Condition | Indirect / delegated body fits | Double-legitimacy design fits | Direct election fits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Function** | narrow service or coordination remit | several linked territorial functions with durable member-government stakes | broad or recurrent cross-sector territorial government |
| **Finance** | member contributions, service charges, limited budget | mixed finance with meaningful shared allocations | compulsory levies, major debt, redistributive or sequencing power |
| **Citizen relation** | residents affected mainly through component governments | residents affected both as members of the larger territory and of component polities | residents directly bound as a territorial public |
| **Politics** | principals remain the component governments | both population-wide and component-based legitimacy are material | territorial body is the real executive and bargaining center |
| **Institutional form** | statutory board, regional assembly of delegates, mayoral conference with clear limits | mixed chamber, double-majority, directly elected forum plus member-government consent rule | directly elected assembly and executive or assembly-responsible executive |
| **Main risk if misused** | proxy rule and hidden accumulation of power | complexity or veto layering without clarity | overbuild or local-democracy erosion if powers are not clearly divided |

## Guardrails

- Do not assume indirect representation is illegitimate in every case; it can be the right form for genuinely bounded co-ordination.
- Do not assume indirect representation stays legitimate after compulsory finance, major debt, or real cross-sector bargaining appears.
- Do not use direct election as an excuse to absorb all municipal or county politics into one larger territorial machine.
- Do not hide the assembly or scrutiny function inside executive councils, ministerial meetings, or mayoral summits.
- Do not answer a metropolitan democratic-deficit problem with stakeholder theater alone.
- Do not design mixed or double-legitimacy bodies so opaquely that nobody can tell whether people or component governments actually control outcomes.
- Do not let indirect bodies accumulate stronger powers without visible review clocks and upgrade triggers.

## Failure modes

- **metropolitan rule by proxy**: a territory is really governed by delegates of existing officeholders with no direct public route to replace the territorial leadership.
- **double-count denial**: institutions ignore the fact that both the larger territorial public and the component governments have legitimate standing.
- **assemblyless metro**: there is a powerful executive or board but no real public chamber with scrutiny capacity.
- **participation-as-alibi**: hearings and consultations are offered in place of an adequate representative chain.
- **direct-election maximalism**: a directly elected tier is created without preserving meaningful lower-tier self-government or functional clarity.
- **upgrade inertia**: a delegated body keeps accumulating coercive or distributive power while its democratic form remains frozen at an earlier stage.

## Practical tests

A polity is matching democratic form to territorial power honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can it explain why a shared body is indirect, mixed, or directly elected in terms of actual powers rather than institutional habit?
2. If the body can compel finance, issue debt, or make repeated cross-sector territorial bargains, do residents have a proportionate route to authorize and replace its leadership?
3. If component municipalities or counties remain constitutionally important, is their standing visibly preserved rather than erased?
4. Does the body have a real public forum with budget, scrutiny, record, and questioning powers?
5. Are participation and stakeholder input clearly supplementary rather than camouflage for thin representation?
6. Are there visible thresholds that would force reconsideration of the body’s democratic form if its powers expand?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared territorial body now makes the consequential bargains of a metropolitan or regional government, but the public can only reach it through delegated incumbents or consultation rituals, then the polity still lacks an honest democratic home for that power.
