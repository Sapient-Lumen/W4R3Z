# 551 — Public prosecution by scope: local diversion, regional ordinary charging, national integrity cases, and no government by prosecutor

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should keep prosecution lawful, impartial, and scale-matched: use community-near intake and diversion for ordinary low-level harm, size ordinary charging offices to regional or metropolitan court catchments large enough for competence and independence, reserve national prosecutors for public-corruption, state-integrity, and interjurisdictional serious crime, and keep cross-border prosecutorial cooperation narrow, reviewable, and rights-bound so neither local vendetta nor central spectacle can govern by charge.

## Why this matters

The archive now says much more clearly how **public safety**, **remedies**, **regulators**, **judiciaries**, **legislatures**, and **civil service design** should work. What it still lacked was a compact constitutional answer to a linked but different question: **who decides whether the state will prosecute, divert, discontinue, escalate, or specially target a case, and at what level should that power live?**

That gap matters because prosecution is one of the sharpest tools in government. Prosecutors sit between police investigation and judicial judgment. They can end weak or abusive cases, insist on better investigation, protect the defence against unlawful evidence, and direct attention toward corruption or grave abuse by officials. But they can also become engines of fear, spectacle, plea coercion, or political settling of scores when the institution is badly placed or badly governed.

Multi-level systems fail here in opposite ways. Some localize prosecution so tightly that charging becomes entangled with courthouse cliques, local media pressure, or elected-avenger incentives. Others centralize it so far upward that routine cases are screened remotely, victims and defendants lose legible entry points, and politically salient casework becomes a national theater. A third failure is functional blur: police, ministers, and prosecutors begin to share one discretionary field without clear legal boundaries, so no one can tell whether a case moved because of evidence, policy, or power.

An ideal government therefore needs a doctrine of **scale-matched prosecution**. Charging should be professional, evidence-led, and reviewable. Diversion and victim-facing access should stay close enough to communities to work. Serious corruption, cross-border crime, and abuse by officials need prosecution capacity strong enough to resist local capture. And the prosecution service should never become a roving sovereign standing above judges, legislators, police, or the rights of the accused.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat prosecution as a distinct constitutional function, not as an annex of police, courts, or cabinet politics

Prosecutors should not be collapsed into police command, judicial decision, or ministerial message discipline. Their job is to decide whether cases should proceed, on what legal basis, with what evidence, and subject to which alternatives, while respecting the rights of suspects, defendants, victims, and the court. That distinct role is easiest to corrupt when it is disguised.

The archive should therefore prefer prosecution services whose powers, duties, and lines of accountability are established by law; whose relationship to police, ministers, and courts is explicit; and whose institutional identity is professional rather than plebiscitary. Government by prosecutor begins when the charging function becomes a floating reserve of state intimidation rather than a bounded public-law office.

### 2. Keep intake, victim contact, and diversion routes close to where ordinary harm appears

At the smallest scales, the prosecution function should usually not mean a fully autonomous local prosecutorial empire. But people still need nearby places where complaints, referral decisions, victim information, restorative options, and low-level case screening can happen without a long trip into a distant regional center. Municipal or district-facing entry points, prosecution counters embedded near trial courts, and structured referral lanes from community safety or youth services can all make the system more usable.

This is also the right layer for carefully governed diversion and restorative routes in minor cases, especially where prosecution would do more damage than good. The archive should prefer local accessibility without local vendetta power.

### 3. Size ordinary prosecution offices to real court and caseload catchments, not to map vanity

The core office that screens ordinary criminal cases, supervises investigations where the legal system assigns that role, and appears in trial court should usually sit at regional, county, district, or metropolitan scale. That level is often large enough to sustain professional staff, training, specialist units, rotation, and independence from courthouse gossip, but still close enough to witnesses, victims, police investigators, and courts to understand the local fact pattern.

Too-small prosecutorial districts breed capture and inconsistency. Too-large nationalized case screening breeds remoteness and administrative drift. The archive should therefore prefer prosecution maps aligned to actual trial-court geography, travel realities, language catchments, and caseload resilience rather than symbolic local sovereignty.

### 4. Reserve national prosecution for integrity crimes, interjurisdictional harm, and the cases local power cannot safely touch

National prosecutors are most justified where ordinary local or regional offices are structurally too weak or too entangled to act impartially: grand corruption, abuse of power by senior officials, complex organized crime crossing regions, major financial crime, systematic rights abuse, electoral crimes with national spillover, and international mutual legal assistance or extradition work.

The point is not that every serious case belongs at the center. It is that some cases require distance from local patronage, common legal strategy across regions, or capacities no smaller office can maintain. Ideal government therefore treats the national prosecution service as a targeted integrity and coordination layer, not as the default owner of all criminal life.

### 5. Make charge, discontinuance, and plea decisions evidence-led, reasoned, and policy-bounded

A prosecution service should not behave like a scorekeeping machine driven by conviction rates, press cycles, or ambient panic. The archive should prefer systems where prosecutors proceed only when a case is founded on sufficient lawful evidence, discontinue or stay cases that are not, and apply any public-interest or opportunity criteria through published guidelines rather than through rumor or favor.

This matters at every scale. Local offices need reasons so they cannot quietly bury allies’ cases. Regional offices need guidelines so similarly situated people are not routed arbitrarily. National offices need disciplined criteria so “high priority” does not simply mean politically useful.

### 6. Put hierarchy under law: instructions should be lawful, written, attributable, and challengeable

Many prosecution services use hierarchy, and hierarchy is not itself the problem. The danger is invisible or partisan hierarchy: whispered orders, campaign promises translated into case selection, or career pressures that bend prosecutors toward what superiors or ministers want rather than what law and evidence support.

The archive should therefore prefer systems in which any case-specific instruction must travel through lawful channels, be recorded in writing, carry reasons, and be challengeable internally or externally where appropriate. A hierarchy that cannot leave an intelligible trace is an invitation to abuse.

### 7. Bind prosecution to defence rights, victim dignity, and judicial independence

Prosecutors do not serve justice by winning at all costs. They should refuse charges when the investigation does not support them, disclose relevant material as the law requires, avoid using evidence obtained by grave human-rights abuse, and respect the independence of judges rather than treating the bench as an obstacle course.

At the same time, victims need information, respectful treatment, and routes to be heard without turning prosecution into revenge brokerage. An ideal prosecution service is neither a victims’ public-relations office nor an adversarial machine that treats fairness as weakness. It is a disciplined public actor whose legitimacy comes from even-handedness.

### 8. Build explicit low-level exit ramps: diversion, conditional discontinuance, and youth-sensitive prosecution policy

Not every legal violation should become a full criminal prosecution. The archive should prefer laws and prosecution policies that allow diversion, conditional discontinuance, restorative processes, treatment referral where appropriate, and especially youth-sensitive alternatives that reduce unnecessary criminalization while respecting victims’ rights and public safety.

This is not an exception to legality; it is part of a lawful prosecution constitution. A system with no exit ramps overloads courts, intensifies inequality, and turns the charge itself into punishment.

### 9. Keep non-criminal and public-interest prosecutor roles narrow, reviewable, and non-monopolistic

Some systems give prosecutors functions outside criminal law: public-interest litigation, civil-status matters, administrative review, protection of children or persons lacking capacity, association oversight, or other interventions. The archive should treat such roles cautiously. Where they exist, they should be established clearly by law, used in ways compatible with equality of arms and access to courts, and not allowed to crowd out ombuds institutions, specialized agencies, or private parties’ own standing.

The prosecution service should not become the state’s universal legal conscience merely because no one else was built.

### 10. Keep cross-border prosecutorial cooperation narrow, ordinary-law, and rights-bearing

International cooperation in prosecution can be necessary for extradition, mutual legal assistance, asset recovery, atrocity accountability, and serious organized crime. But the archive should prefer a narrow-waist model: clear legal basis, dual-criminality and rights filters where applicable, record integrity, review routes, and strong protections against political cases or laundering of abusive evidence through foreign partners.

Transnational prosecutorial cooperation should solve concrete legal problems, not create a roaming post-national prosecutorial state.

## Guardrails

- Do not fuse prosecution with police command or executive communications strategy.
- Do not create tiny local prosecution fiefdoms that depend on personal cliques, courthouse habits, or electoral theater.
- Do not centralize all charging discretion so far upward that routine justice becomes remote and opaque.
- Do not let case-specific instructions remain oral, deniable, or unattributable.
- Do not treat diversion as favoritism for the connected or as a substitute for social policy without safeguards.
- Do not let prosecutors drift into general guardianship over every public-interest problem merely because other institutions are weak.
- Do not let cross-border cooperation bypass domestic rights, court review, or political-offence protections.

## Failure modes

- **campaign prosecutor rule**: prosecution choices are driven by electioneering, media heat, or symbolic toughness markets.
- **courthouse clique capture**: tiny districts entangle prosecutors with local elites, police, or judges so strongly that impartiality becomes fictional.
- **charge-first bureaucracy**: cases are filed reflexively and fairness review happens only after the damage is done.
- **oral-order hierarchy**: ministers or superiors bend casework through invisible instructions that leave no reviewable trace.
- **anti-corruption theater**: national prosecutor units stage selective integrity cases while ignoring ordinary structural corruption or allies.
- **parallel prosecutor state**: non-criminal prosecutor powers expand until they displace ombuds, regulators, and ordinary litigants.
- **cross-border laundering**: abusive evidence, political targeting, or low-integrity notices travel through international cooperation channels and acquire false legitimacy.

## Practical tests

A prosecution architecture passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Are ordinary case intake, victim-facing access, and diversion routes close enough to communities to be usable without creating autonomous local vendetta machines?
2. Are ordinary charging offices sized to real court, language, travel, and caseload catchments large enough for competence and independence?
3. Is the national prosecution layer reserved for cases that genuinely require distance from local capture or coordination across jurisdictions, rather than default ownership of all serious attention?
4. Are charge, discontinuance, plea, and instruction decisions governed by law, reasons, records, and review rather than by hidden pressure or spectacle?
5. Do prosecutors remain bound to defence rights, victim dignity, judicial independence, and rights-safe international cooperation?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can describe **its police and its courts** but cannot show **who may charge, who may stop a weak case, which level handles corruption and interjurisdictional harm, how instructions are recorded, and where diversion sits**, then it still has **criminal procedure without a prosecution constitution**.
