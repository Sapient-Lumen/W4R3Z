# 698 — Shared-regime implementing-partner-and-pass-through constitutions: due diligence, workplans, cash transfer, assurance, suspension, closeout, and no mission by subaward fog

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed implementing-partner-and-pass-through constitution** that separates partner eligibility, due diligence, engagement risk, workplans, funding modality, milestone and expenditure reporting, assurance activity, corrective action, suspension, and closeout — so delivery through external partners does not dissolve into subaward fog, unreviewed dependency, or mission by pass-through habit.

## Why this matters

The archive now explains external participation (`677`), independence-and-conflict discipline (`678`), budget discipline (`683`), procurement discipline (`685`), private-law-claims discipline (`688`), territorial presence (`689`), and enterprise-risk discipline (`695`).

That is enough to explain **how an institution engages outsiders, spends directly, manages vendor contracting, handles claims, plants territorial presence, and governs cross-cutting risk**. It is still not enough to explain **how a shared regime governs delivery when money, implementation work, or service execution travels through external implementing partners rather than the secretariat’s own payroll and direct operating chain**.

That gap matters because many shared regimes deliver through ministries, public agencies, civil-society organizations, technical institutions, consortia, or other partners that receive money, hold responsibilities in a workplan, generate records, and influence whether the regime’s public commitments are actually fulfilled.

Without a typed implementing-partner constitution, eight familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **subaward fog**: the regime can say which programme it funds, but not which outside actor is actually delivering which activity under what authority and with what controls.

The second is **partner-by-friendship government**: entities receive operational roles because they are familiar, politically useful, or historically close, not because they passed visible eligibility, due diligence, and risk review.

The third is **workplan-free funding**: money flows without a sufficiently specific implementation packet tying the transfer to activities, deliverables, reporting duties, and expenditure conditions.

The fourth is **assurance lag**: risk assessment happens at onboarding, but ongoing spot checks, reviews, audits, or performance checks do not keep pace with live delivery risk.

The fifth is **dependency laundering**: the regime uses partners to do work that is politically or operationally central while treating the partner lane as if it were marginal or merely supportive.

The sixth is **corrective-action theater**: weaknesses are found in audits, spot checks, or reviews, but the regime cannot show what changed, who owned remediation, or when escalation should have triggered suspension.

The seventh is **records-and-asset orphaning**: at closeout, nobody can show where beneficiary records, procured assets, unspent balances, or continuing obligations went.

The eighth is **exit denial**: a partner relationship has clearly broken down, but the regime lacks a visible pause, suspension, transfer, or termination lane that protects beneficiaries while stopping unsafe continuation.

Shared regimes need a tighter partner-delivery grammar.

## Pattern pack

### 1) Partner category map

Separate at minimum:

- implementation partners receiving funds or carrying activities;
- strategic collaborators not implementing against a funded workplan;
- vendors or contractors governed primarily through procurement law;
- hosted secretariats or pooled mechanisms;
- observer or advisory bodies with no delivery authority.

Do not let every outside actor be called a “partner” in the same constitutional sense.

### 2) Eligibility, due diligence, and risk assessment lane

Before authorizing operational partnership, require a packet that states:

- legal identity and controlling structure;
- mission fit and conflict considerations;
- financial and administrative capacity;
- safeguarding, integrity, and misconduct checks where relevant;
- prior performance and dependency history;
- risk rating for the proposed engagement.

Due diligence should describe the actor; risk assessment should describe the proposed relationship.

### 3) Workplan and collaboration-plan spine

Require one signed workplan or collaboration plan that states:

- objectives and activities;
- deliverables or milestones;
- timeline;
- budget and eligible expenditure frame;
- reporting duties;
- data, asset, and records responsibilities;
- visibility and communication rules;
- amendment route.

Funding without a workplan is usually just hope with paperwork.

### 4) Funding modality lane

Separate the major funding modalities, such as:

- direct cash transfer or advance;
- reimbursement;
- direct payment to third parties on behalf of the partner;
- in-kind support;
- staged disbursement tied to milestones.

The modality should match both risk and operating reality rather than tradition alone.

### 5) Assurance architecture

Require a visible assurance plan showing which partners receive:

- programme monitoring visits;
- spot checks or expenditure review;
- performance review;
- scheduled audit where risk warrants;
- special review after major incidents or control failures.

Assurance should be risk-based, not purely calendar-based or donor-theater-based.

### 6) Corrective action and escalation ladder

When assurance identifies weaknesses, require one path that separates:

- minor corrective action;
- enhanced monitoring;
- temporary funding hold;
- partial suspension;
- transfer of activities;
- termination and recovery where applicable.

Findings should not disappear into advisory language if continued delivery is unsafe.

### 7) Capacity-building versus substitution rule

State explicitly:

- when the regime is helping build partner capability;
- when it is relying on the partner as a stable delivery arm;
- when the gap is so material that the implementation model itself must change.

Capacity-building language should not be used to excuse indefinite high-risk dependence.

### 8) Data, asset, and beneficiary-protection packet

For each partner-delivery relationship, state:

- who holds beneficiary data and under what privacy rules;
- who owns procured assets and inventories;
- who maintains transaction and programme records;
- who retains evidence for later audit, claims, or evaluation;
- how continuity is protected if the partner exits midstream.

### 9) Closeout and successor lane

Closeout should visibly dispose of:

- financial reconciliation;
- remaining balances;
- unfinished commitments;
- assets and inventories;
- records and data retention;
- beneficiary communication;
- successor arrangements if delivery continues elsewhere.

### 10) Register and review clock

Maintain one live partner register with at least:

- category of relationship;
- legal basis and workplan period;
- current risk rating;
- assurance status;
- open corrective actions;
- next review date;
- suspension or closeout status if applicable.

A regime that cannot list its live implementing partners is already operating through delivery fog.

## Working matrix

| Implementing-partner lane | Core constitutional question | Minimum visible packet |
|---|---|---|
| Eligibility | who may carry delivery authority or receive funds? | category rule, entry criteria, legal-basis check |
| Due diligence | what do we know about the entity? | identity, governance, capacity, integrity profile |
| Engagement risk | is this specific relationship acceptable? | risk rating, mitigations, approval lane |
| Workplan | what exactly is the partner meant to do? | objectives, milestones, budget, reporting, amendment lane |
| Funding | how does money or support flow? | modality, disbursement conditions, expenditure basis |
| Assurance | how will the regime know the work and money are on track? | monitoring plan, spot checks, audits, corrective-action rule |
| Escalation | what happens when the relationship becomes unsafe? | hold, suspension, transfer, termination, recovery path |
| Closeout | how are money, assets, records, and duties wound down? | reconciliation, asset transfer, retention, beneficiary continuity |

## Guardrails

- Do not treat all external relationships as if they raised the same risk or deserved the same controls.
- Do not rely on first-entry due diligence as a substitute for ongoing assurance.
- Do not move money without a clear workplan, reporting basis, and amendment lane.
- Do not let partner-delivery arrangements hide central mission dependence.
- Do not leave records, assets, or beneficiary files constitutionally orphaned at closeout.
- Do not keep a failed partner relationship alive merely because no visible transfer or suspension lane exists.

## Failure modes

- **Subaward fog** — the regime cannot show who is really delivering the work.
- **Friendship selection** — relationship history substitutes for due diligence.
- **Assurance lag** — risks change faster than monitoring and review.
- **Corrective-action theater** — findings accumulate without real escalation.
- **Dependency laundering** — core mission work is outsourced without being governed as such.
- **Closeout orphaning** — assets, data, balances, or obligations are left in limbo.

## Practical tests

1. Can the regime distinguish implementing partners from advisers, observers, vendors, and hosted bodies?
2. Does every live implementing partner have a visible due-diligence packet and relationship-specific risk decision?
3. Is there a signed workplan or collaboration plan tying funds to activities, milestones, and reporting duties?
4. Can the organization explain why the chosen funding modality matches the partner and risk profile?
5. Is there a current assurance plan that changes with partner risk rather than staying static?
6. Can the regime show open corrective actions, owners, and escalation status for partner weaknesses?
7. Are beneficiary data, assets, and records ownership typed before problems arise?
8. Is there a usable suspension, transfer, or termination lane that protects continuity rather than forcing risky continuation?
9. Could an outsider identify all current implementing partners, their status, and next review dates from one register?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can show **partner names, agreements, and transferred funds** but cannot show **partner categories, due diligence, engagement-risk decisions, workplan discipline, funding modality logic, assurance coverage, escalation lanes, and closeout packets**, then it still has **partnership language without a real implementing-partner-and-pass-through constitution**.
