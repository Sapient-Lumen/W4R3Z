# 694 — Shared-regime statistics-and-indicator constitutions: designation, methodology, metadata, revisions, quality assurance, release calendars, and no governance by number theater

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed statistics-and-indicator constitution** that separates designated official series from exploratory analytics, indicator ownership from political sponsorship, methodology from messaging, metadata from headline numbers, revisions from corrections, and release discipline from communications convenience — so evidence does not decay into number theater, indicator shopping, or policy-by-dashboard without professional standards.

## Why this matters

The archive now explains how shared regimes classify and disclose information (`691`), govern digital systems (`692`), and protect personal data (`693`). It also explains interpretation and jurisprudence memory (`679`–`680`) and records discipline (`674`).

That is enough to explain **how information is stored, protected, published, and interpreted**. It is still not enough to explain **which figures count as official statistics or official indicators, who owns the method, what metadata must travel with a number, when revisions are legitimate, how quality is reviewed, when a release may move, and how dashboards relate to the underlying official series**.

That gap matters because many regimes now govern through targets, scorecards, risk dashboards, league tables, alerts, and comparative metrics. Once numbers begin allocating attention, blame, funding, compliance pressure, or emergency posture, the statistical constitution becomes part of the constitutional order.

Without a typed statistics-and-indicator constitution, eight familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **governance by number theater**: a prominent figure travels farther than its method, limitations, or revision rules.

The second is **indicator shopping**: leaders swap metrics when incentives change because no one typed which series are official for which decisions.

The third is **metadata collapse**: users see a number but not its source, definition, coverage, reference period, comparability limits, or known caveats.

The fourth is **revision scandal by surprise**: legitimate revisions look like manipulation because the regime never explained its revision policy or vintage discipline.

The fifth is **dashboard sovereignty**: product teams or communications teams become the real arbiters of indicator meaning.

The sixth is **quality by assertion**: data are called reliable without a visible quality-assurance method, quality dimensions, or review cycle.

The seventh is **method drift**: classification, denominator, imputation, or estimation choices change without a release note or bridge explanation.

The eighth is **release favoritism**: politically convenient publication timing or selective preview undermines equal access to official evidence.

Shared regimes need a tighter evidence grammar.

## Pattern pack

### 1) Official-series designation

Publish a visible register that distinguishes:

- official statistics or official indicator series;
- administrative or operational management metrics;
- experimental or developmental indicators;
- one-off analytical estimates;
- public dashboards that summarize but do not replace the official series.

Do not let every chart count as an official statistic.

### 2) Method owner, release owner, and communications owner

For each official series or consequential indicator, name:

- the methodological owner;
- the release owner;
- the communications or presentation owner;
- the approval lane for material changes.

The person designing the dashboard should not silently become the owner of the method.

### 3) Metadata packet and indicator registry

Require each official series to carry a minimum metadata packet, including:

- definition and unit;
- numerator, denominator, or construction rule where applicable;
- source systems or collection method;
- geographic and population coverage;
- reference period and update cadence;
- disaggregation rules;
- comparability limits;
- known caveats and exclusions;
- contact or owner lane.

A number without metadata is promotional material, not governed evidence.

### 4) Quality dimensions and review cycle

Type a visible quality map that can cover, as relevant:

- relevance or fitness for use;
- accuracy and reliability;
- timeliness and punctuality;
- coherence and comparability;
- completeness and consistency;
- interpretability and accessibility.

Require a review cycle or quality-assurance cadence for consequential series.

### 5) Revision, correction, and vintage discipline

Separate:

- routine revisions from late-arriving data;
- methodological rebasing or re-estimation;
- error corrections;
- retroactive reconstruction of historical series;
- provisional versus final releases.

Each lane should have a visible explanation rule and, where needed, preserved vintages or bridge notes.

### 6) Release calendar and equal-access rule

For official series, publish:

- expected release cadence;
- the authority for delaying a release;
- any pre-release access rules;
- the difference between publication and later commentary.

Equal access is part of statistical integrity.

### 7) Change-log and bridge-note rule

When definitions, classifications, weights, or methods materially change, require:

- a change note;
- an effective date;
- treatment of historical comparability;
- bridge tables or explanatory mapping where feasible;
- a flag if old and new values should not be directly compared.

### 8) Decision linkage map

For high-consequence metrics, state:

- what decisions the indicator may inform;
- what it may not be used for by itself;
- whether corroboration or qualitative review is required;
- who may authorize exceptional use outside the ordinary purpose.

### 9) Dashboard-to-series discipline

Where dashboards, scorecards, or composite indexes exist, require the interface to show:

- whether each displayed value is official, provisional, modeled, or experimental;
- links to the underlying metadata;
- update date and release status;
- whether color thresholds or alerts are policy choices rather than statistical facts.

### 10) Professional independence and challenge lane

Maintain a lane through which statistical or methodological staff can:

- record caveats;
- resist politicized timing or presentation pressure;
- propose methodological improvements;
- trigger review when a headline indicator is no longer fit for use.

## Working matrix

| Evidence lane | Core constitutional question | Minimum visible packet |
|---|---|---|
| Official statistics | what figures carry official standing? | series register, owner, release cadence, metadata |
| Operational metrics | what supports management but is not an official public series? | purpose, owner, caveat label, non-official status |
| Experimental indicators | what is developmental or provisional? | experimental label, method note, review trigger |
| Revisions and corrections | how do values change honestly over time? | revision policy, change note, vintage or bridge rule |
| Quality assurance | why should users trust the series? | quality dimensions, review cadence, issue log |
| Dashboards and composites | how do presentations relate to the official series? | provenance, metadata links, threshold explanation |

## Guardrails

- Do not present politically useful dashboards as if they were self-executing official statistics.
- Do not release headline numbers without metadata, caveats, and provenance.
- Do not let revisions appear ad hoc when a standing revision policy could have made them ordinary.
- Do not let communications teams or platform owners rewrite indicator meaning by interface design.
- Do not collapse official, operational, experimental, and modeled outputs into one undifferentiated data surface.
- Do not let thresholds, color bands, or composite weights masquerade as neutral fact.

## Failure modes

- **Indicator shopping** — different offices cite whichever metric suits the moment.
- **Metadata blackout** — users see the headline value but not its scope, denominator, or exclusions.
- **Revision panic** — ordinary statistical updating looks like corruption because revision rules were never published.
- **Comparability theater** — countries, periods, or units are compared as if definitions and collection practices were stable when they were not.
- **Dashboard capture** — interface owners or communications teams become the real arbiters of evidence.
- **Quality fog** — claims of reliability are made without visible quality checks or review results.

## Practical tests

1. Can the regime name which series are official, operational, experimental, or modeled?
2. Does every consequential indicator have a visible methodological owner and metadata packet?
3. Can a user tell the reference period, coverage, denominator, exclusions, and comparability limits of a published number?
4. Is there a standing revision rule that distinguishes routine revisions, corrections, and methodological breaks?
5. Are release dates, delays, and any pre-release access rules visible and bounded?
6. Can the regime show how it checks quality, not just that it values quality?
7. When methods change, are bridge notes or comparability warnings published?
8. Do dashboards link back to official series and disclose whether thresholds or composites are policy choices?
9. Is there a professional challenge lane for staff to register methodological caveats or resist politicized presentation?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can show **numbers, dashboards, and targets** but not **which series are official, how methods and metadata are governed, how revisions and quality assurance work, and how presentations stay subordinate to the underlying evidence**, then it still has **data surfaces without an honest statistics-and-indicator constitution**.
