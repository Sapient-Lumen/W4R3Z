# 590 — Multilevel impact packets, subsidiarity, territorial effects, rural proofing, and no lawmaking by placeless abstraction

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should require any higher-tier law, regulation, spending program, or platform reform that materially affects lower-tier powers, finances, administrative burden, or territorial outcomes to ship with a **published multilevel impact packet** — a compact subsidiarity, proportionality, territorial-effects, implementation, and consultation record — so central action is argued openly rather than smuggled through placeless abstraction.

## Why this matters

The archive now has stronger doctrines for **scope allocation** (`579`), **mandate-finance congruence** (`581`), **capability congruence** (`582`), **override and preemption** (`584`), **grant rule** (`585`), **function transfer** (`586`), **deconcentration** (`587`), **delegated tasks** (`588`), and **general competence / home rule** (`589`). What it still lacked was one compact doctrine for the question that should arise *before* many of those conflicts harden: **what proof a higher tier owes before changing the terms under which lower tiers govern**.

Without that proof discipline, a polity can recite subsidiarity while legislating as if places were interchangeable. Ministries write uniform rules with no territorial map. Legislatures impose new duties with no estimate of local staffing or software impact. Grant designers attach matching requirements with no account of which territories will be excluded. Platform teams standardize a workflow with no record of which municipalities, islands, remote areas, or weak-capacity regions will struggle to implement it. By the time the damage is visible, the formal decision is already complete and the political story has shifted from **whether this should have been done** to **why local government is failing to comply**.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine for **ex ante multilevel proof**. A serious multi-level constitution should require proposals with meaningful subnational effects to carry a visible packet that answers five questions up front:

1. **Subsidiarity / necessity** — why is higher-tier action needed at all?
2. **Proportionality / burden** — why is this form no heavier than necessary?
3. **Territorial effects** — which kinds of places gain, lose, or face asymmetrical burdens?
4. **Implementation realism** — who will administer, fund, staff, procure, and monitor the change?
5. **Consultation integrity** — who was consulted, when, and how were their objections handled?

## Pattern pack

### 1. Require a multilevel impact packet whenever upper-tier action materially reaches below itself

Not every bill or circular needs a constitutional dossier. But any proposal that materially alters lower-tier powers, duties, funding, administrative burden, implementation systems, or territorial outcomes should not travel alone. It should carry a short, standard packet that lets legislatures, courts, local-government associations, journalists, and the public see the multilevel theory of the change.

The packet should be required for at least these classes of proposal:

- reassignment or narrowing of lower-tier competences;
- new delegated tasks or reporting duties;
- new or changed grants, matching requirements, or co-financing rules;
- platform, registry, data, or procedural mandates that local or regional authorities must implement;
- uniform sector rules likely to affect different territories asymmetrically;
- and emergency measures whose urgency would otherwise be used to bypass normal multilevel scrutiny.

### 2. Distinguish subsidiarity from proportionality from territorial effects

These are related questions, but they are not the same question.

A good packet should therefore keep them separate:

- **subsidiarity** asks whether action at the higher level is actually necessary or whether the goal could be achieved by lower-tier action, lighter coordination, or floor-setting rather than takeover;
- **proportionality** asks whether the chosen instrument, compliance burden, and coercive intensity are no greater than needed;
- **territorial effects** ask how the proposal lands differently across metros, ordinary municipalities, rural areas, islands, border zones, remote settlements, poor places, or asymmetric territories.

When these are collapsed into one vague paragraph, placeless abstraction wins. A ministry can say “national coherence requires this” without showing why local variation is inadequate, why a lighter instrument would fail, or why the burdens fall unevenly across place types.

### 3. Make consultation early enough to change the proposal, not merely decorate it

The packet should disclose **who was consulted, when, by what route, on what draft, and with what response**. Consultation after the main architecture is fixed is not good enough.

The archive should prefer consultation routes that reach both:

- representative associations of municipalities, counties, regions, and autonomous territories where these exist; and
- directly affected operating authorities where a proposal targets a particular institutional type or territory class.

Where proposals are rejected or significantly modified against subnational objections, the packet should say so plainly and publish reasons. A constitutional consultation right is hollow if higher tiers can solicit comments, ignore them silently, and still claim that consultation occurred.

### 4. Use a screening ladder, then escalate to full analysis when territorial or subnational effects are likely to be significant

A good polity does not need every minor measure to carry a book-length assessment. It needs a visible **screening ladder**.

The first step should be a short threshold check asking whether the proposal is likely to:

- affect local or regional powers, finances, staffing, or reporting;
- create asymmetrical territorial effects;
- impose new digital, procedural, or procurement obligations on lower tiers;
- interact with fragile-capacity or peripheral territories;
- or close off local headroom above a common floor.

If the answer is no, the packet can remain short. If the answer is yes, the polity should require fuller analysis of territorial effects, implementation burdens, funding, alternatives, and mitigation.

### 5. Map differentiated effects across place types instead of assuming one average territory

The packet should name the relevant territorial typology instead of hiding behind national averages. Depending on the proposal, that may mean urban/rural, metro/non-metro, island/mainland, capital/periphery, border/interior, fast-growth/shrinking, wealthy/low-capacity, or indigenous/autonomous/general-regime distinctions.

The point is not to produce decorative cartography. The point is to force a truthful question: **which places are being asked to carry the transition cost, compliance burden, or risk?**

A polity that can model national totals but not territorial asymmetry is still governing by placeless arithmetic.

### 6. Include the implementation lane, not just the legal theory

Many multilevel failures happen because the proposal explains the legal objective but not the operating lane. A serious packet should therefore include an implementation section naming:

- which level actually administers the change;
- what staffing, training, procurement, or software adaptation is required;
- what records, data, or registry changes are required;
- what the funding source is;
- what transition period applies;
- and what fallback or support path exists for weak-capacity authorities.

Otherwise the polity keeps adopting “simple” upper-tier rules whose hidden complexity is exported downstream.

### 7. Force explicit comparison against lower-tier and lighter alternatives

A higher-tier proposal should not simply assert necessity. It should say which alternatives were considered and why they were rejected, including:

- local or regional action with common floors;
- optional model rules;
- intergovernmental compacts;
- delegated implementation with local discretion;
- formula funding rather than competitive control;
- pilots, derogations, or phased transition;
- and non-regulatory or information approaches.

This matters because many quiet recentralizations survive by refusing to state the alternative. Once the comparison is visible, it becomes much harder to pretend that full upper-tier command was the only serious option.

### 8. Build in a quality check before adoption

The packet should not be a ceremonial attachment that no one reads. It should pass through a visible review lane before adoption — parliamentary committee scrutiny, an independent better-regulation or regulatory-scrutiny body, a constitutional office, or another public quality-control mechanism depending on the legal system.

What matters is not one universal institutional form. What matters is that a proposal with serious lower-tier consequences can be returned, delayed, or publicly criticized if its subsidiarity case, territorial analysis, or implementation realism is missing or weak.

### 9. Pair ex ante proof with ex post handback and correction

Multilevel impact packets should not end at enactment. The same instrument should name when the settlement will be reviewed and what evidence would justify revision, sunset, handback, simplification, or stronger support.

This is especially important for:

- emergency centralisations;
- temporary grant conditions;
- digital standardisation projects;
- and supposedly uniform national reforms imposed on highly diverse territories.

Without a review clock, an instrument adopted under incomplete information becomes permanent by inertia.

### 10. Keep the packet standardized, short, and comparable

The answer to bad impact discipline is not limitless paperwork. It is a compact, standard, comparable record.

The archive should therefore prefer a packet template short enough to be politically usable but structured enough to prevent omission. A strong template usually includes:

- purpose and legal basis;
- subsidiarity claim;
- proportionality and burden claim;
- territorial-effects screen and, where needed, full territorial analysis;
- lower-tier funding and implementation consequences;
- consultation log and response table;
- alternatives considered;
- review date and correction triggers.

A short honest packet beats a long evasive annex.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat consultation as valid if it happens too late to influence the proposal.
- Do not merge subsidiarity, proportionality, territorial effects, and implementation into one vague recital.
- Do not use national averages to hide asymmetrical territorial burdens.
- Do not impose new lower-tier obligations without naming funding, staffing, digital, and transition consequences.
- Do not let emergency or platform reforms escape multilevel proof merely because they look technical.
- Do not build impact systems so elaborate that only central experts can operate them.

## Failure modes

- **lawmaking by placeless abstraction**: legislation assumes one average territory and notices subnational reality only after failure.
- **consultation theater**: local and regional authorities are asked for views after the decisive architecture is already closed.
- **subsidiarity by assertion**: a higher tier declares necessity without comparing lower-tier or lighter alternatives.
- **implementation dumping**: legal duties are specified, but staffing, software, records, and procurement consequences are not.
- **territorial averaging**: aggregate national benefits hide concentrated burdens on rural, remote, poor, island, or weak-capacity places.
- **technical disguise**: platform mandates, reporting schemas, registry standards, or procurement conditions are treated as neutral administration rather than constitutional steering.
- **packet inflation**: the process becomes so bureaucratic that only formal compliance survives and practical scrutiny dies.

## Practical tests

A polity is using multilevel impact packets honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is there a clear trigger for when upper-tier proposals need a multilevel impact packet?
2. Does the packet separately address subsidiarity, proportionality, territorial effects, implementation, and consultation?
3. Are local and regional authorities or their associations consulted early enough to influence the proposal?
4. Does the packet identify differentiated effects across relevant place types instead of using only national averages?
5. Are funding, staffing, digital, records, and transition consequences for lower tiers named explicitly?
6. Does the proposal compare higher-tier command with lower-tier or lighter alternatives?
7. Is there a public quality-check lane that can reject or challenge weak packets?
8. Does the instrument include a review or handback clock if the reform is temporary, emergency-driven, or highly uncertain?
9. Is the packet short and standardized enough to be comparable across proposals rather than a bespoke fog machine?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a higher tier can materially narrow lower-tier autonomy, impose new duties, or create uneven territorial burdens **without publishing why upper-tier action is needed, how burdens vary by place, how lower tiers will implement the change, and how consultation changed the design**, then the polity does not yet govern by subsidiarity. It governs by **placeless abstraction with downstream blame**.
