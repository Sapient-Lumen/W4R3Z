# 830 — Decision-rule defaults for ideal governments of each scope: local majorities, municipal ordinary rules, federal double keys, and no fake global unanimity

## One-line thesis

The archive should answer the question **what internal decision rule should usually govern at each scope once the body is real?** with scale-matched defaults rather than one voting fetish: **micro-local institutions should seek rough consensus but fall back cheaply to ordinary majority; municipalities, metropolitan governments, and most regional and national lower chambers should decide ordinary business by ordinary majority with reserved supermajorities for constitutional, territorial, debt, or rights-sensitive thresholds; shared-rule chambers may justify double keys where constituent units are genuinely co-authors of the settlement; and continental or global bodies should treat consensus as a discipline of accommodation, not a veto constitution, reserving unanimity only for narrow membership-, treaty-, or sovereignty-touching classes**.

## Why this matters

The archive can now say a great deal, in a compact way, about **what each scope is for** (`790`, `660`), **what regime form usually fits it** (`825`), **where authority should draw its mandate** (`826`), **how long it should last** (`827`), **how it should end** (`828`), and **how offices and chambers should be chosen** (`829`).

What it still lacked was one short answer-key note for a different constitutional question:

**once a chamber, council, assembly, or treaty body is lawfully composed, what vote grammar should it usually use to make decisions without either paralysis or false majoritarian simplicity?**

That gap matters because constitutions often import the wrong decision rule from the wrong scale. Tiny local bodies are forced into rigid parliamentary procedure when informal agreement would do. Diverse municipalities and national lower chambers are trapped by supermajority habits that turn ordinary governing into hostage politics. Federal or plurinational bargains are flattened into crude head-count majorities. Upper-tier bodies then compensate by pretending consensus means everyone truly agrees, when it often means dissent has been priced out of visibility.

The archive therefore needs one compression note on **decision-rule defaults by scope**. The live question is not whether voting exists. It is which decision threshold is honest for the kind of public and the kind of authority sitting at that scale.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from decision class, not from inherited ritual

The right voting rule depends on what kind of decision is being made.

At every scope, the archive should first sort decisions into broad classes:

- **ordinary operating decisions**;
- **budgetary and appropriations decisions**;
- **structural or constitutional decisions**;
- **territorial or shared-rule decisions**;
- **emergency decisions**;
- **membership, accession, withdrawal, or dissolution decisions**.

A polity that uses the same threshold for all of these is usually hiding either paralysis or domination inside procedural simplicity.

### 2. Micro-local bodies should prefer cheap agreement, not procedural grandeur

At neighborhood, village, block, or ward scale, the archive should usually prefer **consensus-seeking with simple fallback rules**. These bodies exist for witness, upkeep, tiny allocations, escalation, and localized stewardship. They should therefore try to surface rough agreement, hear objections, and keep the threshold for action low enough that small-scale maintenance does not die inside procedural theater.

Where a vote is needed, the honest default is usually a simple majority of those present under visible quorum rules, with recall, rotation, or quick revisit options doing more corrective work than elaborate supermajority thresholds.

The archive should distrust any micro-local design that makes tiny steward bodies depend on quorum traps, lawyerly motions, or hard supermajorities for routine upkeep.

### 3. Municipal government should run ordinary law and budget work on ordinary majorities

Municipal government is the first scale where everyday public administration becomes real. Streets must be repaired, budgets must pass, permits and plans must move, and procurement cannot live under permanent constitutional suspense.

The archive should therefore usually prefer **ordinary-majority decision rules** for ordinary municipal legislation, budgets, service priorities, and committee work, while reserving higher thresholds for the small class of decisions that truly alter the city's constitutional shape: charter amendment, boundary change where locally controlled, extraordinary indebtedness, disposal of strategic public assets, or similarly heavy commitments.

This keeps cities governable while still marking the difference between routine political disagreement and genuine constitutional alteration.

### 4. Metropolitan governments should not be trapped by confederal vetoes once metro authority is real

A metropolitan body that can bind the region on transport, housing allocation, trunk infrastructure, or resilience planning should not usually require every constituent municipality to agree to ordinary decisions. Once metro government is real, the archive should usually prefer **ordinary-majority or qualified-majority voting inside a directly legitimated metropolitan chamber**, not a standing municipal veto constitution.

Special double keys may still be justified for a narrow class of decisions that directly redraw burdens among constituent governments, alter accession or exit status, or change the metropolitan constitution itself. But the everyday operating grammar should not let each locality turn regional systems into hostage bargaining.

### 5. Regional and national lower chambers should usually govern by majority, with supermajorities reserved not normalized

At regional and national scale, the archive should usually treat the main popularly representative chamber as the place where ordinary law and ordinary confidence decisions are made by **ordinary majority**, because that is the rule most compatible with responsible government and visible accountability.

Supermajorities still matter. They often belong to constitutional amendment, impeachment, override of certain vetoes, territorial settlement changes, extraordinary delegation, emergency extension, or other decisions that properly demand a wider coalition. But the archive should reject the habit of importing supermajority logic into ordinary governing simply because consensus sounds morally superior.

A legislature that needs extraordinary coalitions for ordinary business will often end up empowering informal veto brokers rather than protecting a genuine constitutional minority.

### 6. Shared-rule chambers may need double keys, but only where shared rule is real

Second chambers, federal councils, plurinational chambers, and similar bodies should not duplicate lower chambers by using the same majoritarian logic for the same representational task. Their decision rules should instead reflect the constitutional work they uniquely perform.

The archive should therefore accept **double-majority, concurrent-majority, or territorial-consent rules** where the chamber really protects constituent units, linguistic nations, or other co-authors of the settlement. But those double keys should be attached to the relevant class of decision — territorial reallocation, constitutional amendment, competence shifts, shared-rule appointments, or similarly settlement-level questions — not spread across every ordinary policy choice unless the polity knowingly chooses a much slower bargain constitution.

### 7. Committee and scrutiny rights should preserve minorities without giving them a government-wide veto

Decision rules are not only about the final floor vote. They are also about agenda access, committee chairs, amendment rights, inquiry powers, hearing rights, and the standing ability of minorities to force explanation, delay, or review.

The archive should therefore distinguish **minority-protection procedures** from **minority-veto constitutions**. Opposition and legislative minorities should usually receive committee representation, information rights, questioning time, and some triggers for special debate or review. But those rights should not silently become a permanent supermajority rule for the whole legislature unless the constitution openly says so.

### 8. Upper-tier continental and global bodies should treat consensus as a method, not a myth

In continental, plurilateral, and global bodies, consensus can be valuable as a diplomacy-forcing discipline. It can flush out who would be bound, who would pay, and where implementation will fail.

But the archive should reject **fake unanimity**: the practice of treating consensus language as though it proves fully shared political will, or of requiring unanimity for so many decisions that the body becomes either inert or governed through side payments, package deals, and informal coercion.

The honest default at upper tiers is usually:

- **consensus-seeking first** for highly sensitive matters;
- **ordinary or qualified majority** for procedural, budgetary, and ordinary operating decisions where the legal framework permits it;
- **unanimity or near-unanimity reserves** only for accession, withdrawal terms, treaty amendment, core competence transfer, or similarly sovereignty-touching questions;
- and **publication discipline** so observers can tell whether the body actually voted, adopted by consensus without objection, or merely buried visible disagreement.

### 9. Emergency rules should compress time, not secretly rewrite who counts

Across all scopes, emergency decision rules may justify lower notice periods, faster sitting requirements, remote participation, or temporary quorum adjustments. But they should not silently rewrite which institutions matter or which majorities count unless the constitution openly authorizes that change.

The archive should therefore prefer emergency rules that **accelerate the ordinary decision path** rather than replace it with executive self-certification or procedural disappearance of the opposition, the municipalities, or the treaty parties.

### 10. Publish the reserved classes clearly or the reserve will eat the ordinary rule

Every polity eventually claims that this debt instrument, that concession, this emergency extension, or that governance memo is too important for the normal rule. If the reserved classes are not stated clearly, exceptions metastasize.

Ideal governments should therefore publish a short, reviewable map of which decisions use:

- ordinary majority,
- qualified majority,
- concurrent or double majorities,
- unanimity,
- or non-voting consensus adoption.

Without that map, the real constitution drifts into ad hoc threshold shopping.

## Scope defaults table

| Scope | Ordinary rule | Reserved higher-threshold class | What that reserve is for | Must not become |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Micro-local** | rough consensus where possible, simple majority fallback | rare supermajority only for dissolution, asset disposal, or self-restructuring | protect the existence of the steward body itself, not routine upkeep | miniature senate procedure |
| **Municipal** | ordinary majority for ordinary law, budget, and service decisions | charter change, major boundary change, extraordinary debt, strategic asset disposal | separate ordinary city politics from constitutional city redesign | city government by permanent blockage |
| **Metropolitan** | ordinary or modest qualified majority inside a real metro chamber | accession or exit, constitution change, major burden-reallocation, territorial settlement | prevent metro operating power from becoming confederal hostage politics while protecting constituent settlement terms | mayoral-veto congress disguised as metro democracy |
| **Regional / provincial / state** | ordinary majority in the main chamber for ordinary law and confidence | constitutional amendment, territorial powers, certain upper-chamber or shared-rule questions | distinguish ordinary governing from settlement change | supermajority government by default |
| **National** | ordinary majority in the main representative chamber for ordinary law and confidence | constitutional amendment, impeachment, veto override, competence transfer, emergency extension, certain shared-rule matters | force wider coalitions only where the constitutional order itself is being altered or exceptionally stressed | routine governing by minority veto |

| **Global** | consensus-seeking for legitimacy-sensitive matters; explicit majority rules for ordinary procedural or programmatic classes where the charter allows | membership, charter amendment, sovereignty-touching mandates, core financing and peace-security classes where the founding text reserves them | keep the body real but narrow-waisted | world governance by invisible veto |

## Diagnostic questions

1. Is the body using a threshold that matches the class of decision, or simply copying ritual from another scale?
2. Could the institution still perform its ordinary vocation if one organized minority withheld consent for six months?
3. Are supermajorities attached to settlement protection, or have they silently colonized ordinary governing?
4. Where a double key exists, can the archive name the distinct constituent unit or co-authored bargain it protects?
5. In upper-tier bodies, can an outsider tell the difference between a true vote, a consensus adoption, and a hidden side-deal?
6. Do emergency rules accelerate lawful decision-making, or do they rewrite the polity while pretending merely to compress time?

## Guardrails

- Do not constitutionalize unanimity for ordinary governing.
- Do not treat consensus language as proof that dissent disappeared.
- Do not use supermajorities to launder minority rule into the ordinary constitution.
- Do not let directly elected metropolitan or municipal bodies be immobilized by constituent-government vetoes.
- Do not create double-key rules without a real shared-rule or plurinational settlement to protect.
- Do not let emergency procedure become a covert competence transfer.
- Do not hide reserved classes in scattered statutes, bylaws, memoranda, or standing-order folklore.

## Failure modes

- **threshold shopping**: actors relabel decisions to trigger the friendliest vote rule.
- **consensus theater**: leaders declare consensus when dissent was merely displaced offstage.
- **ordinary-business supermajoritarianism**: routine governing becomes impossible without extraordinary coalition bargains.
- **confederal metro trap**: a real metropolitan government is immobilized by standing municipal vetoes.
- **false shared-rule key**: double-majority devices protect no real constituent bargain and merely distort ordinary representation.
- **emergency rewrite**: crisis procedure quietly changes who counts, not just how fast the body may act.
- **procedural opacity**: outsiders cannot tell which threshold was used, why it applied, or whether objections were formally recorded.

## Compression rule for the archive

If the archive can name **who is chosen, for how long, and under what regime form** but cannot also say **what majority or consensus rule usually governs ordinary decisions there, which questions are reserved to higher thresholds, and why those reserves fit that scope**, then the design still has **institutions without an honest vote grammar**.
