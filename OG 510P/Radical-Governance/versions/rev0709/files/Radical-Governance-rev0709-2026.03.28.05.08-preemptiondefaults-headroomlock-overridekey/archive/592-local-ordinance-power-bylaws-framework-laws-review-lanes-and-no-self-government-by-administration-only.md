# 592 — Local ordinance power, bylaws, framework laws, review lanes, and no self-government by administration only

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should give **general-purpose local governments** a real but bounded **local ordinance / by-law power** within framework law — enough to make binding local rules on genuinely local matters, subject to legality review, rights floors, and explicit upper-tier reservations — so self-government does not collapse into mere administration of other people’s rules.

## Why this matters

The archive already has notes on **general competence**, **preemption**, **delegated tasks**, **deconcentration**, **concurrent competences**, and **legislatures by scope**. What it still lacked was one compact doctrine for the specific legal power that often separates real local self-government from administration-only localism: whether a municipality, county, or other general-purpose local authority may adopt binding local regulations, ordinances, or bylaws within law.

That missing layer matters because a local authority may be elected, funded, and publicly visible yet still be constitutionally thin if it can only implement statutes, process applications, and deliver services without any meaningful power to shape local norms. In such a system, councils can debate but not really govern. Every practical territorial rule — public-space use, local nuisance, opening hours where lawful, traffic-calming details, local environmental conduct, market ordering, property maintenance, civic procedures, or neighborhood standards within rights floors — must wait for another level to legislate first. The result is usually delay, over-centralisation, and an unhealthy culture of informal permission.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine for **bounded local norm-making power**. Ideal government should distinguish framework law from local ordinance, preserve common rights and legality, reserve core coercive and fiscal powers to express statutory warrant, and still let local self-government make real rules where local conditions matter. What it should reject is the settlement where local government is praised as autonomous while being treated in practice as a branch office with committee minutes.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Give general-purpose local governments a bounded power to make binding local regulations

A municipality that governs a place should ordinarily be able to adopt **binding local regulations**, ordinances, or bylaws on matters that genuinely fall within local public concern and local competence. The archive should therefore prefer a clear constitutional or statutory footing for local norm-making rather than treating every local rule as a delegated favor from the center.

This does not mean unlimited local sovereignty. It means that real self-government includes some power to translate framework law and local democratic choice into binding local rules.

### 2. Separate framework law from local ordinance instead of collapsing both into one level

The clean settlement is usually not total local freedom and not total central scripting. It is **framework law above, local ordinance below**.

Higher tiers should normally set:

- rights floors,
- criminal and civil-status baselines,
- national equality rules,
- fiscal and borrowing rules,
- competition and market-wide baselines where needed,
- and explicit reservations where genuine uniformity is constitutionally required.

Local governments should then be able to legislate inside that envelope on place-specific implementation, civic order, service standards, public-space use, and other clearly local matters.

### 3. Keep local ordinance power strongest in local-order and place-shaping fields, weaker at the coercive core

Local rulemaking is easiest to justify where local conditions actually differ and where the subject is close to lived place. It is much harder to justify where a rule creates strong coercive effects or threatens equal citizenship.

The archive should therefore prefer broadest local ordinance space in fields such as:

- public-space stewardship,
- local nuisance and amenity rules,
- local market and event ordering within higher law,
- municipal service procedures,
- traffic-calming and street-management details where law permits,
- and local environmental or civic-maintenance rules within statutory bounds.

It should be more cautious where the rule would itself create crimes, detention exposure, major inspection powers, serious sanctions, new taxes, or rights-sensitive status effects without express statutory warrant.

### 4. Distinguish ordinance power from taxation, policing, and criminalization

A polity should not deny local rulemaking merely because it fears local coercion. The cleaner move is to separate powers rather than fuse them.

The archive should therefore distinguish:

- **local ordinance power** to make binding rules within competence,
- **fee or charge power** where law authorizes it,
- **inspection and enforcement power** where law defines it,
- **administrative sanction power** where due-process rules are explicit,
- **tax power** only where a tax handle is lawfully assigned,
- and **criminal-law power** mainly at higher levels unless the constitution expressly provides otherwise.

That separation lets local democracy have lawmaking teeth without pretending every local council should be a miniature sovereign legislature.

### 5. Tie local norm-making to elected bodies, public procedure, and codified publication

Local regulations should not emerge as a fog of mayoral circulars, procurement manuals, platform defaults, or manager memos. The archive should therefore prefer a rule that binding local norms are ordinarily adopted by the elected council or another clearly lawful local legislative body, through public procedure with:

- agenda notice,
- readable draft text,
- meeting transparency,
- published vote or responsible act,
- consolidated codification,
- and durable public access to the current version and prior versions.

Local ordinance power without publication discipline quickly becomes rule by municipal folklore.

### 6. Use legality review and court challenge rather than routine a priori tutelage

Local regulations should remain reviewable for legality, competence, rights compliance, and procedural integrity. But ordinary supervision should not turn every local ordinance into a request for pre-clearance by a ministry, prefect, or governor.

The archive should therefore prefer:

- legality review after adoption or on challenge,
- targeted suspension powers only where the law clearly provides them,
- judicial or quasi-judicial challenge routes,
- and narrow exceptional prior approval only for specifically defined high-risk categories.

What it should reject is the culture where local lawmaking formally exists but survives only after routine upstream blessing.

### 7. Keep local headroom open above common floors unless higher law clearly occupies the field

Local ordinance power is hollow if every field is silently occupied by statute, grant condition, software specification, or supervisory guidance. The archive should therefore connect local norm-making to the archive’s broader doctrines of **explicit preemption** and **local headroom**.

Higher law may set common floors or occupy a field, but it should say so openly. Otherwise local governments should be presumed able to legislate above the floor within competence.

### 8. Treat delegated tasks and own local matters differently

A local government may adopt binding local rules within its own competence. That does not mean it should freely rewrite the terms of a **delegated task** owned by a higher tier.

The archive should therefore insist on a visible distinction:

- in **own local matters**, local ordinance power should be real;
- in **delegated tasks**, any local rulemaking should stay within the delegation charter, framework law, and instruction structure;
- in **deconcentrated administration**, local ordinance language is usually misplaced because the office is state administration, not self-government.

Without this distinction, delegated-state blur returns through the back door.

### 9. Prevent shadow rulemaking by grant contracts, operators, and software defaults

A polity can nullify local ordinance power without openly repealing it. It can do so by moving the real rule into platform settings, concession contracts, operator manuals, insurance conditions, model procurement packages, or centrally designed program terms that local councils are not realistically allowed to vary.

The archive should therefore treat **shadow rulemaking** as constitutionally significant. If the real conduct rule for a local function sits somewhere other than the lawful ordinance lane, the polity should say so openly and justify the override under law.

### 10. Give local governments standing and repair routes when their ordinance sphere is unlawfully narrowed

Local lawmaking power is fragile if higher tiers can quietly deny, suspend, or overwrite it without challenge. The archive should therefore prefer standing or equivalent review routes through which local authorities can contest:

- unlawful supervisory blocking,
- misclassification of own matters as reserved or delegated,
- silent displacement by subordinate guidance,
- and field occupation that exceeds the legal warrant.

This is part of the constitutional defense of multi-level government, not a privilege for institutional vanity.

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse local self-government with mere service administration.
- Do not let binding local rules hide in circulars, software defaults, or operator manuals.
- Do not give broad local criminalisation, detention, or tax powers by vague implication.
- Do not require routine prior approval for ordinary local bylaws unless law narrowly justifies it.
- Do not let framework law become total code-by-detail that leaves no real ordinance space.
- Do not treat delegated-task execution as proof of local legislative autonomy.

## Failure modes

- **self-government by administration only**: local councils can deliver and discuss, but cannot make meaningful binding local rules.
- **ordinance without envelope**: local lawmaking exists on paper, but the legal boundary with higher law is too vague to use safely.
- **framework law as total script**: the higher tier calls its rule a framework but leaves no real local normative space.
- **shadow rulemaking**: the real rule is written through grants, concession documents, software settings, or ministry templates instead of lawful ordinance channels.
- **a priori tutelage by habit**: local bylaws nominally exist, but nothing moves without pre-clearance from another tier.
- **coercion by implication**: fines, inspections, or quasi-criminal consequences are attached to local rules without clear statutory authority.
- **uncodified local law**: residents cannot tell what rule is actually in force, what changed, or who adopted it.

## Practical tests

A polity is handling local ordinance power honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Do general-purpose local governments have a clear legal footing to adopt binding local regulations within competence?
2. Is the boundary between framework law and local ordinance legible?
3. Are taxation, criminalization, major sanctions, and strong coercive powers separately and expressly authorized?
4. Are binding local norms adopted through elected local bodies or other clearly lawful democratic procedures rather than through administrative folklore?
5. Are local ordinances published, codified, searchable, and versioned?
6. Is ordinary review legality-based and challenge-ready rather than dependent on routine pre-clearance?
7. Does local headroom remain open above common floors unless higher law explicitly occupies the field?
8. Is local ordinance power distinguished from rulemaking inside delegated tasks or deconcentrated administration?
9. Can local authorities challenge unlawful narrowing of their lawful ordinance sphere?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity says a municipality governs itself but does not let it adopt **binding local rules within law, through public local procedure, with real headroom above common floors and reviewable legality instead of routine tutelage**, then the polity has not built serious local self-government. It has built **administration-only localism with ceremonial democracy attached**.
