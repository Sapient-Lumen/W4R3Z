# 747 — Register-deconsolidation and standalone-restoration packets for absorbed tombstones: reversible grouping, object-specific re-expansion, and no permanent flattening-by-catalog convenience

## One-line thesis

Once the archive can absorb standalone tombstones into grouped historical registers (`745`) and later move those grouped registers across homes or catalogues (`746`), it should also keep one compact **register-deconsolidation and standalone-restoration packet**: one bounded proof object for pulling one absorbed dead-object entry back out into its own standalone memorial surface when grouping proves too lossy, disputed, high-traffic, symbolically weighty, or operationally confusing, so the polity does not mistake one successful consolidation step for permission to keep every dead authority permanently flattened inside a catalogue.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a credible memory grammar for dead public objects.

It can retire a body, scope lane, or closeout shell without constitutional ghosting (`642`, `740`). It can leave one honest tombstone (`741`), reroute it when successor facts drift (`742`), correct it when the public story is materially wrong (`743`), and keep it maintained over time with named stewardship and health checks (`744`). It can absorb a standalone tombstone into a grouped historical register when grouping genuinely improves clarity rather than destroying memory (`745`). And it can later move that grouped register across catalogue homes, domains, or platforms without breaking individually addressable identity (`746`).

What it still lacked was the smallest object for the next ordinary but under-specified move: **what happens when grouping stops being the honest shape**.

That gap matters because consolidation is rarely final in any principled sense. A grouped historical register may begin as an elegant answer to dead-page sprawl and later become too coarse. One absorbed entry may attract heavy legacy traffic after litigation, scandal, compensation work, or renewed public inquiry. A dead body may become symbolically important enough that one line in a catalogue no longer does the civic job. A correction chain may grow complicated enough that the grouped entry can no longer carry the explanatory burden cleanly. A later reorganisation may make the grouped register accurate for most entries but misleading for one specific object whose successor map, records home, archive home, or challenge lane becomes unusually elaborate. Or a search team may discover that the catalogue catches grouped browsing well but fails badly for the actual alias patterns, deep links, or report citations people use when looking for one historically important dead authority.

Without one compact restoration packet, maintainers face two bad choices. They either keep the object buried in the grouped register because consolidation once seemed wise, or they improvise a new standalone page with no rule for what should be carried back out, which URL should become canonical again, how the grouped entry should now behave, or how to preserve the fact that the object spent a real period honestly grouped rather than invisibly deleted and reborn. In both cases, the public record becomes harder to trust. Grouping starts to look irreversible even when it is no longer the truthful shape. Or, worse, re-expansion happens by ad hoc helpdesk edits that quietly rewrite the memorial history and lose the very provenance that made grouping defensible in the first place.

The archive therefore needs one compact packet for **register deconsolidation and standalone restoration**. This packet should not make every grouped memorial temporary by default. But it should force the polity to prove that when one absorbed object needs to come back out into its own page, card, dossier, or memorial route, the restoration is explicit, the object’s historical identity stays continuous, the grouped register keeps an honest trace of the grouped phase, aliases and old paths still resolve, and the restored surface carries forward the routing and correction burden that grouping can no longer bear alone. Without that packet, the archive still permits **permanent flattening by catalogue convenience**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Require one explicit restoration packet before re-expanding an absorbed entry into a standalone memorial surface

A dead authority should not silently reappear as a standalone page just because the grouped register became inconvenient.

If the polity wants to pull one absorbed tombstone back out of a grouped historical register, it should open one explicit packet saying:

- which absorbed entry is being restored;
- from which grouped register, directory, or catalogue it is being pulled;
- what standalone surface will now exist again;
- why grouping is no longer honest enough for this object;
- and which old and grouped URLs, aliases, identifiers, and correction records must continue to resolve after restoration.

Restoration should be a named act, not a side effect of editing a catalogue row.

### 2. Distinguish restoration from simple edit, rerouting, migration, duplication, and accidental fork

A standalone-restoration packet should classify the move correctly.

At minimum it should distinguish:

- **full re-expansion** — the absorbed object regains one standalone canonical memorial surface;
- **partial re-expansion** — the grouped entry remains canonical, but one deeper standalone dossier or correction surface is restored for this object;
- **temporary escalation page** — one standalone page exists for a bounded review, dispute, or inquiry period while the grouped entry remains the main route;
- **mistaken duplicate risk** — the proposed restoration would create two live memorial pages with no clear canonical relation;
- **mistaken fork risk** — the proposed restoration would split one historical object into several pages without a lawful object distinction.

Without typed classes, re-expansion can become accidental duplication disguised as cleanup.

### 3. Preserve one continuous object identity across standalone, absorbed, migrated, and restored phases

The same dead object should remain recognisable as the same dead object throughout every memorial shape.

The packet should therefore preserve continuity by keeping visible:

- the original standalone identity where one existed;
- the grouped-register entry identity used during absorption;
- any later migrated identifier from register rehousing (`746`);
- and the restored standalone identifier now becoming canonical again.

Restoration is not a new birth. It is the next public shape of the same memorial object.

### 4. State clearly why grouping failed *for this object* rather than treating re-expansion as a generic preference flip

The packet should show why one absorbed entry can no longer be honestly represented only as part of the grouped surface.

Typical triggers include:

- unusually high legacy traffic or citation volume;
- materially complex successor or split-successor routing;
- a long or disputed correction chain;
- strong symbolic, legal, or public-interest salience;
- heavy alias or deep-link demand that a grouped register serves poorly;
- or a restoration need created by later catalogue migration, merger, or steward change.

If the polity cannot say why this object needs standalone treatment again, it is probably not ready to restore it.

### 5. Carry forward the full memorial burden into the restored surface rather than restoring only a thin shell

A restored standalone page should not be thinner than the grouped entry it replaces.

The restored surface should carry forward, for the specific object:

- the plain death statement and effective date;
- successor or no-successor facts;
- records home;
- archive home;
- bounded legacy-question lane;
- correction lineage;
- and the fact that the object spent a defined period absorbed into a grouped historical register.

Restoration should add clarity, not create one new empty shell that simply points users back into the old grouped maze.

### 6. Keep the grouped register honest after restoration by leaving a live trace rather than pretending the entry was never grouped

When an entry is restored, the grouped register should not simply delete all evidence that it ever held the object.

The grouped surface should keep one honest trace stating:

- that the object was formerly absorbed here;
- the period during which it was grouped;
- the reason it was restored to standalone form if that reason is public-facing;
- and the exact route to the restored canonical surface.

Otherwise the archive rewrites grouped history instead of preserving it.

### 7. Rewire old standalone, grouped, and migrated lookup paths into one explicit canonical map

Restoration is a routing event as much as an editorial one.

The packet should therefore prove how the polity will handle:

- the original pre-absorption standalone URL where one existed;
- the grouped-register entry URL;
- any later migrated register-home URL (`746`);
- common aliases, acronyms, and identifier forms;
- and major document, report, or citation paths that users still realistically hit.

Users should not have to know which memorial phase an authority is currently in before they can find it.

### 8. Forbid restoration from becoming a shadow resurrection or a conflict between historical surfaces

A restored standalone tombstone is still a memorial object, not a live authority.

The packet should therefore block restoration designs that:

- make the restored page look like a reopened live service front door;
- duplicate current service intake already routed elsewhere;
- replace the actual records or archive home with one convenience page;
- or leave the grouped register and restored surface both sounding canonical without a visible relation.

Re-expansion should improve clarity, not create a two-front historical conflict.

### 9. Use bounded dual-running when moving canonical status back from the grouped register to the restored standalone page

Some restorations are simple. Others involve high traffic, complex alias maps, or external citation habits.

Where the risk is material, the packet should allow a short overlap with:

- one declared start and end for the overlap;
- one visible statement of which surface is canonical during each phase;
- monitoring for wrong-door traffic and alias misses;
- and one explicit exit decision when the grouped entry becomes pointer-only rather than co-equal.

Dual-running should be a safety lane, not permanent canonical ambiguity.

### 10. Preserve a path back to re-absorption, re-migration, or revised grouping if restoration later proves mistaken or temporary

Restoration should be reversible too.

The packet should therefore name:

- the steward who can review whether the restored standalone page remains justified;
- the triggers for re-absorption or partial re-absorption;
- and the requirement that later regrouping preserve the fact of this restoration interval.

The archive should not replace one irreversible grouping ratchet with an irreversible de-grouping ratchet.

## Guardrails

- Do not let a historically important dead object stay buried in a grouped register merely because consolidation once seemed elegant.
- Do not restore a standalone surface without saying whether the move is full re-expansion, partial re-expansion, temporary escalation, duplicate risk, or fork risk.
- Do not treat restoration as a new object or fresh memorial birth when it is really the same dead authority in a new public shape.
- Do not restore a thin shell that drops successor, records, archive, correction, or legacy-question routing now that the object is standalone again.
- Do not erase the grouped phase from the historical record merely because the object has been restored.
- Do not leave old standalone paths, grouped-entry paths, and migrated register-home paths to compete without one visible canonical map.
- Do not let restoration drift into live-service resurrection, faux record-system behavior, or coequal historical front doors.
- Do not make re-expansion irreversible when later evidence shows the grouped form was still better or that only a partial standalone dossier was justified.

## Failure modes

- **permanent flattening by convenience**: a dead object remains trapped in a grouped register long after grouping stopped being honest because nobody opened a formal restoration lane.
- **rebirth fiction**: a restored standalone page is presented as if it were a wholly new memorial object, erasing the absorbed phase and breaking provenance.
- **double-canonical fog**: the grouped entry and restored standalone page both appear authoritative, leaving users unable to tell which route controls.
- **restoration without burden**: a standalone page returns but carries only a title and a redirect, while the real historical facts remain buried in the grouped register.
- **alias fracture on re-expansion**: some users still arrive through grouped-register or migrated-register paths that no longer resolve cleanly once the standalone page returns.
- **endless yo-yo memorial design**: grouped and standalone forms alternate without explicit criteria, leaving dead-object routing shaped by platform taste rather than public truth.

## Practical tests

A register-deconsolidation and standalone-restoration packet is honest when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is the restoration a named act rather than an ad hoc catalogue edit?
2. Does it classify whether the move is full re-expansion, partial re-expansion, temporary escalation, duplicate risk, or fork risk?
3. Does the same dead object keep one continuous identity across standalone, absorbed, migrated, and restored phases?
4. Does the packet explain why grouping stopped being honest *for this object*?
5. Does the restored standalone surface carry forward the full death, successor, records, archive, legacy-question, and correction burden?
6. Does the grouped register keep an honest trace of the absorbed interval and the route to the restored page?
7. Are original, grouped, and migrated lookup paths rewired into one explicit canonical map?
8. Is the restored page still visibly historical rather than a shadow live front door or faux record system?
9. Where traffic is material, is there a bounded overlap with monitoring and one explicit exit decision?
10. Is there a credible review lane for later re-absorption, partial re-absorption, or regrouping if restoration proves mistaken?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can absorb standalone memorial pages into grouped historical registers (`745`) and later move those grouped registers across homes or catalogues (`746`), it should also be able to show one compact packet for **when one absorbed object must be restored to its own standalone memorial surface, how the grouped phase remains part of the object’s public lineage, how old and grouped lookup paths are rewired into one canonical map, how the restored page carries the routing and correction burden grouping can no longer hold, when re-expansion is blocked, and how later regrouping remains reversible**. Otherwise it still permits **permanent flattening by catalogue convenience**.
