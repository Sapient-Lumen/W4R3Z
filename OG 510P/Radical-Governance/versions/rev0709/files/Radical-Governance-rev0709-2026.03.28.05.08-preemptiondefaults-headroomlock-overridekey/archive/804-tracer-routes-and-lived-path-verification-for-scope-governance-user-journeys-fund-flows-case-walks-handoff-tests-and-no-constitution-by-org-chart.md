# 804 — Tracer routes and lived-path verification for scope governance: user journeys, fund flows, case walks, handoff tests, and no constitution by org chart

## One-line thesis

The archive should pair its assessment-mission, finding-register, management-response, and close-out notes with one compact **tracer-route grammar**: **every serious scope review should follow a small set of real or lawfully simulated paths — case, payment, referral, complaint, permit, incident, data handoff, or handback — across institutions, clocks, and records, so governments are not judged only by statutes, org charts, or curated meetings instead of by the routes people and work actually travel**.

## Why this matters

The archive can now say **what the ideal scope stack is** (`790`), **how to benchmark a real arrangement against it** (`791`), **how to stress-test it** (`792`), **when those checks recur** (`793`), **how a polity can move toward a better stack** (`794`), **what proof should stay public** (`795`), **how persistent failure can justify intervention** (`796`), **who may challenge those judgments** (`797`), **which peer class actually counts before scoring begins** (`798`), **what must happen to temporary constitutional objects once “temporary” has to mean something** (`799`), **how a serious assessment mission should be run** (`800`), **how the resulting judgment should be stated lane by lane** (`801`), **how the host must answer those findings with owned repair work** (`802`), and **how claimed fixes are later verified, closed, or reopened** (`803`).

What the archive still lacked was one compact method for a quieter but decisive question:

**how does a reviewer actually follow one real path through the stack rather than infer the whole constitution from the chart on the wall?**

That gap matters because multilevel-governance systems often look coherent in diagrams while failing along lived routes. A municipality says it runs a one-stop front door, but the person is still bounced from city counter to regional office to national portal and back again. A ministry says grants reach local authorities on time, but the money still stalls in an intermediate treasury or arrives with unusable strings. A metropolitan body is supposed to coordinate transport, flood control, or housing placements, but one real incident reveals that the handoff is still telephone folklore. A temporary intervention is said to be winding down, but a live walk of payroll rights, signature authority, data access, and procurement control shows that the host authority still cannot act without the custodian. Another review marks a complaint route “fixed” because a protocol exists, while one traced case still loops between ombuds desk, regulator, municipality, and tribunal without ownership.

Official practice already points toward the missing discipline. WHO’s Service Availability and Readiness Assessment is built around **tracer indicators** and direct facility assessment rather than self-description alone. World Bank PETS and QSDS traditions exist precisely to trace whether money, staff effort, and frontline service realities match the official chain instead of trusting aggregate allocations. OECD work on better public services points to methods such as **customer safaris, mystery shopping, and process mapping** to reveal where a whole route fails even when individual touchpoints look acceptable. The archive should compress that lesson into one note: **serious scope assessment needs traced paths, not only institutional diagrams**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate the tracer route from the benchmark card and the mission as a whole

A benchmark card says **what good looks like**.

An assessment mission says **how a review is run**.

A tracer route says **which real path is followed through the stack to test whether the arrangement actually works in motion**.

The archive should not let those collapse into one another. Without tracer routes, a mission can still remain too conference-room-bound.

### 2. Choose tracer classes before the review starts

The archive should expect the mission header to declare which **route classes** will be traced.

Typical classes include:

- **user journey** — one resident, patient, claimant, student, traveler, voter, permit applicant, or family route through multiple bodies,
- **fund flow** — one grant, reimbursement, equalization transfer, emergency allocation, or contractor payment route,
- **authority path** — one permit, enforcement action, procurement approval, signature chain, or delegated-task route,
- **complaint and remedy path** — one grievance, ombuds case, appeal, or review route across levels,
- **data handoff** — one record, identifier, notification, referral, or eligibility signal crossing institutions,
- **incident route** — one outage, flood, outbreak, or public-safety escalation path,
- **temporary-object path** — one intervention, bridge authority, special-measures object, or handback sequence.

If those classes are not chosen up front, route tracing becomes anecdotal and prestige-picked. Later notes should now also treat `805` as the place to go once the missing problem is not which steps a chosen route contains, but how the route classes, ordinary cases, and negative cases were selected into the review frame at all.

### 3. Follow boundaries, not just offices

A useful tracer route should normally cross at least one real **boundary** that might fail, such as:

- municipal to metropolitan,
- municipal to regional,
- lower tier to national guarantor,
- public body to contracted operator,
- front office to back office,
- ordinary route to emergency route,
- or custodian to handback host.

The point is not to prove that one office can process its own paperwork. It is to see whether the stack works where responsibility actually changes hands.

### 4. Trace clocks, records, and required repeats at each step

A route is not only a map. It is a timed sequence.

For each step, the tracer should note at least:

When later notes need the stricter question of how each traced screenshot, case extract, observation note, or ledger excerpt gets a stable exhibit ID and handling status, they should now also treat `806` as the place to go.

- which body owned the step,
- what record or evidence was required,
- what clock started or stopped,
- whether the person or downstream body had to repeat information,
- and what happened if the step failed.

This keeps route tracing from collapsing into impressionistic site-visit notes.

### 5. Mix documentary tracing with live verification

A tracer route should usually combine two kinds of proof:

- **documentary trace** — laws, workflow charts, system records, receipts, transfer notices, timestamps, bank or treasury records, queue logs, or case notes,
- **live or simulated walk** — site visit, observed handoff, ordinary-user route test, controlled file walk, or lawfully authorized mystery-shopping style check where proportional and appropriate.

The archive should reject the anti-pattern where a route is considered proven because someone showed a flowchart.

### 6. Preserve where the route breaks, loops, or silently mutates

Tracer routes are valuable because they expose pathologies that broad metrics often hide.

The route log should therefore preserve whether the traced path encountered:

- a dead handoff,
- duplicate intake,
- contradictory instructions,
- queue reset,
- channel switch,
- repeated identity or eligibility proof,
- a discretionary workaround,
- an invisible contractor dependency,
- an escalation with no owner,
- or a temporary rule quietly acting as the permanent route.

The archive should prefer one honest break map to ten pages of general praise.

### 7. Keep the sample small but typed and explain why it is enough

The archive should not turn route tracing into endless ethnography.

A small typed set is usually enough if it is chosen honestly and against a declared sampling frame. A mission might trace, for example:

- one ordinary successful route,
- one failed or delayed route,
- one cross-level fund flow,
- one complaint or appeal route,
- and one temporary-object or handback route if the review touches intervention or transition.

What matters is not the volume of traces but whether the chosen routes test the main constitutional seams.

### 8. Treat synthetic or covert route testing as exceptional and disciplined

Some routes can be followed using real case files or overt walkthroughs. Others may require a lawfully simulated user route or test-purchase style method.

Where that is used, the archive should insist on:

- legal authority,
- necessity and proportionality,
- scope limits,
- trained reviewers,
- privacy discipline,
- safe handling of collateral data,
- and a clear record of what the simulation can and cannot prove.

The point is not investigative romance. It is honest testing where declared channels behave differently in practice than on paper.

### 9. Route findings into the right downstream object

A traced path is not a freestanding anecdote. It should feed the existing stack.

- Use `800` to decide which tracer classes belong in the mission.
- Use `801` to state what each traced path actually showed and with what confidence.
- Use `802` to assign owners, dependencies, and closure proof when a traced path reveals a defect.
- Use `803` when the later question becomes whether the repaired path now genuinely works.
- Use `795` to publish a public-safe route summary where outward proof matters.

This keeps the note compact rather than turning it into a parallel review system.

### 10. Reuse tracer routes for close-out and handback verification

A route that exposed the defect is often the best route to retest the fix.

If a municipality once required people to repeat the same documents across three levels, the close-out check should often retrace that same route after repair. If a handback was said to return real authority to the host government, the verifier should often retrace the exact signature, payroll, procurement, and data-access path that previously failed.

The archive should prefer **same-path retest** over closure by broad reassurance.

### 11. Publish route summaries without exposing private case detail

`795` should not dump full case files. But it can usually publish a public-safe summary of:

- the tracer class,
- the number of steps,
- the principal handoff points,
- the main break or success condition,
- the repeated-burden count,
- and the resulting repair or closure posture.

That is enough for outsiders to see whether the arrangement was tested in motion.

### 12. Prefer traced friction over narrated harmony

Many scope arrangements survive by goodwill, personal contacts, or unwritten exceptions. Route tracing will sometimes make a functioning-looking stack look worse. That is a feature, not a defect.

The archive should bias toward the traced path over the narrated harmony when the two conflict.

## Minimum fields for a tracer-route register

| Field | What it tells later actors | Minimum form | Typical failure if missing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tracer class** | what kind of path was followed | user / fund / authority / complaint / data / incident / handback | one-off anecdote with no reusable type |
| **Entry point** | where the route began | named body, channel, or trigger | the path cannot be reproduced |
| **Territorial unit and scope crossing** | which real boundary the route tested | one short scope note | review never tests a true seam |
| **Steps and handoffs** | how many stages the route passed through | short ordered list | break points disappear into prose |
| **Evidence examined** | what records or observations support the trace | packet id or short list | the route becomes hearsay |
| **Clock and repeat burden** | what delays or repeated asks appeared | one short note | friction remains invisible |
| **Break or success condition** | what the path ultimately proved | one short finding | route trace produces no usable judgment |
| **Next use** | where the result goes next | finding / repair / retest / handback review | route trace becomes a dead artifact |

## Use rule for the archive

When later notes need to say not just **that a government by scope looks coherent on paper**, but **that a real path through it has been followed and tested**, the archive should usually do these things in order:

1. **use `798` to choose the right territorial unit and comparator class before route tracing begins**,
2. **use `800` to declare which tracer classes belong in the mission and why**,
3. **use `805` when the missing task is to explain how those route classes, case states, sites, or time slices were selected into the sample and what claims that sample can support**,
4. **use this note to log the path, handoffs, clocks, repeat burdens, and break points**,
5. **use `801` to state the resulting lane finding with confidence and severity**,
6. **use `802` to assign repair work where the trace revealed a defect**,
7. **use `803` to retrace the same path where the question becomes whether the fix or handback now really holds**,
8. **use `795` to publish a public-safe route summary when outward proof matters**.

The archive should not let serious multilevel review end in **constitution by org chart**.

## Guardrails

- Do not let route tracing become prestige-case tourism.
- Do not infer a live path only from process maps or interviews.
- Do not hide repeated-document burdens just because each office behaved “correctly” in isolation.
- Do not treat a temporary workaround as the ordinary lawful route.
- Do not use covert or synthetic route testing without legal basis, necessity, and proportionality.
- Do not collect private case detail beyond what the review actually needs.
- Do not publish route summaries so detailed that they expose individuals unnecessarily.

## Failure modes

- **org-chart constitutionalism** — reviewers infer functionality from formal structure and never follow a real path.
- **handoff theater** — every office appears competent alone, but no cross-tier route actually lands.
- **repeat-burden invisibility** — the state counts completed steps while the person repeats identity, facts, or proof at every seam.
- **cash-path fiction** — funds are allocated on paper but the live transfer route still fails the frontline.
- **temporary-route capture** — an emergency, intervention, or workaround channel quietly becomes the real constitution.
- **route anecdotalism** — one vivid case is retold without typed route class, reproducibility, or evidence discipline.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Did the review declare its tracer classes before beginning fieldwork?
2. Does each traced path cross at least one real boundary where responsibility can fail?
3. Can a reader see what record, clock, or repeat burden attached to each major step?
4. Where the path allegedly works, was there live or documentary proof beyond interview claims?
5. If the path failed, is the break point typed sharply enough to route into finding, repair, retest, handback, or contestability?
6. If the path was later repaired, was the same path or an equivalent one retraced rather than merely re-described?

## Compression rule

If the archive wants to say that a scope arrangement **actually works across tiers**, it should be able to show one short tracer-route register naming the route class, entry point, crossed boundary, steps, evidence examined, clock or repeat burden, break or success condition, and next use in the review stack. If it cannot, the archive is still too close to **constitution by org chart**.
