# 782 — Administrative guidance, circulars, and soft law necessity tests for ideal governments: publicity, legal-status clarity, consultable updates, and no hidden law by guidance

## One-line thesis

Ideal governments should treat **administrative guidance, circulars, manuals, FAQs, enforcement policies, and advisory opinions as a compliance-support and interpretation layer rather than as a covert lawmaking lane**: guidance should be typed by legal status, publicly accessible when it matters to outsiders, consistent with enacted law, versioned and dated, consultable when it materially reshapes burdens, and reviewable when it hardens into de facto law.

## Why this matters

The archive already says that legislatures should not launder lawmaking through delegated legislation (`758`), that regulators should publish priorities and ordinary procedures (`545`), that permit systems should make criteria, clocks, and statuses legible (`573`), and that administrative self-correction should distinguish interpretation from true reopening (`781`). What it still lacked as one compact operating rule was the layer that sits between statute and frontline action:

**what exactly is administrative guidance allowed to do?**

Every real state runs on guidance. Inspectors carry manuals. ministries issue circulars. licensing offices publish FAQs. agencies write compliance handbooks, safe-harbour examples, advisory letters, decision trees, templates, and enforcement policies. Some of this is good administration: it makes expectations understandable, reduces arbitrary variation among officials, and helps people comply without hiring a litigator. But without constitutional discipline the same layer becomes a shadow rulebook. The binding norm remains in theory one thing while the practical norm becomes whatever the latest memo, portal text, internal checklist, or unpublished interpretation says.

That is where hidden law appears. Citizens are sanctioned for breaching “guidance” that was never properly published. Inspectors treat internal manuals as if they were binding law. Agencies change settled practical requirements through FAQs without admitting they have changed the burden. Public-facing guidance becomes stale while internal practice moves on. Or, in the other direction, officials refuse to give any usable guidance at all and preserve discretion by forcing everyone to guess what compliance will mean in practice.

Ideal-government design therefore needs a doctrine of **administrative-guidance necessity**. The point is not to abolish guidance. It is to type it honestly. Some guidance is interpretive explanation. Some is internal management instruction. Some is announced enforcement priority. Some is an advisory-opinion lane on which reliance may justifiably be placed. The live question is whether the polity can show **which type of instrument is in play, what legal status it has, who issued it, whether outsiders can see it, whether it changed practical burdens, whether it was consulted on, how it relates to enacted law, whether old versions remain citable, and what review route exists if guidance is being used as hidden law**.

## Necessity table

| Necessity test | What counts as proof | Honest design consequence | Default answer if proof is weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Need to explain compliance expectations in usable terms** | ordinary people or small organisations cannot infer practical compliance from enacted text alone | issue public plain-language guidance tied to the legal source | leave practical expectations to informal brokerage |
| **Need to harmonise frontline discretion across offices** | materially similar cases are treated differently by office, inspector, or region | publish structured guidance and internal consistency tools, while preserving case-level judgment | let each official reinvent enforcement and interpretation |
| **Need to preserve the line between law and guidance** | agencies are tempted to create new obligations through manuals, FAQs, or circulars | state legal status clearly and prohibit guidance from creating binding duties beyond law | let soft law quietly replace enacted law |
| **Need to make consequential guidance publicly contestable** | updated guidance materially alters burdens, eligibility, evidence demands, or enforcement posture | publish, date, archive, and where appropriate consult on consequential changes | change practical obligations through invisible document drift |
| **Need for fair warning before sanctions or adverse decisions** | enforcement depends on technical expectations or evolving interpretation | align sanctions and adverse action with published rules plus visible guidance and transition discipline | punish first and explain later |
| **Need for reliance-sensitive advisory channels** | actors need prospective clarification before acting | offer typed advisory opinions, no-action letters, or helpdesks with stated reliance effect | force everyone to learn by violation or insider contact |
| **Need to stop internal instructions from governing the public in secret** | internal checklists or manuals effectively decide rights or obligations | disclose outward-relevant portions or restate them publicly in external guidance | treat secrecy as a management convenience |
| **Need to surface de facto lawmaking for review** | courts, ombuds, or legislatures can see that guidance is effectively binding outcomes | preserve challenge routes and audit trails for guidance use | deny review because the instrument is “non-binding” on paper |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Type guidance instruments explicitly by legal status and function

The system should distinguish at least: public interpretive guidance, internal staff instruction, enforcement policy, advisory opinion or advance ruling, technical manual, and educational material. Different publication, reliance, and review consequences attach to each.

### 2. Publish all outward-relevant guidance in one searchable place

If a document can materially affect what outsiders must submit, prove, change, avoid, or expect, it should be publicly accessible in a durable searchable register. The public should not have to guess whether the real rule lives in a helpdesk script, a PDF on an obscure subpage, or an internal circular.

### 3. State what guidance can and cannot do

Each guidance document should identify its legal source, its audience, its status, and its limits. It may explain the agency’s current interpretation or enforcement posture. It may not create new binding duties, narrow statutory rights, or add evidence requirements that the law does not support.

### 4. Treat consequential guidance updates as governance events, not routine website edits

Where guidance materially changes eligibility understanding, filing expectations, inspection practice, safe-harbour logic, or enforcement posture, the change should be versioned, dated, archived, and in significant cases publicly consulted on or at least accompanied by reasons.

### 5. Preserve fair-warning discipline between guidance and enforcement

The harsher the consequence, the stronger the warning duty. Where the practical meaning of a rule depends heavily on technical expectations, new enforcement posture should not arrive first through sanction. Use publication, transition periods, grace windows, or advisory outreach before punitive escalation unless immediate danger justifies faster action.

### 6. Separate internal management instructions from outward-facing law without letting the former secretly govern the latter

Internal guidance can structure workflows, allocation, triage, and consistency review. But if internal criteria are effectively deciding the public’s entitlements, burdens, or chances of sanction, the relevant logic must either be grounded in law and publicly restated or treated as reviewable hidden law.

### 7. Offer typed advisory channels with stated reliance effects

For some domains the state should provide advance clarification: advisory opinions, interpretive letters, pre-application meetings, or no-action positions. The design should say whether reliance is full, rebuttable, time-limited, fact-specific, or merely informative. An advice lane without a declared reliance effect is half-governance.

### 8. Keep superseded guidance citable and visible

Old versions should remain archived with effective dates so courts, auditors, ombuds, and affected people can tell what the public was told at the relevant time. A polity that cannot show yesterday’s guidance cannot honestly adjudicate fair-warning disputes today.

### 9. Connect guidance to review, scrutiny, and audit

Legislatures, courts, ombuds, inspectors-general, and auditors should be able to ask which guidance was applied, by whom, in which version, and with what legal status. “It was only guidance” should not end the inquiry when outcomes clearly depended on it.

### 10. Use guidance to reduce arbitrary variation, not to eliminate judgment or participation

Good guidance supports consistency, but it should still leave room for case-specific reasons, proportionality, waivers where law allows, and public participation where consequences are broad. A good system avoids both personalized improvisation and rule-by-checklist absolutism.

## Guardrails

- Do not create new public obligations through FAQs, manuals, or circulars.
- Do not sanction people for breaching unpublished, inaccessible, or stale guidance.
- Do not hide outward-relevant criteria inside internal documents and call them management only.
- Do not let agencies change practical burdens through quiet portal edits with no archive trail.
- Do not treat advisory opinions as infinitely binding when facts were incomplete, law changed, or danger was obvious.
- Do not pretend that a practically binding instrument is immune from review because it is formally labelled “non-binding”.

## Failure modes

- **hidden law by guidance**: the practical rulebook lives in memos and FAQs rather than in publicly accountable law.
- **fair-warning collapse**: people first learn the agency’s actual interpretation through sanction or refusal.
- **split-brain administration**: public guidance says one thing, internal practice another.
- **version amnesia**: the state cannot show which guidance applied at the time of action.
- **advice without reliance**: helpdesks and advisory letters exist, but nobody knows whether acting on them is safe.
- **checklist absolutism**: guidance meant to improve consistency ossifies into an unreasoned algorithm of administration.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can tell you **that agencies issue guidance, manuals, circulars, FAQs, or advisory opinions** but cannot also show **which instruments are public versus internal, what legal status each has, whether consequential changes are versioned and archived, how guidance is kept within enacted law, when consultation or transition is required, what reliance effect advisory channels carry, and how courts or ombuds can challenge de facto lawmaking**, then it still has **administrative help text without a soft-law constitution**.
