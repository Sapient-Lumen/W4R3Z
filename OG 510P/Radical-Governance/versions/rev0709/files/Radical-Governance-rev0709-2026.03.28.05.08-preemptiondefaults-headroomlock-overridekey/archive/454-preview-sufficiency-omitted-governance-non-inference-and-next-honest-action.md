# 454 — Preview sufficiency, omitted-governance non-inference, and next honest action

## One-line thesis

A snippet, card, toast, queue row, portal tile, or case preview for consequential public AI should state what the preview is sufficient for, what governance-bearing facts are omitted, which tempting inferences are refused, and what the next honest action is.

## Why this matters

Consequential public-AI systems increasingly summarize themselves before they fully explain themselves. A person sees a queue row, a dashboard card, a system-result snippet, a notice excerpt, a case preview, a registry tile, or an appeal status line. That preview is often useful. It can help recognition, rough routing, prioritization, or triage. But it is frequently not enough to support stronger judgments about approval status, authority basis, rights impact, scope, expiry, review posture, public availability, evidentiary weight, or whether a decision route is safe to trust.

When those omitted facts stay invisible, users backfill them from habit. A row that says "approved" may only mean recommended. A card that looks complete may still omit the review lane. A queue preview that names a case may omit whether a human has confirmed the governing basis. A supplier summary may look institutionally adopted when it is only attached evidence. A person who sees the right label may infer the wrong rights story.

The archive should therefore force an explicit preview-sufficiency discipline. Small surfaces may be useful without pretending to be decisive. They should say what they showed, what they omitted, what they are enough for, what they are not enough for, and what action remains honest next.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish recognition sufficiency from governance sufficiency

A preview may be enough for:

- recognizing the right case, program, model, service, or document family,
- identifying which queue or office should look next,
- noticing a mismatch that blocks immediate continuation,
- or selecting a deeper review path.

That does not mean it is enough for:

- rights-affecting action,
- reliance on a live approval state,
- adoption of a supplier or generated claim,
- public explanation of basis,
- or closure of review.

The archive should refuse the common collapse from "recognizable" to "governed."

### 2. Make omitted governance-bearing fields visible

A preview should not look complete merely because its omissions are quiet. When omitted facts remain load-bearing, the surface should say so. Relevant omitted classes include:

- approval or release state,
- authority basis,
- appeal or escalation route,
- expiry or freshness state,
- audience or disclosure class,
- evidentiary status,
- authorship or adoption class,
- and whether the surface is advisory, binding, or still under review.

The archive should prefer visible incompleteness to silent overclaim.

### 3. State what unsafe inference is being refused

A good preview discipline does not merely omit a field; it explains the tempting mistake being blocked. Useful refusal forms include:

- this preview identifies the item but does not prove it is approved,
- this status cue shows request progress but not final determination,
- this summary reflects supplier language and not institutional adoption,
- this excerpt is enough for routing but not for rights explanation,
- this note shows the latest activity but not the currently governing basis.

The point is to block backfilled certainty.

### 4. Name the next honest action

A preview that blocks stronger inference should still help the user proceed. It should identify the next honest action, such as:

- open the full review packet,
- inspect the canonical disclosure head,
- request a human review lane,
- wait for signed approval,
- fetch the case witness,
- or use the public explanation page instead of the queue row.

A preview that refuses inference but gives no route forward is still weak governance.

### 5. Preserve preview receipts when previews shaped consequential conduct

If a preview influenced routing, triage, notification, timing, or user reliance in a consequential process, the archive should preserve what the preview showed and what it explicitly refused to claim. This helps later review answer whether the system induced false certainty or preserved honest boundaries.

### 6. Keep preview truth aligned with deeper surfaces

The preview should resolve cleanly into the full surface it depends on. A row that says "review requested" should open to a surface that explains what review means here. A tile that labels a system should resolve to the canonical head or current packet, not to a stale or ambiguous mirror. Preview honesty depends on a trustworthy deeper layer.

### 7. Treat completeness drift as a governance defect

If preview fields silently widen or shrink without updating what they are sufficient for, the archive should treat that as a real change. A summary surface can become more misleading without changing its visible copy. Preview sufficiency therefore belongs to change control and review, not only to product polish.

## Guardrails

- Do not let a recognizable preview impersonate a complete governance record.
- Do not hide omitted approval, authority, or rights-bearing fields behind tidy cards.
- Do not allow supplier, generated, or partial text to inherit institutional adoption by layout alone.
- Do not refuse inference without naming the next honest action.
- Do not let preview semantics drift independently from the deeper surface they depend on.

## Failure modes

- **recognition-as-approval**: users infer final authority from a familiar label or badge.
- **quiet omission**: critical governance fields are missing but the surface still looks complete.
- **summary overclaim**: a preview compresses more certainty than the underlying record supports.
- **blocked-without-route**: the system refuses stronger use but does not show the next honest action.
- **preview drift**: small surfaces evolve into stronger claims without reopening governance review.

## Practical tests

A preview-sufficiency discipline passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does each consequential preview say what it is sufficient for right now?
2. Does it identify which governance-bearing facts remain omitted or unresolved?
3. Does it state at least the main unsafe inference being refused?
4. Does it offer a next honest action that resolves toward the governing surface or review lane?
5. Can later reviewers reconstruct what the preview showed when it shaped consequential conduct?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a small surface cannot answer **what it is enough for, what it omits, and what stronger claim it refuses**, then it is still pretending that a **preview** is a **governing record**.
