# 797 — Contestability ladders for scope governance: benchmark objections, trigger appeals, intervention review, handback denials, and no constitutional maintenance by self-marking

## One-line thesis

The archive should pair its answer-key, benchmark, stress-test, maintenance, upgrade-path, public-proof, and intervention notes with one compact **contestability ladder**: **benchmark classifications, trigger openings or closures, stress findings used for redesign, intervention orders, renewals, handback denials, and other stack-shaping judgments should all carry typed standing, reasons, interim-relief logic, and final independent review, so governments do not maintain the constitutional stack by marking their own homework and calling the result settled**.

## Why this matters

`790` now gives the archive a compact answer key for what ideal governments at each scope should generally be. `791` gives a short benchmark card for ordinary-time fit. `792` asks whether the arrangement remains honest under shock. `793` puts those checks on a recurring public maintenance rhythm. `794` says how a polity should move in phases toward a better stack. `795` says what outward evidence should stay public while all of that happens. `796` says what support, supervision, special measures, handback, and rescoping ladder should appear when repeated failure becomes serious enough that someone must compel repair. `799` now adds the narrower question of who may challenge expiry, renewal, graduation, or burial judgments attached to a temporary constitutional object.

What the archive still lacked as one compact rule was the next constitutional question:

**who gets to challenge those judgments, how fast, in which lane, and with what power to pause or correct them before they harden into the live stack?**

That gap matters because multilevel systems fail here in predictable ways.

The first failure is **constitutional self-marking**. The same ministry, metro executive, governor, treasury board, or oversight office scores the benchmark, classifies the trigger, closes the maintenance item, declares the intervention proportionate, denies handback, and publishes the proof pack. Everyone is told the system is accountable because reasons exist somewhere, but there is no reliable route for a municipality, region, assembly minority, ombuds, resident, or other affected actor to force an independent second look.

The second failure is **review that comes too late to matter**. By the time a court, tribunal, or constitutional umpire can speak, the records have moved, the budget has been re-routed, the election has been skipped, the temporary controller has renewed itself, the municipality has been publicly branded a failure, or the intervention has already become the real constitution by fatigue.

The archive should reject both pathologies. A serious scope stack needs not only benchmark cards, maintenance ledgers, and intervention ladders, but also one visible contestability rule that says what can be challenged, who may object, what gets paused, what gets reviewed quickly, and which judgments ultimately require an independent final lane.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate contestability from ordinary political disagreement

Not every argument about local, metropolitan, regional, national, continental, or global design is a legal challenge. Some disagreements belong in elections, legislation, negotiation, or ordinary public debate.

The archive should therefore reserve the contestability ladder for **stack-shaping determinations** such as:

- benchmark classifications that materially affect powers, money, or public legitimacy,
- trigger openings or closures that start or end formal review,
- stress findings used to justify retained emergency concentration or redesign,
- maintenance findings that activate compulsory correction,
- transition-gate decisions that move live authority,
- intervention orders, renewals, or escalations,
- handback denials,
- and formal rescoping or override decisions.

The point is to keep the challenge lane serious without pretending every constitutional dispute is merely politics.

### 2. Publish reasons and evidence before the challenge clock starts

A real objection lane cannot begin from rumor or press release. Before the clock starts, the deciding body should publish the minimum intelligible record showing:

- what determination was made,
- the legal basis,
- the evidence window,
- the key findings,
- the practical effects,
- the next implementation step,
- and the available objection or review lane.

Without that package, challenge clocks become trapdoors rather than remedies.

### 3. Give standing to the actors who actually bear the constitutional change

Standing should be broad enough to cover the actors genuinely affected by a scope judgment.

A usable default set includes:

- the directly affected territorial authority or authorities,
- elected organs or a defined minority of members where the authority itself is captured or disabled,
- residents or service users with a direct and non-trivial stake,
- ombuds, audit bodies, or comparable public-interest challengers where law so provides,
- and, for compact or treaty layers, member bodies or institutions whose allocated role is altered.

The archive should reject challenge design that lets only the deciding authority or its supervisory parent define whether the dispute exists.

### 4. Use a typed first-objection lane before forcing full litigation where time allows

Many disputes can be narrowed quickly if the deciding body must answer a structured objection first. That lane can catch factual error, omitted evidence, category mistake, wrong benchmark packet, bad trigger classification, or misdescribed handback condition before everyone is forced into a full court fight.

But the archive should treat this as a **typed first stop**, not as an exhaustion trap. If the determination is urgently coercive or risks irreversible change, the challenger should be able to move directly to interim relief or independent review.

### 5. Keep interim-relief lanes alive whenever the determination risks irreversible stack change

Where a benchmark finding triggers takeover, where a trigger closure blocks review, where a transition gate transfers live authority, or where an intervention renewal would entrench special measures, the archive should prefer an available pause or standstill lane. `645` already gives the archive the broader doctrine against stack change by racing the clock. This note adds the narrower rule that **scope-governance judgments themselves need fast interim review when waiting would defeat the challenge**.

### 6. Distinguish standards of review instead of calling everything “appeal”

Different scope judgments require different review questions.

A useful default matrix is:

- **legality / jurisdiction review** for whether the body had power at all,
- **procedural review** for consultation, reasons, evidence disclosure, and timing,
- **factual-sufficiency review** for whether the determination is supported by the record,
- **proportionality review** for intervention, supervision, and handback denial,
- and **constitutional or competence review** where the dispute reallocates the stack itself.

Without typed standards, challenge lanes either become performative rehearings or excessively deferential rituals.

### 7. Keep a final independent lane for coercive or stack-rewriting judgments

Some matters should not stop at internal reconsideration, mediation, ombuds review, or political oversight. A serious polity needs a final independent lane — judicial, tribunal, constitutional, or equivalent — for coercive scope judgments that materially alter self-government, legal responsibility, or the public’s route to remedy.

That lane may differ by system. The decisive rule is functional: someone outside the deciding chain must be able to confirm, vary, quash, remand, stay, or time-limit the determination with reasons.

### 8. Make renewals and handback denials more challengeable than initial emergency moves

The archive should be especially suspicious once a supposedly temporary condition is being renewed rather than first declared. Initial emergency or rescue action may sometimes justify speed and a narrower prior process. Renewal, continued supervision, or denial of handback should face a stronger reasons duty and an easier path to independent review.

Otherwise temporary measures become hardest to challenge precisely when they are becoming durable.

### 9. Let challenge survive the disappearance or incapacity of the affected body

A municipality or delegated agency under special measures may be too hollowed out, captured, or disabled to authorize litigation in the ordinary way. The archive should therefore permit backup standing for a defined council minority, suspended officeholder, ombuds, auditor, or other public-interest actor where the ordinary corporate mouthpiece is no longer functioning honestly.

Without that rule, the more successful the takeover, the less contestable it becomes.

### 10. Publish challenge posture in the proof pack and maintenance ledger

`795` already says the public should be able to see the live benchmark, stress, maintenance, transition, or intervention posture. That proof pack should also show:

- active objections,
- interim-relief status,
- last ruling,
- next hearing or review date,
- and whether the challenged determination remains in force, is partly stayed, or has been remanded.

Otherwise outward transparency ends precisely where constitutional contest becomes real.

### 11. Use mediation, peer support, or ombuds lanes for repairable disputes — but never as substitutes for final review

Some scope disputes are best softened before they harden. A benchmark disagreement may benefit from expert clarification. A handback disagreement may benefit from a mediated return plan. A factual dispute about maintenance default may be solvable through an ombuds or audit lane.

The archive should welcome those lighter routes where they help. What it should reject is forcing challengers to remain there after the matter has become coercive, constitutional, or irreversible.

### 12. Close the challenge with a typed disposition rather than vague “noted” outcomes

When a challenge ends, the outcome should be one of a small number of recognizable dispositions, such as:

- confirmed,
- varied,
- stayed,
- quashed,
- remanded for reasons or further evidence,
- converted into a narrower support measure,
- or escalated into formal rescoping.

A polity that only says the objection was “considered” is still practicing constitutional maintenance by self-marking.

## Contestability matrix

| Determination being challenged | First objection lane | Interim-relief question | Final independent lane |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Benchmark classification** | clarification or reconsideration before benchmark owner or designated review office | does the score trigger immediate fiscal, supervisory, or reputational consequences that should pause? | tribunal, court, audit or constitutional lane depending legal effect |
| **Trigger opening or closure** | trigger-review officer, audit, or oversight body | would closure suppress a required review or allow drift to continue unexamined? | court, ombuds, or constitutional lane where the closure blocks lawful review |
| **Stress finding used for redesign or retention** | after-action objection or review panel | is the finding being used to retain emergency concentration or force redesign before challenge? | court, legislature, constitutional lane, or other independent reviewer |
| **Maintenance default or overdue classification** | ledger correction or oversight objection | will the classification open sanctions, intervention, or blocked handback before review? | administrative or judicial review as law provides |
| **Transition gate / cutover decision** | transition-board objection | would implementation make the change practically irreversible before a ruling? | court or constitutional lane with stay power |
| **Intervention order / renewal / escalation** | supervisory reconsideration only if genuinely rapid | should support, special measures, or renewal be paused, narrowed, or time-limited now? | court, constitutional court, tribunal, or equivalent independent lane |
| **Temporary-object expiry / graduation / burial judgment** | temporary-object board objection or equivalent reasons challenge | does the polity have enough proof to extend, ordinary-ise, terminate, or archive this exceptional arrangement? | court, constitutional lane, tribunal, or other independent review route with power to remand, narrow, or block the change |
| **Handback denial** | handback-board objection with evidence right | does denial continue an already exceptional custody that now needs stronger proof? | court or constitutional lane with power to order return, phased return, or proper redesign route |
| **Formal rescoping or override decision** | opinion / override challenge where law provides | would the redesign take effect before legality, consent, or consultation review can bite? | constitutional or apex judicial review, plus any referendum or consent lane required by law |

## Guardrails

- Do not let the same body benchmark, intervene, renew, deny handback, and finally judge its own correctness.
- Do not start objection clocks before reasons and evidence are public enough to answer.
- Do not make first-objection procedures a trap that blocks urgent independent review.
- Do not deny standing merely because the directly affected authority is already under heavy control.
- Do not let temporary or emergency measures become less reviewable as they age.
- Do not let outward proof packs omit the live challenge posture.
- Do not let a determination remain practically irreversible merely because review is slow.

## Failure modes

- **constitutional self-marking**: the deciding chain also monopolizes review.
- **late remedy illusion**: formal review exists, but only after the live stack has already changed irreversibly.
- **standing blackout**: the body under intervention is the only actor allowed to challenge, so no one can actually file.
- **exhaustion trap**: internal reconsideration becomes a way to run out the clock before real review.
- **renewal deference drift**: the second, third, or tenth renewal receives less scrutiny than the first emergency move.
- **opaque challenge posture**: the public can see the benchmark or intervention, but not the objection, stay, or remand status.
- **noted-but-not-decided review**: objections are “received” or “considered” without a typed disposition or consequence.

## Practical tests

A polity is handling scope-governance contestability honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is there a declared list of stack-shaping determinations that are formally challengeable?
2. Do challenge clocks begin only after reasons, evidence, and effect are published intelligibly?
3. Can directly affected authorities, backup institutional actors, and at least some public-interest challengers obtain standing where appropriate?
4. Is there a fast route to interim relief when a determination risks irreversible stack change?
5. Are legality, factual sufficiency, proportionality, and constitutional-competence questions kept distinct enough to be reviewed honestly?
6. Does a final independent lane exist for coercive or stack-rewriting judgments?
7. Are renewals, graduation claims, and handback denials at least as reviewable as first interventions?
8. Does the public proof pack show active objections, stays, remands, and next review dates?
9. Do challenge outcomes end in a typed disposition rather than vague acknowledgment?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can show **how it benchmarks, drills, maintains, upgrades, proves, and even intervenes in a scope arrangement**, but cannot also show **who may challenge those determinations, what can be paused, which lane gives final independent review, and how the public can see the live objection posture**, then it still has **constitutional maintenance by self-marking**.
