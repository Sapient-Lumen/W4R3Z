# 574 — Electronic identification, signatures, seals, and document authentication by scope: community attestation, municipal enrollment, regional federation, national trust frameworks, and no government by unverifiable file

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat **electronic identification, trust services, signatures, seals, time-stamps, and document authentication** as the **constitutional trust layer** of government: keep assisted enrollment, local fact correction, and ordinary witnessing close to people and firms; allow municipal and other frontline offices to serve as practical entry points into proofing and service use; pool federation and shared technical operations where subnational scale genuinely adds capacity; reserve national or constitutional-state authority for the legal trust framework, assurance levels, authoritative source linkage, trusted lists, revocation discipline, and long-term verifiability of state acts; and use continental and global layers for standards, narrow recognition, and document authentication across borders rather than for a fantasy of one world identity stack.

## Why this matters

The archive now has stronger notes on **civil registration**, **entity registries**, **land administration**, **administrative geography**, **public records**, **quality infrastructure**, and **administrative procedure**. What it still lacked was the trust layer that lets those systems speak authoritatively in digital form: the credential that proves a person is the same person who enrolled, the institutional seal that proves an order came from a real authority, the time-stamp that proves a record existed when claimed, the trust list or equivalent that lets a relying party decide whether to accept a signature, the revocation channel that stops a lost or compromised credential from remaining ambient authority, and the cross-border authentication lane that lets a public document travel without diplomatic theatre.

That gap matters because a modern state does not only need to know facts. It needs to make those facts, acts, and attestations **checkable**. A birth record, company extract, permit, court filing, procurement notice, tax payment, benefits decision, land record, or delegated authorization should not become doubtful merely because it crosses an office boundary, changes channel, or becomes electronic. If the trust layer is badly designed, the result is **government by unverifiable file**: screenshots standing in for source records, PDFs that cannot be validated, portals that claim authenticity without proof, institutional emails treated as signatures, local offices improvising their own trust rules, paper-only rescue channels that quietly become the real system, and cross-border use collapsing into couriered stamps and informal discretion.

An ideal governance archive therefore needs one compact note on how identity management and trust services should be distributed by scope. The right answer is not to put every authentication decision in a global or even national mega-database, and not to let each municipality improvise its own root of trust. It is to keep human help and fact correction near lived reality, keep constitutive legal identity tied to authoritative registries, keep trust frameworks law-owned and auditable, distinguish personal signatures from institutional seals, preserve non-digital routes where rights are at stake, and keep cross-border recognition narrow enough to remain lawful, intelligible, and contestable.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate legal identity, electronic identification, authentication, and authorization

The archive should insist that these are different layers. Legal identity is the state-recognised status of a natural or legal person. Electronic identification is the digital representation used to point to that identity. Authentication is the act of confirming that a claimant controls the credential or mechanism presented. Authorization is the separate question of what the authenticated subject may do.

Good systems therefore refuse common category errors. A strong login does not itself create a legal right. A signed document does not by itself prove the signer had power to bind an institution. A municipal service account does not become the sovereign source of citizenship, company status, land title, or professional qualification. The trust layer should bind to authoritative records and explicit authority maps rather than silently replacing them.

### 2. Keep community witnessing and lived fact correction close to the ground, but do not let neighbourhoods become sovereign roots of trust

At the smallest scales, people often know whether an address exists, whether a person is known in the community, whether a local business actually trades, whether a representative relationship is real, or whether a document mismatch is obvious. Systems that ignore those facts become brittle and exclusionary. Community organisations, trusted witnesses, and local assistants can therefore have a legitimate role in helping people gather evidence, correct source records, access assisted-digital channels, and contest obvious identity or document errors.

But the archive should reject the idea that neighbourhood bodies or tiny local units should run their own sovereign public-key hierarchies, invent their own legal signature equivalence rules, or define their own independent standards for what counts as official digital proof. Local witnessing is an input into a lawful trust framework, not a substitute for one.

### 3. Municipalities and other frontline offices should be the ordinary entry points for enrollment help and local transaction trust

Most people meet the state at municipal scale: city hall counters, local registrars, libraries, benefits desks, licensing windows, planning counters, schools, clinics, or assisted-digital centres. Ideal systems should use those proximate interfaces for enrollment support, identity-proofing appointments where law allows, recovery help after device loss, basic signature and seal verification assistance, copy certification or witnessing where law permits, and clear routing into the authoritative registry or trust-service provider.

This design keeps the human side of trust close to people without turning every city into its own sovereign trust island. A resident should be able to walk into a recognisable public office, obtain help recovering or renewing a credential, understand whether a signed document is genuine, and update obvious service-facing details. But the authoritative decision about identity proofing standards, signature equivalence, credential status, or cross-jurisdiction recognition should not depend on local folklore.

### 4. Subnational or regional layers should pool federation, audit, and specialist operations where constitutional design or scale justifies it

In federations and large decentralised states, provinces, states, or regions may lawfully control significant parts of civil status, professional licensing, or digital public-service delivery. Even in unitary states, specialist operational capacity may be pooled above small municipalities. The archive should therefore allow higher subnational layers to operate shared federation gateways, regional credential-service platforms, common enrollment infrastructure, regional incident response, or shared trust-service procurement when that increases competence and reduces fragmentation.

But pooled operation is not a license for uncontrolled drift. If regions or states run meaningful portions of the trust stack, the polity still needs a common grammar for assurance levels, relying-party obligations, signature and seal effects, credential lifecycle events, technical conformance, and auditability. Otherwise the result is a patchwork in which every service must rediscover what another jurisdiction’s credential means.

### 5. National or constitutional-state authority should own the legal trust framework

Some parts of the trust layer are too central to public order and due process to be left to local improvisation. The archive should therefore prefer a law-owning level for the basic legal effect of electronic signatures and seals; the designation and supervision of trust-service providers or equivalent authorities; the relationship between electronic and paper form; the assurance framework for identity proofing, authentication, and federation; authoritative source linkage to civil-registration and entity-registry systems; trusted lists or equivalent trust anchors; incident reporting; revocation and suspension duties; archival and long-term validation rules; and the treatment of delegated representation.

In federations, constituent units may retain substantial room to issue or operate credentials and trust services. But there still needs to be a constitutional or national rulebook that tells the whole polity what a credential proves, how a signed act can be verified, which signatures bind natural persons, which seals bind institutions, how time-stamps and archival evidence work, and what a relying party must do before rejecting or accepting a trust artifact.

### 6. Build digital trust on authoritative source registries, not on duplicated shadow identity stores

A trustworthy digital identity ecosystem should not force every service portal, contractor, or agency to maintain its own silent copy of the person or company it thinks it serves. The archive should therefore prefer designs where electronic identification binds back to authoritative source systems for the underlying legal facts: civil registration or population records for natural persons, business or association registries for legal persons, and named authoritative sources for attributes that matter to a transaction.

This does not require one all-seeing database. It requires disciplined linkage. The trust layer should know what is constitutive, what is asserted, what is merely cached, what may be reused, what may be disclosed, and what expires. A digital wallet, certificate, token, or account should not quietly become the only surviving proof of an underlying legal fact that belongs in a source register.

### 7. Distinguish personal signatures from institutional seals and delegated representation

A recurring governance mistake is to treat every digital act as if it came from an individual acting alone. But many public acts are institutional. A ministry order, court extract, procurement notice, business-register extract, tax certificate, or land record often needs proof that it came from the institution, not merely from some named employee. The archive should therefore clearly separate **electronic signatures** for persons from **electronic seals** or equivalent institutional authenticity tools for public authorities and legal persons.

It should also insist on explicit treatment of delegated representation. When one person signs or acts for a company, municipality, ministry, or another person, the relying party needs a legible chain showing both identity and authority. Good systems therefore bind representation to explicit registries, mandates, or case-specific delegations rather than asking every recipient to infer authority from email domains, job titles, or stationery aesthetics.

### 8. Make the full credential lifecycle real: issuance, activation, maintenance, suspension, revocation, expiry, and recovery

A trust layer is only as credible as its failure handling. The archive should prefer systems that treat enrollment, issuance, activation, renewal, loss, theft, compromise, device change, suspension, revocation, expiry, death, organisational dissolution, and role termination as first-class lifecycle events. A credential that cannot be reliably suspended or revoked is not a secure public instrument; it is merely a convenient fiction.

This matters especially where rights and coercion are involved. Courts, tax authorities, procurement systems, land registries, benefits systems, health systems, and police or corrections interfaces should not continue to honour stale, compromised, or role-detached credentials because revocation is operationally awkward. Recovery channels should be humane and accessible, but they should not become back doors that silently weaken the whole trust system.

### 9. Preserve non-digital and assisted-digital routes whenever access to rights or essential services is at stake

Ideal governments should treat trust services as enabling infrastructure, not as a permission slip to disappear people who lack devices, connectivity, literacy, or safe access to personal hardware. The archive should therefore prefer digital trust frameworks that preserve assisted-digital enrollment, in-person recovery, paper or human-readable fallback where rights depend on access, and channel parity for people whose circumstances make high-assurance digital use difficult.

This is not nostalgia. It is constitutional seriousness. A state that tells a person to obtain healthcare, benefits, legal aid, a permit, or a hearing only through a digital trust mechanism they cannot practically use has converted a trust framework into an exclusion framework.

### 10. Keep technical stacks interoperable and technology-neutral, but do not let vendor convenience define public trust

The archive should prefer open, inspectable, standards-based trust infrastructures that permit interoperability across agencies, channels, devices, and relying parties. Public trust should not be trapped inside one proprietary application, one contractor’s black-box verifier, or one national app store relationship. Technology neutrality matters because the trust layer should survive device churn, market exit, and constitutional change.

But technology neutrality is not the same as standards nihilism. A polity still needs named assurance levels, technical baselines, conformance tests, cryptographic hygiene, trusted lists or equivalent publication, and orderly change control. Otherwise "neutrality" becomes a euphemism for every provider doing something incompatible.

### 11. Use continental and global layers for recognition, interoperability, and document travel — not for one world identity ministry

Cross-border trust should be narrow and practical. The archive should therefore favour continental or global layers that define standards, facilitate mutual recognition where political communities have agreed to it, support trusted-list publication, and simplify the use of public documents abroad through tools like apostilles or equivalent recognition instruments. Those layers should help a document or credential travel; they should not become the sole issuer of everyone’s identity.

The right global move is usually to improve recognition and verification, not to centralise origination. A public document should be able to move across borders with a clear chain of authenticity. A digital signature or seal should be verifiable by relying parties beyond its home jurisdiction when the relevant legal framework says so. But no responsible governance design should leap from that need to a single transnational database that pretends all identity and trust questions can be centrally solved.

### 12. Every consequential digital state act should remain verifiable years later

The archive should prefer systems that make official acts durable and replayable: signed or sealed decisions, attached metadata on issuing authority and time, visible certificate status or equivalent trust information, archivable validation evidence, and a stable way for later users to verify that the record was authentic at the time of issue even if certificates have since expired or systems have changed.

This matters because public authority is not ephemeral. People challenge old tax decisions, prove old company status, validate old procurement notices, enforce old judgments, and rely on old land and civil-status documents. If the state cannot later show how an electronic act was authentic when issued, it has not really digitised authority. It has merely shifted fragility from paper loss to digital amnesia.

## Distribution by scope

### Community and micro-local

Community-scale governance should help with witnessing, interpretation, assisted access, error surfacing, and trust repair. It may support community attestation, identity-proofing accompaniment, translation, delegated-authority explanation, and safe routing into the formal system. It should never be forced to carry sovereign trust-anchor responsibility.

### Municipal or county

Municipal scale is the ordinary human front door: enrollment appointments, recovery help, local verification assistance, copy certification or witnessing where law allows, and ordinary acceptance of signed or sealed public documents. Municipalities may issue local service credentials for municipal systems, but those should sit inside the wider trust framework rather than impersonate national legal identity.

### Regional, provincial, or state

This layer is appropriate for pooled federation services, common operational platforms, supervisory capacity in federal systems, regional service-provider audit, and specialist incident handling where fragmentation would otherwise produce weak security and weak usability.

### National or constitutional state

This level should own the legal framework for electronic identification and trust services, the assurance architecture, the linkage to authoritative registries, the regime for signatures and seals, the publication of trusted lists or equivalent anchors, revocation and status discipline, the rules for public-sector relying parties, and the long-term evidentiary status of digital public acts.

### Continental and global

This layer should provide open standards, narrow recognition frameworks, trusted-list interoperability where agreed, document authentication for foreign use, and model-law or treaty pathways for cross-border recognition. It should not become the single issuer of identities for all people and organisations.

## What ideal governments should avoid

They should avoid treating login products as constitutional identity systems; letting every agency build its own identity store; treating scanned signatures as the only authenticity method for public acts; confusing institutional authority with an employee’s personal account; leaving revocation, expiry, and delegation ambiguous; allowing vendor lock-in to define the state’s trust architecture; forcing essential-rights access through one device-dependent channel; and assuming that cross-border recognition requires a world identity database.

Most of all, ideal governments should avoid **government by unverifiable file**. A public act should be checkable, attributable, time-bounded, and contestable. If the state cannot later explain who or what authenticated an action, under which trust framework, against which source records, with what authority, and with what revocation or archival status, then the state has not built digital trust. It has built a fragile theatre of authenticity.


## Failure modes

- **government by unverifiable file**: public acts circulate as screenshots, PDFs, or portal downloads that look official but cannot be reliably validated for origin, integrity, time, or status.
- **municipal root-of-trust fantasy**: local offices improvise sovereign trust frameworks, producing incompatible credentials and ad hoc recognition rules across the same polity.
- **identity-authorization collapse**: a login or credential is treated as proof of power to act, sign, bind, or decide without checking the underlying mandate or legal capacity.
- **sealed-by-email theater**: institutional authenticity is inferred from an email address, document template, or staff account instead of a verifiable seal, signature, or equivalent trust artifact.
- **revocation fog**: lost, stolen, expired, compromised, or role-detached credentials continue to function because status checking and lifecycle governance are weak.
- **wallet exclusion**: a nominally modern trust framework quietly becomes unusable for people lacking devices, stable connectivity, privacy, literacy, or safe recovery channels.
- **shadow identity sprawl**: every agency and vendor keeps its own silent identity copy, so mismatches multiply and source registries stop being authoritative.
- **cross-border theater of stamps**: document travel remains slow, discretionary, and opaque because recognition relies on folklore or courier ritual rather than clear competent-authority and verification lanes.

## Practical tests

A digital trust architecture is scale-matched and constitutionally serious when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can the polity clearly distinguish legal identity, electronic identification, authentication, and authorization?
2. Do people and firms have a recognisable local front door for enrollment help, recovery, and obvious fact correction without forcing each municipality to become its own sovereign trust island?
3. Is there a law-owning level that defines the legal effect of signatures, seals, time-stamps, trust-service supervision, revocation, and long-term validation?
4. Do credentials and attestations bind back to authoritative source registries rather than becoming isolated shadow truths inside service portals?
5. Can a relying party tell whether a signed or sealed document is genuine, current, and attributable to the right person or institution?
6. Are delegation and representation legible enough that institutional action is not mistaken for a merely personal login event?
7. Can people whose rights depend on state access still use assisted-digital or non-digital routes when necessary?
8. Do continental or global layers help with verification and recognition without turning into a universal identity ministry?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can tell you **how to log in** but cannot tell you **which source registry the credential binds to, what assurance level it carries, whether a document was signed or sealed by the right actor, how revocation works, how an old digital act remains verifiable, and what the cross-border recognition lane actually is**, then it still has **digital access without a constitutional trust layer**.
