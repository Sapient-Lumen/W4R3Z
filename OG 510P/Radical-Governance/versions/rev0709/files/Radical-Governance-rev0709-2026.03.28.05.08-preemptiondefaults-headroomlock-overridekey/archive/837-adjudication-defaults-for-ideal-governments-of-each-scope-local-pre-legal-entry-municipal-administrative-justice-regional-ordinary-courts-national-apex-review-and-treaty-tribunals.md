# 837 — Adjudication defaults for ideal governments of each scope: local pre-legal entry, municipal administrative justice, regional ordinary courts, national apex review, and treaty tribunals

## One-line thesis

The archive should answer the question **where should adjudication itself honestly live at each scope?** with scale-matched defaults rather than court fetish or courthouse minimalism: **micro-local bodies should offer witness, record, mediation, and easy entry into law rather than pretending to be sovereign courts; municipalities should be the first scale with a legible administrative-justice front door for ordinary daily disputes, but not the last word on legality against themselves; metropolitan and regional governments with real cross-boundary power should have equally real ordinary-court, tribunal, or legality-review homes rather than sending people on a host-city treasure hunt; national government should carry the apex and constitutional-review layer that secures rights, uniform due-process floors, and competence settlement across the stack; and continental or global bodies should use treaty-bound courts and tribunals only where the mandate is genuinely real, without pretending to be either a world judiciary of everything or a diplomacy-only zone immune from law**.

## Why this matters

The archive can now answer a compact run of scope questions: **what each scale is for** (`790`), **which regime form usually fits it** (`825`), **where authority should draw its mandate** (`826`), **how long it should last** (`827`), **how it should end** (`828`), **how offices and chambers should be chosen** (`829`), **what vote grammar should govern once the body is real** (`830`), **what money constitution should usually finance it honestly** (`831`), **how it should be scrutinized and corrected** (`832`), **where coercive power should live** (`833`), **where authoritative public facts and records should live** (`834`), **what emergency grammar should fit it** (`835`), and **how public voice should ordinarily re-enter it** (`836`).

What the archive still lacked was one short answer-key note for a different constitutional question:

**once a polity exists and people need binding resolution of disputes, review of administrative action, or settlement of competence conflicts, what adjudicative bodies should actually sit at that scale?**

That gap matters because bad government often hides not only in who rules, but in **where judgment lives**.

One failure is **local sovereign cosplay**: a village assembly, neighborhood committee, or ward body starts acting like a tiny court of final jurisdiction, deciding status, punishment, land rights, or exclusion without the independence, procedure, or remedy stack that actual adjudication requires.

A second failure is **municipal self-judging**: the city runs permits, code enforcement, benefits, fines, land-use decisions, and service exclusions, but the only review lane is another desk inside the same executive chain. People are told review exists, but city hall is still judging city hall almost all the way up.

A third failure is **cross-boundary forum fog**: a metropolitan transport, land-use, housing, flood-control, or utility authority becomes real in practice, yet nobody can say which tribunal, court, or appeals body actually hears its cases without routing the claimant through a host municipality, parent ministry, or improvised board committee.

A fourth failure is **national court hoarding**: ordinary disputes and first-instance review are thinned below while the national apex is treated as the normal venue for everything serious. That does not create constitutional dignity. It creates delay, distance, and judicial overload by design.

The archive therefore needs one compression note on **adjudication defaults by scope**. The live question is not whether law exists in the abstract. It is **where binding judgment, review of administration, and final constitutional settlement should honestly live for the kind of power exercised at that scale**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from dispute class and remedy need, not from court prestige

Adjudication design should begin from one simple question:

**what kind of dispute is this body likely to generate, and what degree of independence, procedure, specialization, and escalation does fair resolution actually require?**

Later archive work should therefore sort adjudicative needs into at least five lanes:

- **pre-legal or early-resolution entry**;
- **administrative reconsideration and tribunal review**;
- **ordinary civil or criminal adjudication**;
- **constitutional or competence review**;
- and **treaty or supranational adjudication**.

Not every scope should own all five lanes in equally thick form. But a polity that cannot say which of these lanes begins where usually has paper legality rather than an honest justice map.

### 2. Micro-local bodies should default to witness, mediation, and legal entry — not miniature sovereign courts

At neighborhood, village, ward, block, or other steward scale, the archive should usually prefer **pre-legal justice entry** rather than full adjudication.

Typical honest ingredients include:

- witnessing and recording facts early;
- helping parties surface the right municipal, ombuds, tribunal, or court lane;
- low-stakes mediation or customary settlement only where participation is free, exit is easy, and higher-law rights remain intact;
- and immediate escalation when liberty, exclusion, serious money, violence, land title, family status, or rights-sensitive questions are involved.

The archive should distrust any design that lets a micro-local body become a final court for sanctions, coercive orders, or durable status judgments. The honest default here is **reachable legal entry plus upward route**, not a tiny sovereignty drama.

### 3. Municipal government should be the first full front door of administrative justice, but not the last word against itself

The municipality is the first level where most people repeatedly encounter binding public administration in ordinary life. It is therefore the first scale that should usually own a **legible administrative-justice front door**.

The archive should usually expect things like:

- reasons-giving and reconsideration rights for permits, fines, code action, benefits, procurement exclusions, and service denials;
- accessible municipal hearing officers, appeal boards, tribunals, or equivalent first-review lanes where the legal order uses them;
- low-cost, intelligible entry into ordinary courts for small claims, legality review, or injunction where necessary;
- legal-aid or navigation support proportionate to the city’s reach into daily life;
- and records discipline strong enough that review is not defeated by missing files.

But the archive should reject **municipal finality**. A city may host the first honest review of daily administration. It should not ordinarily be the final judge of its own legality where rights, coercion, or serious public-law questions are concerned.

### 4. Metropolitan government should not govern shared systems on borrowed dockets

Once transport, water, land-use, housing allocation, freight, flood management, or major utilities are metropolitan in fact, the archive should usually prefer a metropolitan adjudication constitution that is metropolitan in fact as well.

That normally means:

- a named tribunal, administrative-court route, regulator-appeal lane, or ordinary-court map that directly recognizes the metropolitan body;
- user review routes legible to riders, residents, permit seekers, tenants, utilities customers, and affected communities;
- planning, tariff, concession, or service disputes routed through the metropolitan legal act itself rather than a patchwork of host-city shortcuts;
- and interim-relief paths that can stop irreversible system decisions before the merits disappear.

The archive should reject **host-city forum dependency** once metropolitan power is real. A body that binds the shared city should not require outsiders to reverse-engineer which constituent government’s court, clerk, or committee might grudgingly hear the case.

### 5. Regional, provincial, and state governments should usually carry the ordinary-court and administrative-review backbone

Regions, provinces, and states are often the natural territorial home for most **ordinary first-instance adjudication, appeals, and administrative review**, whether the legal order uses integrated national courts, partly territorialized hierarchies, or explicitly state-level courts.

The archive should usually expect at this scale:

- first-instance courts close enough to people to remain reachable but large enough to sustain competence and independence;
- appellate structure that can harmonize within the territory without forcing every dispute straight to the nation;
- administrative courts or equivalent review lanes for acts of municipalities, regions, and sectoral agencies where the legal order uses them;
- specialist courts or chambers only where case type and scale genuinely warrant them;
- and competence-conflict routes visible to municipalities and lower territorial bodies.

This scale is often where the ordinary judicial map becomes real. The archive should therefore resist both decorative duplication and over-central concentration. The test is not whether every federation copies one court model. The test is whether **people and governments can tell where ordinary adjudication lives and reach it without absurd distance or confusion**.

### 6. National government should carry the apex and constitutional-review layer, not every first-instance burden

At national scale, the archive should usually prefer the thickest concentration of:

- constitutional review or equivalent rights-and-competence settlement;
- apex appellate functions for legal coherence;
- due-process floors and procedural codes;
- judicial-governance rules that protect independence, impartiality, and resources;
- and the final settlement of intergovernmental competence disputes where the constitution requires it.

But the archive should distrust **apex hoarding**. A serious national constitution needs a strong top layer, yet ordinary disputes should not have to become national constitutional theater just to receive a fair hearing.

National government is usually the guarantor of the judicial constitution. It is not thereby entitled to swallow every courthouse, every tribunal, and every accessible entry lane below.

### 7. In federations, the honest question is clarity of jurisdiction and rights access, not loyalty to one court architecture

Federal systems vary. Some use integrated court systems; others use dual or partly dual hierarchies; still others add specialist constitutional courts or tribunals.

The archive should therefore avoid fetishizing one template. Use a simple test instead:

- can persons, municipalities, regions, and the center tell **which court hears what**;
- can rights claims move upward without dead-ending in territorial ambiguity;
- can competence conflicts between levels of government be settled without constant political hostage-taking;
- and can inconsistent jurisprudence be controlled without making first-instance justice unreachable?

If those answers are yes, the court architecture may be honest even if it is not symmetrical. If those answers are no, the archive should treat the judicial design as part of the governance problem rather than as neutral background furniture.

### 8. Administrative justice should sit close to everyday government action, but remain independent enough to review it honestly

The archive should keep repeating one rule: **most people meet the state through administration, not through constitutional litigation**.

That means later archive work should usually look for:

- reconsideration rights that are real rather than ceremonial;
- tribunals, hearing officers, or administrative courts with enough separation from the executive chain to review it honestly;
- procedural fairness and reasons-giving at the point where people actually lose permits, benefits, services, or licenses;
- and affordable pathways into binding remedy before harm hardens into lived exclusion.

Where the live question is not where adjudication lives but how remedies stack from complaint through ombuds, tribunal, court, and constitutional review, use `494` alongside this note. Where the live question is not the location of adjudication but the routing of conflicts across cross-boundary bodies, use `821` alongside this note.

### 9. Continental and plurilateral bodies should use treaty courts and tribunals only where the mandate is real

At continental or plurilateral scale, the archive should reject both fantasies at once:

- that every serious compact requires a general sovereign court of everything; and
- that treaty bodies can govern real common markets, rights regimes, basins, grids, or standards with no adjudication worth the name.

The honest default is usually a bounded mix of:

- treaty or regional courts where rights or treaty interpretation genuinely require them;
- arbitration, panels, boards of appeal, or specialized tribunals where the mandate is narrower;
- direct recourse for member states, institutions, or in some systems affected persons where the legal order expressly provides it;
- and strong publication and jurisdiction rules so outsiders can tell which disputes belong there.

The archive should distrust **upper-tier judicial creep** just as much as upper-tier lawlessness. The question is whether the compact has enough real delegated power to justify a real adjudicative lane, and no more.

### 10. Global adjudication should stay sparse, real, and treaty-bound

At global scale, the archive should usually prefer a narrow judicial waist.

That means things like:

- state-to-state adjudication and advisory functions where the Charter or treaty order provides them;
- treaty-bound courts or tribunals in specific domains that states have actually accepted;
- and review of genuinely global legal obligations only through institutions with explicit jurisdiction.

What the archive should reject is both **world-court cosplay** and **diplomacy-only denial**. The globe does not need a universal courthouse for every ordinary dispute. But neither should it pretend that irreducibly global obligations can never culminate in law-backed judgment.

### 11. Court location, digital access, and legal aid are part of constitutional design, not implementation trivia

An honest adjudication map should ask who can actually use it.

Later archive work should therefore usually look for:

- territorial proximity of first-instance courts or trusted entry points;
- digital access that reduces distance without widening the justice gap;
- translation, disability access, and assisted entry;
- legal aid or navigation support where procedure would otherwise become exclusion;
- and judicial-map choices that balance quality, independence, and closeness to users.

A court hierarchy that is beautiful on a constitutional diagram but practically unreachable to most of the governed field is still a bad governance design.

### 12. Require one short adjudication holding in every applied scope verdict

When later archive work uses `811` or `791` on a live case, it should normally be able to add one short holding such as:

- **the municipality issues permits and fines at scale but still lacks any honest administrative-justice front door before people are pushed into distant ordinary courts**;
- **the metropolitan authority now governs real shared systems, yet affected people still cannot tell which tribunal or court directly hears challenges to its acts**;
- **the region has become the real platform for service delivery and regulation, but its first-instance and appellate map is too thin or too remote for the work it now does**;
- **the national apex is carrying too much ordinary dispute load because the lower judicial map has been hollowed out**;
- or **the treaty body’s delegated authority is now real enough to require a clearer judicial or quasi-judicial lane than peer diplomacy alone**.

That keeps applied verdicts from stopping at the words **“rule of law”**, **“metro”**, **“federal”**, or **“treaty body”** without naming the actual home of binding judgment.

## Diagnostic questions

1. What dispute class is actually at issue here: pre-legal conflict, administrative review, ordinary adjudication, constitutional review, or treaty adjudication?
2. Is the first real review lane close enough to the people or governments affected to be usable in time?
3. Does the municipality have an honest administrative-justice front door, or only self-review inside the same executive chain?
4. Has a metropolitan or regional body accumulated enough real power that it now needs its own named tribunal or court route rather than borrowed host-city forums?
5. In a federation, can persons and governments tell which court or tribunal hears which dispute without jurisdictional roulette?
6. Is the national apex protecting rights and coherence, or quietly carrying ordinary first-instance burdens that should live lower in the stack?
7. At upper tiers, does a real adjudicative lane exist only where the treaty mandate is real, explicit, and accepted?
8. Do court geography, digital procedure, language, legal aid, and disability access make the adjudication map usable in practice?

## Guardrails

- Do not let micro-local bodies become tiny sovereign courts for serious public-law, liberty, land, family-status, or rights questions.
- Do not call a municipality complete if people still cannot find an intelligible administrative-justice front door for ordinary daily-state disputes.
- Do not let metropolitan bodies bind real shared systems while hiding adjudication in host-city leftovers.
- Do not build regional governments that regulate and spend at scale while leaving ordinary courts, appeals, and legality review too thin or too remote.
- Do not force every serious rights or competence dispute into improvised political bargaining because the judicial constitution is blurry.
- Do not confuse a strong national apex with a reason to nationalize every usable first-instance lane.
- Do not create treaty courts or tribunals for prestige where the upper-tier mandate is too thin to justify them.
- Do not treat distance, legal aid, language, or digital exclusion as implementation trivia once they determine who can actually obtain judgment.

## Failure modes

- **local sovereign cosplay**: a micro-local body acts like a final court without independence, procedure, or remedy.
- **city hall judging city hall**: municipal review remains trapped inside the same executive chain that made the decision.
- **forum fog**: affected people cannot tell which tribunal or court directly hears acts of a metropolitan or cross-boundary body.
- **apex hoarding**: national top courts absorb disputes that should have been resolved earlier and closer.
- **federal jurisdiction roulette**: rights and competence conflicts bounce across unclear territorial and national lanes.
- **treaty-law without judgment**: upper-tier bodies exercise real delegated power while adjudication remains a diplomatic shrug.
- **court-map exclusion**: the formal court exists, but distance, digital gaps, language, or cost make it practically unreachable.
- **specialization theater**: specialist tribunals proliferate without enough caseload, independence, or clarity to justify themselves.

## Compression rule for the archive

If the archive can name **what authority should exist at a scope, how it is chosen, how it is financed, how it is watched, and how the public re-enters it**, but cannot also name **where ordinary administrative review begins, where binding judgment lives, how competence conflicts reach final settlement, and which adjudicative bodies do not belong at that scale**, then the design still has **government without an honest home for judgment**.
