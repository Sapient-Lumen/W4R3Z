# 639 — Scope conformance before go-live: implementation attestations, readiness gates, and no stack change by deployment drift

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should require one public **scope-conformance attestation before go-live** for any materially stack-altering measure: budgets, regulations, grants, contracts, staffing, digital workflows, supervisory routines, public communications, and remedy routes should be checked against the adopted packet, opinion, override, and live register so that implementation cannot quietly move power farther than law and consent actually authorised.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a much stronger discipline for honest multilevel design. It says **where functions should usually live** (`579`, `602`, `603`), **what burden of proof applies when scope is contested** (`628`, `629`), **which legal lane may move a function and how handback should work** (`630`, `631`), **when the stack must be reopened and how it should be recorded** (`632`, `633`, `634`), and **how concrete scope-changing proposals should travel through packets, opinions, overrides, and clear-statement rules** (`635`-`638`).

What it still lacked was the last-mile doctrine: **how to stop implementation from becoming a second, unofficial constitution-making lane.**

That gap matters because many scope changes drift not at the moment of enactment but at the moment of deployment. The statute looks narrower than the budget instructions. The budget looks narrower than the grant conditions. The grant conditions look narrower than the procurement. The procurement looks narrower than the software defaults. The software looks narrower than the operational guidance. The guidance looks narrower than the actual supervisory habit. By the time the system goes live, the adopted settlement has been quietly tightened, centralized, or territorially widened without any new packet, opinion, or override.

That failure is especially common in multilevel systems because real authority is often exercised through **implementation surfaces** rather than through one clean constitutional text: who signs the grant, who sets the mandatory fields, who may refuse an exception, who owns the case system, whose budget line may freeze action, which staff are seconded or borrowed, which help desk citizens can actually reach, and which review route can interrupt a decision before fait accompli. A polity can therefore appear to have followed all the right legal steps while still arriving at a live stack that is different from the one it publicly authorised.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine of **scope conformance before go-live**. Earlier notes govern what may be proposed, classified, approved, and interpreted. This note governs what may actually be switched on.

Without that doctrine, the archive would still allow one recurring failure mode: **stack change by deployment drift**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat go-live as a separate constitutional moment, not a technical afterthought

A materially stack-altering reform should not be considered constitutionally complete merely because the relevant law has passed or the compact has been signed. There should be a second question just before operations begin: **does the live implementation still match what was lawfully authorised?**

That question matters because many of the most consequential reallocations happen after enactment, when procurement teams, platform owners, treasuries, ministries, host authorities, and crisis managers translate abstract approval into concrete routines. Go-live is therefore not only an administrative milestone. In multilevel government it is often the moment when the practical constitution of the stack becomes real.

### 2. Require one public scope-conformance attestation before operational start

For any reform that materially changes the live scope register, the archive should prefer one public scope-conformance attestation linked to the same proposal identifier as the packet, opinion, override, and register entry where applicable.

That attestation should state, in one place:

- the legal instrument or package being implemented;
- the authoritative before/after scope-diff packet;
- the scope opinion classification and any override statement;
- the exact go-live boundary being certified;
- the budgets, transfers, grants, staffing plans, contracts, digital systems, forms, guidance, and public channels that are in scope;
- the remedy and complaint routes that will exist on day one;
- any temporary workaround, residual exception, or phased rollout that remains;
- and the named reasons why the implementation is judged conformant enough to go live.

Without that object, multilevel implementation remains too easy to govern by scattered documents and oral assurance.

### 3. Check all coupled surfaces, not only the black-letter text

A serious conformance review should inspect the full operating bundle, not only the law or high-level memorandum. At minimum it should compare the authorised stack against:

- appropriations, transfers, grant conditions, and payment controls;
- subordinate regulation, circulars, manuals, and mandatory templates;
- procurement specifications, service contracts, concession terms, and platform statements of work;
- staffing authorities, secondment plans, training, escalation charts, and employer-of-record choices;
- case systems, identity and access rules, workflow gates, audit logs, and records classification;
- public notices, official channels, forms, and assisted-digital arrangements;
- and complaint, reconsideration, appeal, ombuds, or judicial review routes.

If any one of those surfaces materially reallocates who can decide, refuse, supervise, fund, or review, then the implementation is not conformant merely because the statute still sounds acceptable.

### 4. Distinguish ordinary implementation detail from material deployment drift

The doctrine should not confuse every operational difference with constitutional misfit. Some implementation choices remain ordinary so long as they do not materially affect who governs.

Deployment drift becomes material when implementation:

- turns a preserved lower-tier discretion into mandatory compliance;
- changes who may initiate, refuse, license, prioritise, allocate, or pause action;
- substitutes upper-tier workflow, budget control, or platform permissions for lower-tier judgment;
- creates effective territorial authority wider than the approved map;
- narrows remedy routes or delays review until after irreversibility;
- or leaves legal title formally below while shifting real command above.

Where those effects appear, the polity should not describe the problem as mere delivery detail.

### 5. No budget, staffing, or supervision conformance without commensurate capacity and lawful control

A reform should not be certified as scope-conformant if the receiving tier lacks the money, staff, records, systems, or supervisory clarity needed to exercise the competence honestly.

That means conformance should fail when:

- responsibilities have moved without commensurate resources;
- the lower tier remains nominally responsible but cannot hire or retain competent staff;
- grants or approvals leave the upper tier holding the real veto while disclaiming ownership;
- supervision extends beyond what the lawful lane permits;
- or a host authority, ministry, or vendor retains the indispensable operational keys after formal handoff.

A live stack is not honest when law says one level governs but capability and control still sit somewhere else.

### 6. Digital systems and procurement should be treated as constitutional implementation surfaces

The archive should be explicit that software configuration, identity and access controls, procurement clauses, and managed-service arrangements can be scope-changing surfaces even when no new law is being passed.

A system that decides who can approve a case, who can override a refusal, whose standards are mandatory, who sees which records, which territory a workflow applies to, or whether local officials may depart from a template is not merely administrative plumbing. It is part of the live allocation of power.

The same is true of procurement. If a contract or statement of work assumes a supervisory authority, data access right, territorial remit, or operating command that the packet never authorised, then the reform should return for correction or re-clearance rather than go live under the banner of technical necessity.

### 7. When conformance fails, choose an honest lane instead of improvising through launch pressure

A polity should prefer a small set of downstream lanes when pre-launch conformance fails:

- **fix before launch** — the implementation can be corrected within the authorised settlement;
- **re-clear and amend** — the live design now differs materially enough that packet, opinion, or override must be revisited;
- **temporary constrained go-live** — a bounded pilot or emergency lease may proceed only with visible limits, clocks, and residue tracking;
- **abort or defer** — the stack is not honest enough for live use yet.

This matters because deadline pressure often produces the worst constitutional shortcuts. A truthful polity should be able to say that the reform is not ready, instead of pretending that operational necessity silently amended the stack.

### 8. Delay register finality until the live operating bundle matches the approved change

The canonical scope register (`633`) should be able to distinguish between **authorised**, **implemented**, and **live-conformant** status. A reform may be lawfully adopted yet still not be ready to count as the new operational baseline.

That means the register should not present a scope change as fully live merely because the legal instrument exists. It should show whether the budgets, staff, systems, supervisory routines, public channels, and remedy routes have actually reached the authorised state, and whether any exceptions remain open.

This preserves a visible difference between constitutional intention and constitutional operation.

### 9. Keep a short post-launch variance window so drift is caught early

Even a careful pre-launch attestation will miss some drift. The archive should therefore prefer one short post-launch variance review that compares the first live period against the conformance claims and logs any material mismatch back into the trigger registry (`632`).

That review should be especially alert to:

- emergency workarounds becoming routine,
- vendor or host-government control persisting beyond transition,
- review routes failing in practice,
- local discretion disappearing through training or platform habit,
- and fiscal throttles functioning as undeclared command.

Where material mismatch appears, the polity should update the register honestly and reopen the proper lane rather than normalise the drift.

### 10. Keep the doctrine bounded so it disciplines consequential deployment without freezing ordinary administration

The archive should not demand a constitutional launch ceremony for every staffing roster, API release, or budget transfer. The doctrine belongs to **material stack effects**: whether implementation has changed who governs, who bears risk, who controls resources, who supervises, who can review, and which territory the live rule now reaches.

Ordinary project management remains ordinary. The point is that **deployment should not complete a constitutional change that the public decision never openly made**.

## Guardrails

- Do not certify go-live solely on the basis of enacted text while ignoring budgets, procurement, staffing, digital permissions, or remedy routes.
- Do not treat borrowed staff, host-government admin rights, or vendor operational control as harmless transition detail when they determine who really governs.
- Do not let grant conditions, reporting templates, or workflow rules impose upper-tier command that the approved stack did not authorise.
- Do not update the canonical scope register from “authorised” to “live” unless the operating bundle has been checked for conformance.
- Do not allow launch deadlines, funding windows, or crisis tempo to substitute for a named re-clearance or temporary exception lane.
- Do not turn the doctrine into exhaustive ceremony for ordinary implementation details that do not materially affect the allocation of power.

## Failure modes

- **stack change by deployment drift**: the live operating bundle centralizes or widens authority beyond the approved settlement.
- **paper federalism, live centralism**: law leaves competence below while budget, staffing, or systems keep real command above.
- **procurement constitutionalism**: contractual terms do the work of public-law allocation without saying so.
- **platform completionism**: software defaults finish a scope transfer that legislation only hinted at.
- **handoff theater**: title appears to move but records, money, staff, or admin keys never really transfer.
- **register fiction**: the public scope map claims a live arrangement that the operational bundle has not actually achieved.

## Practical tests

A polity is handling pre-launch scope conformance honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Before operational start, is there one public attestation linking the approved packet, opinion, override, and go-live bundle?
2. Does conformance review inspect budgets, grants, staffing, procurement, systems, public channels, and remedy routes rather than only enacted text?
3. Can the reviewers distinguish ordinary delivery detail from material drift in who decides, who funds, who supervises, and who can review?
4. Would the receiving tier still be able to exercise the function honestly with the money, staff, systems, and legal discretion it actually has on day one?
5. Are software permissions, workflow gates, and contractual assumptions checked as possible scope-changing surfaces rather than dismissed as mere technicalities?
6. If implementation has drifted beyond authorisation, is there a clear lane to fix, re-clear, constrain temporarily, or defer rather than launch anyway?
7. Does the canonical scope register distinguish authorised change from genuinely live-conformant change?
8. Is there a short post-launch variance review to catch early drift before it hardens into constitutional custom?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a stack change can be defended on paper only because the statute looked acceptable, while the live budgets, staff, systems, contracts, or supervisory routines would make a different level govern in practice, then the polity is not implementing the reform honestly. It is governing by **stack change through deployment drift**.
