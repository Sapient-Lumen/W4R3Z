# 813 — Legitimacy-chain templates for cross-boundary scope forms: delegated councils, principal-owned backbones, double-key authorities, directly elected tiers, and no heavy power by convenience composition

## One-line thesis

Once the archive has said **which cross-boundary institutional form is honest**, it should also be able to say **what legitimacy chain belongs to that form**: thin connectors should be run by delegated elected principals with no hidden hard powers, shared-service backbones should remain clearly owned by the member governments whose duties they support, bounded functional authorities should carry an elected anchor plus explicit double-key gates for major debt, rule, boundary, or remit changes, and general-purpose territorial tiers should normally rest on directly elected chambers with their own scrutiny and executive responsibility rather than on convenience boards pretending to carry full constitutional weight.

## Why this matters

The archive can now do three things that matter greatly for ideal governments by scope.

`790` says what the default stack is. `810` says how to assemble threshold claims before the archive says that a metro, county-pooling, regional-platform, or legitimacy-thickness line is really crossed. `812` says what institutional form is honest once the need is already real: thin connector, shared-service backbone, bounded functional authority, or general-purpose elected tier. `814` says what fiscal chain belongs to that form once the money question becomes live. `815` says what administrative chain belongs to it once the question becomes who actually employs, operates, keeps records, or carries continuity. `816` says what oversight chain belongs to it once the question becomes who scrutinizes, audits, investigates maladministration, or hears challenge routes.

What the archive still lacked was one smaller but recurring judgment:

**once the form is chosen, who should actually sit in it, and which decisions must clear which legitimacy gates?**

That gap matters because many live governance systems do not fail at the level of abstract form alone. They fail at the level of composition and decision chain.

A metropolitan forum remains labelled “co-ordination” even though an unelected secretariat and a loose mayoral table now control real burden-sharing. A shared-service body is called purely administrative even though nobody can tell whether the member municipalities, the host, or the pooled executive owns the consequential choices. A transport or basin authority is given a respectable charter, but then accumulates debt, land leverage, and portfolio expansion through a board design too thin for what it now decides. Or a genuinely political cross-boundary tier is denied a directly elected chamber because incumbents find indirect composition easier to negotiate.

The result is a specific constitutional error: **the form may be nominally right, but the legitimacy chain is mismatched to the gravity of the power being exercised.**

Older archive notes already contain pieces of the answer. `490`, `548`, `596`, `607`, `610`, and `753` all help. But the archive did not yet have one short bridge note that turns those scattered insights into a reusable template for the current ideal-governments-by-scope run.

Official materials strongly support such a bridge. The European Charter of Local Self-Government ties local self-government to councils or assemblies freely elected by direct, equal, universal suffrage and to executive organs responsible to them. OECD work on metropolitan governance shows that institutional diversity is real but that accountability and democratic deficit concerns become sharper as powers grow. OECD work on inter-municipal shared services shows that pooled provision works better when law clearly specifies forms, governance, responsibilities, structure, composition, reporting, and cost-sharing. OECD work on regional governance treats directly elected deliberative bodies as a defining feature of decentralised regional government rather than an optional ornament. The archive should compress those lessons into one rule: **do not let composition lag behind consequence.**

## Pattern pack

### 1. Use this note after form choice, not instead of it

This note does not decide whether a cross-boundary threshold is crossed.

Use `810` for that.

This note does not decide whether the honest institutional answer is a connector, backbone, bounded authority, or full tier.

Use `812` for that.

This note applies only after the archive can already say what kind of form is honest, but still needs to say:

- who occupies the decision room,
- who remains the legal principal,
- which decisions can be taken internally,
- which decisions need ratification or a second key,
- and when indirect composition stops being an honest democratic chain,
- and where the separate fiscal-chain question should be handed to `814` rather than smuggled into board design prose.
- and where the separate oversight-chain question should be handed to `816` rather than smuggled into composition prose as if board shape already settled independent review.
- and where the separate decision-rule question should be handed to `820` rather than smuggling quorum, supermajority, unanimity, deadlock, and emergency grammar into the legitimacy holding itself.

### 2. Separate four legitimacy chains before inventing hybrids

The archive should usually begin with four recognizable legitimacy chains that pair with the four form choices in `812`.

| Form | Default legitimacy chain | What carries the democratic title | What does **not** count as enough by itself |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Thin connector** | delegated council / forum of elected principals | the constituent elected governments | secretariat autonomy, leader summits, or technical committees making binding choices off-book |
| **Shared-service backbone** | principal-owned service governance with member ratification and reporting | the member governments that retain the legal duties | a host municipality or pooled manager becoming the real unreviewable sovereign |
| **Bounded functional authority** | elected-anchor board plus explicit charter and double-key gates for heavy decisions | the authority’s public charter plus elected principals or chamber that can really steer, review, or ratify consequential moves | expert-only boards, pure creditor logic, or delegated convenience composition once powers widen |
| **General-purpose elected tier** | directly elected deliberative chamber plus accountable executive | the electorate of the territory itself | indirect brokerage, rotating summits, or appointed boards pretending to carry territorial politics |

The archive should not start with bespoke compromise design until one of these legitimacy chains has first been ruled out honestly.

### 3. Thin connectors should stay delegated and visibly non-sovereign

A thin connector is honest when the democratic title clearly remains in the member governments and the connector is plainly a forum for:

- alignment,
- agenda-setting,
- shared diagnosis,
- non-binding strategy,
- voluntary project preparation,
- or common monitoring.

The right composition is usually a delegated council or forum made up of elected officeholders or clearly authorised public representatives from the member governments, with a small secretariat that serves the table rather than replacing it.

The archive should resist three temptations here:

- giving the secretariat real distributive discretion,
- treating summit custom as a substitute for legal authority,
- or letting “consensus culture” hide the fact that some municipalities are now being bound without a visible vote or review path.

A thin connector therefore does **not** need its own full democratic chamber, but it does need a visible line back to elected principals and a hard ban on hiding binding choices in the connector lane.

### 4. Shared-service backbones should preserve principal ownership and ratification of consequential shifts

A shared-service backbone exists because several governments keep the political title to a function while pooling administrative machinery, specialist staff, procurement, digital systems, case handling, or continuity capacity.

Its legitimacy chain should therefore begin from a simple premise:

**the member governments remain the principals unless the archive has said otherwise.**

That means the backbone should normally have:

- a founding agreement or charter that names functions, responsibilities, structure, composition, cost-sharing, duration, exit, and termination;
- a governing committee or board composed through the member governments rather than through free-floating appointments alone;
- reporting duties back to the member governments;
- explicit service-level, quality, and budget controls;
- and a clear statement of which changes need member ratification rather than internal managerial discretion.

Ordinary operating decisions may sit with the pooled executive. But the archive should require member-government ratification or pre-authorisation when the backbone would otherwise drift into substantive policy choice, large liability exposure, portfolio expansion, or politically consequential prioritisation among places.

### 5. Bounded functional authorities need an elected anchor before they need direct-election maximalism

A bounded functional authority is the honest answer when one cross-boundary system needs continuous stewardship, asset management, system balancing, or operational governance.

The archive should not leap automatically from “narrow authority” to “direct election now.” But it should insist that the body have an **elected anchor** strong enough for what the authority can actually do.

That elected anchor may take several honest forms depending on the function and the polity:

- a board dominated by elected local or regional officials from the constituent governments,
- a statutory chamber or committee that can approve plans and interrogate the authority,
- a ministerially or legislatively accountable structure with hard publication and review duties,
- or, where the authority is already broad and politically salient enough, direct election.

The archive should prefer **elected-anchor adequacy** over abstract purity. But adequacy must be real. Once a functional authority carries major debt, compulsory charges, coercive powers, binding spatial consequences, or persistent distributive conflict, expert-heavy or appointment-heavy composition becomes progressively harder to defend.

### 6. Use explicit double-key gates for high-consequence moves below the full-tier threshold

The archive needs one compact doctrine for bodies that are heavier than a connector or backbone, but not yet honest as a full general-purpose tier.

That doctrine is the **double-key gate**.

A double-key gate means certain categories of decision cannot be validly made by the authority board or executive alone. They need a second legitimacy key such as:

- ratification by member governments,
- approval by a constituent elected chamber,
- approval by a directly elected regional or metropolitan assembly,
- or another declared public-law second key that is legible to the citizen.

The archive should normally require some double-key discipline for decisions such as:

- major borrowing or guarantee exposure,
- compulsory levies or tariff structures with quasi-tax effect,
- charter expansion,
- boundary change,
- portfolio enlargement into a second or third politically salient field,
- major asset disposal,
- or binding long-horizon plans with strong land-use and distributive consequences.

This lets a bounded authority remain bounded without pretending that all its consequential choices are merely technical.

### 7. General-purpose territorial tiers should normally have directly elected deliberative ownership

Once the archive has concluded that the honest answer is a general-purpose territorial tier — metropolitan, county-style, regional, or otherwise — it should usually stop searching for convenience composition.

At that point the democratic title should normally sit in:

- a directly elected deliberative chamber for the territory,
- an executive responsible to that chamber or directly to the electorate under the constitution’s chosen model,
- ordinary scrutiny, audit, disclosure, and review routes,
- and an intelligible relationship between taxing, borrowing, planning, and accountability powers.

Indirect composition may still appear in special settlements, transitional stages, federal bargains, or second chambers with bounded veto domains. But the archive should treat those as typed exceptions, not the default answer for a body making recurrent multi-sector territorial choices.

The key rule is simple: **once the politics is truly territorial, the electorate of that territory should normally own the chamber.**

### 8. Keep advisory participation distinct from voting title

Cross-boundary bodies often benefit from advisory participation by service users, civil society, firms, unions, universities, Indigenous institutions, professional bodies, or other stakeholders.

The archive should welcome that where the task benefits from wider knowledge or trust-building.

But it should still keep one sharp distinction:

- **advisory participation** may broaden information, deliberation, and scrutiny;
- **voting title over public authority** must remain clearly tied to the body’s public-law legitimacy chain.

This blocks two common errors: pretending that stakeholder seats cure democratic deficit, and pretending that a body can quietly privatise its governing title by mixing public office with sectoral representation in one indistinct board.

### 9. Publish one public map of composition, powers, and gates

Whatever the form, the archive should require one outward-facing composition map that says in a few lines:

- who sits on the body,
- who appointed or elected them,
- what term and removal rules apply,
- which decisions the body may take alone,
- which decisions require a second key,
- where scrutiny, audit, complaints, and judicial review live,
- and where the legal principal remains if the body is not itself the full sovereign home of the function.

A citizen should not have to infer these facts from several statutes, memoranda, or intergovernmental customs.

### 10. Name escalation triggers from lighter to heavier legitimacy chains

A legitimacy chain is not permanent merely because it was once appropriate.

Later archive work should say what would force an upgrade, such as:

- a connector becoming the real place where burdens are allocated,
- a backbone deciding substantive priorities among member governments,
- a bounded authority accumulating debt, coercion, or cross-sector remit,
- or a general-purpose body still being run through indirect brokerage after its politics has become fully territorial.

When those triggers appear, the archive should stop saying only that the form has matured. It should also say that the **legitimacy chain** has matured and now requires a thicker democratic key.

### 11. Name downshift tests as well

The archive should also guard against permanent over-thickening.

If a crisis board has narrowed back to one technical system, if debt exposure has vanished, if a temporary regional command has expired, or if pooled administration has returned to straightforward service support, then a heavier legitimacy chain may no longer be necessary.

Direct election, double-key ratification, or broad chamber forms should not survive merely because they once solved a harder problem.

### 12. Require one short legitimacy-chain holding in applied verdicts

When `811` is used for a live case, the archive should normally be able to add one short holding such as:

- **honest current form is a shared-service backbone; member municipalities retain legal title and all portfolio-expansion decisions require member ratification**;
- **honest current form is a bounded transit authority; ordinary operations sit with the board but debt, charter expansion, and spatial-framework adoption require a second legitimacy key**;
- **honest current form is now a metropolitan tier; indirect mayoral composition is no longer adequate and the territory should own a directly elected chamber**.

That keeps applied judgments from stopping at the word “authority” or “metro” without saying how democratic title and consequential decisions are actually routed. Where the unresolved issue is no longer composition but dues, contributions, assigned revenues, equalization, or capital authority, the packet should also carry a short fiscal-chain holding under `814`. Where the unresolved issue is no longer board design but operator home, records home, or employer-of-record design, the packet should also carry a short administrative-chain holding under `815`. Where the unresolved issue is no longer who sits in the room but who audits, investigates maladministration, hears complaints, or provides judicially legible redress, the packet should also carry a short oversight-chain holding under `816`. Where the unresolved issue is no longer whether a second key exists but what ordinary vote, supermajority, unanimity reserve, quorum, deadlock, or emergency rule belongs to the body, the packet should also carry a short decision-rule-chain holding under `820`.

## Guardrails

- Do not let a secretariat or host arrangement become the real sovereign by habit.
- Do not treat stakeholder participation as a substitute for a public legitimacy chain.
- Do not let a bounded authority expand its remit, debt, or coercive reach without new gates.
- Do not leave general-purpose territorial politics inside rotating leader summits or convenience boards.
- Do not assume direct election is required for every bounded authority, but do not assume it is never required either.
- Do not allow one body’s composition to remain frozen after the gravity of its decisions has materially changed.

## Failure modes

- **composition laundering** — a body exercises heavy power through a board design justified only by negotiation convenience.
- **host sovereignty by stealth** — a host municipality or ministry becomes the real decision center inside a pooled body meant to remain jointly owned.
- **expert-board overreach** — technical expertise is used to legitimise choices that are actually distributive or constitutional.
- **stakeholder-seat theater** — advisory participation is used as a substitute for democratic ownership.
- **indirect-chain exhaustion** — a territorial body remains indirectly composed after its politics has plainly become first-order and public.
- **gate collapse** — debt, charter, or remit expansion happens without a second legitimacy key even though the authority remains below full-tier status.

## Practical tests

A legitimacy-chain holding is honest when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Has the archive already said which form is honest, so the live question really is composition and decision chain?
2. Is the democratic title visibly carried by the right actor for that form: member governments, elected anchor, or territorial electorate?
3. Can a citizen tell who remains the legal principal for the function and who merely operates or co-ordinates it?
4. Are high-consequence decisions clearly separated from routine operating decisions?
5. Where the body is not a full elected tier, is there a declared second key for debt, remit expansion, major coercive shifts, or binding long-horizon commitments?
6. Where the body is a full territorial tier, does it have directly elected deliberative ownership or a clearly typed constitutional reason for departing from that default?
7. Are scrutiny, audit, complaints, and review routes mapped publicly rather than inferred from custom?
8. Is the holding short enough that later archive work can cite it without reopening the whole argument about democracy, delegation, and board design?

## Compression rule for the archive

If the archive can say **the threshold is crossed** and **the honest institutional form is X**, but still cannot say **who should sit in the room and which decisions require which legitimacy key**, then it still lacks a compact constitutional holding and is likely to drift back into scattered note citation, convenience composition, or hidden power by board design.
