# 758 — Delegated-legislation and executive-rulemaking necessity tests for ideal governments: framework statutes, purposes, scope, time limits, scrutiny, publication, and no government by decree laundering

## One-line thesis

Ideal governments should treat delegated legislation as a **bounded implementation instrument rather than a spare legislature**: primary law should carry the main political choices, delegations should state purposes, subject matter, scope, and when needed time limits, parliaments should retain real scrutiny power, the public should be able to see and challenge what is made in their name, and executive rulemaking should never become a laundering route for major constitutional, fiscal, rights, or territorial change.

## Why this matters

The archive already says a great deal about **executives by scope** (`493`), **legislatures by scope** (`548`), **regulators and enforcement** (`545`), **budgeting and treasury** (`547`), **electoral administration** (`552`), **administrative procedure** (`573`), **local ordinance power** (`592`), **public-law homes for shared territorial power** (`611`), **regulatory homes for shared territorial power** (`618`), **constitutional review** (`756`), and **emergency powers** (`757`).

What it still lacked was one compact doctrine for a narrower but recurring constitutional question:

**when is executive rulemaking a necessary implementation lane, and when is it an undisclosed transfer of legislative power?**

That gap matters because many constitutions and statutes proclaim legislative supremacy while quietly allowing large parts of the legal order to be made through ministerial regulations, executive decrees, agency rules, circulars, or “temporary” emergency instruments. Some delegation is unavoidable in a complex state; legislatures cannot themselves draft every technical standard, fee schedule, procedural form, or operating specification. But without a visible delegation grammar, secondary legislation becomes a hiding place for major policy choices, last-minute rights burdens, fiscal extractions, or territorial rewiring that should have remained in primary law.

Ideal-government design therefore needs a doctrine of **delegated-legislation necessity**. The live design question is not whether delegation exists, but whether it is typed, bounded, reviewable, and subordinate enough that the legislature remains the true lawmaker on societally important questions.

## Necessity table

| Necessity test | What counts as proof | Honest design consequence | Default answer if proof is weak |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Need for technical or implementation detail** | the legislature can set aims and main choices, but detailed operational rules require expertise, updating, or granular specification | delegated rulemaking under a framework statute | keep the issue in primary law |
| **Need for speed without constitutional displacement** | rapid updating is required, but not enough to justify emergency powers or decree government | accelerated delegated rulemaking with notice, publication, and review | ordinary legislation or urgent legislative procedure |
| **Need for local or sectoral adaptation** | rules differ honestly across places, infrastructures, or regulated sectors | delegation to the level or body closest to the implementation burden, within statutory bounds | one central executive rulebook for convenience |
| **Need for specialized regulatory judgment** | independent or expert judgment is necessary to apply standards inside a bounded mandate | typed delegation to a regulator with reasons, consultation, and review | ministerial discretion without clear legal limits |
| **Need for temporary supplementary powers** | transitional implementation or emergency residue requires short-term rulemaking flexibility | sunset-bound delegated powers with reporting and expiry | open-ended enabling clause |

## Pattern pack

### 1. Keep the main political choices in primary law

The archive should begin from a firm constitutional rule: issues with major impact on rights, taxation, public spending authority, criminal exposure, institutional design, territorial competence, or durable policy direction should normally be resolved in statute.

Delegated legislation is appropriate mainly for implementing the legislature’s decision, not for making the core decision on the legislature’s behalf. If the delegated act would answer the question the parliamentary debate was supposed to settle, the design has delegated too much.

### 2. Require delegations to state purpose, subject matter, scope, and when needed time

Every serious delegation should answer four questions explicitly:

- what policy purpose the delegated power serves;
- what subject matter it covers;
- how far the rulemaker may go;
- and whether the delegation is continuing or expires after a stated period.

The archive should reject enabling clauses that merely authorize the executive to make “such provisions as may be necessary” without enough substantive boundaries to know what is actually being transferred.

### 3. Reserve especially sensitive fields unless the constitution or statute speaks with unusual clarity

Some topics require a higher burden of proof before delegation is honest: taxation design, criminal offences and penalties, coercive policing powers, broad rights restrictions, major fiscal reallocations, constitutional amendment by workaround, and territorial reorganization.

This does not mean no secondary law touches such fields. It means the legislature must carry the core normative burden and any delegated component must be unusually specific, narrow, and reviewable.

### 4. Match the rulemaker to the task instead of defaulting everything to the cabinet center

Different delegated acts belong in different hands. Some should be made by the cabinet or head of government; some by a line ministry; some by a specialist regulator; some by a municipality, metro, or region operating inside a statutory framework.

The archive should therefore prefer **typed delegation lanes** over generic executive ownership. Delegation should follow implementation logic and constitutional competence, not merely the convenience of the political center.

### 5. Build consultation, reasons, and publication into the delegated-law lane

Delegated legislation should not emerge as a surprise artifact. The public should be able to know that a rule is proposed, why it is justified, what legal authority supports it, and how comments or objections can be made.

Ideal governments should therefore require publication, visible legal basis, statement of reasons, and proportionate consultation for significant delegated acts, with especially strong disclosure where rules impose costs, sanctions, or rights burdens.

### 6. Give parliament a real scrutiny lane after delegation, not just a symbolic memory of control

Delegation does not end legislative responsibility. Legislatures need a live scrutiny grammar for secondary legislation: laying requirements, committee review, disallowance or annulment routes where constitutionally appropriate, and visible registers of instruments actually made under statutory authority.

The archive should reject systems in which parliament grants broad delegation once and then loses practical sight of what is being done with it.

### 7. Keep judicial review live and intelligible

Delegated legislation is law only because a higher law authorizes it. Courts therefore need a clear route to test whether a rulemaker stayed within the statute, respected procedure, gave lawful reasons, and complied with constitutional limits.

The archive should insist that delegated acts remain reviewable for jurisdiction, legality, proportionality where relevant, procedural defect, and inconsistency with the parent norm. A rule that cannot be challenged is too close to government by instruction.

### 8. Treat subdelegation as an exception that also needs law

A legislature may delegate to the executive, but that does not automatically justify the executive delegating again to administrators, contractors, or opaque guidance systems.

If subdelegation is needed, the enabling law should say so, set limits, and identify which matters may be further delegated and which must stay with the named public authority. Otherwise responsibility vanishes down the chain.

### 9. Distinguish guidance from law, and policy from binding norm

Circulars, manuals, interpretive notes, algorithmic decision rules, and procurement terms can quietly harden into de facto law if the archive does not force a distinction between guidance and binding legal effect.

Ideal governments should therefore require that anything binding on the public or materially decisive for rights, obligations, or access be issued through the proper legal instrument and not through unofficial explanatory text pretending not to be law.

### 10. Sunset exceptional delegated powers and consolidate the legal order afterward

Transitional or crisis-related delegated powers may sometimes be honest. But they should expire, be reported on, and either be replaced by ordinary legislation or be allowed to die.

The archive should therefore require that special enabling clauses, fast-track decree powers, and implementation residuary powers carry sunset logic, renewal tests where necessary, and consolidation of the surviving law so the legal order does not become unreadable through layers of temporary instruments.

## Guardrails

- Do not delegate the main political choice when the legislature can and should make it itself.
- Do not use vague enabling clauses that fail to specify purpose, subject matter, and scope.
- Do not hide major rights, fiscal, criminal, or territorial choices in secondary legislation.
- Do not allow guidance, circulars, or software rules to substitute for proper legal instruments.
- Do not assume parliamentary scrutiny exists just because delegation began in a statute.
- Do not permit unreviewable or unpublished delegated acts.
- Do not let emergency or transition delegations become permanent by neglect.
- Do not allow subdelegation to dissolve public accountability down the chain.

## Failure modes

- **decree laundering**: major policy choices are formally attributed to delegated legislation to avoid normal legislative conflict.
- **skeleton-statute drift**: parliament passes framework-free statutes that leave the real law to later executive choice.
- **guidance-as-law creep**: manuals, FAQs, portals, or procurement terms become binding without lawful instrument form.
- **scrutiny blackout**: delegated acts are technically legal but practically invisible to the legislature.
- **chain-of-delegation blur**: power is delegated downward until nobody can clearly name the accountable public authority.
- **temporary-forever rulemaking**: crisis or transitional powers remain in use long after their justification has expired.
- **publication failure**: the public is expected to obey rules they cannot reliably find, date, or authenticate.
- **parent-statute evasion**: delegated law quietly outruns the statute that supposedly authorizes it.

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **that the executive may make delegated legislation** but cannot also say **which matters must stay in primary law, what statute authorizes the rule, what purposes and limits bind it, how parliament can scrutinize it, how the public can find it, and how courts can test it**, then it still has **implementation rhetoric without a delegation constitution**.
