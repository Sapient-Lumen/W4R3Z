# 643 — Scope-reactivation certificates: dormancy, fresh clearance, and no constitutional necromancy

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should require one public **scope-reactivation certificate** before any materially stack-bearing authority, exception lane, shared body, or supervisory hold returns from retirement, dormancy, suspension, or expiry: old text, old systems, old habits, and old crisis muscle memory should not make public power live again without fresh legality, current rights checks, updated scope classification, and a visible register change.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a much stronger doctrine for the full visible life cycle of stack-bearing authority. It says **where functions should usually live** (`579`, `602`, `603`), **how burdens of proof and lawful rescoping should work** (`628`-`631`), **when the stack must be reopened and how it should be recorded** (`632`-`635`), **how scope-changing proposals should be classified, overridden, interpreted, and checked before launch** (`636`-`639`), and **how live arrangements should be observed, settled, retired, and cleaned up** (`640`-`642`).

What it still lacked was one explicit doctrine for a recurring multilevel failure mode: **the return of old authority without a new constitutional act**.

That failure appears in many forms. A temporary ministry clearance lane expires in law but operators keep routing cases through it because the workflow still contains the field. A metropolitan emergency command dissolves after crisis, yet the old incident portal, mailing list, and budget reserve make it the de facto front door again at the next scare. A retired authority is formally merged, but the vendor contract, domain name, and administrator roles survive, so people act as if the dead body still has hands. A dormant statutory power, unused for years, is suddenly invoked against a new political target with no fresh explanation of fit, consent, or rights impact. The archive already knows that temporary authority should not auto-resume (`453`) and that stale approvals should not renew themselves by use (`459`). What it still needed was the scope-stack version of the same rule.

That gap matters because old authority is often easier to revive than new authority is to justify. The legal text may still exist. The platform may still be installed. The procurement may still be live. The staff may still remember the workaround. The public may still recognize the old logo. Reactivation can therefore feel administrative, even when it is constitutionally material. But a polity that allows dormant, suspended, expired, or retired scope arrangements to come back by convenience is not governing by law and consent. It is governing by **constitutional necromancy**.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine of **scope-reactivation certificates**. Retirement (`642`) says how authority leaves the live stack. This note says how it may return, if it may return at all. Without that doctrine, the archive still permits one serious failure mode: **government by revival of whatever residue was cheapest to awaken**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish ordinary continued use from true reactivation

Not every exercise of authority is a reactivation event. Reactivation begins when a materially stack-bearing arrangement was previously marked as retired, dormant, suspended, sunsetted, handed back, emergency-only, or otherwise non-live, and is now proposed to become live again in whole or in part.

The key question is not whether someone can point to old text or old practice. The key question is whether the arrangement was already treated, in the live stack, as inactive or ended. If yes, bringing it back is not routine administration. It is a new constitutional moment.

### 2. Require one explicit scope-reactivation certificate before return to live status

Any materially stack-bearing return should travel through one named public act: the **scope-reactivation certificate**.

That certificate should state, in one place:

- what arrangement is being reactivated;
- whether the return is full, partial, temporary, emergency-bounded, or standby-only;
- the legal basis for return;
- the current scope packet, opinion, and override path where relevant;
- the affected canonical scope register entries;
- the practical go-live boundary for the reactivated authority;
- the rights, review, complaint, and challenge routes that will apply;
- the reasons old retirement, dormancy, suspension, or handback no longer govern;
- and the next review, sunset, or settlement clock.

Without that object, old powers return through scattered traces rather than through one accountable decision.

### 3. Dock reactivation to the prior lineage instead of pretending it is starting from zero

A reactivation certificate should not erase history. It should link back to the earlier life-cycle record where relevant:

- the prior **scope-retirement certificate** (`642`);
- any earlier **scope-settlement certificate** (`641`);
- the last **variance ledger** or observation window (`640`);
- the original **scope-diff packet** (`635`);
- the current **scope opinion** (`636`) and any **scope override** (`637`);
- and the live **canonical scope register** entry being restored, replaced, or newly forked (`633`).

That lineage matters because some returns are honest repairs while others are attempts to smuggle back a previously rejected design. The polity should not force later readers to rediscover that difference from folklore.

### 4. Classify what kind of return is actually being attempted

The archive should not let “reactivation” blur unlike events into one vague comeback story. The certificate should classify the return openly. At minimum it should distinguish:

- **like-for-like reactivation** — the same authority returns with materially the same scope;
- **partial reactivation** — only named functions, territories, permissions, or exceptions come back;
- **temporary bounded reactivation** — a short emergency or transition return with visible limits;
- **standby activation** — the arrangement becomes activatable again under named triggers but is not yet fully live;
- **reactivation-with-redesign** — the return is materially changed enough that the polity should admit it is not really the old arrangement returning unchanged.

This prevents old branding from hiding new constitutional content.

### 5. Treat old legality as evidence, not as enough

A dormant or retired legal basis may matter, but it should not be treated as self-executing proof that reactivation is still justified. The polity should ask again:

- do the territorial facts still fit;
- do the democratic and consultation requirements still fit;
- do current rights and remedy routes still work;
- do current fiscal and administrative capacities still support honest exercise;
- and would the same allocation still pass today’s burden-of-proof and honesty tests?

A power that once made sense may no longer fit current geography, technology, finance, or self-government guarantees. Reactivation should therefore begin from **fresh proof**, not from nostalgia plus a dusty clause.

### 6. Legacy systems, procurement, and emergency muscle memory should never self-reactivate authority

The archive should be explicit that old platforms, contracts, mailing lists, seals, templates, help desks, or crisis routines do not themselves make an authority live again.

Reactivation becomes constitutionally suspect when:

- a legacy system still routes approvals through a dead or dormant holder;
- vendor admin roles, workflow flags, or emergency playbooks re-enable powers without fresh legal routing;
- old grant or treasury templates restore upper-tier vetoes that had been retired;
- or staff continue using a suspended lane because it is easier than retraining.

In those cases the correct description is not “continuity.” It is attempted reactivation by residue.

### 7. Re-check self-government, supervision, and challengeability at the moment of return

A return to live status should not be judged only by the needs of the returning authority. It should also be tested against the rights of the authorities and publics affected by the return.

The certificate should therefore say:

- what consultation was required and with whom;
- whether any territorial or constituent consent lane was needed;
- what supervision powers revive and under what legal limits;
- how local or regional autonomy is preserved or narrowed;
- and what administrative, judicial, audit, parliamentary, or intergovernmental challenge routes are open.

Otherwise reactivation becomes a way to reintroduce control without reopening the rights questions that retirement, handback, or earlier settlement may have resolved.

### 8. Keep live, dormant, standby, suspended, and retired states visibly distinct in the scope register

The canonical scope register should not collapse all non-ordinary states into a single ambiguous field. It should visibly distinguish at least:

- **live**,
- **provisional live**,
- **standby**,
- **dormant**,
- **suspended**,
- **retired**,
- and **reactivating**.

That matters because the public and downstream operators need to know whether an authority is currently governable, merely activatable under named triggers, legally dead, or in the middle of a fresh return process. Ambiguity at this point invites exactly the kind of folklore and operational drift the recent doctrine cluster has been trying to eliminate.

### 9. When reactivation is not honest, choose a different lane instead of pretending the old one came back cleanly

Not every return should be called reactivation. When the old arrangement no longer fits, the polity should use an honest downstream lane instead:

- **keep retired** — the old arrangement remains dead;
- **build a new successor** — the need is real, but it requires a new design rather than revival of the old one;
- **temporary constrained lease** — a short exceptional activation may proceed only with hard clocks and narrow scope;
- **repair the receiving tier instead** — the problem is capability, not true need for return;
- **re-clear as redesign** — the returning arrangement is materially different enough that ordinary reactivation language would misdescribe it.

This matters because revival stories often hide the polity’s unwillingness to admit that it is really creating something new.

### 10. Pair reactivation with a new clock instead of restoring institutional immortality

A reactivation certificate should not simply wake the old arrangement and leave it on indefinite life support. It should set:

- the return date and operational boundary;
- the next review or settlement clock;
- any temporary ceiling or trigger condition for stand-down;
- the conformance and observability lanes that apply after return;
- and the conditions under which the arrangement must again suspend, retire, or seek redesign.

Reactivation should therefore reopen the life-cycle chain rather than bypass it. The point is not to trap every return in endless provisionality. It is to prevent the sentence “we had this power before” from becoming a license for institutional immortality.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat old statutory text, legacy contracts, or remembered workflow as sufficient authority for return to live status.
- Do not allow retired, dormant, suspended, or emergency-only arrangements to self-reactivate through platform defaults, budget templates, or crisis custom.
- Do not describe a materially changed comeback as like-for-like reactivation when it really needs a new packet, opinion, or consent lane.
- Do not revive supervision, veto, or approval powers without reopening the self-government and remedy questions they affect.
- Do not collapse live, standby, dormant, suspended, and retired states into one ambiguous register status.

## Failure modes

- **constitutional necromancy**: a dead or dormant authority becomes socially or operationally live again through residue, panic, or convenience without a fresh constitutional act.
- **legacy-text resurrection**: actors point to old law as though prior enactment alone proves present fitness and legality.
- **emergency boomerang**: a crisis lane that was meant to close after one event quietly becomes the default command route at the next disturbance.
- **platform-led revival**: systems, templates, credentials, or vendor workflows restore a past stack arrangement that law and public process had already ended.
- **reactivation laundering**: a materially redesigned arrangement is mislabeled as mere revival in order to dodge current review, consultation, or consent.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Does return from retirement, dormancy, suspension, or expiry require one explicit scope-reactivation certificate rather than use, memory, or convenience?
2. Is the certificate linked to the prior retirement, settlement, packet, opinion, override, and register lineage where relevant?
3. Does it classify the kind of return openly instead of telling one generic comeback story?
4. Is old legality treated as evidence to examine rather than automatic present-day authorization?
5. Are legacy systems, contracts, templates, and crisis routines prevented from self-reactivating authority?
6. Are consultation, self-government, supervision, and challenge routes re-checked at the moment of return?
7. Does the live scope register distinguish live, standby, dormant, suspended, retired, and reactivating states clearly?
8. When the old arrangement no longer fits, is the polity forced into a different honest lane rather than fake reactivation?
9. Does the return come with a new review, conformance, observability, or stand-down clock instead of institutional immortality?
10. Can a future reader tell exactly why the old arrangement came back, in what form, and under what fresh constraints?

## Closing image

A constitutional stack should not have zombies. When an old competence, emergency lane, supervisory hook, or shared body is truly needed again, the polity should be able to say: **this is what is returning, this is why the old closure no longer governs, this is the fresh legal and democratic basis, these are the rights and review routes now in force, and this is the clock under which the revived layer will live or die again**. Anything weaker is just **constitutional necromancy**.
