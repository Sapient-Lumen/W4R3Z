# 516 — Markets and company power by scope: local entry, metro space, national antitrust, and fair global rules

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should govern markets by keeping entry barriers, permits, public procurement access, and everyday consumer signal detection close to local life; using metropolitan and regional institutions to manage commercial geography, logistics, and market systems that cross municipal borders; reserving competition law, merger control, company law, securities rules, labour and consumer floors, and strategic industrial discipline to the nation; and using the global layer for trade predictability, anti-cartel cooperation, tax-base protection, and narrow common rules that prevent regulatory arbitrage without constitutionalizing corporate power above democracy.

## Why this matters

A polity can get democracy, welfare, and infrastructure mostly right on paper and still be ruled in practice by concentrated company power. The archive already says a great deal about capture, public integrity, utilities, labour, and food. What it still needs is one compact note on how ordinary market life and large firm power should be distributed across levels of government.

Markets are often governed badly because everyday entry and regional commercial geography are mistaken for the same problem as merger control, multinational tax games, or securities regulation. They are not. A street market permit, a taxi or vendor rule, a logistics corridor, an abuse-of-dominance case, a procurement standard, a corporate-governance code, and a global minimum tax arrangement sit at different scales. When one level tries to do all of them, it either strangles daily commerce with remote rules or leaves major concentrations of power essentially unchecked.

An ideal constitution therefore needs to separate small-scale market access from large-scale market power, and domestic market governance from the narrower global rules needed to stop cross-border arbitrage. The aim is neither laissez-faire mythology nor permanent ministerial micromanagement. It is plural, fair, contestable markets embedded in democratic law.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from open entry and anti-domination, not from aggregate output alone

A market constitution should ask whether new entrants, workers, consumers, and smaller producers can actually participate without being extorted, locked out, or silently subordinated by dominant intermediaries. Output alone can hide cartels, monopsony, rent extraction, and territorial exclusion.

### 2. Give micro-local institutions signal roles over everyday market failure

Residents, cooperatives, chambers, unions, market traders, and neighborhood organizations often detect exploitative fees, licensing choke points, local cartels, vendor harassment, and practical consumer harm earlier than national authorities do. They are useful for signal detection, consultation, and route correction. They should not run parochial anti-competition regimes or protectionist gatekeeping on behalf of incumbents.

### 3. Treat municipalities as governors of fair local entry, not as sovereign antitrust states

Municipal government is often the right level for vending rules, high-street and market design, transparent permits, local inspection practice, business-facing service speed, fair access to municipal procurement, nuisance control, and the reduction of petty administrative barriers that protect insiders. Cities can widen entry and reduce friction. They should not try to replace national competition authorities or invent incompatible company-law regimes.

### 4. Use metropolitan and regional institutions where real markets outrun one city

Commercial rents, logistics hubs, commuter catchments, wholesale markets, ports, airports, freight corridors, tourism systems, and housing-labour interactions often operate at metropolitan or regional scale. The archive should therefore prefer metro or regional governance for strategic land, freight, wholesale facilities, market observatories, and coordination where municipal fragmentation would otherwise let bottlenecks and local vetoes govern the economy.

### 5. Reserve anti-concentration and company law to the nation

National government is usually the right level for antitrust and competition law, merger review, abuse-of-dominance enforcement, company law, securities regulation, bankruptcy, core consumer protection, labour floors, corporate disclosure, and industrial-policy discipline. Those are the main tools for making firms legible to the polity and preventing private concentrations from becoming constitutional rivals.

### 6. Pair industrial policy with competition discipline rather than treating them as enemies

A serious polity may need strategic investment, public options, procurement leverage, or sector-building policy. But industrial policy without competition discipline becomes cartel policy by another name. The archive should therefore prefer structures where development strategy, procurement, and competition review can coexist without one silently swallowing the other.

### 7. Keep the global layer narrow: trade transparency, anti-arbitrage, tax floors, and cooperation against transnational concentration

The global layer should not function as a one-way constitutional shield for large firms. Its proper role is narrower: provide predictable trade rules and transparency, support cooperation against anti-competitive conduct that crosses borders, reduce races to the bottom in corporate taxation, and keep technical standards from becoming disguised barriers. That is enough to matter and still leaves democratic choice about domestic market design intact.

### 8. Treat procurement and market design as constitutional tools, not clerical purchasing

Public purchasing, concession design, market access rules, and platform interoperability choices shape whether economies stay plural or tip into dependency. The archive should therefore treat procurement and market-design choices as constitutional instruments that can either widen entry or lock public and private life into a few dominant suppliers.

## Guardrails

- Do not let municipalities weaponize licensing and zoning to protect incumbents against entrants.
- Do not confuse regional economic development with permissive concentration.
- Do not leave merger control, securities rules, or corporate disclosure to fragmented local regimes.
- Do not let trade or tax arrangements become a hidden veto on democratic market reform.
- Do not narrate procurement neutrality in ways that ignore concentration, lock-in, and public dependence.

## Failure modes

- **local protection rackets**: permits and inspections become tools for incumbent capture.
- **fragmented market geography**: each municipality guards its border while the real commercial system sprawls across the region.
- **national passivity toward concentration**: the state praises competition while dominant firms entrench through mergers, vertical lock-in, or political leverage.
- **industrial policy by favoritism**: subsidies and procurement pick winners without discipline, transparency, or rivalry.
- **global arbitrage constitutionalism**: firms play jurisdictions off each other on tax, disclosure, and standards until democratic states mostly administer the race.

## Practical tests

A scale-matched market constitution passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Are local entry barriers, permits, and public procurement rules designed to widen access rather than protect insiders?
2. Are metropolitan and regional institutions used where freight, wholesale markets, ports, or commercial geography obviously cross borders?
3. Does national government actually own antitrust, merger review, company law, disclosure, and core market-fairness rules?
4. Can industrial policy and procurement be used without exempting favoured firms from competition and accountability?
5. Does the global layer concentrate on predictable trade, anti-arbitrage, and cooperation against transnational concentration rather than insulating firms from democratic correction?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can describe **its business climate and growth strategy** but cannot show **who removes local entry choke points, which level governs regional market geography, who disciplines mergers and dominant firms, and how global tax and trade rules stop arbitrage instead of rewarding it**, it still has **an economy without a market constitution**.
