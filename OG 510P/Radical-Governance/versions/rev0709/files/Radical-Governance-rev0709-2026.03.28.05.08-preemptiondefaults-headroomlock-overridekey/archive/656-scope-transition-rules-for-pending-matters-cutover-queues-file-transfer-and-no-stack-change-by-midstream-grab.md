# 656 — Scope transition rules for pending matters, cutover queues, file transfer, and no stack change by midstream grab

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should require every **materially stack-altering act** to carry explicit transition rules for pending applications, permits, investigations, appeals, contracts, liabilities, records, and service queues — with named cutover dates, forum rules, file-transfer duties, and clock-preservation — so old matters do not silently jump sovereigns, stall in limbo, or get re-decided by midstream grab.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a strong doctrine for **when** the stack may change and **how** the change becomes visible.

It says how scope fits levels (`602`, `628`-`630`), when rescoping must be reviewed (`632`-`635`), how changes are classified and cleared (`636`-`639`), how they are watched, settled, retired, and reactivated (`640`-`643`), how defects and review lanes work (`644`-`652`), which sources outrank which (`653`), when a scope act is public enough to count (`654`), and why it should not rewrite the past by backdating (`655`).

But one practical constitutional gap still remains.

A polity may know exactly **which new arrangement is lawful** and still fail to say what happens to the **stock of matters already underway** when the cutover arrives.

A permit application was filed with the municipality last week, but the region becomes the new planning authority tomorrow. A social-benefit claim is mid-review when the function is re-centralised. A public-health investigation has already begun under one legal home when emergency command moves upward. A procurement, grant, or capital project is half-awarded under one tier but will be supervised by another. An appeal was lodged to the old body, but the new body claims the whole function. Operators are told to re-route the queue, yet the records, time limits, and complaint routes still point backward.

Those are not merely implementation details.

They are the place where multilevel systems often smuggle in a deeper constitutional claim: **if the pending stock is not explicitly assigned, whoever grabs the live queue becomes the real sovereign**.

That is dangerous for at least five reasons.

First, it undermines legal certainty. People cannot tell whether the filing rules, deadlines, reasons requirements, review path, or substantive law governing their matter changed when the authority changed.

Second, it defeats the archive's anti-backdating discipline (`655`). Even without rewriting the calendar, a sponsor can still seize a whole body of old matters and behave as though the new holder had always been entitled to decide them.

Third, it corrodes legitimate expectations. People who used the right route on the right day can find themselves punished because the polity changed its mind midstream.

Fourth, it damages administrative memory. Files, evidence trails, service histories, and appeal clocks get stranded between institutions.

Fifth, it invites constitutional opportunism. A sponsor that cannot lawfully reopen concluded matters may still try to dominate **pending** ones by leaving the cutover vague.

The archive already has adjacent instincts on official notice (`575`), case lineage (`578`), records transfer (`585`), conformance before go-live (`639`), runtime observability (`640`), promulgation (`654`), and retroactivity (`655`). What it still lacked was one scope-specific rule saying: **a stack change is not fully honest until the polity says what happens to the live stock of pending matters**.

That is the role of **scope transition rules for pending matters**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Every materially stack-altering act should carry one pending-matters schedule

A polity should not treat transition of live matters as an afterthought.

Every materially stack-altering act should therefore include one compact pending-matters schedule stating, at minimum:

- the cutover date and time,
- which matters stay with the old authority,
- which matters move,
- which matters are split by stage,
- which law governs each bucket,
- who holds the file and the clock,
- who gives notice,
- and which review route applies during transition.

Without that schedule, the stack change remains incomplete even if the new legal home is otherwise clear.

### 2. Separate future inflow from existing stock

A scope reform often has two different questions:

- who receives **new matters after cutover**, and
- who finishes or inherits **matters already underway**.

Those do not have to have the same answer.

In many cases the honest solution is:

- new filings go to the new holder,
- pending first-instance matters stay where they were lodged unless transfer is clearly justified,
- pending appeals stay with the review system originally attached to the challenged act,
- concluded matters remain concluded unless a lawful reopening route already exists,
- and ongoing supervision, enforcement, or compliance duties follow an explicitly named successor rule.

The archive should resist the lazy fiction that the future home of a function must automatically own the whole inherited stock.

### 3. Bucket the live stock by procedural posture, not just by policy area

Good transition design should classify matters by where they are in the process, not only by subject matter.

At minimum, a polity should distinguish among:

- matters not yet filed,
- matters filed but not yet accepted,
- matters accepted and under first-instance examination,
- matters awaiting hearing or decision,
- matters already decided but still under reconsideration or appeal,
- matters in enforcement or supervision,
- investigations already opened,
- contracts, grants, or procurements already launched,
- liabilities and obligations already attached,
- and records or archives kept for future review.

If those buckets are collapsed into one phrase like *all pending matters transfer*, the polity is usually hiding a midstream grab.

### 4. Preserve filing dates, clocks, and queue position across any lawful transfer

If a pending matter is lawfully transferred, the transfer should not erase the claimant's procedural place in line.

The receiving authority should inherit:

- the original filing date,
- any completeness determination already made,
- service and notice history,
- evidentiary submissions,
- hearing dates where possible,
- statutory clocks,
- and the party's place in the queue.

A transfer should be a **change of holder**, not a covert demand to start again.

### 5. No one should lose rights because the state changed the map midstream

Where a cutover creates confusion, the burden should fall on the polity, not on the individual or lower-tier body.

That means, at minimum:

- missed deadlines caused by official ambiguity should be excused,
- service on the old authority during a short transition window should still count,
- parties should not be forced to guess the new competent body without notice,
- and challenge periods should not quietly expire while government is still reassigning files.

In hard cases the honest default is to stop or extend the relevant clock until notice and responsibility are clear.

### 6. Pending appeals and review routes deserve special protection

The archive should be especially skeptical when a scope reform changes the forum for reviewing acts already taken.

If Authority A made the original decision, and Authority B later becomes the new holder of the function, the polity should not casually let B also decide the appeal from A's old decision unless the law very clearly provides for that and the review remains fair.

Otherwise the reform can become a way of redesigning the remedy mid-case.

As a default, the review lane attached to the original act should stay stable for that act unless a clearly lawful transition arrangement preserves equivalent protection.

### 7. Records, reasons, and personnel continuity should travel with the stock

A pending-matters rule is false if it moves legal title but not operational memory.

Whenever stock is transferred, the polity should explicitly assign:

- who holds the authoritative file,
- where the system of record moves,
- who signs continuity attestations,
- who remains reachable for factual clarification,
- how evidentiary integrity is preserved,
- and how parties can inspect the file after transfer.

Where necessary, staff secondments or continuity teams may be justified for a bounded period. But they should be typed as transition support, not as a hidden constitutional extension of the old arrangement.

### 8. Money, contracts, and liabilities should not be left behind the queue

Administrative stock is not just paperwork.

Pending matters often carry budget lines, contract commitments, grant obligations, fee flows, litigation risk, or future compensation duties. The archive should therefore require transition schedules to answer not only **who decides**, but also:

- who pays,
- who defends,
- who indemnifies,
- who inherits ongoing contract obligations,
- and who must answer for earlier procedural defect.

Otherwise the polity can dump the liabilities on one body while moving the decision power elsewhere.

### 9. Public notice should include both general cutover notice and case-level notice where needed

A general public announcement that *the function now belongs to the region* is not enough.

Where pending matters are individually identifiable, the relevant parties should receive case-level notice explaining:

- whether their matter stays or moves,
- which authority now owns it,
- what remains the same,
- what deadline changes, if any,
- where to send future submissions,
- and how to challenge the transition if they think it is unlawful.

This preserves legal certainty and keeps the cutover from becoming an inside-government routing fact that outsiders discover only after losing time.

### 10. When the transition design is incomplete, the default should favour continuity rather than opportunistic capture

A polity should declare one fallback rule for ambiguity.

The best default is usually: **the pre-cutover holder keeps responsibility for already-pending matters until a lawful and sufficiently specific transfer occurs**.

Sometimes a neutral continuity cell or expressly named successor body may be better.

What the archive should reject is the opposite default: whoever now claims the general competence may simply seize all live stock by implication.

### 11. Transition should end through visible exhaustion or certification, not by folklore

Pending-matters transition is a temporary constitutional condition.

The polity should therefore say how it ends.

For example:

- the old authority keeps the inherited stock until the stock is exhausted,
- or transfer continues only until a dated certificate shows all files, clocks, review routes, and records have moved,
- or a mixed arrangement expires after a fixed period and unresolved matters move by a second, clearly defined lane.

Without a visible end state, temporary transition teams become immortal dual government.

## Guardrails

- Do not let the new holder of a function quietly seize all pending stock by implication.
- Do not require parties to refile merely because the polity changed authority.
- Do not let appeal or review forums mutate mid-case without clear legal basis and equivalent protection.
- Do not transfer legal title without also transferring records, clocks, and budget responsibility.
- Do not let ambiguous notices cause missed deadlines and then blame the filer.
- Do not leave old and new authorities free to deny ownership of the same pending matter.
- Do not let temporary continuity teams become permanent shadow holders of the function.

## Failure modes

- **midstream grab**: the new authority claims all pending matters because the reform was silent.
- **limbo transfer**: the old authority stops acting before the new one is operational.
- **restart extortion**: parties are told to begin again from zero after lawful filing.
- **appeal trap**: the review forum changes mid-case in a way that weakens recourse.
- **records amputation**: the file moves incompletely or cannot be reconstructed.
- **double government**: both old and new bodies act on the same stock without a priority rule.
- **liability dumping**: the old body keeps the blame while the new body takes the live discretion, or vice versa.
- **immortal transition**: a temporary handoff team persists for years and becomes the real constitutional settlement.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Does the act say what happens to pending matters, or only who owns future inflow?
2. Are applications, appeals, investigations, contracts, liabilities, and records bucketed by procedural posture rather than lumped together?
3. If a matter transfers, do filing dates, clocks, queue position, and evidence travel with it?
4. Could a party lose a deadline, forum, or remedy merely because government changed its map midstream?
5. Is the polity using transition to do indirectly what retroactivity would do directly?
6. Have records, staff continuity, and budget authority been assigned honestly enough for the transition to work?
7. Are parties receiving case-level notice where their own matter is being moved?
8. If the schedule is ambiguous, who benefits from the ambiguity — the public or the authority trying to capture the stock?
9. Is there a visible end condition for the transition, or is the polity drifting into permanent dual government?
10. Could an outsider reconstruct who owned a live matter on any given day without private explanation from the sponsor institution?

## Closing image

A good multilevel constitution should be able to say: **when we move a function, we will not use the live queue as spoil**. New authority may govern tomorrow; old filings, open appeals, active investigations, and inherited records still need an honest rule today. The test is simple: **a stack change is not complete until the polity can say, for every matter already in motion, who holds it, which law and clock govern it, and how the person inside it keeps their rights.**
