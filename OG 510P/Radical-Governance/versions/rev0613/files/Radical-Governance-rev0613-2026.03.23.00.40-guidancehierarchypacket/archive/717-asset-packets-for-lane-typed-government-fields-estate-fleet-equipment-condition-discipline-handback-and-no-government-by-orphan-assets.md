# 717 — Asset packets for lane-typed government fields: estate, fleet, equipment, condition discipline, handback, and no government by orphan assets

## One-line thesis

Once the archive has lane packets, interface packets, a control plane, operating modes, repair and sunset rules, evidence, challenge, participation, oversight, amendment discipline, delegation rules, a fiscal packet, a capability packet, and an information packet for a field, it should also publish one narrow **asset packet** saying which lane owns and stewards the field’s estate, fleet, equipment, depots, labs, stores, and critical spares; who holds custody, maintenance, calibration, and replacement duty; what condition and resilience floor applies; what lease, host-site, or operator dependencies exist; and what handback rule survives exit or transfer — so the field does not decay into government by orphan assets, lease camouflage, or maintenance exile.

That sequence now explains who owns the field, how seams work, how it shifts mode, how the public can challenge it, how money, workforce, and information stay visible, and how delegated slices remain subordinate.

But one common real-world question still lacks a compact constitutional object:

**where do the field’s physical means of governing actually live once law, money, staff, and information become operational?**

The archive already has broader doctrine here.

- `518` says public investment and procurement should not hide maintenance and system stewardship beneath ribbon politics.
- `544` says delegated public functions cannot become constitutional escape hatches through contractors, concessionaires, or platforms.
- `586` says function transfer without staff, assets, records, contracts, and liabilities is only reassignment by legal text alone.
- `615` says service homes must include continuity, operator-chain legibility, and no government by memorandum network.
- `617` says real territorial government needs a capital home with lifecycle and asset-condition discipline rather than a project list.
- `619` says contract homes need real exit and handback capacity rather than concession patchwork.
- `690` says field continuity fails when premises, movement, stock, and equipment remain implicit.
- `713` says delegation is unsafe when the public lane loses step-in or handback capacity.
- `714` says a fiscal packet should name capital and reserve lanes, but money alone does not say who actually keeps the doors, vehicles, kit, and critical spares governable.
- `715` says a capability packet should name the workforce core, but staff without the physical substrate of work are still stranded.
- `716` says an information packet should name the canonical case and record homes, but a field still needs one visible answer to where its physical operating means reside.

This note does not replace those. It compresses them into one reusable packet for the newer field-constitution stack.

Without an asset packet, fields drift in predictable ways.

First, they drift by **orphan assets**: buildings, vehicles, specialist kit, labs, depots, or stores are essential to the field, but no lane can plainly say who owns them, who can dispose of them, who approves replacement, or who carries the maintenance backlog.

Second, they drift by **lease camouflage**: the field appears to have physical capacity, but the practical sovereign is whichever landlord, host agency, operator, or framework owner controls the premises, badges, keys, loading rights, plant rooms, and closure decisions.

Third, they drift by **maintenance exile**: fiscal and political attention fund visible expansion while maintenance, calibration, inspection, spare-parts discipline, and end-of-life replacement are exiled into invisible service contracts, host-government work orders, or unowned backlog.

Fourth, they drift by **handover fiction**: the field can supposedly retake, redesign, transfer, or sunset a service, but nobody has a complete register of the real estate, fleet, equipment, warranties, manuals, condition data, keys, badges, maintenance history, and critical spares needed to keep the field alive after a cutover.

An asset packet exists so a field can have a physical operating constitution as explicit as its authority, money, workforce, and information constitutions.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate the asset packet from the fiscal and information packets

`714` already says where the money architecture sits. `716` already says where canonical case and record truth sit.

The asset packet answers a different question: **what physical means must the field possess, steward, access, maintain, and recover in order to govern at all?**

A fiscal packet may say a transport field has a lawful capital lane and reserve ladder. An asset packet then has to say which lane stewards depots, rolling stock, maintenance equipment, critical spares, ticketing hardware, and access-controlled sites, and what happens when any of them become unsafe, unavailable, or trapped in another body’s custody.

If a note still says only that the field needs “infrastructure” or “facilities”, it has not yet described a governing field. It has described a backdrop.

### 2. Name one asset steward lane and one visible asset-class map

The packet should begin by naming the **asset steward lane**: the lane that is answerable for the field’s physical operating means even if some assets are leased, hosted, borrowed, concessioned, or maintained by others.

The packet should also publish one visible **asset-class map** covering at minimum the classes that matter to field continuity, such as:

- buildings, sites, and critical rooms;
- fleet and mobile equipment;
- specialist equipment, instruments, and calibrated kit;
- depots, warehouses, stores, and critical spare holdings;
- safety gear and continuity stock;
- and any shared or hosted physical assets without which the field cannot perform its public duty.

The point is not one giant spreadsheet in the note. It is one public answer to the question: **what physical substrate does this field actually depend on?**

### 3. Distinguish legal ownership, operational custody, access control, and maintenance duty

A serious asset packet should not collapse these into one vague word such as “responsible.”

For each critical asset class, the packet should distinguish:

- who legally owns or leases it;
- who physically controls custody and access;
- who operates it day to day;
- who maintains, calibrates, inspects, and certifies it;
- and who approves repair, replacement, disposal, or retasking.

This matters because fields often fail through split reality. One body owns the building, another controls badges, a contractor maintains the plant, a ministry holds the capital budget, and the service lane is blamed when the field cannot function.

### 4. Keep hosted, leased, shared, concessioned, and borrowed assets visible rather than letting them disappear into folklore

Many consequential assets are not directly owned by the field.

The packet should therefore say which critical assets are:

- leased from landlords;
- hosted by another public body;
- controlled through a concession or franchise;
- borrowed through mutual-aid or shared-service agreements;
- or embedded in an operator or partner site.

It should also say what rights of access, use, continuity, inspection, and step-in the public lane retains.

Otherwise a field claims to be governable while its real operating capacity is sitting behind somebody else’s locks and bargaining power.

### 5. Put condition, maintenance backlog, replacement cycle, and calibration discipline on one visible surface

A field should not need a crisis to discover that essential assets are unsafe, expired, badly maintained, or impossible to replace in time.

The packet should therefore say:

- what condition and criticality tiers exist;
- how inspection, testing, servicing, and calibration are scheduled;
- what backlog or deferred-maintenance rule is visible;
- what replacement horizon applies to aging or obsolescent assets;
- and what minimum spare, redundancy, or substitution rule preserves continuity for critical classes.

This is where the field stops treating physical reliability as decorative estate management and starts treating it as constitutional continuity.

### 6. Bind safety, accessibility, security, and resilience into the same packet

A good asset packet should not split these into different moral universes.

For critical asset classes, the packet should say:

- what safety and statutory-compliance regime applies;
- what accessibility and public-use floor still binds;
- what physical-security and key-control regime applies;
- what climate, hazard, or continuity adaptations are required;
- and what degraded-mode substitutes remain lawful if a site, fleet, or equipment class fails.

Otherwise one office optimises cost, another optimises security, another worries about access, and nobody is visibly accountable for the whole physical operating constitution.

### 7. Connect the asset packet directly to delegation, transfer, and sunset rules

The packet should dock explicitly to `586`, `713`, and `707`.

That means saying, for critical asset classes:

- what handback bundle is required at operator exit or function transfer;
- what manuals, warranties, maintenance histories, calibration records, parts lists, and keys or credentials must accompany handback;
- what condition floor must be met at return or transfer;
- what assets may be stranded, transferred, novated, sold, or retired;
- and what successor lane takes custody during sunset or rescoping.

A field that can change providers or transfer a function only on paper, while the live estate and kit remain trapped elsewhere, does not really own its own future.

### 8. Let asset stress trigger repair, amendment, capability review, or fiscal review

A normal asset packet should say what conditions force review, such as:

- repeated site unavailability or host-body lockout;
- critical equipment failure becoming routine;
- maintenance backlog breaching a visible threshold;
- repeated operation from temporary premises or substitute kit;
- inability to identify, locate, or verify critical spares;
- operator exit or transfer blocked by incomplete asset, warranty, or maintenance records;
- or a field whose public duties have outgrown the physical substrate it still claims is adequate.

Some of those triggers may open a repair packet. Others may require amendment, fiscal redesign, delegation review, capability rebuild, or outright re-scoping. The packet should say so explicitly.

### 9. Keep the packet small enough that later field notes can cite it rather than rebuilding estate-and-asset theory from scratch

A normal asset packet should fit in one compact block.

If a later note needs pages of prose to explain who owns the sites, who controls access, who maintains the fleet, where critical kit is located, how condition is tracked, and what survives provider exit, then the field still lacks a reusable physical operating constitution.

## Compact asset packet

A normal asset packet should usually fit in one table-like block:

| Field | Asset steward lane and critical classes | Custody, condition, and host rule | Handback / replacement rule | Review trigger and consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Housing** | housing lane stewards temporary-accommodation sites, repair depots, inspection kit, accessible units, emergency stock, and contractor-held keys or plant access needed for habitability | host boroughs or landlords may hold premises, but access rights, safety checks, condition reporting, and emergency-entry rules remain visible to the housing lane; critical classes carry an accessibility and safeguarding floor | operator or landlord exit requires keys, access lists, maintenance history, snagging register, warranties, asset-condition record, and emergency-stock inventory; unsafe stock cannot be handed back as a surprise backlog | repeated use of unsuitable temporary sites, unowned repair backlog, or access lockout opens repair, fiscal reset, or landlord/operator replacement |
| **Transport** | transport lane stewards depots, stations, vehicles, control-room kit, maintenance tools, power or signaling interfaces, and critical spares | operators may keep day-to-day custody, but the lane still names who controls depots, safety certifications, asset condition, and route-critical spare availability; host-infrastructure interfaces remain visible | franchise or operator replacement requires fleet condition files, maintenance records, spares ledger, depot access continuity, manuals, and key-control reset; replacement cycle cannot be postponed by contract opacity | repeated cannibalization of spare parts, depot lock-in, or fleet-condition drift opens repair, delegation review, or capital reprioritisation |
| **Care** | care lane stewards assessment sites, respite beds, transport vehicles, assistive devices, clinical or safeguarding equipment, and emergency continuity stock | shared hospitals or provider sites may host assets, but the lane still names who certifies safety, maintains devices, controls access, and protects continuity when a provider fails | provider exit or transfer requires device inventories, care-related equipment logs, maintenance and cleaning records, accessibility condition, and continuity stock handback | repeated operation from unsuitable buildings, expired kit, or inaccessible continuity stock opens oversight escalation, repair, or re-insourcing decision |

The exact field content will vary, but the packet form should stay recognizable.

## Failure modes

- **government by orphan assets**: essential sites, fleet, equipment, or stores matter to the field, but no lane can say who really stewards them.
- **lease camouflage**: the field appears to possess physical capacity, but the practical sovereign is a landlord, host body, or operator holding the keys, access rights, or closure decision.
- **maintenance exile**: upkeep, calibration, and replacement are politically and fiscally invisible until continuity fails.
- **host-site dependency**: the field claims autonomy while operating from premises or physical systems it cannot lawfully control during stress.
- **critical-spares folklore**: continuity depends on parts, stock, or specialist kit that nobody has inventoried, classified, or replenished visibly.
- **handover fiction**: provider exit, transfer, or sunset is supposed to happen, but the live estate and equipment cannot actually be retaken in working order.
- **asset-register theater**: inventories exist, but they do not say condition, criticality, custody, access rights, or continuity consequences.
- **temporary-premises drift**: a field runs for years from “temporary” sites, borrowed vehicles, or substitute kit without reopening its design, fiscal, or capability constitution.

## Anti-escape-hatch tests

1. Can the field name one asset steward lane for each critical asset class?
2. Can it distinguish legal ownership, operational custody, access control, and maintenance duty rather than hiding them inside one vague responsibility word?
3. Is there one visible asset-class map covering the physical substrate that continuity actually depends on?
4. Are leased, hosted, concessioned, shared, or borrowed assets explicit enough that the public lane can say what rights it still retains?
5. Can the field show condition, maintenance, calibration, and replacement discipline rather than only purchase history or nominal ownership?
6. Are safety, accessibility, security, and resilience obligations carried in the same packet rather than scattered across unrelated offices?
7. Can provider exit, function transfer, or sunset happen with a real handback bundle of keys, manuals, warranties, maintenance history, and condition proof?
8. Do repeated physical-operating failures reopen repair, delegation review, fiscal redesign, or amendment instead of another temporary workaround and another forgotten lease?
