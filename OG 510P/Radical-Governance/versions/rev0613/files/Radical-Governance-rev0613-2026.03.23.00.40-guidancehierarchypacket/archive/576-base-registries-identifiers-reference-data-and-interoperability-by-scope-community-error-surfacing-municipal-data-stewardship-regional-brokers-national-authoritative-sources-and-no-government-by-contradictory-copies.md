# 576 — Base registries, identifiers, reference data, and interoperability by scope: community error surfacing, municipal data stewardship, regional brokers, national authoritative sources, and no government by contradictory copies

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should treat **base registries, identifiers, reference data, and interoperability** as the **constitutional integration layer** of government: keep error surfacing, assisted correction, and local fact challenge close to people and firms; use municipalities and other frontline bodies as disciplined stewards and consumers of authoritative data rather than rival sovereign databases; pool interconnection, exchange infrastructure, and semantic support above fragmented local jurisdictions where scale adds competence; reserve national or constitutional-state authority for designating authoritative sources, assigning identifier rules, governing access, and maintaining shared code lists and metadata; and keep continental or global roles narrow enough to support cross-border evidence exchange, schemas, and semantic alignment without becoming a world master database.

## Why this matters

The archive now has tighter notes on **civil registration**, **land administration**, **addresses and administrative geography**, **entity registries**, **public records**, **trust services**, **administrative procedure**, and **official notice**. What it still lacked was the layer that explains how those official truths should relate to one another once the state stops being imagined as one office with one ledger.

That gap matters because modern governments do not fail only through bad law or bad intent. They also fail through **contradictory copies**: one office holds an outdated address, another issues a permit against a stale entity record, a third uses a different parcel identifier, a fourth demands a certificate that another public office already holds, and no one can clearly say which source is authoritative, which identifier governs, what metadata defines the meaning of the field, or who is allowed to correct the record. The result is administrative folklore disguised as digital modernity.

An ideal archive therefore needs one compact note on the constitutional integration layer. The right answer is not one giant database for everything, and not agency-by-agency duplication held together by clerical improvisation. It is a governed architecture of **authoritative sources, stable identifiers, shared reference data, explicit metadata, lawful exchange, logged reuse, correction routes, and narrow cross-border interoperability**. The state should be able to reuse what it already knows without asking residents and firms to carry documentary burden forever, but it should do so through disciplined authority maps rather than through ambient data hunger.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate authoritative source, working copy, local cache, and derived view

The archive should insist that these are different things. An authoritative source is the legally or administratively designated public record that owns a class of truth, such as civil status, address, parcel geometry, company existence, or a code list. A working copy is a downstream operational dataset used to perform a task. A local cache is a temporary or synchronised copy kept close to service delivery. A derived view is an analytic, reporting, or service-assembly output built from one or more upstream sources.

Good systems therefore reject a common failure: once a downstream office has a convenient copy, it starts acting as though it owns the underlying fact. That is how governments drift into contradictory copies. The point of interoperability is not merely to move data around; it is to preserve the distinction between **source authority** and **service convenience**.

### 2. Keep community error surfacing and lived-fact correction close to the ground, but do not let micro-local bodies mint sovereign truth by habit

At the smallest scales, people often know that a household has moved, that a business is a ghost, that a building number is wrong, that a death was never recorded, or that a person is being trapped by a stale or conflicting identifier. Community organisations, street-level workers, and trusted intermediaries can therefore play a legitimate role in surfacing mismatches, helping people navigate correction routes, and identifying when state data no longer matches lived reality.

But the archive should reject the idea that micro-local actors should quietly become the ultimate source of legal truth about identity, land, company status, or addressability merely because they see the problem first. Community proximity is valuable for **error discovery and assisted correction**, not as an unbounded substitute registry sovereignty.

### 3. Municipalities and other frontline bodies should be the ordinary public stewards of integration at the service edge

Most people meet the state through municipal counters, local registrars, permit desks, tax offices, libraries, clinics, schools, and other frontline institutions. Ideal systems should use these practical interfaces to help people correct records, declare changes, understand which source is authoritative, and avoid resubmitting evidence the state already possesses.

This does not mean every municipality should operate an independent master database. It means municipalities should be the ordinary service edge where authoritative data is consumed, challenged, and in some cases updated through governed interfaces. Frontline bodies should know which record owns which fact, which identifier should be used, when a local observation can update the source directly, when it must trigger review upstream, and how to show residents the status of that correction.

### 4. Regional, provincial, state, or intermunicipal layers should pool interconnection infrastructure where fragmentation would otherwise win

Tiny jurisdictions should not each have to invent a service bus, metadata catalogue, exchange gateway, code-list service, reference-data publication stack, or semantic-mapping programme. Where many municipalities share labour markets, utility systems, courts, transit, hospitals, or administrative ecosystems, the archive should prefer pooled integration infrastructure above the smallest scale.

The right answer is often a regional or provincial broker layer: shared data-exchange rails, common schemas, conformance support, metadata services, and operational coordination that let local agencies connect to authoritative sources without each building a bespoke mesh. The aim is not to erase local government. It is to prevent public interoperability from collapsing into vendor islands and one-off point-to-point arrangements.

### 5. National or constitutional-state authority should designate authoritative sources, identifier rules, and reference-data governance

Some parts of the integration layer are too central to legality, rights, statistical coherence, and whole-of-government usability to be left to local improvisation: which register is authoritative for which fact class; how identifiers are assigned, retired, merged, or corrected; which reference data and code lists are official; which metadata must accompany exchanges; what logging and audit requirements apply; what lawful basis exists for access and reuse; and how source conflicts are resolved.

The archive should therefore prefer a national or constitutional-state **authoritative-source map** and **identifier governance floor**. In federations, constituent units may own important registries and schemas, but there still needs to be a clear law-owning level that says which facts are constitutive, which are derivative, and which institution is allowed to overrule whom. People and firms should not have to discover the ontology of the state by trial and error.

### 6. Treat once-only as a burden-reduction discipline, not as an excuse for ambient state appetite

A good polity should not keep demanding the same certificate or fact from people and firms when one public authority already has it in governed form. The archive should therefore prefer once-only disciplines in which public bodies reuse official evidence at the request of or for the benefit of the user, through lawful, logged, and purpose-bounded exchange.

But once-only should not mean unlimited invisible circulation. It is not a licence for every public body to pull everything from everywhere. The state should reuse evidence because it has a lawful reason, a clear service context, an access rule, a logging trail, and a correction route. Burden reduction without bounded governance is just administrative surveillance with better branding.

### 7. Publish the semantic and technical means for reuse: metadata, code lists, schemas, interfaces, and service responsibilities

Interoperability fails less often because bytes cannot move than because meanings drift. A field name looks familiar but means something else. One address is a service address, another a location address. One code list is current, another is stale. One identifier is temporary, another constitutive. One exchange endpoint exists, but no one knows the conditions of access or the quality guarantees of the source.

The archive should therefore prefer systems that publish enough for disciplined reuse: metadata, glossary terms, code lists, schema versions, API or interface documentation, service responsibilities, assurance levels, and contact points for correction. A government that says a registry is authoritative but does not publish the semantic and technical means for others to connect has not yet governed interoperability; it has only declared aspiration.

### 8. Cross-border and global layers should stay narrow: semantic alignment, evidence exchange, and mutual recognition rails

Cross-border administration is real governance work. Migration, trade, family life, education, licensing, taxation, company operations, and statistics all create cases where evidence and data must move across borders. These cases benefit from common schemas, code lists, evidence vocabularies, interconnection rails, and carefully bounded recognition or exchange frameworks.

The archive should therefore prefer continental and global roles that remain narrow: standards, semantic alignment, mutual-recognition scaffolding, and treaty- or regulation-bound evidence exchange. What it should reject is the fantasy that the solution is one sovereign world base registry or one global master data lake. Cross-border interoperability should reduce documentary burden and translation failure without pretending all public authority has been constitutionalized at planetary scale.

### 9. Keep provenance, access logs, version history, and correction routing as first-class records

Integration is not trustworthy when data merely appears where it is needed. The archive should prefer systems that can later show which source supplied which fact, under what legal basis, through which interface, at which time, using which identifier and schema version, with what downstream transformations, and with what subsequent corrections.

This links the integration layer back to the archive’s work on records, trust services, administrative procedure, public memory, and notice. A polity that cannot later explain *which source was authoritative, which copy was derived, which exchange occurred, which identifier linked the records, and who corrected the mismatch* does not really have governed interoperability.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat every local database copy as if it had become an authoritative source by mere operational convenience.
- Do not let agencies demand evidence from people and firms when a governed public source already holds the relevant fact.
- Do not turn once-only into unrestricted background circulation of personal or organizational data.
- Do not let identifier assignment, merger, retirement, or correction remain ad hoc or vendor-defined.
- Do not publish schemas without code lists, glossaries, version discipline, and ownership responsibilities.
- Do not build interconnection through one-off bilateral pipes when a shared broker or common exchange layer is the obvious scale match.
- Do not let reference data drift silently across agencies until the same code means different things in different systems.
- Do not pretend that cross-border interoperability requires a universal master database rather than bounded exchange and recognition rails.
- Do not discard provenance and access logs once the data has been reused downstream.

## Failure modes

- **government by contradictory copies**: multiple offices hold conflicting versions of the same fact, and no one can say which one governs.
- **once-only theatre**: leaders promise burden reduction while residents still shuttle certificates between offices because exchange is not actually governed.
- **identifier chaos**: duplicate, unstable, or opaque identifiers make records impossible to link or wrongly linked.
- **semantic drift**: fields, code lists, and reference data look aligned but silently mean different things across agencies.
- **integration by vendor folklore**: public interoperability depends on contractor memory, bespoke mappings, and undocumented pipes.
- **authoritative-source ambiguity**: agencies reuse data without a clear account of who owns the truth class and who may correct it.
- **regional fragmentation**: each municipality builds its own exchange stack, so cross-boundary services collapse into patchwork workarounds.
- **global database fantasy**: cross-border burden reduction is pursued through over-centralised ambitions instead of narrow recognition and exchange lanes.
- **unreplayable reuse**: later reviewers cannot reconstruct which source data was used, under what basis, with what transformations.

## Practical tests

An integration architecture is scale-matched and lawful when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can the polity clearly distinguish authoritative source, working copy, local cache, and derived view?
2. Do people and firms have a recognisable local public route to challenge stale or conflicting official data?
3. Are municipal and frontline systems designed to consume and update authoritative data through governed interfaces instead of becoming rival master databases?
4. Are exchange gateways, metadata services, and semantic support pooled above fragmented local jurisdictions where scale adds competence?
5. Is there a clear law-owning level that designates authoritative sources, identifier rules, access conditions, and conflict-resolution authority?
6. Does once-only reduce burden through lawful, logged, purpose-bounded reuse rather than through unrestricted data circulation?
7. Are schemas, code lists, glossaries, interface documents, and ownership responsibilities published well enough for disciplined reuse?
8. Do cross-border arrangements stay narrow enough to support evidence exchange and mutual recognition without pretending to create one sovereign database?
9. Can reviewers later reconstruct which source supplied which fact, under what basis, through which interface, with what corrections?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can move data between offices but cannot tell you **which source owns the fact, which identifier governs the linkage, which code list defines the meaning, which rule authorises the exchange, which log records the reuse, and how a person can correct the result**, then it still has **data transfer without a constitutional integration layer**.
