# 426 — Equivalent human channels and assisted-digital parity

## One-line thesis

A public service does not stay fair merely because an AI path exists; people need reachable, timely, equivalently functional human or non-AI alternatives.

## Why this matters

Public institutions often promise that “a human is available” while the real service design says otherwise:

- the non-AI route is hidden,
- the alternative path is slower or more burdensome,
- people must first fail in the automated channel before help appears,
- support exists only during narrow hours,
- disabled users can technically request another route but cannot get one on equal terms.

Official service practice points in a stronger direction. GOV.UK’s service guidance says teams must make sure everyone who needs the service can use it, must research assisted-digital needs, and must tell users that assisted support is available. The guidance describes support through in-person, telephone, and webchat channels and stresses that users should not be excluded when they cannot or will not complete tasks online. Accessibility Standards Canada’s 2025 framework goes further by requiring equivalent alternative approaches, accessible disclosure and contestability information, and a multi-level optionality mechanism that lets impacted people request a timely alternative decision-making process either without AI or with direct human oversight and verification.

The archive should therefore treat **channel parity** as part of AI governance, not as a separate customer-service nicety.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Prove who needs alternatives before designing the channel mix

A service should not assume that digital-first equals accessible-enough. Teams should research:

- who cannot or will not use the automated channel,
- who needs support because of disability, language, trust, literacy, connectivity, or crisis conditions,
- where the AI path changes the burden of navigating the service.

If the team claims no alternative channel is needed, it should be able to defend that claim with evidence.

### 2. Offer an equivalent alternative, not a degraded exception

An alternative route should be equivalent in practical terms:

- similar timeliness,
- similar scope of service,
- no extra fee,
- no punitive delay,
- no hidden evidentiary disadvantage.

A fallback that exists only on paper or only after damage is not a real safeguard.

### 3. Advertise the alternative wherever the automated service appears

Users should learn about human or non-AI alternatives:

- on the service start page,
- inside the transaction,
- in letters, emails, notices, and scripts,
- in complaints, review, and appeal communications.

Do not force users to discover the alternative only by calling a general help line or escalating informally.

### 4. Support multiple assistance modes

Different users need different forms of support. Services should be able to provide assistance:

- in person,
- by phone,
- by webchat or relay,
- through trusted intermediaries where lawful and appropriate.

This is particularly important where identity, benefits, disability, housing, or immigration contexts raise both urgency and asymmetry.

### 5. Make opting for human handling possible at meaningful moments

Alternative routes matter most when the person can still avoid or mitigate harm. The archive should prefer choice points:

- before a consequential automated step,
- after a warning or risk flag,
- after an adverse or ambiguous outcome,
- during review or correction.

A human route that opens only after final denial is too late to count as design parity.

### 6. Measure parity, not just availability

Governance should track whether alternative channels are actually equivalent by comparing:

- wait times,
- completion rates,
- adverse-outcome rates,
- successful challenge rates,
- accessibility complaints,
- costs imposed on users.

Otherwise institutions measure the existence of a fallback rather than its usability.

### 7. Keep assistance and oversight mechanisms themselves accessible

It is not enough for the service interface to be accessible if the complaint form, oversight mailbox, appeal workflow, or support tooling is not. The archive should require accessibility across:

- notices and explanation documents,
- support tools used by staff,
- complaint and appeal channels,
- monitoring and governance processes.

## Guardrails

- Do not charge users for assisted access.
- Keep the alternative route visible and easy to trigger.
- Protect privacy when assistance is delivered through intermediaries.
- Train staff to handle escalation without forcing users back into the automated path.
- Review parity metrics after each major service redesign or AI scope expansion.

## Failure modes

- **slow-lane fallback**: the human route exists but is dramatically slower.
- **hidden alternative**: users are not told the non-AI or human option exists.
- **partial alternative**: the fallback cannot complete the same task end-to-end.
- **conditional dignity**: users must justify their need for help in intrusive or adversarial ways.
- **inaccessible recourse**: the complaint or review process is harder to use than the original service.

## Practical tests

A parity regime passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Has the team researched who needs human or non-AI alternatives?
2. Can users access an alternative route without punitive delay or cost?
3. Are support options clearly advertised at the points where users need them?
4. Can impacted people request a timely alternative process without AI or with direct human oversight?
5. Does the service measure whether the fallback is genuinely equivalent in practice?

## Compression rule for the archive

If the only realistic path is still the automated one, the service has not created a true alternative — it has created a **courtesy exception**.
