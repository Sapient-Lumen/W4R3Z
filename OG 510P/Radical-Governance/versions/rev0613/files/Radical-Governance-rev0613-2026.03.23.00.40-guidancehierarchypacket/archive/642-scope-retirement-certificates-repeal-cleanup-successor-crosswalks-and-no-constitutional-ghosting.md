# 642 — Scope retirement certificates, repeal cleanup, successor crosswalks, and no constitutional ghosting

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should require one public **scope-retirement certificate** whenever a materially stack-bearing competence, exception, body, supervisory lane, or temporary upper-tier hold is repealed, handed back, merged away, or superseded: retirement should close legal authority, budgets, systems, public channels, and review routes together, preserve historical memory, and refuse any afterlife of government by leftover residue.

## Why this matters

The archive’s stack-governance cluster now covers a great deal of the life cycle. It says **how scope should fit and when it must be reopened** (`628`-`632`), **where the live stack should be written down and remembered** (`633`-`634`), **how stack-changing proposals should be described, classified, overridden, and interpreted** (`635`-`638`), **how they should be checked before launch and watched after launch** (`639`-`640`), and **how provisional operation should become settled live status** (`641`).

What it still lacked was a clean doctrine of **ending**. A reform can now be launched honestly, observed honestly, and settled honestly — yet still leave behind a ghost constitutional layer when it later expires, is reversed, is merged into another tier, or is partially handed back. A grant condition disappears from law but remains in budget routines. A metropolitan workaround is legally dissolved but its portal, case queues, and admin permissions remain the public’s real front door. A temporary supervisory approval lane expires on paper, but operators keep asking for clearance because nobody visibly shut the channel. A merged authority loses its legal identity, but the old logo, complaint mailbox, reporting template, and vendor roles keep performing statehood after the funeral.

That omission matters because constitutional clutter does not disappear merely because the live stack moves on. The archive already rejects government by folklore on the way in. It should also reject **government by residue on the way out**. Otherwise repeal creates ambiguity instead of closure, handback creates duplicated authority instead of restored authority, and supersession creates overlapping memories in which nobody can later say which public body truly died, which one inherited the function, what liabilities and records moved, or whether the old layer remains partly alive through systems and workflow custom.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine of **scope-retirement certificates**. A settlement certificate (`641`) says what became the live baseline. A retirement certificate says what ceased to be live, what replaced it if anything did, what residue was cleaned up, what history was preserved, and what future readers should treat as definitively closed. Without that step, the archive still permits one serious failure mode: **constitutional ghosting**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Require one explicit retirement act for any materially stack-bearing closure

A materially stack-bearing competence, exception lane, oversight hook, shared territorial body, temporary upper-tier hold, or special-status arrangement should not be treated as gone merely because a sunset date passed, a statute was repealed, a merger was announced, or practice drifted elsewhere. Closure should require one named public act: the **scope-retirement certificate**.

That act is the moment when the polity says, in effect: this authority, body, or exception is no longer live; this is the date and legal basis of closure; and these are the successor or cleanup consequences.

### 2. Dock the retirement certificate to the whole lineage chain

The retirement certificate should link back to the full constitutional lineage where relevant:

- the prior **scope-diff packet** (`635`);
- the **scope opinion** (`636`);
- any **scope override** (`637`);
- the affected **canonical scope register** entries (`633`);
- the relevant **scope jurisprudence digest** if adjudication shaped the closure (`634`);
- any **handback or reversibility lane** that earlier justified temporary upward concentration (`631`);
- any **scope-settlement certificate** if the retiring arrangement had previously been promoted to settled live status (`641`).

A reader who cannot follow that chain should not be asked to believe that the old arrangement truly ended.

### 3. Classify what kind of retirement actually occurred

Not every closure is the same. The retirement certificate should therefore classify the event openly. At minimum it should distinguish:

- **full extinction** — the authority, exception, or competence lane ends without a like-for-like successor;
- **handback retirement** — powers return to a prior or repaired lower-tier holder;
- **supersession retirement** — the old arrangement ends because a newly authorised successor replaces it;
- **absorptive merger retirement** — the old entity or lane ends because its functions and liabilities are folded into another continuing body;
- **partial retirement** — only named functions, territories, supervisory hooks, or exception fields are closed while others remain live.

This prevents the sentence “the body was abolished” from hiding materially different constitutional outcomes.

### 4. Retire legal authority and operational residue together

A retirement certificate should not merely cite the repealing instrument. It should force one visible cleanup checklist across the coupled surfaces that make public authority feel real:

- legal powers and named competences;
- budgets, grants, earmarks, and reporting lines;
- staff positions, delegations, secondments, and signing authority;
- contracts, procurements, step-in rights, and vendor admin roles;
- case systems, portals, domains, APIs, forms, and identity-and-access permissions;
- seals, branding, public channels, and addresses for service;
- complaint, appeal, audit, and supervision routes;
- and records, data retention, and archival custody.

If those surfaces are not closed, migrated, or re-bound openly, the retired authority often remains socially live even after legal death.

### 5. Publish one successor crosswalk where anything survives

When retirement is not pure extinction, the public should get one clear **successor crosswalk**. It should say:

- which powers moved where;
- which pending cases moved where;
- which liabilities and contracts moved where;
- which records custodian now holds the archive;
- which complaint, appeal, or review route now applies;
- and which public channels are canonical going forward.

Redirects and forwarding links may be helpful, but they should never hide that a constitutional holder changed. A portal redirect should say “this authority has ended; your matter is now handled by X” rather than impersonating continuity.

### 6. Preserve historical memory without preserving live ambiguity

Retirement should not mean deletion. The retired layer should remain visible in the scope register and jurisprudence digest as **retired**, with dates, legal basis, and successor links where relevant.

That preserves auditability, rights history, and institutional memory. But the archived record should not be allowed to masquerade as current authority. The archive should prefer one historically preserved but visibly dead record rather than quiet disappearance or apparent ongoing life.

### 7. Block post-retirement exercise of authority without fresh clearance

After retirement, any continued exercise of the retired authority’s powers should be presumptively unlawful unless a fresh packet, opinion, and register update openly reauthorise it.

This matters because many ghost layers survive through “temporary carry-on” habits: legacy approvals signed on old letterhead, expired supervisory clearance that operators still seek, dormant committees that resume meeting, or retired system roles that still authorize actions because nobody rotated credentials. Retirement should therefore trigger active closure of decisional permissions, not just publication of a note.

### 8. Keep unresolved liabilities, disputes, and challenge rights visible

A body or lane may retire while disputes, audits, liabilities, or claims remain live. The retirement certificate should therefore say:

- which claims survive against whom;
- where pending judicial or administrative matters now sit;
- who has standing to challenge the retirement or its implementation;
- what temporary custodianship applies while matters wind down;
- and what review clocks still run.

Otherwise retirement can become a technique for dissolving accountability rather than only dissolving the institution.

### 9. Give retirement both a short public notice and machine-readable state change

The archive should prefer one short human-readable retirement statement plus one structured machine-readable update. The public notice should explain the closure, successor, and practical next steps in plain language. The machine-readable layer should mark the register entry as retired, update successor links, close exception flags, and schedule any post-retirement sweep dates.

Without this split, the polity drifts toward either obscure registry changes that no one understands or eloquent goodbye statements that cannot update the working stack.

### 10. Run one short post-retirement sweep for ghost residue

Retirement should not end with the announcement. The certificate should set one short cleanup sweep to check whether the dead layer still survives through:

- live budget lines,
- unrevoked system permissions,
- active URLs or forms,
- stale public signage,
- vendor escalation paths,
- operator manuals,
- or continued public routing.

If residue remains, the polity should either finish cleanup or admit openly that the old arrangement was not truly retired after all.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat repeal, merger, or sunset as sufficient closure if operational residue still performs the old authority.
- Do not hide successor changes behind silent redirects, inherited logos, or generic continuity language.
- Do not delete retired records in ways that erase rights history, liabilities, or challenge paths.
- Do not allow post-retirement exercise of authority under legacy credentials, templates, or workflow custom without fresh clearance.
- Do not let retirement dissolve accountability for pending disputes, audits, or obligations.

## Failure modes

- **constitutional ghosting**: an authority is legally dead but remains socially live through portals, permissions, workflows, or public habit.
- **repeal without cleanup**: law changed, but budgets, staff delegations, or system roles still embody the old stack.
- **redirect laundering**: a successor takes over, but the public is never told that the lawful holder changed.
- **historical erasure**: closure deletes the record so later reviewers cannot reconstruct who held power, when, and under what legal basis.
- **accountability evaporation by dissolution**: disputes or liabilities become harder to pursue because the retiring institution’s exit route was never named.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Does closure of any materially stack-bearing arrangement require one explicit scope-retirement certificate rather than silence, drift, or a bare repeal reference?
2. Is the certificate linked back to the packet, opinion, override, register, jurisprudence, handback, and settlement lineage where relevant?
3. Does it classify what kind of retirement occurred instead of implying one generic disappearance state?
4. Does it close legal authority and operational residue together across budgets, staffing, systems, channels, and remedy routes?
5. Is there one public successor crosswalk whenever anything survives or moves?
6. Are retired arrangements preserved historically but marked clearly dead rather than deleted or left ambiguously live?
7. Is continued exercise of retired authority blocked unless fresh clearance reauthorises it?
8. Are pending disputes, liabilities, and challenge routes still visible after retirement?
9. Does the public get a readable retirement notice while the machine-readable stack is updated accordingly?
10. Is there one short post-retirement sweep to detect ghost residue in practice?

## Closing image

A constitutional stack should end as honestly as it begins. When a competence, exception, body, or supervisory hold is truly over, the polity should be able to say: **this layer is dead, this is who inherited what if anything, these routes and residues were closed, this history remains visible, and nothing old still governs by leftover trail or login**. Anything weaker is just **constitutional ghosting**.
