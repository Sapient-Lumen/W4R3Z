# 678 — Shared-regime independence and conflict discipline: declarations, recusal, confidentiality, secondments, donor firebreaks, and no expertise by capture

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed integrity constitution** that separates disclosure, assessment, management, recusal, exclusion, confidentiality, secondment controls, donor firebreaks, and post-service limits — so expertise, review, and advisory work do not harden into captured governance.

## Why this matters

The archive now has notes on representation (`671`), officer and secretariat power (`673`), subsidiary bodies (`676`), and external participation (`677`).

But shared regimes often depend on technical experts, rapporteurs, review teams, advisory groups, secondees, philanthropic support, and close engagement with non-state actors. Those inputs can improve competence. They can also create a shadow constitution of influence if independence and conflict rules remain vague.

Without a typed integrity constitution, five familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **expert capture**: the same knowledgeable people shape the evidence base, draft the text, review the implementation, and assess their own prior work.

The second is **donor pathway government**: earmarked funding, sponsored travel, or programmatic dependence quietly determine which questions receive attention and which do not.

The third is **secondment laundering**: staff or advisers embedded from outside institutions are treated as neutral capacity even where their home-organization interests remain relevant.

The fourth is **disclosure without management**: forms are filed, but no visible rule says what happens once a material conflict is found.

The fifth is **confidentiality asymmetry**: outsiders receive privileged information without clear limits, while the public is told merely to trust the process.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **independence and conflict discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish integrity lanes rather than calling everything a conflict of interest

A serious regime should separate at least:

- general ethics obligations,
- personal conflict disclosure,
- organizational conflict review,
- independence requirements for experts and reviewers,
- confidentiality duties,
- gifts and hospitality restrictions,
- secondment rules,
- donor and earmark controls,
- recusal or exclusion decisions,
- and post-service limits where the stakes justify them.

One blurred idea of “conflict policy” is rarely enough.

### 2. Require disclosure before appointment and again before each sensitive assignment

Conflicts are assignment-specific as well as person-specific.

The archive should therefore prefer a rule that disclosure occurs:

- at nomination or appointment,
- before each committee, panel, or review assignment,
- when circumstances materially change,
- and after appointment if a new issue arises.

Otherwise the regime mistakes one old disclosure file for a living integrity system.

### 3. Define what must be disclosed broadly enough to catch real dependence

Material conflicts are not only direct payments.

The regime should ask for disclosure, fit to the role, of matters such as:

- employment and consultancies,
- board or fiduciary roles,
- grants and sponsored research,
- travel support,
- equity or intellectual-property interests,
- litigation or advocacy positions,
- close institutional affiliations,
- and any prior involvement in preparing the matter now under review.

The point is not punitive overcollection. It is to surface facts that could reasonably impair independence or create unfair informational advantage.

### 4. Separate disclosure from assessment and management

A filled form does not manage a conflict.

The regime should therefore name:

- who reviews disclosures,
- what threshold triggers further inquiry,
- what management options exist,
- who decides among them,
- and where the resulting management action is recorded.

Possible actions may include public notation, partial participation limits, recusal from one item, exclusion from one assignment, redesign of the review team, or refusal of appointment.

### 5. Type recusal, restriction, and exclusion by role and risk

Not every conflict requires the same remedy.

The archive should prefer rules that distinguish:

- advisory participation with disclosure,
- participation without drafting authority,
- participation without voting,
- item-specific recusal,
- document-access restriction,
- exclusion from a review team,
- and in serious cases, ineligibility for the role.

A regime should avoid both extremes: pretending every conflict is harmless, and assuming every disclosed interest requires total banishment.

### 6. Apply stronger independence rules where the regime claims neutral expertise or verification

The stricter the neutrality claim, the stronger the integrity lane should be.

Expert committees, scientific panels, inspectors, peer reviewers, investigatory units, and compliance reviewers should face tighter disclosure, assignment-screening, confidentiality, and recusal rules than ordinary open consultation participants.

A member representative may lawfully advocate a position. A supposedly neutral reviewer may not occupy the same constitutional posture.

### 7. Control secondments and embedded support openly

Borrowed capacity is sometimes necessary. It is never neutral by default.

The regime should therefore state:

- who may second staff or experts,
- for which functions secondments are prohibited or tightly limited,
- how role separation is maintained,
- who pays the secondee,
- what confidentiality and loyalty duties apply,
- and what disclosure of home-institution ties remains visible.

Where the role involves oversight, review, enforcement, or normative drafting, the archive should presume a stronger firebreak against embedded external control.

### 8. Build donor and earmark firebreaks

Integrity problems do not begin only with personal finances. Institutional dependence can bend the agenda before any individual form is filed.

A serious regime should therefore disclose or govern, as appropriate:

- donor-funded positions,
- earmarked support for specific workstreams,
- sponsored meetings or travel,
- in-kind support,
- and the line between acceptable support and agenda capture.

The archive should prefer a rule that support may not buy role selection, evidentiary weighting, privileged drafting access, or reviewer assignment.

### 9. Bind confidentiality to assignment and survival rules

Many integrity-sensitive roles require access to non-public material. That is precisely why confidentiality should be typed.

The regime should state:

- what confidential information may be accessed,
- by which role,
- under what undertaking,
- for how long the duty survives,
- what copying, sharing, and retention rules apply,
- and what happens if a conflict emerges after access has already been granted.

### 10. Publish one integrity map across the regime

A serious shared regime should maintain one visible map showing:

- which roles require declarations,
- which roles require heightened independence,
- who reviews disclosures,
- what management options exist,
- what secondment and donor rules apply,
- what confidentiality undertakings are required,
- and where recusal, exclusion, or appeal decisions sit.

If users must infer integrity discipline from scattered forms, ethics pages, consultant contracts, and annual reports, the regime has not built an integrity constitution.

## Working matrix

| Integrity question | Honest rule | Why | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| when interests must be disclosed | before appointment, before assignment, and on material change | keeps integrity rules live | one stale disclosure at initial appointment |
| what gets reviewed | personal and organizational interests plus prior involvement | catches real dependence and informational advantage | limiting review to direct current payments only |
| who decides management | named reviewer and management authority | turns disclosure into action | filing forms no one meaningfully assesses |
| what management tools exist | notation, restriction, recusal, exclusion, redesign, or refusal | matches remedy to role and risk | all-or-nothing conflict handling |
| how borrowed capacity is governed | explicit secondment and donor firebreaks | prevents hidden control through support channels | treating embedded support as neutral by default |
| how confidentiality works | role-bound access, survival duty, and breach consequence | protects sensitive processes without opacity theater | privileged access with no explicit duty or expiry |

## Guardrails

- Do not treat disclosure as the same thing as conflict management.
- Do not let experts review, verify, or adjudicate matters in which they recently played a formative advocacy or drafting role without visible management.
- Do not treat secondees and embedded advisers as neutral capacity by default.
- Do not allow donors or sponsors to buy assignments, agenda priority, or privileged drafting lanes.
- Do not grant confidentiality-sensitive access without a typed undertaking and survival rule.
- Do not hide recusal or exclusion practice behind purely discretionary case-by-case folklore.

## Failure modes

- **expert capture**: a small epistemic elite controls drafting, evidence, and review at once.
- **donor pathway government**: funding structure silently sets the constitutional agenda.
- **secondment laundering**: borrowed staff operate as if they were independent officials while carrying external loyalties or incentives.
- **disclosure theater**: forms exist but nobody can say what consequences follow.
- **confidentiality without integrity**: privileged access is granted without adequate review, undertakings, or exclusion rules.
- **recusal opacity**: participants vanish from decisions or remain on them with no visible rationale.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it distinguish ethics, disclosure, independence, confidentiality, secondment, and donor rules rather than collapsing them into one vague policy?
2. Are disclosures required before appointment, before assignment, and after material change?
3. Does the regime ask about relevant financial, professional, institutional, and prior-involvement interests?
4. Is there a named assessment and management lane after disclosure?
5. Are recusal, restriction, exclusion, and refusal of appointment typed separately?
6. Do reviewer, inspector, or expert-verification roles face stronger independence rules than ordinary consultation participants?
7. Are secondments and donor-funded support openly governed through firebreaks?
8. Are confidentiality duties attached to role, access, and survival rules?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can say **who sits in its committees and who may speak into its process** but cannot say **who disclosed what, who reviewed it, what management followed, which roles face heightened independence rules, and how donor or secondment pathways are constrained**, it still has **expertise and participation without an honest integrity constitution**.
