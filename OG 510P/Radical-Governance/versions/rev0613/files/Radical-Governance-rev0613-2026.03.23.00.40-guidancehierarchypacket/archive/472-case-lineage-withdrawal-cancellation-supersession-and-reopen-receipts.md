# 472 — Case lineage, withdrawal, cancellation, supersession, and reopen receipts

## One-line thesis

Consequential public-AI cases, appeals, incidents, and review requests should preserve typed lineage across withdrawal, cancellation, supersession, duplicate-merge, expiry, and reopening, with predecessor-successor links and reasoned receipts, so no rights-bearing matter silently disappears.

## Why this matters

The archive already knows how to say that a case exists, that a request is pending, and that a review is live. What it still lacked was a hard rule for what happens when a case stops being the active head.

A person files an appeal and later submits a corrected packet. An operator opens an incident, then merges it into a larger event. A supplier challenge is marked duplicate. A claimant withdraws one request and reopens another after new evidence arrives. A department cancels an intake as out of scope. In each of those moments, the danger is the same: a rights-bearing matter can look *gone* even though what actually happened was more precise.

If the archive cannot say whether a matter was withdrawn by the submitter, cancelled by the institution, superseded by a newer head, merged as duplicate, expired after named non-response, or reopened with continuity to the prior file, then disappearance starts to impersonate closure. That is not merely a records problem; it is a governance problem.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Give non-active case states typed names

At minimum, distinguish:

- **withdrawn** by the submitter or representative,
- **cancelled** by the institution,
- **superseded** by a newer case or artifact head,
- **merged duplicate** into another active matter,
- **expired or abandoned** after a named clock and notice path,
- and **reopened** after a prior closure state.

A generic inactive or closed label is too weak for consequential matters.

### 2. Preserve predecessor-successor links

Whenever a matter stops being the active head because another matter now carries the work, preserve both directions:

- predecessor case,
- successor case,
- reason for replacement,
- actor who caused the change,
- and timestamp.

People should not have to infer lineage from matching names or dates.

### 3. Keep the clock effect explicit

A lineage event should say what happens to the clocks.

Examples:

- appeal deadline preserved,
- response timer tolled,
- review clock restarted,
- or matter terminated with no continuing time rights.

Without clock truth, lineage becomes decorative rather than operative.

### 4. Separate voluntary withdrawal from institution-side cancellation

These are different governance acts.

- **Withdrawal** says the submitter chose to stop or replace the matter.
- **Cancellation** says the institution stopped the matter according to a named basis.

One should not be rewritten to look like the other in downstream summaries.

### 5. Treat supersession as narrowing, not deletion

If a newer packet, filing, or review request supersedes an older one, the archive should preserve the older matter as historical basis rather than erasing it. Supersession should answer:

- what was replaced,
- what remained unchanged,
- what new material caused the shift,
- and which public or internal surfaces now point to the new head.

### 6. Make duplicate merge visible without laundering disagreement

Duplicate-merge logic should preserve whether the duplicate was truly identical, substantially overlapping, or only administratively consolidated. Otherwise institutions can use duplicate handling to hide recurring complaints or repeated challenge attempts.

### 7. Reopen with continuity, not folklore

A reopened matter should preserve the prior head, the trigger for reopening, and the exact question of continuity:

- fresh matter with historical reference,
- or resumed matter with continuing clocks, evidence, and obligations.

A reopen should never depend on staff memory alone.

### 8. Surface lineage on both public and operator views where appropriate

Where a case has a public status surface, it should disclose enough lineage to answer whether the matter still exists, where it moved, and whether prior evidence remains in force. Operator views may carry more detail, but the public surface should still avoid disappearance by silence.

## Guardrails

- Do not let closed or inactive stand in for typed lineage state.
- Do not let a successor case inherit authority without a visible predecessor-successor link.
- Do not let withdrawal, cancellation, and supersession collapse into one generic resolved state.
- Do not restart or terminate clocks without saying so.
- Do not use duplicate merge to hide repeated complaints, repeated harms, or repeated challenge attempts.

## Failure modes

- **disappearance by status compression**: a matter looks gone with no explanation of what replaced it.
- **false closure by supersession**: a new head quietly erases the old matter's operative history.
- **clock laundering**: rights expire or restart without any explicit lineage receipt.
- **duplicate sinkhole**: repeated filings vanish into one supposedly duplicate bucket.
- **reopen folklore**: continuity depends on staff memory rather than typed records.

## Practical tests

A lineage-preserving case system passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can a user tell whether a matter was withdrawn, cancelled, superseded, merged, expired, or reopened?
2. If a new head replaced an old one, is the predecessor-successor link explicit?
3. Does the lineage event say what happened to the clocks and obligations?
4. Can duplicate merge be audited without losing evidence of repetition?
5. Can a reopened matter be reconstructed without relying on human memory?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a consequential matter is no longer the active head, but the archive cannot also say **what happened, who caused the transition, what replaced it, and what happened to the clocks**, then the archive is still letting **case disappearance impersonate closure**.
