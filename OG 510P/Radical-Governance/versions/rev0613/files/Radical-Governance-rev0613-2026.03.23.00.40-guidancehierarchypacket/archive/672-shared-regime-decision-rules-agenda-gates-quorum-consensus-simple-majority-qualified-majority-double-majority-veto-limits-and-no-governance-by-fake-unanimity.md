# 672 — Shared-regime decision rules: agenda gates, quorum, consensus, simple majority, qualified majority, double majority, veto limits, and no governance by fake unanimity

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed decision constitution** that separates agenda adoption, proposal circulation, quorum to open, quorum to decide, consensus practice, simple-majority procedure, qualified-majority substantive voting, double-majority shared-rule decisions, unanimity or veto for only narrow sovereignty-sensitive questions, and deadlock breakers — so bodies can act without either fake-unanimity theater or casual majoritarian override.

## Why this matters

The archive now has notes on integration depth (`663`), burden formulas (`664`), domestic landing (`665`), compliance (`666`), differentiated commitments (`667`), change (`668`), entry (`669`), exit (`670`), and representation (`671`).

But another constitutional object remains under-specified:

**once the right participants are in the room, how does the body actually decide?**

Without a typed decision constitution, shared regimes usually drift into one of three pathologies.

The first is **fake unanimity**: everyone says the body acts by consensus, but the real rule is informal pressure, silence, exhaustion, or chair-declared inevitability.

The second is **casual majoritarian override**: a body treats every question as though the same bare majority rule should govern budget tweaks, treaty interpretations, admissions, constitutional amendments, and procedural housekeeping alike.

The third is **deadlock without path**: a question is blocked in a subsidiary body or by a single participant, but the regime has no escalation rule, blocking-minority definition, review clock, or fallback lane.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **decision-rule discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate agenda and proposal gates from the final decision rule

A body is already exercising power before the final vote.

Agenda adoption, item deletion or deferral, allocation to committees, document circulation, and disclosure of legal, administrative, or budget implications all shape what can be decided and when. The archive should therefore require the regime to publish:

- who drafts the provisional agenda,
- who may propose supplementary items,
- who adopts or amends the agenda,
- what notice and circulation periods apply,
- and when a proposal is too late to be voted.

This is part of the decision constitution, not background administration.

### 2. Separate quorum to convene from quorum to decide

Some regimes require only enough presence to open debate, but a higher threshold to take a decision. Others use one threshold for both.

The archive should insist that bodies say explicitly:

- what quorum is needed to open,
- what quorum is needed to continue,
- what quorum is needed to decide,
- and how remote or proxy presence counts.

A body should not pretend it has authority to decide just because some microphones are on.

### 3. Use consensus as a working method, not as a fiction machine

Consensus can be useful because it preserves buy-in and reduces public winner–loser cycles. But consensus becomes dishonest when silence, fatigue, or ambiguous chair language stands in for actual non-objection.

The archive should therefore prefer a visible consensus rule that says:

- what counts as formal objection,
- who may object,
- how the chair tests for consensus,
- when reservations are recorded,
- and when failure to reach consensus triggers another decision lane.

Consensus should mean **no formal objection**, not **no one was willing to fight on camera**.

### 4. Use simple majority mainly for ordinary procedure and internal housekeeping

Not every decision needs a supermajority.

Simple majority is usually the honest rule for matters such as:

- procedural motions,
- meeting management,
- election where the rules say so,
- routine approvals,
- and other questions that do not alter the constitutional balance of the regime.

The archive should resist extending bare-majority logic to core institutional bargains just because it is faster.

### 5. Use qualified majorities for consequential but non-foundational substantive decisions

Many decisions matter too much for bare majority but do not justify unanimity.

Qualified-majority rules are often the right fit for:

- interpretations,
- waivers,
- budget or finance approvals,
- some admissions,
- some institutional appointments,
- and other consequential steps where wider support is required but a single holdout should not be dispositive.

The rule should say exactly what the threshold is and whether abstentions count.

### 6. Use double-majority rules where both units and underlying population or stake matter

Some regimes are not merely clubs of formally equal members. They are also systems where people, territory, or exposure matter.

In those fields, the archive should allow a double-majority rule requiring support by:

- a threshold of members or states,
- and a threshold of population, financial weight, or other constitutionally relevant stake.

But the relevant second limb must be declared in advance. A double-majority system should not smuggle weighting into the room only when politically convenient.

### 7. Reserve unanimity and veto for narrow constitutional or sovereignty-sensitive questions

Unanimity should be the exception, not the atmospheric default.

The archive should therefore reserve unanimity or veto-like powers for narrower categories such as:

- constitutional refounding,
- accession to highly consequential commitments where the text so provides,
- changes to core competence boundaries,
- and other existential matters where forced imposition would destroy the bargain itself.

Even there, the regime should say who holds the veto, on what questions, with what review or reconsideration clock, and whether constructive abstention or module opt-out is available instead.

### 8. Define blocking minorities and anti-hostage rules where the regime uses weighted or double-majority systems

A regime that uses qualified or double-majority voting should say what it takes to block, not only what it takes to pass.

Otherwise one actor may posture as decisive blocker without actually meeting the threshold. The archive should therefore prefer visible rules for:

- what counts as a blocking minority,
- whether abstention blocks,
- whether absent participants count,
- and how challenges to the count are resolved.

Blocking power should be constitutional, not theatrical.

### 9. Give subsidiary-body deadlock an escalation path

Not every committee should be a final cemetery.

If a subsidiary body cannot resolve a matter, the archive should require the regime to say whether the issue:

- moves up to a higher body,
- returns with drafting instructions,
- triggers mediation or consultations,
- carries to the next session,
- or lapses unless renewed.

A body that cannot decide should still know what happens next.

### 10. Publish a decision-rule map by body and by question type

The biggest source of confusion is often not the rule itself but its scope.

A serious regime should therefore maintain one visible map showing, for each body and each question type:

- agenda rule,
- circulation rule,
- quorum,
- ordinary decision rule,
- special-majority rule where relevant,
- who certifies the outcome,
- and what record of abstentions, objections, and vote totals is kept.

The archive should reject any regime where readers must reconstruct decision rules from custom, chair summaries, and memory of last year's emergency compromise.

## Working matrix

| Question type | Likely honest rule | Why | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| agenda adoption, scheduling, referral, ordinary procedure | simple majority or chair-plus-appeal under standing rules | keeps the body governable | letting agenda power hide as neutral clerical work |
| routine resolutions or ordinary administration | simple majority unless the constituting text says otherwise | speed and legibility | pretending every housekeeping issue needs unanimity |
| consequential but non-foundational substantive decisions | qualified majority | requires broader coalition without full veto | bare-majority override of major distributive choices |
| shared-rule decisions where both members and population or stake matter | double majority | protects both unit equality and underlying weight | one-dimensional voting in a visibly two-dimensional polity |
| constitutional amendments, core competence shifts, refounding | unanimity or very high supermajority if the text so provides | preserves the constitutional bargain | hiding constitutional change inside ordinary voting rules |
| committee deadlock or failed consensus | escalation, carry-forward, or referral rule | prevents silent burial | endless hostage-taking by unresolved blockage |

## Guardrails

- Do not call silence "consensus" unless the rulebook says silence counts and everyone knows the consequences.
- Do not use the same bare-majority rule for housekeeping, waivers, accession, amendment, and constitutional redesign.
- Do not hide agenda, referral, and circulation power outside the decision constitution.
- Do not let absent or abstaining members magically transform thresholds by implication.
- Do not give vetoes without naming their scope, trigger, and review lane.
- Do not leave subsidiary-body deadlock without an explicit next step.

## Failure modes

- **fake unanimity**: the chair announces consensus where there was only exhaustion or ambiguity.
- **threshold laundering**: a body claims wide support without saying whether the applicable rule was simple, qualified, or double majority.
- **agenda capture**: gatekeeping on agenda or circulation quietly determines outcomes before debate begins.
- **blocking theater**: actors claim veto power they do not legally possess under the actual rule.
- **committee graveyard**: unresolved questions disappear into subsidiary bodies with no escalation path.
- **constitutional change by ordinary vote**: a regime uses everyday decision rules to alter core bargains that should have traveled through amendment or refounding lanes.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it distinguish agenda and proposal gates from the final decision rule?
2. Are quorum-to-open and quorum-to-decide both visible?
3. If it uses consensus, is the objection rule explicit?
4. Are procedural matters, consequential substantive matters, and constitutional matters assigned to different rule families where appropriate?
5. If it uses qualified or double-majority voting, are the thresholds and blocking conditions public?
6. Are unanimity or veto rights confined to a clearly typed class of questions?
7. Does deadlock in a subsidiary body trigger an explicit next step?
8. Is there one public decision-rule map by body and question type?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can say **that decisions are made** but cannot say **how items get onto the agenda, what quorum applies, what counts as consensus, when majority becomes qualified or double majority, when veto or unanimity is lawful, and what happens after deadlock**, it still has **institutional language without an honest decision constitution**.
