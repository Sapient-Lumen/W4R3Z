# 652 — Scope desuetude limits, abandonment fictions, and no stack change by atrophy

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should not let **non-use, weak use, under-enforcement, dormancy, or degraded capacity** silently extinguish protected competence: materially stack-bearing authority may require review, repair, reactivation, or even lawful reassignment, but it should not be treated as lost merely because it was not exercised often, boldly, or recently enough.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a strong anti-drift chain for scope honesty. It says how a function fits a level (`602`, `628`-`630`), how scope review is triggered (`632`-`635`), how stack changes are classified and cleared (`636`-`639`), how they are watched, settled, retired, and reactivated (`640`-`643`), and how defects, pauses, severability, remand, relabeling, waiver, estoppel, and prescription should be handled (`644`-`651`).

That closes many ways of manufacturing title too cheaply. A sponsor cannot simply relabel a scope change as guidance. It cannot claim constitutional surrender from silence. It cannot turn reliance, sunk cost, or long operation into lawful authority. And it cannot say: we held this power long enough that the calendar made it ours.

But the mirror-image loophole still matters.

A central ministry says: the municipality has barely exercised this competence for years, so it has effectively abandoned it. A region says: the smaller local authorities were too weak to use their formal planning powers, so real planning authority has now naturally migrated upward. A shared service operator says: the member governments stopped meaningfully steering this function a long time ago, so their formal home should now be treated as historical only. A reviewer says: perhaps the competence was never lawfully reassigned, but since the entitled tier has not acted on it in practice, the question is now academic.

All of those arguments share one hidden premise: **unused authority stops being authority**.

Sometimes inactivity does matter. Dormancy can signal misfit, incapacity, fear, fiscal starvation, or duplication. A power that exists only on paper may require capability repair, connector repair, temporary assistance, or a fresh scope review. In some cases, a function truly should move — but through the right public lane.

What inactivity should not do, by itself, is answer the legal question of who lawfully governs what.

If the archive left that move unanswered, then even after rejecting title by duration, a sponsor could still say: **very well — then call it loss by atrophy**.

That is the role of **scope desuetude limits**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Keep non-use separate from lawful extinction

The first rule should be simple: a polity should distinguish **failure to exercise a competence well or often** from **lawful loss of the competence itself**.

Non-use may justify:

- capability repair,
- emergency support,
- shared-service assistance,
- triggered review,
- or a proposal for formal reassignment.

Non-use should not, by itself, count as repeal, surrender, or constitutional extinction.

### 2. Do not confuse incapacity with consent

Weak exercise often reflects resource and capability failure rather than genuine constitutional choice.

A municipality may stop acting because it lacks staff, money, data, equipment, or legal confidence. A region may under-use a competence because national ministries keep reclaiming the practical tools. A community tier may retreat because every consequential step is second-guessed by supervisory or digital chokepoints.

Those conditions can make authority fragile in practice. They should not be casually reinterpreted as willing surrender of the authority itself.

### 3. No desuetude through higher-tier crowd-out

A higher tier should not be allowed to crowd out a lower tier operationally and then point to the resulting inactivity as proof that the lower-tier power has withered away.

That pattern often appears through:

- detailed templates or guidance that leave no real discretion,
- grants or procurement conditions that pre-empt practical choice,
- software defaults that centralize decision rights,
- emergency routines that never hand back,
- or supervisory habits that make local initiative too costly to attempt.

A fair system should not let prior crowd-out become evidence of later abandonment.

### 4. Dormancy should trigger diagnosis before reassignment

If a competence has gone dormant, the first question should not be: who can take it over permanently? It should be: **why did it go dormant?**

The archive already has tools for that diagnostic work: capability congruence (`582`), distress and special measures (`583`), grant distortion (`585`), delegated-task blur (`588`), and scope audit triggers (`632`).

A polity should therefore ask whether the problem is:

- simple under-capacity,
- fiscal starvation,
- excessive supervision,
- digital lock-in,
- territorial mismatch,
- institutional duplication,
- or genuine vocation misfit.

Only some of those diagnoses justify reassignment. Many call instead for repair.

### 5. Protected self-government should be especially resistant to atrophy logic

The deeper the competence sits inside protected self-government, the weaker the desuetude argument should be.

Dormant initiative over a peripheral tool is not the same as claimed loss of:

- own competences,
- elected control,
- territorial planning power,
- local rulemaking,
- or ordinary control over coercive or distributive decisions.

Where the constitutional core is implicated, the burden should stay on the sponsor of change to show a lawful reassignment route, not on the weaker tier to prove constant vigorous exercise.

### 6. Under-exercise should feed support duties, not extinction fictions

Where a polity believes a tier is not using a competence well enough, the first honest response is often support:

- training,
- staffing help,
- shared technical services,
- fiscal equalization,
- interoperable tools,
- clearer legal guidance,
- or temporary supervision bounded by handback rules.

The existence of these repair options is one reason desuetude should be a weak path to extinction. If under-exercise can be repaired, the polity should not jump straight to saying the competence has silently died.

### 7. Do not let emergency substitution mature into ordinary loss

A function may be exercised by another tier for a period because of crisis, collapse, or special measures. That may be necessary. But emergency or distress substitution should remain visibly temporary unless and until the polity openly chooses a different settlement.

Otherwise temporary rescue becomes a path to permanent atrophy: the original holder stops acting because it has been displaced, and the displacement is later cited as proof that the original competence no longer truly exists.

### 8. Require explicit public acts for true surrender, repeal, or merger

If a competence is genuinely to be extinguished, merged, or permanently relocated, the archive should require the same visible lane it requires for other stack change:

- a packet (`635`),
- an opinion (`636`),
- an override if needed (`637`),
- a clear statement (`638`),
- and honest settlement or retirement machinery (`641`-`643`).

Desuetude limits add the companion rule: absent those acts, weak use is evidence of stress, not proof of lawful extinction.

### 9. Put the burden on the actor claiming abandonment

If a sponsor argues that a competence has effectively lapsed through non-use, the sponsor should have to show more than dormancy.

At minimum, it should explain:

- what legal act supposedly extinguished or relocated the competence;
- why non-use is not better explained by incapacity, fiscal pressure, crowd-out, or fear;
- what repair options were tried first;
- what review or challenge route remained realistically available to the entitled tier;
- and why a fresh lawful reassignment is not the more honest route.

Without that burden, desuetude arguments become an easy tool for powerful actors to harvest weakly defended powers.

### 10. Every inactivity-based disposition should update the live stack honestly

If a reviewer concludes that prolonged dormancy matters, the resulting public object should say exactly how.

It should state whether the result is:

- no change in title but a capability-repair order,
- temporary assistance,
- special measures with handback,
- a triggered scope review,
- prospective reassignment through a fresh lawful act,
- or a true retirement backed by the proper lane.

That prevents a quiet slide from “this competence was not being exercised well” to “everyone may now speak as though it no longer belongs here.”

## Guardrails

- Do not treat weak use, rare use, or non-use as self-executing repeal.
- Do not mistake incapacity, intimidation, or dependence for consent.
- Do not let crowd-out by a stronger tier become evidence that the weaker tier no longer owns the competence.
- Do not let emergency substitution mature into ordinary constitutional extinction without a fresh public act.
- Do not jump from dormancy to reassignment before trying diagnosis and repair.
- Do not narrate prospective restructuring as proof that the old title had already died on its own.

## Failure modes

- **desuetude federalism**: a competence is treated as extinct because it was not used often enough.
- **crowd-out laundering**: a stronger tier suppresses real exercise, then cites the resulting dormancy as proof of abandonment.
- **capacity-collapse forfeiture**: weak staffing, money, or systems are translated into constitutional loss.
- **emergency petrification**: temporary substitution during crisis becomes ordinary title because the original holder stopped acting while displaced.
- **paper-power cynicism**: real under-exercise is used to sneer at formal competence without testing whether repair would make the competence honest and live again.
- **non-use equals no-right myth**: the mere fact of dormancy is treated as though it were a visible repeal or surrender act.
- **retrospective extinction storytelling**: after a fresh reassignment is chosen, actors rewrite history as though the prior competence had already vanished years earlier.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Has the allegedly abandoned competence actually been repealed, merged, or reassigned by a visible public act, or is the argument resting mostly on dormancy?
2. Is non-use better explained by capacity weakness, fiscal starvation, supervisory pressure, digital lock-in, or fear of being overruled?
3. Did a higher tier contribute to the inactivity it is now citing as proof of abandonment?
4. Were repair options tried before reassignment was proposed?
5. Is the dormant competence part of the protected core of self-government where atrophy logic should be especially weak?
6. Did crisis substitution or special measures suppress the original holder's activity in a way that makes current dormancy a poor indicator of true misfit?
7. Would the polity still claim desuetude if the weaker tier had been well-funded and technically equipped the whole time?
8. Is the reviewer treating under-exercise as a problem to diagnose, or prematurely as proof that the constitutional home was always fictional?
9. If reassignment now seems wise, why is a fresh lawful packet not better than a story that the competence died by itself?
10. Has the live scope register been updated honestly to distinguish dormancy, repair, temporary substitution, prospective reassignment, and true extinction?

## Closing image

A constitutional stack should not let one actor say: **you did not use this power often enough, boldly enough, or recently enough, so it is no longer yours**. Dormancy may reveal weakness. Weakness may justify help, review, or even redesign. But a lawful competence should leave the stack through visible public authority, not by slowly starving in the dark. The honest answer is: **repair first, reassign openly if truly necessary, and never mistake atrophy for lawful extinction**.
