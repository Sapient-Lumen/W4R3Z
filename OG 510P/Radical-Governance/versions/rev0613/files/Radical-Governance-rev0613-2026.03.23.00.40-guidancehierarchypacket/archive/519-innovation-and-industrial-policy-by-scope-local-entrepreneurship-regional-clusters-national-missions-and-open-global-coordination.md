# 519 — Innovation and industrial policy by scope: local entrepreneurship, regional clusters, national missions, and open global coordination

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should govern innovation and industrial policy by keeping entrepreneurship support, practical diffusion, local supplier development, and maker or skills ecosystems close to firms and workers; using metropolitan and regional institutions to cultivate clusters, applied research linkages, transition strategies, and shared innovation infrastructure; reserving mission setting, major R&D finance, competition-safe sector policy, strategic trade and resilience choices, and national innovation floors to the nation; and keeping the global layer narrow, focused on open science, standards, subsidy transparency, technology cooperation, and anti-arbitrage trade rules rather than a world ministry of industry.

## Why this matters

A polity can secure rights and public services yet still fail to generate productive renewal. The archive already says much about education, work, markets, climate, and digital public infrastructure. What it still needs is one compact note on who should actually steer innovation and industrial change across scales.

Innovation policy is often mistyped in two ways. One is **capital-city missionism**: the nation writes grand strategies, funds prestige laboratories, and then wonders why diffusion, supplier upgrading, and regional transition never arrive. The other is **municipal boosterism**: every city declares itself a global tech hub and hands out inducements without the research base, labour pool, or coordination needed to make that claim real. Industrial policy then swings between subsidy races and laissez-faire sermons, while clusters, skills, and supply chains are left to luck.

An ideal government therefore needs a scale-typed innovation constitution. It should keep practical learning and small-firm capability close to the ground, place cluster and transition governance where real regional systems exist, reserve national capacity for missions and major sector tools, and keep the global layer narrow but important where standards, subsidy races, and knowledge exchange cross borders.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from diffusion and capability, not just frontier prestige

A good innovation constitution asks whether ordinary firms, workers, schools, and regions can absorb and use knowledge, not only whether a few frontier labs or unicorn firms exist. Innovation without diffusion widens territorial and class gaps.

### 2. Give micro-local institutions signal and access roles, not mini industrial ministries

Libraries, community colleges, maker spaces, cooperatives, unions, local business networks, and neighborhood institutions often see skill bottlenecks, supplier gaps, and entrepreneurial barriers early. They can help with outreach, access, and practical experimentation. They should not run fragmented subsidy wars or pretend every block needs its own sovereign industrial strategy.

### 3. Treat municipalities as governors of entrepreneurship conditions and local supplier development

Municipal government is often the right level for small-business interfaces, permitting speed, affordable workspace, incubation tied to real local strengths, municipal procurement access for smaller firms, and practical links between schools, colleges, and employers. Cities can widen participation in innovation. They should not assume that municipal branding alone creates a cluster or that tax giveaways can substitute for capability.

### 4. Use metropolitan and regional institutions where innovation ecosystems are actually territorial

Innovation clusters, applied research networks, industrial transitions, logistics ecosystems, advanced training pathways, and many supply chains usually operate at metropolitan or regional scale. The archive should therefore prefer metro or regional governance for cluster institutions, smart-specialisation choices, shared labs and testbeds, transition compacts, and innovation infrastructure that no one municipality can credibly own alone.

### 5. Reserve missions, major R&D finance, and competition-safe industrial strategy to the nation

National government is usually the right level for mission-oriented research funding, major science institutions, national innovation agencies, export and resilience strategy, strategic procurement at scale, sector-wide transition tools, competition-safe industrial policy, and the legal architecture for intellectual property, competition, labour, and securities. Those are the levers that can shape long-cycle direction without forcing every place to improvise a sovereign techno-state.

### 6. Pair industrial policy with openness, evaluation, and sunset discipline

A serious polity may back sectors, shape value chains, or build strategic capabilities. The archive should still prefer structures where industrial support is transparent, time-bounded, evaluated, and revisable, rather than becoming permanent rent shelter for politically connected incumbents.

### 7. Keep the global layer narrow: open science, standards, subsidy transparency, and fair trade constraints

The global layer should not dictate one industrial destiny for every place. Its proper role is narrower: support scientific cooperation and knowledge sharing, maintain open technical standards where needed, improve transparency around subsidies and trade frictions, and reduce destructive arbitrage in technology and production rules. That helps states cooperate without freezing domestic development strategy.

### 8. Treat regional transition as constitutional, not as cleanup after national strategy

When energy systems shift, legacy industries decline, or new technologies rewire value chains, the territorial pain is regional even when the political announcement was national. The archive should therefore treat regional transition institutions, retraining compacts, and shared adjustment finance as part of the constitutional design of industrial policy.

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse innovation policy with prestige real-estate branding.
- Do not let every municipality run a subsidy race for mobile firms.
- Do not let national mission language bypass competition, labour, or territorial fairness.
- Do not treat clusters as excuses for ignoring lagging regions and ordinary firms.
- Do not let global trade or IP rules become one-way shields against democratic industrial adjustment.

## Failure modes

- **boosterist fragmentation**: every city markets itself as a hub while real capability remains thin.
- **frontier-only innovation**: elite labs thrive while diffusion to ordinary firms and workers fails.
- **subsidy-clientelism**: industrial policy becomes a channel for permanent favored incumbents.
- **transition abandonment**: regions asked to absorb industrial change receive rhetoric instead of institutions.
- **global rule lock-in**: domestic innovation strategy is quietly subordinated to external standards or arbitrage games.

## Practical tests

A scale-matched innovation constitution passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Are entrepreneurship support and practical capability-building close enough to firms, workers, and local institutions to be usable?
2. Are metropolitan or regional institutions used where clusters, supply chains, and transition systems clearly exceed one municipality?
3. Does national government own the main mission, R&D, trade, and competition-safe industrial-policy instruments?
4. Are industrial supports transparent, evaluated, and sunset-able rather than permanent spoils?
5. Does the global layer concentrate on open science, standards, subsidy transparency, and fair trade constraints rather than acting as a remote industrial sovereign?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can describe **its innovation slogans and industrial ambitions** but cannot show **who broadens local participation, which level governs real clusters and transition regions, who owns national mission tools, and how global rules avoid hardening into anti-development lock-in**, it still has **an innovation narrative without an innovation constitution**.
