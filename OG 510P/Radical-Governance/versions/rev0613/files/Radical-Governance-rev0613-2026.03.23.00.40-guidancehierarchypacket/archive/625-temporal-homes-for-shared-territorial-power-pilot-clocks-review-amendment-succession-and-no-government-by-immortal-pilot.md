# 625 — Temporal homes for shared territorial power: pilot clocks, review, amendment, succession, and no government by immortal pilot

## One-line thesis

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, it should stop living in indefinite provisional status and acquire an explicit temporal home: a declared start basis, time-bounded pilot and transition rules where experimentation is still honest, published review clocks, visible renewal and amendment routes, sunset for temporary derogations, and lawful succession or dissolution rules so the polity does not govern for decades as an “interim” arrangement that nobody ever formally chose to keep.

## Why this matters

The archive already says when co-operation becomes government, and it now says what democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, participatory, knowledge, service, planning, capital, regulatory, contract, workforce, digital, emergency, redress, and territorial homes that government should have. What it still lacked was the **temporal analogue**.

That omission matters because many metropolitan and regional arrangements live in constitutional half-light. They begin as pilots, transition boards, temporary joint committees, implementation agencies, or prefigurative authorities. The political story says they are provisional. The operational reality says they are already making repeated territorial bargains on money, plans, services, regulation, data, and capital. Years later they are still there, but their legal and democratic treatment remains that of a stopgap.

This is one of the easiest ways to hide real government. A body can stay under-constitutionalized not because nobody relies on it, but because nobody ever forces the question of whether the experiment has ended, whether the transition succeeded, whether the settlement should be amended, or whether the institution should be reversed, absorbed, or dissolved.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine for the moment when a shared tier should stop living on silence, rollover, or pilot residue and acquire an honest time-structure of its own.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish pilot, transition, and permanent government

A pilot tests a bounded approach under uncertainty. A transition arrangement carries an agreed move from one constitutional state to another. A permanent tier is an enduring part of the governmental order.

The archive should keep these distinct. Confusion among them is one of the main ways institutions become both powerful and unowned.

### 2. Time-box experimentation where experimentation is still honest

Experimentation can be legitimate when the function is genuinely exploratory, the scope is bounded, the risks are known, and the legal basis says what is being tested. In that zone, time limits are a safeguard rather than a burden.

The archive should therefore prefer explicit pilot clocks, limited territorial or sectoral scope, predefined learning questions, and visible end dates for experimental arrangements.

### 3. Do not let whole-territory government remain in pilot status once repeated binding bargains are real

Once a metropolitan or regional body is repeatedly allocating burdens, sequencing capital, setting service obligations, carrying public budgets, or making binding territorial trade-offs, its provisional label should not excuse constitutional thinness. At that point the question is no longer whether experimentation is useful. The question is whether the polity is now governing by an immortal pilot.

### 4. Put review clocks in the founding basis instead of relying on political memory

A real shared tier should have review points that are written into its chartering law, statute, compact, or equivalent hard-law basis. Review should not depend on one minister remembering, one mayor demanding it, or one crisis triggering panic.

The archive should therefore prefer declared review intervals, named review bodies, published criteria, and a duty to produce and publish findings.

### 5. Separate operational performance review from constitutional reopening

Every institution needs operational scrutiny. Not every performance issue should reopen the whole constitutional settlement. The archive should distinguish:

- routine performance review of services, delivery, and management,
- scheduled constitutional review of powers, territory, finance, representation, and intergovernmental fit,
- and exceptional review triggered by material failure, legal change, or major territorial shift.

Without this distinction, review either becomes toothless ritual or perpetual destabilization.

### 6. Require affirmative renewal for temporary powers, waivers, and transition privileges

Temporary derogations often outlive the circumstances that justified them: special grants, relaxed procedures, host-government staffing, transitional voting rules, extraordinary borrowing permissions, emergency adaptations, or delegated ministerial exceptions.

The archive should reject silent rollover. Temporary advantages, shortcuts, and exceptions should either expire automatically or require an explicit public act of renewal with reasons.

### 7. Make amendment routes visible before conflict arrives

Shared territorial tiers change over time. Competences widen or narrow. Boundaries move. Representation formulas become unbalanced. Fiscal arrangements misfit. Service or regulatory roles harden.

An honest temporal home therefore needs visible amendment routes: who may propose change, what consultation is required, which majorities apply, which matters demand state legislation, which require constituent-government consent, and what review routes exist.

### 8. Build lawful succession and dissolution rules, not just birth stories

A polity that can create a shared tier should also know how that tier is altered, merged, absorbed, wound down, or dissolved. The archive should therefore prefer predeclared treatment for:

- assets and liabilities,
- debt and guarantees,
- staff and labor obligations,
- records and archives,
- live contracts and concessions,
- pending complaints, hearings, and litigation,
- ongoing services and emergency responsibilities,
- and democratic representation during handover.

Otherwise abolition becomes a constitutional spill rather than a governed act.

### 9. Use both internal and independent evaluation, and publish both

Institutional learning usually fails when only insiders review themselves or only external evaluators write reports that never dock back into administration. The archive should prefer paired review: internal learning close to implementation plus independent evaluation able to test whether objectives were met and whether reversal or redesign is warranted.

### 10. Tie evaluation to a response duty

A review that produces no required response is mostly ceremony. The archive should prefer a visible response lane: retain as is, amend, scale up, constitutionalize, scale down, reverse, or dissolve. Published evaluation should dock to a published governmental answer.

### 11. Keep review periodic, not permanently open

There is a trap opposite to immortal institutions: institutions whose core settlement is never secure because every bargaining round reopens territory, competences, finance, or representation. The archive should resist that too.

A temporal home should make reform possible without making the institution permanently provisional. Review clocks should discipline reopening, not universalize it.

### 12. Treat “temporary” labels as evidence-bearing claims, not rhetorical shields

If a body is called temporary, interim, pilot, prefigurative, transitional, or experimental, the archive should ask: temporary until what, measured how, reviewed by whom, and ending in which lawful state? If those answers are missing, the temporary label is probably constitutional camouflage.

## Threshold matrix

| Signal | Still honest temporary arrangement | Temporal home now required |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Purpose** | bounded learning or transition task | recurring territorial government is already happening |
| **Time basis** | explicit end date or review trigger | provisional label persists without a real clock |
| **Review** | predefined evaluation questions and review route | performance and constitutional review are both ad hoc or absent |
| **Renewal** | continuation needs an affirmative public act | exceptions and powers roll over silently |
| **Change** | amendment path is narrow because scope is narrow | powers, territory, finance, or representation may need structured revision |
| **End-state** | absorption, upgrade, or closure path is already known | no one can explain what happens if the body is upgraded, reversed, or dissolved |

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse time-bounded experimentation with under-constitutionalized permanence.
- Do not allow transition arrangements to become the permanent substitute for a real settlement.
- Do not keep whole-territory government in pilot status once repeated binding bargains are real.
- Do not let temporary waivers, host dependencies, or special grants survive by silence.
- Do not make evaluation optional after major territorial or institutional reform.
- Do not let evaluation become a report-writing ritual with no required public response.
- Do not reopen the whole constitutional settlement every year under the banner of flexibility.
- Do not design abolition, merger, or reversal after debts, staff, services, and records are already tangled.

## Failure modes

- **government by immortal pilot**: a body exercises real power for years under a provisional label that never forces constitutional choice.
- **permanent transition**: an institution remains in “implementation” status long after the underlying reform has effectively settled.
- **silent rollover**: temporary powers, privileges, or exceptions continue with no affirmative renewal.
- **review nihilism**: major reforms are never independently evaluated, so no one knows whether objectives were met.
- **reform amnesia**: lessons from prior rounds are not fed into later redesign.
- **perpetual reopening**: the institution never stabilizes because every budget or election reopens the full settlement.
- **abolition by spill**: closure, merger, or reversal occurs without clear succession for staff, contracts, debt, archives, or live cases.
- **temporary-word camouflage**: “pilot,” “interim,” or “experimental” language becomes a shield against democratic, fiscal, or legal upgrade.

## Practical tests

A polity is handling temporal design honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can it say whether a shared metropolitan or regional body is a pilot, a transition arrangement, or a permanent tier?
2. If the body is temporary, does the founding basis state the end date, review trigger, learning questions, and lawful next step?
3. Are operational review, constitutional review, and exceptional failure-triggered review kept distinct?
4. Do temporary exceptions, waivers, or privileges expire automatically or require visible renewal with reasons?
5. Is there a published amendment route for changes to competences, territory, finance, representation, or intergovernmental standing?
6. Are internal and independent evaluations both used, and are the results public?
7. Does each major review require a published governmental response rather than leaving findings to rot?
8. If the body were merged, reversed, or dissolved, are succession rules for assets, debt, staff, records, contracts, services, and live proceedings already knowable?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared metropolitan or regional body is still described as **temporary, interim, pilot, or transitional**, but no one can say **when that status ends, what review forces the next choice, how continuation is renewed, or how amendment, reversal, or dissolution would lawfully work**, then the polity has not built an honest temporal home. It is still governing by **immortal pilot**.
