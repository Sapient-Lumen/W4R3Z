# 665 — External commitment landing paths: signature, ratification, transposition, direct effect, operational owners, and no treaty by press release

## One-line thesis

Cross-border and global commitments should not stop at signature or ratification: an honest polity should show the full **landing path** from external commitment to domestic legal effect, operational owners, budgeting, reporting, review, and subnational fit, so treaty participation does not masquerade as implementation.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a stronger grammar for **ideal governments by scope** (`660`–`664`), but one major bridge was still too implicit.

A regime may be well designed at the international or continental level and still fail at the moment it touches domestic government. Many systems know how to celebrate signature, announce ratification, or invoke international obligation in speeches. Far fewer can show, in one legible chain, **how that obligation acquires domestic force, who must actually carry it, how lower tiers are implicated, what budget or staffing backs it, and what review route corrects failure**.

That missing bridge matters in at least four recurring ways.

First, external commitments often die in the gap between **consent** and **operationalization**. The state has joined, but no ministry owns the work, no regulator has rulemaking authority, no reporting channel exists, and no appropriation has been made.

Second, federal, regional, or local systems often smuggle in domestic constitutional conflict by speaking as though international consent automatically settles internal competence. It usually does not. A state can bind itself externally and still need a visible domestic settlement for which internal tier carries which duty.

Third, legal systems differ. Some obligations may have forms of direct domestic effect; others require transposition, framework legislation, delegated rules, regulator codes, contract changes, or budget authority. The archive should not pretend there is one universal landing mechanism.

Fourth, the same external regime can arrive through several domestic lanes at once: statute, administrative rule, inspection manual, data-reporting system, budget line, procurement clause, or municipal operating practice. Without one landing map, implementation dissolves into a hundred invisible fragments.

This note therefore adds a compact doctrine for **domestic landing discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate the external act from the domestic landing act

A polity should distinguish at least six moments:

1. negotiation or signature,
2. consent to be bound,
3. entry into force internationally,
4. domestic legal landing,
5. operational implementation,
6. compliance review and correction.

Those moments can be close together or far apart. But they should not be rhetorically collapsed into one event called "adoption".

### 2. Name the domestic landing type explicitly

Every external commitment should declare how it lands domestically. Common landing types include:

- **direct domestic effect** for sufficiently clear norms in systems that allow it,
- **transposition legislation** where domestic law must incorporate the rule,
- **framework statutes** that authorize later domestic rulemaking,
- **delegated regulation** by ministries or regulators,
- **licensing, inspection, or supervisory codes** for sector operators,
- **budget and program acts** where performance depends on finance and administrative capacity,
- **contract, procurement, or concession updates** where delivery chains carry the obligation.

The archive should refuse the lazy formula in which a government says it has "implemented the treaty" without naming which landing type actually makes that claim true.

### 3. Publish one domestic owner chain

Every landed commitment should have a visible owner chain:

- who interprets it centrally,
- who issues secondary rules,
- who funds it,
- who reports,
- who inspects or monitors,
- who answers complaints,
- and which court, tribunal, or review body handles challenge.

Some commitments also require a named focal point or co-ordination node that can speak externally and route internally. Where that is true, the focal point should be public and operational, not ceremonial.

### 4. Match the landing path to the obligation shape

Different external commitments need different domestic landing kits.

A reporting or notification treaty may need a focal point, cross-agency co-ordination, and data pipelines more than it needs a dramatic new criminal law. A technical standards regime may need regulator rulebooks, inspectors, and laboratory or certification capacity. A rights treaty may need judicial review, remedy routes, independent bodies, and procedural guarantees. A financing commitment may need appropriation law and intergovernmental transfer design.

The archive should therefore match the landing kit to the real obligation shape rather than assuming all implementation is "pass a law".

### 5. Do not let external commitment silently rewire the internal stack

A state may assume obligations internationally while still needing an internal constitutional settlement about which domestic tier carries the function.

If municipalities, regions, provinces, or states must deliver a major part of compliance, then the landing path should say so openly: what is national, what is subnational, what remains delegated, what money follows, what review applies, and whether any scope change is temporary or permanent.

The archive should reject the shortcut in which external commitment is used as a vague reason for domestic recentralization without a separate lawful scope settlement.

### 6. Surface reservations, declarations, and opt-outs where they alter the bargain

Not every party accepts every commitment in identical form. Reservations, interpretive declarations, opt-outs, differentiated timelines, and notified differences can materially change the real obligation.

The public-facing landing path should therefore show:

- what was accepted in full,
- what was reserved or declared,
- what timetable or derogation applies,
- and what domestic consequences flow from those limits.

Otherwise the polity ends up with two dishonest fictions at once: international overclaim and domestic under-implementation.

### 7. Pair legal landing with an implementation packet

A landed commitment is not yet a live commitment unless the operational packet exists.

That packet may include:

- budget authority,
- staffing,
- guidance,
- case systems,
- reporting schemas,
- inspection protocols,
- training,
- translation into local procedure,
- and public notice explaining how the governed will encounter the rule.

The archive should continue to distinguish **legal landing** from **operational readiness**.

### 8. Build review lanes that can actually correct failure

Domestic landing should also declare the correction path.

That means at minimum:

- legislative scrutiny,
- administrative or judicial challenge where rights are affected,
- audit or oversight where public capacity is involved,
- and periodic concordance checks asking whether domestic practice still matches the external commitment as amended or interpreted.

A treaty that cannot be challenged, audited, or periodically reconciled in domestic life is only half-landed.

### 9. Require concordance review after material external change

External regimes evolve. Amendments enter into force. standards move. reporting templates change. compliance deadlines mature. technical annexes update.

The archive should therefore require a visible domestic concordance review after material external change. The question is not merely whether the polity remains a party. It is whether the domestic landing path still matches the live external rule.

## Landing matrix

| External commitment shape | Usual domestic landing form | Minimum owner kit | Common failure mode |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| treaty with clear self-executing or directly effective norms | direct domestic effect plus interpretive guidance where needed | justice ministry, courts, front-line agencies, public notice | pretending direct effect eliminates the need for operational ownership |
| directive-style or framework obligation | transposition statute or equivalent domestic incorporation measure | lead ministry, legislature, delegated rulemaking lane, deadline tracking | claiming implementation before domestic measures exist |
| technical standards regime | sector regulations, supervisory manuals, licensing and inspection rules | regulator, inspectors, testing or certification capacity, reporting routes | joining the regime without sector oversight capacity |
| reporting / notification / alerting regime | focal point, interagency protocol, data and escalation pipeline | national focal point, sector nodes, emergency routing, audit trail | naming a focal point without internal co-ordination power |
| spending or financing commitment | appropriation and budget/program design | treasury, line ministry, transfer formulas, performance reporting | ratification with no money, staffing, or transfer design |
| multilevel territorial obligation | national settlement plus explicit assignment across regional/local tiers | national lead, subnational duty map, finance path, remedy lane | using international commitment to hide domestic recentralization |
| operator-facing implementation | procurement, franchise, concession, or compliance clauses plus enforcement | contracting authority, regulator, inspectors, operator reporting | assuming public obligations automatically bind outsourced delivery chains |

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse signature with domestic legal effect.
- Do not confuse ratification with operational readiness.
- Do not invoke an external obligation as a blank cheque for hidden rescoping.
- Do not leave focal-point or co-ordination roles symbolic.
- Do not let reservations, declarations, or notified differences disappear from public explanation.
- Do not assume courts alone can operationalize a commitment that plainly requires budgets, guidance, data, and staff.
- Do not assume sector operators are bound unless contracts, licences, or supervisory rules actually carry the obligation.

## Failure modes

- **treaty by press release**: signature or ratification is narrated as implementation.
- **one-act implementation fiction**: a single legal act is treated as enough even though rules, budgets, and procedures are missing.
- **focal-point theater**: a national contact point exists on paper but lacks routing authority or cross-government links.
- **federal laundering**: the center binds externally and silently pushes costs or duties downward without a visible internal settlement.
- **reservation blackout**: the public is told the polity joined a regime, but not what was reserved, delayed, or narrowed.
- **operator gap**: outsourced or regulated providers carry real duties in practice, but the landing packet never reaches them.
- **stale concordance**: the external regime evolves while domestic implementation remains frozen at an old version.

## Practical tests

A domestic landing path passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Has the polity separated signature, consent, entry into force, domestic legal landing, operational readiness, and compliance review?
2. Has it named the exact domestic landing type for each major obligation?
3. Is there one visible owner chain for rulemaking, funding, reporting, complaint handling, and review?
4. If lower tiers carry any part of compliance, is the internal scope and finance settlement public?
5. Are reservations, declarations, opt-outs, or notified differences visible to the governed?
6. Does an implementation packet exist beyond bare legal text?
7. Are challenge, audit, and legislative or judicial review lanes clear?
8. Is there a concordance review trigger for material external change?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can show that it has **joined** an external regime but cannot show **how the regime lands in domestic law, who carries it operationally, how lower tiers or operators are bound, what money and systems back it, and how drift will be reviewed**, then it still has **external commitment without domestic constitutional landing**.
