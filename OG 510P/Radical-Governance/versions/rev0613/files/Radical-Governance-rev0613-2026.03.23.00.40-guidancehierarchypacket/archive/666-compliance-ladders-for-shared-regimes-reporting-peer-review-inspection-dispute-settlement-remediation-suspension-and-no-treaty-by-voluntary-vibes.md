# 666 — Compliance ladders for shared regimes: reporting, peer review, inspection, dispute settlement, remediation, suspension, and no treaty by voluntary vibes

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **graduated compliance ladder** matched to the problem class — from reporting and peer review through technical verification, corrective action, dispute settlement, and where necessary suspension or other consequence — so co-operation does not depend on vibes and enforcement does not jump straight from silence to punishment.

## Why this matters

The archive now says more clearly what the **global toolkit** is (`662`), how cross-border regimes should choose their depth (`663`), and how shared burdens should be apportioned (`664`). But those gains leave one practical question hanging:

**how does a shared regime know whether members are actually doing what they agreed to do, and what happens when they are not?**

That question cannot be answered with one generic word like "enforcement".

Some regimes are mainly about comparable disclosure and mutual learning. Some need peer review and rating. Some need independent inspection because the relevant facts can be hidden. Some need adjudication because reciprocal rights are at stake. Some need corrective-action plans and capability support before punishment makes sense. Some need suspension or other hard consequence because the stakes are too high for permanent soft non-compliance.

Without a compact doctrine here, institutions oscillate between two bad poles:

- **voluntary-vibes governance**, where parties self-report and everyone politely hopes for the best, and
- **premature hard-sanctions governance**, where regimes threaten punishment before building the reporting, verification, and remediation ladders that make judgments credible.

This note therefore adds a typed compliance architecture.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate disclosure, assessment, verification, correction, and consequence

A compliance regime should distinguish at least five functions:

1. disclosure or reporting,
2. assessment,
3. independent verification where needed,
4. corrective action and remediation,
5. consequences for persistent or grave breach.

Many systems fail because they collapse those functions into one word like "monitoring". The archive should keep the ladder typed.

### 2. Start with reporting that is regular enough to be governing, not ceremonial

Most shared regimes need an information baseline before they can judge compliance honestly.

That means recurring reports, common templates, deadlines, contact points, data definitions, and enough comparability that one member cannot hide behind incomparable formats. Reporting is the floor, not the whole building.

### 3. Use peer review where convergence, learning, and reputational pressure are strong enough

Peer review is appropriate when the regime benefits from comparability, dialogue, and iterative improvement, and when facts are sufficiently visible that technical peers can judge them without a standing inspectorate.

The archive should especially value peer review where the task is to improve legal frameworks, administrative practice, or standard implementation across many members without pretending every shortfall is defiance.

### 4. Use independent technical verification where incentives to conceal are strong and the stakes are high

Some regimes cannot rely on self-report plus peer discussion because the relevant facts are hidden, strategic, or safety-critical.

There the honest lane is independent verification: inspections, audits, validation missions, sampling, or other technical measures carried out by an institution with enough autonomy and expertise to test what members say against observable reality.

The archive should especially expect this in domains where undetected breach creates catastrophic spillover or where self-reporting alone invites systematic evasion.

### 5. Pair findings with corrective-action plans before treating every failure as defiance

Not every gap is bad faith. Some are capacity failures, sequencing problems, or partial implementation gaps.

A serious regime therefore needs remediation lanes: recommendations, agreed action plans, timelines, technical assistance, and follow-up review. This is not softness. It is the mechanism that separates improvable weakness from persistent refusal.

### 6. Use ratings and public gradation only when the underlying criteria are explicit

Some peer or monitoring systems legitimately classify jurisdictions or parties through ratings, findings, or staged determinations.

That can be useful, but only if:

- the criteria are public,
- the evidence basis is challengeable,
- the phase distinction between legal framework and practice is visible,
- and the route from one rating to another is known in advance.

Otherwise ratings become prestige theater or geopolitical signalling instead of governance.

### 7. Use dispute settlement when reciprocal legal rights and obligations are central

Where parties hold reciprocal entitlements under a rule system — market access, trade commitments, or other mutually binding obligations — the regime may need formal dispute settlement.

That lane should be reserved for real legal controversies that require determination, interpretation, and remedy, not for every implementation shortfall. Adjudication is strongest when the regime can specify who breached what obligation, against whom, under what agreed text.

### 8. Reserve suspension and other hard consequences for persistent or grave non-compliance

Some breaches are too serious for endless dialogue. Others become serious because the member repeatedly ignores reporting, review, remediation, or adjudicated findings.

In those cases the regime may need hard consequences: suspension of privileges, restricted participation, intensified inspection, or other legally grounded limits.

But the archive should insist that these consequences come late in a visible ladder unless the breach is immediately grave. Hard consequence without prior architecture looks arbitrary; no consequence at all invites hollow commitment.

### 9. Always publish the re-entry path

A regime that can escalate but not de-escalate is poorly designed.

The compliance ladder should therefore say how a party returns to ordinary standing: what evidence closes the finding, who validates completion, whether monitoring remains heightened for a period, and how historical non-compliance is preserved without making rehabilitation impossible.

## Compliance matrix

| Problem class | Default compliance floor | Stronger lane when needed | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| co-ordination-heavy public-risk regimes | recurring reporting, focal points, comparability, technical support | expert review and targeted validation | pretending co-ordination works with no recurring disclosure |
| standards-convergence regimes | peer review against public criteria and implementation evidence | rating, follow-up cycles, targeted audits | calling every shortfall defiance when capacity support would solve it |
| high-hazard or concealment-prone regimes | independent technical verification and inspection | intensified inspection, special measures, suspension | trusting self-report where hidden breach has catastrophic risk |
| reciprocal rights regimes | consultations and structured dispute settlement | rulings, authorized countermeasures, compliance review | using ad hoc power politics instead of agreed legal process |
| mixed regimes with repeated but repairable gaps | recommendations, corrective-action plans, timed follow-up | public gradation, escalation, consequence for persistent breach | endless recommendations with no escalation path |
| regimes protecting club goods or shared credibility | transparency, peer review, public findings | suspension or restriction of privileges for persistent non-compliance | keeping all privileges while findings accumulate without effect |

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse reporting with verification.
- Do not confuse peer review with adjudication.
- Do not impose ratings unless the criteria and improvement path are public.
- Do not jump to sanctions where the regime never built basic disclosure or remediation lanes.
- Do not stop at recommendations when persistent breach clearly erodes the regime.
- Do not hide serious consequence inside informal political retaliation.
- Do not escalate without an equally visible path back to good standing.

## Failure modes

- **treaty by voluntary vibes**: a regime has obligations but no standing ladder beyond self-reporting.
- **monitoring blur**: disclosure, assessment, verification, and enforcement are all called "monitoring", leaving no one sure what has actually occurred.
- **peer-review cosplay**: reviews happen, but criteria, evidence, and follow-up consequences are too weak to change behaviour.
- **inspection without remediation**: technical findings accumulate with no realistic correction pathway.
- **sanction leap**: political actors demand punishment before the regime has built credible disclosure and validation.
- **consequence void**: recommendations recur indefinitely with no escalation for persistent breach.
- **no re-entry constitution**: a member can be downgraded or suspended but not clearly restored.

## Practical tests

A compliance architecture passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Has the regime separated reporting, assessment, verification, remediation, and consequence?
2. Are recurring disclosure duties frequent and comparable enough to govern from?
3. Is peer review used where learning and convergence are the right first instrument?
4. Is independent verification used where facts are hidden or the stakes are unusually high?
5. Are findings paired with corrective-action plans, timelines, and technical assistance where appropriate?
6. If ratings or gradations exist, are their criteria and upgrade paths public?
7. Where reciprocal rights are central, is there a real dispute-settlement lane?
8. Are suspension or other hard consequences reserved for grave or persistent breach under visible rules?
9. Is there an explicit route back to ordinary standing once deficiencies are corrected?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can describe **what members ought to do** but cannot show **how compliance is reported, assessed, verified, corrected, escalated, and eventually restored**, it still has **common rules without a real compliance constitution**.
