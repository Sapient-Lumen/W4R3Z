# 655 — Scope retroactivity limits, future-only defaults, curative acts, and no stack change by backdating

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should treat **materially stack-altering acts as future-facing by default**: if a sponsor wants retroactive effect, retroactive validation, or a backdated effective date, it should have to travel through an unusually narrow, explicit, legally justified lane with preserved **review routes**, **judicial remedy**, and clear **legal authority** rather than letting the stack change by historical rewrite.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a dense constitutional honesty chain for multilevel scope.

It says how scope fit is assessed (`602`, `628`-`630`), how drift is triggered for review (`632`-`635`), how proposals are classified and cleared (`636`-`639`), how they are watched, settled, retired, and reactivated (`640`-`643`), how defects and review lanes are handled (`644`-`647`), how anti-circumvention, consent, and reliance limits work (`648`-`652`), and which public surfaces count and when they count (`653`-`654`).

That closes many routes for cheating.

A sponsor cannot easily relabel a scope change as guidance, claim consent from silence, or rely on the loudest dashboard. It is increasingly hard to move the stack without packets, opinions, overrides, promulgation, and live register updates.

But one large loophole still remains.

A ministry says the new metropolitan authority was legally created in March, but its authority should count from the start of the budget year in January. A national law says a shared emergency command is now the proper holder of a function and then quietly declares all actions taken during the previous six months to have been lawfully taken under that new arrangement. A settlement certificate says the temporary central hold is now over, but also implies that the lower tier had never really possessed the competence during the provisional period. A retirement act dissolves a body today but narrates it as though the old authority had already died months earlier. A court or ministry saves past transactions for continuity and someone then retells that saving clause as proof that the unlawful scope arrangement itself was always valid.

All of these moves try to win the same prize: **if the polity can rewrite the legal past, it can often avoid admitting that the stack was previously wrong**.

Sometimes retroactive adjustment does have a place. Law may need to correct a clerical date. A legislature may need to preserve third-party continuity after a defect. A court may limit the past effects of a ruling to prevent chaos. A protective rule may even need narrowly justified retrospective benefit.

But none of those cases requires the stronger claim that a materially stack-altering settlement may ordinarily be **backdated into constitutional truth**.

If the archive left that move unanswered, then even after insisting on clear statements, authentic promulgation, and lawful cure, sponsors could still say: **fine — we will do the constitutional laundering later by changing the effective date**.

That is the role of **scope retroactivity limits**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Future-only should be the default for materially stack-altering acts

A polity should begin from a simple constitutional presumption: **materially stack-altering acts operate prospectively unless law very clearly and lawfully says otherwise**.

That default protects legal certainty, challenge rights, and ordinary public understanding of who governed when.

It also keeps the archive's newer packet, opinion, override, promulgation, and register machinery from being defeated by later historical rewrite.

### 2. Distinguish retroactive evidence, retroactive saving, and retroactive title

Not every backward-looking move is the same.

A polity should distinguish among at least three different things:

- **retroactive evidence correction** — clarifying what was already publicly true, such as fixing a publication timestamp or typographic error;
- **retroactive saving or continuity protection** — preserving some past acts or transactions so third parties are not punished for governmental defect;
- **retroactive title** — claiming that a new or defective scope arrangement was lawfully in force during an earlier period.

The archive should treat the first as often tolerable, the second as sometimes necessary, and the third as presumptively suspect.

### 3. Curative acts should cure prospectively wherever possible

When a materially stack-altering act was defective, the honest remedy is usually one of these:

- fresh authorization from now on,
- prospective cure,
- staged transition,
- selective saving of past third-party reliance,
- or an openly narrowed remedy.

The archive should prefer those routes over broad curative clauses that claim the defect never mattered.

A cure may fix legality going forward without fictionalizing the past.

### 4. Retroactive scope change should face the heaviest skepticism where coercion, money, supervision, or review routes are involved

The strongest presumption against retroactivity should apply where the claimed backdated change affects:

- who may coerce or inspect,
- who may tax, spend, charge, borrow, or seize,
- who supervised or could replace lower-tier action,
- who heard complaints or appeals,
- who exercised emergency command,
- or which public body residents had to obey.

Those are exactly the places where backdating can most easily erase accountability.

### 5. Retroactive clauses should not erase accrued challenge or defense rights

Even where some backward-looking saving is justified, a polity should be reluctant to extinguish rights that already accrued during the disputed period.

If people, municipalities, regions, workers, or counterpart bodies had a review route, defense, appeal, or objection at the time, a later scope act should not casually erase that route by declaring the past structure lawful after all.

Otherwise retroactivity becomes not merely a dating rule but a method for destroying recourse.

### 6. Urgency should use provisional lanes, not later backdating theater

A crisis may justify fast temporary concentration. The archive already has lanes for urgent action (`631`, `639`, `654`).

What a crisis should not justify is later pretending that the emergency holder had always possessed the competence from an earlier date, before the lawful emergency act actually existed or before the public head became operative.

The honest route is:

- emergency act now,
- publication now,
- temporary status now,
- review clock now,
- and later settlement, handback, or fresh authorization if needed.

Not: operate first and backdate the title later.

### 7. Historical cleanup should annotate the old stack, not rewrite it out of existence

Sometimes later records need to describe a previously messy period. The archive should allow careful historical annotation.

For example, the live register may say:

- this period was disputed,
- this function was exercised defectively,
- these acts were later saved for continuity,
- this cure was prospective only,
- or this ruling changed the legal understanding from a certain date.

What it should not say is that a contested or unlawful arrangement had been the lawful holder all along unless a constitutionally proper authority has truly and explicitly supplied that exceptional effect.

### 8. Put the burden on the sponsor when retroactivity is claimed

If a sponsor wants a materially stack-altering act to reach backward, the sponsor should have to show all of the following:

- the precise legal authority for retroactive effect,
- the exact period being reached backward,
- the public reasons why prospective operation is insufficient,
- the rights and reliance interests that would be harmed,
- why narrower saving or transition devices would not work,
- and how challenge rights remain preserved.

Absent that showing, the archive should presume prospective-only operation.

### 9. Save past acts narrowly without narrating saved acts as proof of valid title

A court or legislature may sometimes preserve past payments, permits, service actions, filings, or administrative steps to avoid punishing innocent third parties.

That may be necessary.

But the archive should insist on a second sentence: **saving some acts is not the same thing as validating the constitutional title under which they were purportedly taken**.

The polity should therefore separate:

- what is being saved,
- for whose protection,
- for what exact period,
- and whether the underlying scope title remains defective, cured only prospectively, or finally regularized.

### 10. Every retroactivity decision should leave a visible chronology trail

If any backward-looking effect is recognized, the public surfaces should show a clean chronology:

- adoption date,
- publication date,
- ordinary effective date,
- any claimed retroactive window,
- the legal source of that claim,
- whether the effect is title, saving, evidence correction, or remedial limitation,
- and whether the claim is contested.

That trail should appear in the authentic head (`654`), the scope register (`633`), and the related packet or review object.

Otherwise the polity will slowly slip into government by implied backdating.

## Guardrails

- Do not let curative language casually rewrite who governed during an earlier period.
- Do not let emergency residue be regularized by narrating it as if it had always been lawfully authorized.
- Do not let saving clauses for third parties be misread as full validation of defective scope title.
- Do not let retroactive operation erase accrued review, defense, complaint, or appeal rights.
- Do not let registers, dashboards, or press summaries flatten disputed chronology into one convenient date.
- Do not treat clerical correction as a pretext for constitutional backdating.

## Failure modes

- **backdated federalism**: a new holder of competence is declared to have governed from an earlier date for convenience.
- **curative-laundering clause**: a legislature uses a cure provision to imply the old unlawful stack had always been lawful.
- **saving-equals-title confusion**: protection of third-party acts is retold as proof that the underlying authority was valid.
- **emergency-origin myth**: a crisis workaround is later narrated as if it had already been ordinary legal title.
- **retroactive recourse erasure**: a later scope act wipes away challenge rights that existed during the disputed period.
- **single-date flattening**: adoption, publication, actual operation, backdated claim, and final settlement are collapsed into one false date.
- **register revisionism**: the live stack surfaces quietly replace contested chronology with an administratively convenient story.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Is the polity correcting the public record, saving particular past acts, or claiming full retroactive scope title? Which one exactly?
2. What is the explicit legal authority for any backward-looking effect?
3. Would prospective cure plus narrow third-party saving solve the problem more honestly than retroactive title?
4. Does the retroactive claim affect coercion, money, supervision, appeals, or emergency command, where skepticism should be highest?
5. Are accrued challenge, defense, complaint, or appeal rights being narrowed by the backward-looking move?
6. Is a sponsor trying to use later settlement to pretend the earlier provisional or defective period had already been lawful?
7. Could the same practical continuity be protected without changing the legal story of who governed when?
8. Do the authentic head, packet, register, and review surfaces all show the same dated chronology?
9. If the original scope event had been fully lawful and public, would anyone still need retroactivity now?
10. Would an outsider be able to reconstruct the true sequence without private explanation from the sponsor institution?

## Closing image

A good multilevel constitution should be able to say: **we can protect people from chaos without lying about who held power when**. It may save acts, limit remedies, or authorize the future. What it should resist is the temptation to wash an earlier defect by moving the calendar backward. The honest rule is: **repair forward where possible, save narrowly where necessary, and never let the stack change by backdating**.
