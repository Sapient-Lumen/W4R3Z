# 619 — Contract homes for shared territorial power: procurement, franchising, change-control, exit capacity, and no government by concession patchwork

## One-line thesis

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, it needs an honest **contract home** as well as a democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, participatory, knowledge, service, planning, capital, and regulatory one: a visible buyer-or-commissioner function, explicit make-buy-franchise tests, lawful procurement and concession routes, disciplined contract and change control, published performance and amendment trails, real handback and step-in capacity, and no territorial government by concession patchwork.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a substantial constitutional-upgrade ladder for shared territorial power. It can say when co-operation becomes government, what democratic form should follow, what fiscal base and operating core are needed, what accountability and public-law basis are required, how the tier should be wired into wider intergovernmental order, how residents should participate directly, what common knowledge spine the territory needs, how services, plans, capital, and regulation should be organised, and why real territorial government needs visible rulebooks instead of operator custom.

What it still lacked was the **contract analogue**.

That absence matters because many metropolitan and regional governments become real not only when they tax, plan, regulate, or operate, but when they decide how the territory will buy, franchise, concession, outsource, bundle, rebundle, extend, vary, rescue, or retake consequential services and assets. In practice, those decisions often determine the real constitution of transport systems, waste chains, utility interfaces, digital platforms, maintenance regimes, and long-lived infrastructure more than speeches, strategies, or even statutes do.

A shared tier may therefore have an assembly, budget, staff, legal personality, service powers, and a regulatory lane, yet still remain practically half-built if the real territorial bargains are hidden inside host-city procurement offices, inherited framework contracts, concession extensions, bespoke memoranda, vendor dashboards, change orders, or emergency direct awards. In that situation, government is real but its market-facing operating spine remains disguised.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine for the **contract home of shared territorial government**: when a real metropolitan or regional tier must stop governing through franchise patchwork and build its own visible buyer-or-commissioner constitution.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish the contract constitution from the service and regulatory constitutions

A metro or region should not be treated as having a contract home merely because it has service operators or a regulatory rulebook. The service constitution says **who is responsible for the service map and continuity**; the regulatory constitution says **what binding rules and permissions apply**; the contract constitution says **how the territory enters, manages, varies, disciplines, and exits market-facing commitments**.

If the real territorial bargains live in tenders, franchise agreements, concessions, framework call-offs, PPP deals, outsourced operations, major managed-service contracts, or long-tail amendment practice, then the shared tier needs more than generic procurement support borrowed from elsewhere.

### 2. Give the territory one visible buyer-or-commissioner spine

Once shared territorial power is real, the archive should prefer one visible territorial buying and commissioning spine: a named authority that can translate territorial plans, service obligations, and capital priorities into categories, pipelines, tenders, concessions, contract portfolios, and performance regimes.

This does not require one mega-office to buy everything. It requires one public answer to the question: **who speaks for the territory when it enters binding market relationships?** Without that answer, the territory is governed by whichever member government, utility, agency, or incumbent operator happens to hold the pen.

### 3. Use an explicit make-buy-franchise-partner test before locking in delivery form

A shared tier should not default to outsourcing, franchising, concessions, PPPs, intergovernmental delivery, or direct operation by habit. The archive should prefer an explicit comparison among the credible options before long-lived commitments are made:

- direct public provision,
- public-public shared service,
- standard procurement,
- concession or franchise,
- PPP or other risk-sharing infrastructure form,
- and bounded partnering where law and context justify it.

The point is not ideology. It is to prevent territorial governments from sleepwalking into delivery forms that misallocate risk, hide spending, weaken accountability, or trap future governments in inherited bargains.

### 4. Keep award authority, contract management, and operator performance distinct enough to stay honest

A contract home becomes dishonest when the same office defines need, writes the deal, approves the award, manages the relationship, verifies the service, approves amendments, and interprets disputes without meaningful separation. The archive should therefore prefer visible functional distinction among:

- the body that defines territorial need,
- the body that runs the competition or equivalent award process,
- the body that manages contract performance,
- the operator or supplier that performs,
- and the body that hears complaints, audits the record, or reviews disputes.

The split need not always mean separate institutions, but it should be real enough that contracting does not collapse into self-certification by the same actors who made the original bargain.

### 5. Dock the contract home into budget, service, capital, and regulatory disciplines

Territorial contracts should not float free of the wider constitutional kit. A shared tier should be able to show how each major contract or concession docks to

- an approved service obligation or capital objective,
- a lawful source of operating or capital funding,
- the territory’s regulatory rulebook,
- asset ownership and maintenance duties,
- and continuity or handback rules if the arrangement fails.

This matters especially for concessions, franchises, and PPP-style structures, where off-budget appearance, contingent liabilities, or change-order politics can quietly defeat the fiscal, service, capital, and regulatory constitutions that the archive has already built.

### 6. Treat contract management and change control as a standing public function, not an after-award orphan

A territorial government does not have an honest contract home if it can run tenders but not manage the resulting obligations. The archive should prefer a visible contract-management discipline that says

- what performance data the supplier must provide,
- how service quality and asset condition are checked,
- who can approve variations,
- what thresholds trigger reprocurement, escalation, or reapproval,
- how disputes are logged and resolved,
- and how lessons from implementation feed future buying.

Without this, the real constitution of the territory lives in informal variation practice and supplier leverage rather than in the original public decision.

### 7. Publish the contract life, not only the award ceremony

An honest contract home should not disappear once the contract is signed. The archive should prefer publication, to the extent lawful, across the full life of major territorial contracts and concessions: the procurement or award route, contract text, term, value logic, amendments, waivers, key performance commitments, major incidents, termination or extension decisions, and handback outcomes.

Otherwise the public sees only the award headline while the actual territorial constitution is rewritten later through amendments, direct awards, rescue deals, and quiet renewals.

### 8. Build real exit, handback, transfer, and step-in capacity before failure arrives

A metro or region is not truly governing its suppliers if it has no realistic way to exit or retake a failed arrangement. The archive should therefore prefer contracts and concessions with visible rules for

- data and records transfer,
- asset condition and handback,
- software and interface continuity where relevant,
- staff-transition planning where lawful and necessary,
- emergency substitute operation or step-in,
- and time-bounded recovery or reprovision routes.

Otherwise the territory’s supposed freedom to terminate is decorative, and the incumbent supplier becomes the de facto sovereign of continuity.

### 9. Keep category strategy and professional buying capacity at the territorial scale

A real shared tier should not rely entirely on occasional borrowed lawyers, host-city buyers, or consultants when it repeatedly procures the same classes of service, asset, or technology. The archive should prefer a minimum professional buying and commercial spine at territorial scale: category knowledge, market intelligence, standard playbooks, risk registers, and enough institutional memory to detect when the same mistakes are being purchased again.

This is not a plea for giant procurement bureaucracies. It is a plea against territorial government by episodic consultant memory and incumbent vendor advantage.

### 10. Treat concessions, franchises, and PPPs as constitutional choices, not only commercial ones

Some contracts are ordinary purchases. Others effectively allocate pricing power, investment timing, data access, labour conditions, infrastructure risk, or emergency continuity for years across an entire territory. The archive should treat the latter as constitutional choices.

That means they should face stronger public explanation, comparison against alternatives, scrutiny, amendment discipline, and handback planning than an ordinary purchase order. Otherwise metropolitan or regional government may speak democratically in public while being ruled commercially in practice.

## Contract-home matrix

| Question | Thin or dishonest arrangement | Honest contract home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Who binds the territory to suppliers?** | host-city pen, inherited frameworks, incumbent deals | visible buyer-or-commissioner spine for the shared tier |
| **How is delivery form chosen?** | outsourcing or concession by habit | explicit make-buy-franchise-partner comparison |
| **Where do contracts dock?** | deals float free of plan, budget, and regulation | contract portfolio tied to service, fiscal, capital, and regulatory constitutions |
| **Who manages the contract after signature?** | no clear owner after award | named contract-management and change-control function |
| **How visible is the contract life?** | award notice only; amendments and waivers disappear | publication across award, performance, amendment, extension, and exit |
| **Can the territory really exit failure?** | theoretical termination with no transfer path | handback, step-in, and recovery rules are built in |
| **What commercial memory exists?** | episodic consultants and operator memory | territorial category strategy and professional buying capacity |
| **How are consequential deals treated?** | concessions and PPPs handled as routine commercial paperwork | constitutionally consequential commitments get elevated scrutiny and comparison |

## Failure modes

- **concession patchwork**: the real territorial constitution lives in a pile of incompatible franchises, concessions, direct awards, and legacy frameworks.
- **award-ceremony government**: political attention ends at signature while amendments, incidents, and extensions quietly rewrite the bargain later.
- **change-order constitution**: the formal tender is less important than the informal variation culture that follows it.
- **host-buyer dependency**: the territory appears autonomous but still relies on a municipality, ministry, or utility to buy and negotiate for it.
- **vendor memory state**: the supplier knows the service, assets, interfaces, and failure modes better than the public authority, so bargaining power quietly migrates outward.
- **commercial lock-in disguised as continuity**: weak handback, data portability, or step-in rules make “continuity” an argument for perpetual incumbency.
- **PPP opacity**: delivery form hides real spending, contingent liabilities, or long-term risk allocation from budget and scrutiny processes.
- **procurement-as-clerical-tail**: the territory treats commercial capacity as paperwork and discovers too late that it outsourced one of the main places where government becomes real.

## Bottom line

Once shared territorial power becomes real government, it should not let procurement offices, franchise documents, concession agreements, or amendment practice become its hidden constitution. It needs its own visible contract home: one buyer-or-commissioner spine, explicit delivery-form tests, disciplined contract management and change control, transparent contract lifecycles, and real exit and handback capacity.
