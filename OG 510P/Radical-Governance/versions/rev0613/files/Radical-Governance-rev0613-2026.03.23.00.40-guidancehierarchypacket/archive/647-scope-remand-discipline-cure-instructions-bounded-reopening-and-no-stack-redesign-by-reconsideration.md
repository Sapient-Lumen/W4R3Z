# 647 — Scope-remand discipline: cure instructions, bounded reopening, and no stack redesign by reconsideration

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should treat **remand** in materially stack-altering disputes as a **bounded cure lane**, not as an open invitation to redesign the scope stack: a reviewing body that sends a package back for correction should specify exactly what defect must be cured, what remains frozen or intact, what consultation or consent must be rerun, and when the matter has crossed the line into a fresh packet, opinion, override, or constituent decision.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a much stronger doctrine for the visible life cycle of stack-bearing authority. It says **where functions fit and when the stack must be reopened** (`602`, `628`-`635`), **how stack-changing proposals are classified and interpreted** (`636`-`638`), **how implementation and live operation are checked** (`639`-`643`), and **how defect, pause, and partial invalidity should work** (`644`-`646`).

It even already says, in the clear-statement note, that courts and reviewers should sometimes be able to say: **this claimed scope consequence is too unclear; return through the proper lane** (`638`).

What the archive still lacked was one compact doctrine for the hard part after that sentence.

A reviewer finds that a metropolitan authority was given supervisory power by a vague finance statute and returns the measure for clarification. A court says a central ministry may not keep a local planning veto without rerunning the consultation lane. A constitutional chamber preserves a narrower remainder of a package, but sends the finance spine back for repair. An audit body says the emergency holdover can continue only briefly while the lawful competence route is redone. Everybody then asks the same practical question: **what exactly is open on remand, and what is not?**

Without one doctrine here, remand becomes a constitutional smuggling route. Sponsors come back with a “cure” that quietly widens territory, changes the fiscal burden, adds new supervisory hooks, switches the operational holder, or narrows review routes beyond the defect that was actually identified. The language of correction then becomes a cover for redesign.

The opposite error also appears. Some systems treat remand as if nothing can move until the whole package is rebuilt from zero, even where the defect was narrow and a bounded repair would restore legality without wider institutional damage.

An honest archive needs a rule between those errors. It should allow **bounded cure** without allowing **stack redesign by reconsideration**.

That is the role of a compact doctrine of **scope-remand discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat remand as a repair lane, not as a fresh blank cheque

A remand exists to cure a named defect in a named package, not to reopen every design question that sponsors wish they had won the first time.

The default should therefore be:

- the original package remains the reference object;
- the reviewing body identifies the precise defect or unresolved question;
- only the minimum necessary field is reopened;
- and everything beyond that field stays either operative, frozen, or excluded according to the remand order.

If sponsors want something materially broader than the remand field, they should not call it cure. They should start a new packet.

### 2. Require one public remand statement with exact open and closed questions

Where a materially stack-bearing matter is sent back, the polity should produce one public **scope-remand statement** or equivalent order. That statement should say:

- what instrument, package, or live arrangement is being remanded;
- what defect, ambiguity, or gap triggered remand;
- which questions are **open** for cure;
- which questions are **closed** and may not be relitigated inside the cure lane;
- whether interim standstill, freeze, narrowing, or continuity measures remain in force;
- what consultation, consent, reasons, or evidence work must be rerun;
- what clock applies to the cure;
- and what fallback baseline governs if the cure fails or expires.

Without this object, each actor imagines a different scope of reconsideration.

### 3. Distinguish four remand types so cure does not hide redesign

Not every remand should be treated as the same.

At minimum the archive should distinguish:

- **clarification remand** — the claimed scope consequence was too unclear and needs explicit legal expression without changing the underlying settlement;
- **procedural remand** — the substantive design may survive, but required consultation, reasons, consent, or classification must be redone lawfully;
- **conformance remand** — the legal design stands, but budgets, staffing, software, contracts, or review routes must be brought back into alignment;
- **redesign remand** — the defect is so structural that honest cure would require changing the territorial map, competence mix, finance spine, supervision pattern, or remedy architecture, and therefore the matter must re-enter the fresh packet lane.

This classification stops a redesign remand from masquerading as a mere procedural tidy-up.

### 4. Keep closed questions truly closed unless the reviewer explicitly reopens them

If the remand concerns consultation timing, sponsors should not quietly revisit territorial reach. If it concerns the fiscal spine, they should not also widen supervisory power. If it concerns operator conformance, they should not use the cure to rewrite legal title.

The default should be that **closed questions stay closed** unless the remand statement explicitly says otherwise.

That discipline matters because multilevel packages often bundle many coupled choices together. Once the matter is back on the sponsor’s desk, the temptation is to relitigate the whole bargain under the cover of compliance work.

### 5. Rerun consultation or consent whenever the cure changes who is burdened, bound, or displaced

A cure is not exempt from participation duties merely because it occurs after review.

If the revised cure package changes:

- which tier holds the competence;
- which territory is bound;
- which authorities lose discretion;
- how resources are redistributed;
- what supervision or approval relation applies;
- or what residents, firms, or local communities must now live under,

then the consultation or consent route should be rerun at the level the archive already requires (`590`, `595`, `630`, `645`).

The key anti-theater rule is simple: **remand does not waive the rights of newly affected parties.**

### 6. Keep interim continuity separate from sponsor advantage

Many remands occur while services, permits, funding flows, or emergency coordination still need to continue. The archive should therefore allow temporary continuity bridges, but those bridges should not hand the sponsor a bargaining advantage.

Interim arrangements should therefore:

- be visibly temporary;
- preserve records and review rights;
- avoid irreversible expansion;
- stay as narrow as continuity requires;
- and not be treated as evidence that the sponsor’s preferred final design has already won.

A temporary host, fallback operator, or narrowed supervisory path should keep the lights on. It should not pre-judge the cure.

### 7. Require one remand-return object before the cured package is treated as live or settled

When the cure work comes back, the polity should issue one visible **remand-return statement** or equivalent object saying:

- what the remand required;
- what the sponsor changed;
- what remained untouched;
- whether the cure stayed inside the remand field;
- whether fresh consultation or consent occurred where required;
- whether the result is accepted, partly accepted, or rejected;
- and whether the matter is now operative, still provisional, or sent into a fresh packet lane.

Otherwise the public record will show only the original defect and the later enactment, with no clear bridge between them.

### 8. Give remands expiry and fallback consequences

A remand should not become an indefinite constitutional holding pattern.

The remand statement should therefore carry:

- a cure clock;
- any interim expiry date;
- the fallback baseline that returns if cure does not arrive in time;
- and a default consequence if the sponsor misses the clock, such as reversion, retirement, or reopening under a new packet.

This matters because sponsors often prefer the ambiguity of “under reconsideration” to the discipline of either lawful cure or handback.

### 9. Update the same public stack memory surfaces as every other scope event

A remand and its return should not live only in litigation prose, committee minutes, or ministry correspondence. It should update the same public memory objects the archive already uses:

- the **canonical scope register** (`633`);
- the **scope jurisprudence digest** (`634`);
- the relevant **packet, opinion, override, nullity, standstill, and severability chain** (`635`-`646`);
- and any linked **observability, settlement, retirement, or reactivation** objects where the live arrangement changed (`640`-`643`).

At minimum the register should be able to say:

- **remanded for bounded cure**;
- **running on interim continuity bridge**;
- **returned and cured within remand field**;
- **returned but exceeded remand and reopened as new package**;
- or **remand expired; fallback restored**.

### 10. When cure starts changing the constitutional bargain, stop calling it cure

This is the doctrine’s central boundary. If the remand response changes the bargain so deeply that it effectively creates a different territorial, fiscal, supervisory, or democratic settlement, the polity should stop pretending it is merely correcting the old package.

That is especially true where the response changes:

- the basic holder of power;
- the protected discretion of local or regional authorities;
- the territorial footprint;
- the resource burden or equalization logic;
- the review and remedy path;
- or the temporariness/permanence classification.

At that point the honest move is a **fresh packet, fresh opinion, and—where required—fresh override or constituent consent**.

## Guardrails

- Do not let a remand reopen closed questions unless the remanding body expressly did so.
- Do not treat cure as a waiver of consultation, consent, or review rights for newly affected authorities or communities.
- Do not let interim continuity bridges become bargaining leverage or evidence of final title.
- Do not permit cure to expand territory, supervision, or fiscal burden under the label of compliance work.
- Do not keep remand status indefinite; every remand should have clocks and fallback consequences.
- Do not hide remand and return inside case-specific prose; update the public stack memory.

## Failure modes

- **open-field remand**: sponsors use a narrow defect finding to renegotiate the whole settlement.
- **cure laundering**: a materially broader redesign is presented as simple compliance.
- **consultation bypass on return**: newly burdened authorities or communities are told the time for participation has passed.
- **interim capture**: the temporary operator or fallback host becomes the de facto winner because the remand drags on.
- **clockless reconsideration**: the system remains in suspended ambiguity because no deadline or fallback was named.
- **memory gap**: future readers can see the defect and the later package, but not the bounded logic linking the two.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Can the polity state exactly what question the remand reopened and what questions remained closed?
2. Is the cure being classified as clarification, procedural, conformance, or redesign remand?
3. Does the remand-return object show whether the cure stayed inside the remand field?
4. If the cure changes who is bound, burdened, supervised, or displaced, were consultation or consent routes rerun?
5. Are interim continuity measures visible, narrow, temporary, and non-prejudicial?
6. Is there a clear expiry, fallback baseline, and consequence for missed cure clocks?
7. Has the register been updated to show remand, interim operation, cure, or reversion?
8. Can a future reader tell whether the final live arrangement is the original package cured, a narrowed remainder, a fallback reversion, or a wholly new design?
9. If the sponsor wanted to reopen more than the defect required, was that pushed into a fresh packet lane?
10. Does the polity know when to stop saying “remand” and start saying “new constitutional choice”?

## Closing image

A constitutional stack should be able to say: **this defect must be cured**, **only this field is reopened**, **this temporary arrangement holds in the meantime**, and **if the sponsor wants more than that, the matter goes back through the front door**. Anything weaker invites **stack redesign by reconsideration**.
