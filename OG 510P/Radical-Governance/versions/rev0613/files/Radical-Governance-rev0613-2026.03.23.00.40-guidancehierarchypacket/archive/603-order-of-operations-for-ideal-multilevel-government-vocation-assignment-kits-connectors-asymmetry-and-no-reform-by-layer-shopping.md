# 603 — Order of operations for ideal multilevel government: vocation, assignment, kits, connectors, asymmetry, and no reform by layer-shopping

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should be designed in a disciplined order: start from the class of problems and the real geography they inhabit, assign responsibilities by explicit scope tests, fit each chosen level with a minimum complete institutional kit, bind that kit to finance and capability, add coordination/review/equalization connectors, and only then allow principled asymmetry and transfer rules; reject reforms that start by picking a favored level first and inventing reasons afterward.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a stronger answer to three adjacent questions: **which functions belong at which scope** (`579`), **what minimum constitutional kit each scope needs** (`565`), and **what the ideal government of each scope should look like** (`602`). What it still lacked was a compact design method for policymakers, constitutional drafters, transition teams, and institutional critics who need to move from theory to sequence.

That missing method matters because multilevel reform often fails before the first institution is even drawn. Some systems begin from **map worship**, assuming inherited tiers must stay as they are and then stuffing mismatched powers into them. Others begin from **ideological layer preference**, deciding in advance that localism or centralism is morally superior and then laundering that preference through selective arguments. Others begin from **institutional mimicry**, drawing the same chamber, cabinet, and bureaucracy at every scale whether or not the function, geography, or democratic density justifies it.

A better design sequence starts from fit. The question is not simply who should govern, but **what class of problem exists, what scale is real, what institutional kit makes that scale honest, what connectors keep the stack coherent, and what failure path corrects the design when it goes wrong**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from functions and problem geographies, not from inherited tiers

Design should begin with the actual class of problem: intimate service, neighborhood stewardship, municipal place management, metropolitan network coordination, regional system-building, national redistribution, cross-border compacting, or planetary-risk governance. It should also begin with the real geography of that problem: block, municipality, labor market, basin, corridor, province, nation, or treaty field.

The archive should therefore reject reforms that start with a favorite tier and ask only how much can be stuffed into it. A scale is not justified because it already exists on a map or because a ministry prefers it.

### 2. Name each scope’s governing vocation in one sentence before assigning powers

Before writing institutional detail, a serious polity should be able to describe the governing vocation of each level in one sentence that differs materially from the levels below and above it. Micro-local units notice, convene, and escalate. Municipalities govern ordinary place. Metropolitan bodies govern shared urban systems. Regions hold together territorial platforms and some shared rule. Nations guarantee equal citizenship, solidarity, and macro-constitutional order. Continental and global bodies solve explicitly named cross-border problems.

If a level cannot state its vocation clearly, it is highly likely to become either ornamental or expansionist.

### 3. Assign responsibilities by explicit tests rather than by slogan

Each major function should pass visible assignment tests before being placed. The archive already uses a compact grammar: intimacy, spillovers, coercive-risk safeguards, solidarity and risk pooling, infrastructure topology, and commons or cross-border externalities. Those tests should be run function by function rather than collapsed into one vague appeal to subsidiarity or efficiency.

This means that different lanes of a field may live at different scales. Local intake may stay municipal, technical review may pool regionally, funding may sit nationally, and standards may sit in a treaty-bound cross-border layer. The correct answer is often not one level but a typed stack.

### 4. Ask whether the scale is real in practice, not only in law

A constitution can invent a tier on paper, but it cannot conjure a functional urban region, watershed, logistics corridor, hospital catchment, or labor market into a smaller or larger shape just by drawing a boundary. Multilevel design should therefore test whether the geography that matters operationally is actually present and whether the chosen government matches it closely enough to govern honestly.

This is especially important at metropolitan and regional scales. If the shared system is real but the government is not, power will migrate anyway into opaque bargains, single-purpose authorities, ministries, or fiscal side deals.

### 5. Fit the minimum complete kit only after scope assignment

Once a function is placed, the chosen level needs the minimum complete kit required to govern it honestly. That kit is not identical at every scale, but it should always be legible. At minimum it usually includes:

- a public forum or representative channel proportionate to the level’s powers,
- an executive or administrative spine that can actually implement decisions,
- a money model that matches the mandate,
- a records and disclosure discipline,
- and a review or remedy path when decisions injure people, places, or lower/upper tiers.

The archive should reject the reverse order, where an institution is copied first and only later searched for a job.

### 6. Bind political, administrative, and fiscal design together

A level is not genuinely empowered because it has elections alone, or staff alone, or money alone. The political, administrative, and fiscal dimensions of decentralization have to line up. If a level is elected but has no capable administration, it becomes democratic shell government. If it has staff but no political discretion, it becomes deconcentrated administration in costume. If it has mandates without revenue or equalization, decentralization degrades into blame transfer.

The archive should therefore treat mandate-finance congruence and capability congruence as design steps, not late implementation cleanups.

### 7. Add vertical and horizontal connectors before declaring the stack finished

Even correctly assigned powers fail without connective tissue. The design sequence should therefore add, in plain view:

- vertical coordination where investment, rights, or regulation span levels,
- horizontal cooperation where fragmented municipalities or agencies need shared capacity,
- equalization so local self-government does not become inherited tax-base inequality,
- and interoperability of records, indicators, and official facts so responsibility remains traceable.

A stack without connectors is not decentralization with freedom; it is fragmentation with better rhetoric.

### 8. Lock in review, redress, intervention limits, and handback paths

Every consequential multilevel design needs a failure constitution. People need appeal and review routes. Lower tiers need recourse when upper tiers silently override, starve, or displace them. Upper tiers need lawful rescue lanes when a lower tier collapses. Emergency or distress interventions need thresholds, time limits, and handback clocks.

The archive should therefore reject governance stacks that can describe authority in normal times but become improvisational during conflict, failure, or crisis.

### 9. Allow asymmetry late, and only for real differences

Asymmetry is often necessary for capitals, islands, indigenous or treaty-grounded autonomies, giant city-regions, sparsely populated territories, borderlands, and other exceptional geographies. But it should come late in the design order, after the polity has already specified the default stack, assignment tests, minimum kits, and common rights floors.

That sequencing matters because otherwise asymmetry becomes a euphemism for prestige bargaining, partisan carve-out, or constitutional evasiveness.

### 10. Publish the design packet, not just the constitutional headline

A serious reform should leave behind one visible packet that names:

- the function being assigned or moved,
- the test it passed,
- the geography it is meant to govern,
- the institutional kit required,
- the finance and capability package,
- the coordination and review connectors,
- the transition plan,
- and the conditions for later review, adjustment, or handback.

Without that packet, later governments inherit a headline structure but not the reasoning needed to preserve or correct it.

## Guardrails

- Do not begin from a preferred level and then rationalize upward or downward.
- Do not confuse inherited administrative maps with real governing geographies.
- Do not assign powers before checking whether the receiving level has money, staff, records, and review capacity.
- Do not finish a decentralization design before specifying equalization, intervention limits, and remedy routes.
- Do not use asymmetry as a cover for favors, fear, or prestige deals.
- Do not let metropolitan or regional realities be governed only through informal coordination because the formal stack is politically tidier on paper.

## Failure modes

- **layer-shopping**: reformers start with the level they ideologically prefer and search for justifications later.
- **map worship**: inherited territorial boundaries are treated as self-validating regardless of real spillovers or system geographies.
- **kit-before-job design**: institutions are copied from another scale before anyone can name their governing vocation.
- **three-leg mismatch**: political, administrative, and fiscal decentralization are designed separately and never line up.
- **connector neglect**: the stack is assigned, but no equalization, coordination, interoperability, or review architecture follows.
- **asymmetry by patronage**: exceptions are granted through bargaining rather than principled criteria.
- **paper metro / paper region**: the polity names a level without giving it the public legitimacy or administrative depth needed to govern the systems used to justify its existence.

## Practical tests

A multilevel design sequence is serious when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. For each level, can the polity state a distinct governing vocation in one sentence?
2. For each major function, is there a visible record of the assignment test it passed?
3. Does the chosen geography match the operational geography of the problem closely enough to govern honestly?
4. Has each empowered level received a minimum complete kit sized to its actual work?
5. Do political, administrative, and fiscal design line up at each level?
6. Are vertical coordination, horizontal cooperation, equalization, and interoperability specified rather than assumed?
7. Are review, emergency intervention, and handback paths explicit?
8. If asymmetry exists, can the polity explain why it is principled and what keeps it from becoming arbitrary?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a reform can tell you **which level is being empowered** but cannot tell you **what problem it is solving, what geography made that level the right one, what institutional kit was required, how finance and capability were matched, what connectors keep the stack coherent, and how the design gets corrected when it fails**, then it still has **a jurisdictional rearrangement without an order of operations for ideal multilevel government**.
