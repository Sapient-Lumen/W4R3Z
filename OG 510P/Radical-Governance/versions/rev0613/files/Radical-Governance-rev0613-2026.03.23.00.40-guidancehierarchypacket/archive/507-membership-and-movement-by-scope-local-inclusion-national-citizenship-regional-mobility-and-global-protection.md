# 507 — Membership and movement by scope: local inclusion, national citizenship, regional mobility, and global protection

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should split membership and movement cleanly by scale: local governments make residence workable and non-exclusionary in daily life; national governments control citizenship, ordinary admission rules, and status determination under law; regional bodies can widen lawful mobility and burden-sharing where states consent; and global institutions should enforce refugee, anti-statelessness, and human-rights baselines so border control never becomes a permission slip for rightlessness.

## Why this matters

Questions of who belongs, who may move, who may stay, and who must be protected are among the most explosive in politics because they mix identity, labor markets, welfare systems, coercive power, and moral panic. They are also frequently mishandled because governments collapse several different functions into one border story.

The municipality cannot issue nationality, but it can decide whether a resident can realistically enroll a child in school, access basic services, or participate in local life without falling into administrative invisibility. The nation can define citizenship and immigration status, but it should not monopolize every integration decision or pretend that local schools, housing markets, clinics, and employers do not carry the real weight of settlement. Regional compacts can enlarge mobility and coordinate standards. Global law exists because states cannot be the sole judges of refugee protection, statelessness, or the minimum rights of people under their jurisdiction.

An ideal government therefore needs a movement-and-membership constitution by scope. It should distinguish daily inclusion from nationality, lawful mobility from arbitrary exclusion, and border administration from the human rights floor that survives the border.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from rights of persons, not only statuses on paper

The archive should begin from the principle that all persons under a state's jurisdiction have rights, whether or not they hold citizenship or regular status. Citizenship matters, but it is not the source of basic humanity. A polity that governs migration only through status labels will predictably generate detention, invisibility, exploitation, and administratively manufactured precarity.

### 2. Give local government responsibility for residence, access, and integration in ordinary life

Municipal and neighborhood institutions are where movement becomes settlement. Schools, primary care access, emergency shelter, language access, labor-market navigation, civil registration touchpoints, tenant protection, and everyday inclusion live close to the ground. Local government should therefore be evaluated on whether residents can function and participate, not on whether it can mimic national border sovereignty.

### 3. Reserve citizenship, nationality, and core admission rules to the national constitutional level

Nationality law, naturalization, passports, deportation authority, border procedure, and the formal legal architecture of admission are generally national functions because they concern equal citizenship, international representation, and the coercive power of the state. The archive should keep those functions at the national level under legislation, judicial review, and rights constraints, rather than diffusing them into local improvisation or opaque security bureaucracy.

### 4. Distinguish status determination from long-run integration

Even where asylum, residency, or immigration decisions are national, the long-run work of inclusion is territorial and social. The archive should therefore reject a design in which the same institutions that police entry also monopolize housing placement, school inclusion, work access, and civic participation. Status determination and integration need coordination, but they should not collapse into one permanent enforcement frame.

### 5. Use regional governance to widen lawful mobility and share burdens where consent exists

Regional bodies can do real work where neighboring states choose deeper cooperation: mutual recognition, labor mobility, student mobility, portable benefits, common visa or border standards, relocation mechanisms, and burden-sharing for reception. These arrangements work best when they are rule-bound and reviewable. They should enlarge freedom and coordination without erasing democratic consent or local capacity realities.

### 6. Keep global governance focused on protection floors, cooperation, and anti-rightlessness

Global institutions should not attempt to run ordinary migration administration for the world. Their role is narrower and essential: refugee protection baselines, anti-refoulement principles, anti-statelessness work, human-rights oversight, support for humane and orderly migration cooperation, and assistance where displacement overwhelms state capacity. The ideal global layer prevents membership politics from creating zones of legal abandonment.

### 7. Reject statelessness and inherited civic invisibility as design failures

A well-designed polity should treat statelessness, indefinite limbo, and intergenerational documentation failure as constitutional defects, not regrettable edge cases. Nationality laws should avoid arbitrary deprivation, gender or ethnic discrimination, and bureaucratic traps that leave people unrecognized for life. The archive should prefer presumptions and procedures that reduce rightlessness rather than multiplying it.

### 8. Pair border authority with remedy, transparency, and ordinary-law discipline

Because migration governance touches detention, expulsion, family life, labor, and discrimination, it requires visible reasons, review routes, interpretable procedures, legal aid access where stakes justify it, and data that can expose abuse without dehumanizing people. A border is not a constitutional vacuum. The archive should insist that migration administration remains inside ordinary law even when politics tries to push it outside.

## Guardrails

- Do not let local authorities become shadow border agencies that deny ordinary services through administrative fear.
- Do not let national governments monopolize integration questions that are inherently territorial and local.
- Do not confuse the legal power to exclude with the legal permission to deny basic rights.
- Do not design regional mobility systems without real solidarity and implementation capacity.
- Do not tolerate statelessness, indefinite limbo, or arbitrary deprivation of nationality as routine policy tools.

## Failure modes

- **status absolutism**: every practical question is collapsed into legal status, producing exclusion from school, care, housing, and work.
- **border spillover governance**: local institutions become informal enforcement arms and residents retreat from ordinary services.
- **integration vacuum**: the nation decides entry while no level is actually responsible for making life workable after arrival.
- **regional freedom without burden-sharing**: mobility widens but reception, housing, and public service responsibilities remain politically unowned.
- **manufactured rightlessness**: people are detained, expelled, or left undocumented without meaningful review, nationality pathways, or protection.

## Practical tests

A scale-matched movement-and-membership system passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Are all persons under jurisdiction protected by basic rights regardless of citizenship or migration status?
2. Do local governments make residence, service access, and integration workable in daily life?
3. Are citizenship, admission, and status decisions clearly national, lawful, and reviewable?
4. Where regional mobility exists, are portability and burden-sharing built in rather than assumed?
5. Does the global layer constrain refoulement, statelessness, and rightlessness without pretending to replace domestic administration?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **who guards the border** but not **which level makes residence workable, which level grants membership, which level shares mobility burdens, and which safeguards prevent rightlessness**, it still has **a border regime without a movement-and-membership constitution**.
