# 691 — Shared-regime information-classification-and-disclosure constitutions: public, on-request, internal, confidential, strict, harm tests, downgrading, external sharing, and no secrecy by folder folklore

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed information-classification-and-disclosure constitution** that separates public, request-only, internal working, confidential, strictly confidential, personal-data, and security-sensitive lanes — with named marking authority, handling rules, external-sharing conditions, review clocks, and request-and-review paths — so information is governed by visible rules instead of folder folklore, indiscriminate secrecy, or accidental disclosure by habit.

## Why this matters

The archive now explains how shared regimes publish their official paper trail (`674`), how they structure external participation (`677`), how they protect sensitive institutional independence (`687`), and how they maintain territorial presence and field continuity (`689`–`690`).

That is enough to explain **what should be published**, **who may see some parts**, and **why some information requires functional protection**. It is still not enough to explain **how the regime actually types information in day-to-day operations, who may mark or unmark it, what may be shared externally and on what basis, how requests are reviewed, and when secrecy expires or is narrowed**.

That gap matters because information disorder becomes constitutional disorder very quickly.

Some regimes publish almost everything but cannot protect staff data, security-sensitive logistics, or third-party confidences. Others mark half their working environment “confidential” without any visible basis, owner, review clock, or downgrade lane. Some distinguish public from secret but have no honest middle lane for request-only or internal-working material. Others circulate sensitive drafts externally because nobody can say which collaboration tool, mailbox, repository, or export path is approved for which class of information. Jointly held information gets stuck because parties did not type ex ante how consent, redaction, or public-interest override works. Archived materials become permanently dark because no one joined retention, disclosure, and archival-access rules into one lifecycle.

Without a typed information constitution, seven familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **secrecy by folder folklore**: staff rely on local naming habits, inherited mailing lists, or informal “do not share” norms instead of an official classification map.

The second is **overclassification as convenience**: embarrassment, controversy, or unfinished thinking are treated as if they were security or privacy harms.

The third is **internal-working limbo**: materials are neither clearly public nor properly protected, so disclosure practice depends on personalities rather than rules.

The fourth is **third-party contamination**: donor, vendor, state, partner, and jointly owned materials are mixed into ordinary records without any visible sharing conditions.

The fifth is **request-lane theater**: the regime says it supports access but cannot show how requests are logged, routed, partially fulfilled, internally reviewed, or denied.

The sixth is **declassification amnesia**: information receives a restrictive label once and then never re-enters review, even after the original sensitivity has expired.

The seventh is **archive blackout**: records reach long-term custody without a typed relationship between retention, sensitivity, access conditions, and eventual opening.

Shared regimes need a tighter grammar.

## Pattern pack

### 1) Default access rule and category ladder

Publish one visible classification ladder that, at minimum, distinguishes:

- **Publicly available** information that should be published proactively.
- **Available on request** information that is not proactively published but is normally disclosable through a request lane.
- **Internal working** information that is non-public by default but not presumptively classified for grave-harm reasons.
- **Confidential** information where disclosure would materially injure protected interests.
- **Strictly confidential** information where unauthorized disclosure, alteration, or destruction could cause grave institutional, member, operational, or personal harm.
- Overlays for **personal data**, **security-sensitive material**, **legal privilege**, and **third-party confidential material**.

Do not pretend that every non-public item belongs in one bucket.

### 2) Marking authority and information ownership

Require each classifiable information stream to have:

- one named **information owner** or accountable business owner;
- one named lane for **initial marking**;
- one lane for **challenge, correction, downgrade, or upgrade**;
- one lane for **handover when ownership changes**.

No employee should be able to create durable secrecy merely by typing “confidential” into a filename without a recognized authority basis.

### 3) Handling rules by category

For each information class, state the minimum handling packet:

- approved storage environments;
- approved collaboration and transmission channels;
- printing, copying, export, and translation rules;
- external-sharing conditions;
- retention and archival routing;
- destruction or sanitization rules where applicable.

A category without handling rules is only a label, not a working constitution.

### 4) Request, redaction, and internal review lane

If the regime claims any commitment to disclosure or transparency, it should maintain one visible request lane that includes:

- a submission point;
- minimum request specificity rules;
- logging and tracking;
- routing to the relevant business owner;
- partial disclosure and redaction rules;
- time expectations;
- one internal review or reconsideration lane.

The regime should be able to show what happened to a request, not merely announce abstract openness principles.

### 5) Harm tests, public-interest override, and partial disclosure

Separate **reasons for non-disclosure** from **mere institutional discomfort**.

Where the regime uses confidentiality exceptions, it should type:

- which protected interest is engaged;
- what harm is plausibly expected from disclosure;
- whether redaction or partial release can reduce that harm;
- whether an exceptional public-interest override exists for imminent, serious, or accountability-relevant cases;
- who may authorize such an override and how the record is kept.

### 6) Third-party, jointly owned, and entrusted material

Require a special lane for information that is:

- received from member governments or participants under confidence;
- jointly owned with another institution or party;
- produced under donor, vendor, or implementing-partner agreements;
- shared under investigation, audit, or security protocols.

The packet should state whether onward disclosure is barred, consent-dependent, case-by-case, summary-only, or redaction-capable. No shared regime should discover its confidentiality rules only after a dispute begins.

### 7) Review clocks, downgrade, and declassification

Every restrictive marking above ordinary internal-working status should carry one of:

- a named review date,
- a triggering event for review,
- a maximum default period before recheck,
- or an explicit statement that the information is event-bound rather than time-bound.

Serious regimes should treat secrecy as reviewable and exhaustible unless a continuing protected interest is shown.

### 8) Archive and lifecycle link

Join the information constitution to records and archives discipline:

- classification should travel with the record;
- archival transfer should preserve sensitivity metadata;
- archived records should inherit a visible access rule rather than an eternal closure assumption;
- declassification or release decisions should feed back into archival access.

### 9) External sharing and collaboration rules

Publish one short rule set for sharing with external parties:

- who may share;
- which classes may be shared without special permission;
- when a confidentiality agreement or data-sharing instrument is required;
- whether summaries or extracts may be shared where full documents may not;
- how shared copies are marked and logged.

### 10) Logging and auditability

Log:

- requests for disclosure,
- denials and reasons,
- internal reviews,
- exceptional disclosures,
- downgrades and declassifications,
- and external transfers of confidential or strictly confidential material.

A regime that cannot reconstruct its own disclosure history does not have a real information constitution.

## Working matrix

| Information lane | Default posture | Typical basis | Minimum extra discipline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public | publish proactively | public accountability, routine institutional information | canonical publication point, authenticity, version control |
| On request | disclose through request lane | not published by default but not harm-protected | request log, partial release and review lane |
| Internal working | restricted to role need | unfinished analysis, draft coordination, operational routine | storage/channel rules, no permanent secrecy by habit |
| Confidential | controlled sharing | privacy, commercial confidence, negotiations, legal or operational sensitivity | named owner, approved channels, review clock |
| Strictly confidential | tightly controlled | grave harm to persons, missions, security, or institutional interests | minimal distribution, enhanced logging, challenge and review authority |
| Archived sensitive material | conditional access | continuing but possibly decaying sensitivity | transfer metadata, opening conditions, future review clock |

## Guardrails

- Do not use classification to hide illegality, misconduct, policy embarrassment, or mere political inconvenience.
- Do not let “internal” become a secret bucket with no owner, no review clock, and no disclosure path.
- Do not let third-party confidentiality clauses silently override the regime’s whole access architecture.
- Do not create one disclosure portal for the public and a separate undocumented disclosure culture for insiders.
- Do not separate archives from disclosure policy so completely that no one can tell when old records may open.

## Failure modes

- **Folder folklore** — staff infer sensitivity from local repository customs rather than a constitutional map.
- **Attachment drift** — sensitive material leaves through ordinary forwarding, translation, or collaborative drafting channels.
- **Joint-paper deadlock** — records with multiple owners become unshareable because no ex ante consent or redaction rule exists.
- **Reviewless secrecy** — temporary sensitivity hardens into permanent closure.
- **Request opacity** — the regime receives access requests but cannot explain denials or internal review outcomes.
- **Archive freeze** — historical records remain inaccessible because no release or opening lane was joined to archival transfer.

## Practical tests

1. Can the regime explain its full information ladder in one page without collapsing every non-public item into “confidential”?
2. Is there a named authority for initial marking, challenge, and downgrade?
3. Can staff tell which collaboration or transmission tools are allowed for each information class?
4. Does the regime distinguish request-only disclosure from genuinely protected confidentiality?
5. Are joint, donor, vendor, and member-state materials covered by explicit onward-sharing rules?
6. Can the regime show a harm test, redaction path, and internal review lane for denied disclosure requests?
7. Do restrictive markings carry review clocks or event-based reconsideration triggers?
8. Are archived records linked to explicit access conditions rather than permanent darkness?
9. Are exceptional disclosures, partial releases, and declassifications logged?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can show **what it publishes** but not **how it types information, who may mark and unmark it, how requests and reviews work, what can be shared externally, and when secrecy narrows or expires**, then it still has **records and transparency language without an honest information-classification-and-disclosure constitution**.
