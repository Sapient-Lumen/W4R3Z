# 697 — Shared-regime strategic-planning-and-results constitutions: general programmes, programme budgets, theories of change, baselines, targets, reviews, and no mission by project pile

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed strategic-planning-and-results constitution** that separates long-horizon strategy, medium-term programme budgets, theories of change, outcome and output ladders, indicator and baseline rules, target-setting, reprogramming, and mid-cycle review — so the institution does not drift into mission by project pile, target theater, dashboard government, or budget without strategy.

## Why this matters

The archive now explains budget discipline (`683`), statistics-and-indicator discipline (`694`), enterprise-risk-and-internal-control discipline (`695`), and evaluation-and-learning discipline (`696`).

That is enough to explain **how money is adopted, how evidence is typed, how major risks are governed, and how learning should feed back**. It is still not enough to explain **how a shared regime turns mandate into a governed delivery architecture before the money is spent and before the evaluation arrives**.

That gap matters because many shared regimes carry ambitious mandates, broad political declarations, and crowded project lists without one visible ladder connecting:

- the governing body’s strategic direction;
- the secretariat’s medium-term programme architecture;
- the theory of change connecting secretariat work to member and partner action;
- the outcome/output/results chain;
- the indicators, baselines, and targets used to measure progress;
- the review and reprogramming lane when implementation reality changes.

Without that constitutional bridge, eight familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **project-pile government**: every worthy idea becomes a line item, but the institution cannot show which projects belong to which strategic objective or outcome path.

The second is **budget-without-strategy**: resources are allocated, but the connection between the adopted budget and the strategy is too thin to explain why this work rather than other work was funded.

The third is **strategy-without-operating packet**: a high-level plan exists, but nobody can show the outcome chain, output responsibilities, or review cadence that should make the plan operational.

The fourth is **target fiction**: indicators and targets are presented without clear baseline discipline, measurement ownership, or explanation of what is actually within the shared regime’s control versus the responsibility of members and partners.

The fifth is **output worship**: the institution counts meetings, guidelines, missions, or disbursements and quietly treats those as outcome achievement.

The sixth is **reprogramming by convenience**: priorities shift during implementation, but the change route is invisible, so the real strategy is rewritten through informal budget drift or managerial discretion.

The seventh is **review blackout**: midterm or annual review occurs, but outsiders cannot see what was learned, what changed, what underperformed, and what was deliberately dropped.

The eighth is **dashboard government**: indicator surfaces become the apparent constitution even though the deeper strategic logic, target basis, and resource trade-offs remain untyped.

Shared regimes need a tighter strategy-to-results grammar.

## Pattern pack

### 1) Strategy ladder

Separate at least four layers:

- constitutional or treaty mandate;
- governing-body strategy or general programme of work;
- programme budget or medium-term delivery architecture;
- annual or biennial implementation and review packet.

Do not let one glossy strategy document pretend to do every job at once.

### 2) Theory-of-change packet

Require one visible theory-of-change packet that states:

- what the regime itself directly controls;
- what depends on members, partners, or country uptake;
- where the institution contributes influence rather than command;
- key assumptions and external dependencies.

A shared regime should not claim direct authorship of every downstream societal change.

### 3) Outcomes and outputs grammar

Use one outcome/output ladder that distinguishes:

- longer-horizon joint or system outcomes;
- secretariat-controlled outputs or services;
- enabling functions and institutional capacities;
- cross-cutting priorities that cut across the ladder.

Do not collapse outputs, outcomes, and impacts into one rhetorical word such as “results”.

### 4) Indicator, baseline, and target discipline

For each consequential indicator, show:

- definition and metadata owner;
- baseline date and baseline value rule;
- target year and target-setting basis;
- data source and refresh cadence;
- whether the indicator measures secretariat performance, joint outcome progress, or broader context.

Targets should not be produced by optimism alone.

### 5) Resource alignment packet

Tie each budget line, programme area, or major workstream to:

- a strategic objective;
- one or more outcomes;
- the responsible office or level;
- the main delivery instrument;
- the main indicator or review signal.

A programme budget should be explainable as a strategy map, not only as an accounting table.

### 6) Priority-setting lane

Use one visible priority-setting lane for:

- country or member demand;
- cross-border or global spillover needs;
- mandatory versus discretionary functions;
- underfunded but high-priority work;
- criteria for deprioritization.

Priority setting should be governed before scarcity forces hidden rationing.

### 7) Mid-cycle review and reprogramming lane

Require a named review lane that distinguishes:

- performance that is on track;
- performance at risk;
- underperformance requiring redesign;
- targets requiring recalibration;
- activities to stop, merge, or defer;
- resource shifts that require governing-body notice or approval.

Review should be allowed to change the programme, but only through a visible lane.

### 8) Delivery responsibility map

For each major output or workstream, identify:

- accountable office;
- supporting offices or regions;
- member-state or partner dependency;
- operational bottlenecks;
- decision forum for slippage or redesign.

The results chain should not be ownerless.

### 9) Public results story versus technical evidence

Keep separate:

- public narrative and communication;
- the technical results framework;
- underlying indicator metadata;
- internal management signals;
- formal governing-body performance assessment.

Communications should summarize the governed framework, not replace it.

### 10) Sunset, carry-forward, and successor logic

Require every strategic cycle to state:

- what ends with the cycle;
- what carries forward unchanged;
- what must be redesigned before the next cycle;
- which targets or indicators are retired, revised, or inherited;
- what evaluation and review findings shaped the successor plan.

A new strategy should not appear as a discontinuous miracle or a silent rollover.

## Working matrix

| Strategy-and-results lane | Core constitutional question | Minimum visible packet |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy | what broad direction did the governing body actually adopt? | strategy document, strategic objectives, adoption act |
| Results chain | how does secretariat work connect to joint outcomes? | theory of change, outcome/output map, dependency notes |
| Indicators | how is progress measured and by whom? | indicator definitions, baselines, targets, metadata owner |
| Resource alignment | how does the budget express the strategy? | budget-to-objective crosswalk, office ownership, delivery instrument |
| Priority setting | how are trade-offs made under scarcity? | criteria, country-input lane, deprioritization rule |
| Review and reprogramming | how does the programme change when reality shifts? | review packet, redesign lane, approval rule, public explanation |
| Succession | how does one strategic cycle hand off to the next? | carry-forward table, retired indicators, successor rationale |

## Guardrails

- Do not let the programme budget float free from the adopted strategy.
- Do not treat every indicator as if it measured something the secretariat fully controls.
- Do not mistake outputs for outcomes or outcomes for social impact.
- Do not let public dashboards outrank the underlying results framework and metadata.
- Do not let reprogramming happen only through donor earmarks, crisis improvisation, or managerial habit.
- Do not launch a successor strategy without showing what was carried forward, dropped, or redesigned.

## Failure modes

- **Project pile** — the work programme is a list of activities with no real strategic spine.
- **Target theater** — targets are displayed without visible baseline logic, ownership, or causal credibility.
- **Output worship** — production counts impersonate public-value achievement.
- **Review blackout** — midterm review happens, but nobody can see what changed.
- **Budget drift** — money moves faster than the governing strategy.
- **Dashboard capture** — the communicative surface becomes more authoritative than the governed results chain.

## Practical tests

1. Can the regime show one current strategy ladder linking mandate, strategic objectives, programme budget, and implementation packet?
2. Does the current plan state a visible theory of change and the boundary between secretariat control and partner/member dependence?
3. Are outcomes, outputs, and enabling capacities kept analytically separate?
4. Does each key indicator have a baseline, target year, metadata owner, and data source?
5. Can the budget be read as a strategy map rather than just a ledger?
6. Is there a visible lane for deprioritizing or stopping work, not only for adding new commitments?
7. Does the mid-cycle review distinguish on-track work, at-risk work, redesign, and deliberate discontinuation?
8. Could an outsider tell which changes required governing-body approval and which fell within routine management reprogramming?
9. Does the successor strategy explain what it inherited, corrected, or retired from the prior cycle?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can show **a strategy document, a budget table, and a public dashboard** but cannot show **its theory of change, outcome/output ladder, baseline and target discipline, priority-setting rule, review-and-reprogramming lane, and successor logic**, then it still has **results language without a real strategic-planning-and-results constitution**.
