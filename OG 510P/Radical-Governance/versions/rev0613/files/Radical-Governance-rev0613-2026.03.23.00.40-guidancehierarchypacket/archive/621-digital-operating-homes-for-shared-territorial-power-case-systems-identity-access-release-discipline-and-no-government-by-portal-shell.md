# 621 — Digital operating homes for shared territorial power: case systems, identity/access, release discipline, and no government by portal shell

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, it needs an honest **digital operating home** as well as democratic, fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, participatory, knowledge, service, planning, capital, regulatory, contract, and workforce ones: a named digital authority and product-governance lane; canonical case/workflow and system-of-record choices; its own role-and-access model rather than host-government logins; bounded, interoperable exchange with registries and member governments; explicit release/change/incident discipline; continuity and cyber safeguards; and channel parity between portal, API, call-centre, and in-person routes — so the territory stops being governed through host-city accounts, vendor-admin shadows, and glossy front-end shells with no constitutional core behind them.

## One-line thesis

Real metropolitan or regional government needs its own **digital operating constitution**: a named digital authority, canonical case/workflow and records systems, explicit identity-and-access rules, bounded interoperable exchange, release and incident discipline, continuity safeguards, and channel parity — not government by portal shell.

## Why this matters

The archive now says when shared territorial power becomes real government, what democratic form it should take, what fiscal, administrative, accountability, public-law, intergovernmental, participatory, knowledge, service, planning, capital, regulatory, contract, and workforce homes it needs, and why those homes should be visible rather than hidden inside consortium habit or host-government workaround.

What it still lacked was the **digital operating analogue**.

That absence matters because many shared territorial bodies now act through software long before they acquire a fully honest digital constitution. A metro or region may have a public website, ticketing app, permit portal, planning dashboard, operator API, or member-government data feed, yet still be practically hollow if the real case management, role administration, audit logging, incident handling, release control, records retention, and business continuity all sit in a host municipality, a ministry tenant, or a vendor-controlled cloud estate. In that arrangement the polity may look digitally modern while remaining constitutionally dependent on somebody else’s credentials, change windows, admin rights, and data-export goodwill.

The archive therefore needs one compact doctrine for the **digital operating home of shared territorial government**: when a real metropolitan or regional tier must stop living inside borrowed systems and become able to run, govern, secure, and evidence its own digital operations.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish the digital operating constitution from broad digital public infrastructure

The shared tier’s digital operating constitution is not the same thing as national digital public infrastructure. National identity, payments, notifications, interoperability rails, and base registries may still sit mostly at national scale. The question here is narrower and more institutional: **how does this territory actually run its own digital work?**

A metro or region should not be treated as digitally complete merely because it can plug into national rails or buy a user-facing portal. The digital operating constitution is the answer to who owns the core products, workflows, access rules, logs, records, integrations, and recovery duties that make the tier’s real powers executable.

### 2. Give the shared tier a named digital authority once powers are real

Once shared territorial power becomes real, the archive should prefer one visible public answer to the question: **who is responsible for the digital operating model of this government?** That does not require a gigantic ministry of technology. It does require a named authority or leadership lane with responsibility for architecture, standards, product governance, release decisions, supplier control, and cross-service coherence.

If digital decisions are still made only by host-city IT, by a favored operator, or by whichever vendor owns the current platform, then the shared tier is not yet digitally self-governing.

### 3. Give the territory canonical case/workflow and system-of-record choices

A real shared tier should be able to state where its live work actually happens: what constitutes the authoritative case or workflow system for permits, plans, contracts, inspections, service incidents, complaints, or equivalent territorial functions; which systems are merely presentation layers; and where the system of record sits for each consequential workflow.

Portal shells, spreadsheets, inboxes, and chat groups should not quietly become the government. The archive should prefer declared systems of record, explicit workflow states, linked records retention, and visible cutover rules when systems change.

### 4. Own the role-and-access model instead of borrowing host-government accounts

A shared territorial body becomes digitally dishonest when its real powers are still exercised through another government’s accounts, directory groups, shared administrator privileges, or informal credential sharing. The archive should prefer one visible role-and-access model for the shared tier: named roles, least-privilege access, separation of duties, joiner/mover/leaver controls, and explicit treatment of elected members, staff, contractors, secondees, regulators, operators, and auditors.

Using a host identity provider or national trust service can be acceptable. What is not acceptable is allowing the constitutional question of **who may do what in this government** to remain hidden inside another organisation’s account structure.

### 5. Keep interoperability broad enough to work and narrow enough to remain governable

Real territorial government needs data exchange with municipalities, operators, ministries, regulators, and national registries. But interoperability is not a license for unrestricted visibility or silent pooling. The archive should prefer declared interfaces, purpose-bound exchange, metadata and logging, named data stewards, and explicit minimisation of replicated personal and operational data.

A digitally honest shared tier can explain both **how systems connect** and **why the connection is limited**.

### 6. Treat release, change, and incident management as constitutional, not merely technical

Where software mediates permits, service access, finance, planning, regulation, or public records, release discipline becomes part of governance rather than an internal convenience. The archive should therefore prefer visible product ownership, change approval lanes, rollback paths, incident clocks, severity levels, user communication rules, and after-action review for serious failures.

Otherwise real territorial power is exercised through unreviewed deployments, silent configuration drift, and emergency administrator improvisation.

### 7. Keep channel parity so the portal is not the constitution

A real shared tier should not treat the web portal or mobile app as the whole of government. The archive should prefer channel parity across portal, API, call-centre, email, assisted-digital, and in-person routes where rights-bearing access is at stake. Users should not lose rights, queue position, or evidentiary standing merely because they entered through the “wrong” channel.

This is especially important for cross-boundary metropolitan and regional services, where residents often encounter the shared tier indirectly and may not know which institution owns which digital surface.

### 8. Bound vendor-admin power and preserve exit, portability, and replay

A shared tier may buy software, hosting, support, and specialist digital services. But the archive should prefer arrangements in which the polity can see administrator roles, export its data and logs, preserve evidentiary records, change suppliers, and continue critical functions if a vendor relationship fails.

Otherwise the practical sovereign becomes the platform operator that controls admin keys, workflow logic, or data extraction.

### 9. Link digital operation to records, disclosure, and review rights

A digital operating home is incomplete if it cannot sustain the territory’s records, disclosure, audit, and challenge obligations. The archive should prefer explicit links between case systems and records schedules, preservation of decision trails and audit logs, searchable official records, and response routes for access, correction, complaint, and review.

If an institution can transact digitally but cannot later reconstruct what it did, with what version, on what basis, and through which official record, then it still lacks an honest digital operating constitution.

### 10. Treat resilience and cyber discipline as continuity duties of government

The archive should prefer continuity planning, recovery-time assumptions, backup and restoration discipline, dependency mapping, exercised incident playbooks, and proportionate digital-security risk management for the shared tier’s most consequential systems. This is not about maximal securitisation; it is about preserving public continuity, trust, and lawful operation when software, suppliers, or networks fail.

A polity that governs transport, planning, utilities coordination, permitting, or public funds across a territory should not discover during an outage that it has no independently governed digital recovery path.

## Digital-home matrix

| Question | Thin or dishonest arrangement | Honest digital operating home |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Who governs the digital estate?** | host-city IT, ministry tenancy, or vendor de facto rule | named digital authority with product and architecture responsibility |
| **Where does live work happen?** | portal fronts, inboxes, and spreadsheets act as the state | declared case/workflow systems and named systems of record |
| **Who can do what?** | borrowed accounts, shared admin, role ambiguity | explicit role-and-access model with separation of duties |
| **How do systems connect?** | silent extraction or ad hoc file passing | declared interfaces, bounded exchange, logging, and stewardship |
| **How do changes go live?** | silent deploys and emergency improvisation | release/change control, rollback, incident clocks, and review |
| **Can people use different channels safely?** | portal-only or channel hierarchy by accident | parity across portal, API, assisted-digital, call-centre, and in-person routes |
| **What happens if a vendor fails?** | admin captivity and poor export rights | portability, exit, replay, and continuity capacity |
| **Can decisions be reconstructed later?** | weak logs and no records linkage | preserved decision trails, records discipline, and reviewability |

## Failure modes

- **government by portal shell**: the front end looks coherent but the institution cannot show the systems, access rules, and records beneath it.
- **borrowed login state**: the shared tier’s real powers are exercised through another government’s accounts and privilege structure.
- **vendor-admin sovereignty**: supplier staff effectively control administrator rights, workflow logic, or extraction of authoritative data.
- **spreadsheet federation**: key territorial decisions are still coordinated by manual workarounds rather than governed systems.
- **silent deploy government**: real public rules or access conditions change through unannounced releases and configuration drift.
- **API without constitution**: systems exchange data widely but nobody can explain purpose, authority, minimisation, or review.
- **records amnesia**: the body can transact digitally but cannot later reconstruct the official record or decision trail.
- **outage constitutional crisis**: continuity depends on goodwill from a host government or vendor because the shared tier has no independent recovery discipline.

## Practical tests

A shared territorial digital constitution passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can the territory state who owns digital architecture, product governance, and change authority for its consequential systems?
2. Can it name the authoritative case/workflow systems and systems of record for its major powers rather than pointing only to portals and dashboards?
3. Are identity, role, privilege, and administrator controls explicit enough that constitutional power is not hiding inside borrowed accounts or shared admin?
4. Are integrations with municipalities, operators, and national registries purpose-bound, logged, and minimised rather than open-ended?
5. Do release, rollback, incident, and outage disciplines exist for the systems through which public power is exercised?
6. Can residents and counterparties reach the territory through multiple valid channels without losing rights, priority, or evidence?
7. Can the polity preserve records, export data and logs, change suppliers, and recover critical operations without begging a host government or platform operator for constitutional mercy?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a metropolitan or regional body can show **its portals, apps, dashboards, and integration claims** but cannot show **a named digital authority, authoritative case/workflow systems, explicit identity-and-access rules, bounded exchange, release and incident discipline, records linkage, channel parity, and continuity/exit capacity**, then it still lacks **an honest digital operating home for shared territorial power**.
