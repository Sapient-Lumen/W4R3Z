# 542 — Intergovernmental machinery: lawful councils, compacts, formula finance, and no government by summit

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should govern the spaces **between** levels through lawful intergovernmental machinery—standing councils, mandatory consultation, rule-bound compacts, formula finance, shared operational forums, and independent dispute routes—so coordination is continuous, reviewable, and bounded rather than a contest of press conferences, emergency bargaining, and grant blackmail.

## Why this matters

The archive already has a scope ladder, a subsidiarity rule, a competence-conflict note, and a compact constitutional synthesis of what governments at each level should be. What it still needed was one dedicated note on **how those levels should work together once powers are split but problems remain shared**.

That missing layer is where many systems fail. Constitutions allocate powers, but day-to-day government often happens in the space between text and execution: ministers bargain over grants, mayors discover mandates after budgets are announced, provinces learn about competence changes through press releases, and emergency forums quietly become permanent shadow cabinets. In weak systems, the center governs by selective funding, governors govern by veto threat, and everyone describes improvised summitry as co-operation.

An ideal multi-level polity needs something more disciplined. Intergovernmental relations should be treated as constitutional machinery with known forums, known powers, known records, known finance rules, and known review routes. Otherwise, the stack becomes formally divided but operationally feudal.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat intergovernmental relations as a constitutional subsystem, not diplomatic folklore

Where several levels of government coexist, there must be standing machinery for consultation, coordination, co-decision where justified, and dispute handling. These mechanisms should be designed in law, charter, or comparably hard public rules. They should not depend entirely on personal chemistry among incumbents or on the convenience of whichever ministry currently controls the purse.

### 2. Separate consultation, coordination, co-decision, and supervision

Intergovernmental forums often fail because they blur unlike functions. A healthy system distinguishes at least four modes:

- **consultation**, where one level must hear another before acting;
- **coordination**, where several levels align implementation on shared systems;
- **co-decision**, where constitutional balance or shared resources justify a joint decision rule;
- **supervision or legality review**, where a higher level or court checks compliance with law.

Blurring these modes lets supervision masquerade as partnership or lets a talking shop impersonate binding shared rule.

### 3. Build standing councils at the right density instead of one mega-forum

One annual summit of presidents, prime ministers, governors, or mayors is not an intergovernmental constitution. The ideal architecture is layered. It usually includes:

- a strategic apex forum for major constitutional, fiscal, or crisis questions;
- sector forums for transport, health, housing, water, education, and other shared systems;
- administrative networks for implementation, data standards, and operating protocols;
- and association or assembly channels through which local and regional governments can speak collectively rather than only as petitioners.

The archive should prefer many bounded forums with explicit remits over one symbolic mega-table that produces communiqués but no routable decisions.

### 4. Make consultation early, mandatory, and documented when higher levels change the operating conditions of lower ones

When a national or regional government changes mandates, standards, reporting burdens, territorial boundaries, or the fiscal conditions under which lower tiers operate, consultation should happen **before** the decision hardens. It should be structured enough that lower levels can see the proposal, the clock, the evidence, the reasons, and the uptake path. Consultation after announcement is not consultation. It is notification theatre.

### 5. Use compacts and joint authorities for genuinely shared systems

Some problems are not solved by polite consultation because several governments must actually build, fund, or operate the same system. Metropolitan transit, watershed management, hospital referral regions, freight gateways, emergency communications, and some digital-public-infrastructure rails often need joint authorities, delegated compacts, or other hard-law bodies that can act in their own name.

The ideal polity therefore keeps a clear distinction between:

- a forum that talks,
- a compact that binds,
- and a joint body that can budget, procure, hire, or operate.

Without that distinction, “co-operation” becomes a slogan for shared responsibility with no agent capable of delivery.

### 6. Prefer formula finance and published transfer rules over bargaining finance

Intergovernmental relations become corrosive when essential money arrives mainly through ad hoc negotiation, political loyalty, or discretionary rescue. The archive should therefore prefer formula-based transfers, equalization rules, transparent co-financing formulas, and published capital-allocation criteria wherever possible.

Negotiation will never disappear, but it should sit on top of a predictable floor. Otherwise, finance becomes a covert command system through which higher levels can settle constitutional questions without ever admitting that they are doing so.

### 7. Provide a visible dispute ladder from negotiation to independent resolution

Shared systems and divided powers will produce conflict even in good-faith polities. The answer is not to pretend conflict is pathology. It is to route it. A healthy system should have a dispute ladder such as:

1. good-faith bilateral negotiation;
2. structured mediation or a standing intergovernmental committee;
3. arbitration or expert determination where the issue is technical or formula-bound;
4. and judicial or constitutional review where legal authority or rights are genuinely in question.

This helps prevent every disagreement from becoming either a court war or a fiscal hostage situation.

### 8. Keep records, agendas, and outcomes public enough to stop shadow federalism

Intergovernmental politics often drifts into opacity because it happens between executives rather than inside legislatures. The archive should resist that drift. Agendas, attendance, agenda-setting authority, minutes or outcome notes, dissent, agreements, unresolved items, and follow-up duties should be public by default except where narrow confidentiality is genuinely necessary.

The point is not to perform transparency while decisions remain informal. The point is to stop major parts of the constitution from disappearing into unminuted summit culture.

### 9. In crisis, scale coordination upward by thresholds and hand it back down explicitly

Crisis forums are necessary, but they should not quietly become a second constitution. Emergency coordination should name the trigger for escalation, the powers temporarily exercised, the finance terms, the review route, and the handback condition. Intergovernmental crisis machinery is strongest when it speeds joint action without silently rewriting the ordinary distribution of powers.

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse executive summitry with a real intergovernmental constitution.
- Do not let consultation happen only after announcement or budget lock.
- Do not make shared systems depend on forums that cannot bind, finance, or operate.
- Do not settle constitutional questions indirectly through discretionary grants.
- Do not leave every serious conflict to either raw bargaining or immediate constitutional litigation.

## Failure modes

- **government-by-summit**: episodic leader meetings substitute for standing institutions.
- **consultation theatre**: lower tiers are “consulted” only after key decisions are irreversible.
- **shadow federalism**: real constitutional power migrates into opaque executive bargaining.
- **grant coercion**: higher tiers use discretionary money to win competence disputes they could not win in law.
- **joint-body fiction**: a shared system is declared common responsibility, but no body can actually deliver it.
- **permanent emergency coordination**: crisis forums retain powers or habits that should have returned to the ordinary constitution.

## Practical tests

An intergovernmental architecture passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Are there standing, lawful forums for consultation, coordination, and dispute handling rather than only ad hoc summits?
2. Can lower tiers see proposals early enough to influence decisions that materially affect their mandates or resources?
3. Are shared systems backed by compacts or joint authorities when simple consultation is not enough?
4. Do core transfers and equalization rules rely mainly on formulas and published criteria rather than discretionary bargaining?
5. Is there a visible dispute ladder that can escalate from negotiation to independent resolution without collapsing into fiscal blackmail?
6. Are the records of intergovernmental decisions public enough to prevent major constitutional choices from disappearing into executive folklore?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **powers are divided across levels** but cannot also say **which forums consult, which bodies bind, which compacts operate, which transfers are formula-based, and where disputes go when politics fails**, it still has **a map of governments without a constitution for governing between them**.
