# 682 — Shared-regime language and text discipline: official, working languages, translation, interpretation, authentic texts, terminology control, and no law by convenience copy

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed language-and-text constitution** that separates official languages, working languages, authentic texts, translated texts, interpretation duties, terminology control, and convenience copies — so legal meaning does not drift toward whichever language, rendering, or summary is easiest for insiders to circulate.

## Why this matters

The archive already has notes on documentary spine (`674`) and interpretation discipline (`679`). Those notes say that records matter and that authentic texts, interpretive rules, and guidance status should stay distinct.

A multilingual regime still faces a narrower but constant danger:

**the regime may know that several texts and languages exist without actually governing how language moves authority.**

Many shared regimes operate with several official languages, fewer working languages, and even narrower day-to-day drafting habits. Speeches are interpreted live. Documents are translated later. Some texts are equally authentic. Some are official translations of a controlling language text. Some are secretariat working drafts or convenience consolidations. Some summaries are functionally decisive even though they were never adopted as authentic text.

Without a typed language-and-text constitution, six familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **convenience-copy government**: the text that governs in practice is the one most widely forwarded, not the one with the highest legal status.

The second is **working-language capture**: a language used for drafting or day-to-day operations quietly displaces formally equal languages.

The third is **translation drift**: unofficial or stale translations keep circulating after the authentic or updated text has changed.

The fourth is **interpretation inequality**: participants nominally have language rights, but floor access or effective participation depends on fluency in one insider language.

The fifth is **terminology wobble**: key legal terms mutate across documents because the regime has no terminology control or concordance discipline.

The sixth is **false authenticity**: a summary, annotated version, or consolidated convenience text is treated as if it were the adopted legal text.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **shared-regime language and text discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Publish one language-and-text map

A serious regime should say in one place:

- which languages are official,
- which are working languages,
- which texts are equally authentic,
- which texts are official translations but not authentic originals,
- what live interpretation is guaranteed,
- and which copies are explanatory or convenience only.

If users must infer legal status from file names, letterheads, or what translation happens to be posted first, the regime is already governing partly by linguistic accident.

### 2. Separate official language status from working-language practice

An official language is not necessarily the same thing as a working language for drafting or internal administration.

The archive should therefore prefer a rule that names:

- formal language equality where it exists,
- operational drafting languages where they exist,
- and the safeguards used to stop working habit from becoming hidden legal hierarchy.

### 3. Type authentic text, official translation, and convenience copy separately

The regime should visibly distinguish:

- authentic legal text,
- officially published translation,
- consolidated or annotated working text,
- summary or explanatory note,
- and unofficial convenience copy.

This is the minimum defense against law by forwarded PDF.

### 4. Guarantee interpretation duties where participation rights depend on speech

If a body promises multilingual participation, it should say:

- which meetings receive interpretation,
- among which languages,
- what happens when a participant speaks outside those languages,
- and whether the participant must supply interpretation into a working language first.

Language access is not ceremonial if floor rights matter.

### 5. Build terminology control for legally loaded terms

A serious multilingual regime should keep one terminology or concordance discipline for terms that carry recurring legal weight.

That does not require pretending every language is mechanically interchangeable. It requires the regime to notice when a recurring term has drifted enough to matter.

### 6. Tie publication and legal reliance to text status

The archive should prefer a rule that every document states explicitly whether it is:

- authentic,
- officially translated,
- provisional,
- corrected,
- consolidated,
- or informational only.

Reliance should track status, not convenience.

### 7. Keep language equality from collapsing into timing inequality

Even where several languages are formally recognized, practical inequality often enters through timing: one version appears first, is used in negotiations, and becomes the de facto controlling version before other language communities can react.

The regime should therefore say what can happen before full language release and what cannot.

### 8. Publish correction and replacement links across languages

When a text is corrected, replaced, or superseded, the regime should cross-link every language version and every known convenience copy lane that it controls.

Otherwise stale language versions will keep producing live error.

### 9. Keep secretariat drafting help constitutionally modest

Secretariats often prepare translations, draft consolidations, speaking notes, or bilingual tables. Those are useful. They should not inherit authenticity by polish.

The archive should therefore prefer a rule that secretariat-produced aids carry legal-status labels and link back to the adopted text.

### 10. Build one public text-status register

A serious regime should be able to show, in one place:

- document identifier,
- language,
- status,
- authenticity class,
- date,
- supersession or correction links,
- and whether an interpretation or translation service is available for deliberative use.

Without that spine, multilingual governance decays into convenience-copy folklore.

## Working matrix

| Text or language object | Honest rule | Why | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| official language | state its procedural rights and publication duties clearly | keeps equality visible | symbolic equality with no usable access |
| working language | name its operational role without inflating its legal rank | prevents working-language capture | pretending drafting habit is neutral |
| authentic text | mark as legally controlling or equally authoritative where law says so | preserves legal certainty | false authenticity by summary or draft |
| official translation | label as official but distinct where not itself authentic | supports access without confusion | translation drift becoming hidden law |
| interpreted floor speech | define interpretation guarantees and participant obligations | preserves usable participation | language rights in name only |
| convenience copy or consolidation | link back to adopted text and status label | improves access without legal drift | law by forwarded PDF |

## Guardrails

- Do not let the fastest or most widely circulated text outrank the formally higher-status text.
- Do not let one working language quietly become the regime’s hidden constitution.
- Do not blur authentic texts, official translations, summaries, and convenience consolidations.
- Do not promise multilingual participation without saying what interpretation is actually guaranteed.
- Do not let terminology drift accumulate across recurring legal terms without concordance review.
- Do not leave corrected or superseded language versions live without visible replacement links.

## Failure modes

- **law by convenience copy**: the easiest version to circulate becomes the law in practice.
- **working-language capture**: drafting habit silently outranks formal language equality.
- **translation drift**: stale renderings remain the operational baseline.
- **interpretation inequality**: participation rights depend on fluency in the insider language.
- **terminology wobble**: recurring legal terms change meaning across documents and meetings.
- **false authenticity**: summary, digest, or annotated text borrows legal force it never received.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it publish one language-and-text map distinguishing official languages, working languages, authentic texts, translations, and convenience copies?
2. Are official and working language roles separated explicitly rather than left to custom?
3. Does every text or document carry a visible status label and authenticity class?
4. Are interpretation duties at meetings and the obligations of participants speaking outside the core languages stated explicitly?
5. Is there a terminology or concordance discipline for recurring legal terms?
6. Are correction, supersession, and replacement links carried across language versions?
7. Does the regime say what may happen before all relevant language versions are available?
8. Is there one public register showing language, status, date, and legal reliance class?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can **draft, translate, interpret, and circulate text** but cannot say **which languages carry what rights, which texts are authentic, which translations are official, which copies are merely convenient, and how stale or corrected versions are controlled**, it still has **multilingual procedure without an honest language-and-text constitution**.
