# 669 — Shared-regime entry ladders: observers, signatories, provisional application, accession, admission thresholds, depositaries, entry into force, and no membership by affiliation claim

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed participation ladder** that distinguishes observer, applicant, signatory, provisional applier, party/member, and deeper-module participant — with actor eligibility, admission body, deposited instrument, effective date, rights bundle, and reviewable public status — so the public can tell who is merely near a regime and who is actually bound.

## Why this matters

The archive now says more clearly how shared regimes should choose depth (`663`), allocate burdens (`664`), land commitments domestically (`665`), escalate compliance (`666`), differentiate obligations (`667`), and change over time (`668`). But one constitutional object is still missing:

**how exactly does an entity enter a shared regime, and what statuses exist on the way in?**

Without that object, participation language becomes sloppy very quickly.

A government may call itself a "member" because it attends meetings as an observer. A ministry may announce that a treaty has been "joined" because it signed, even though signature alone does not yet make the state a party. A regime may treat provisional application as if it were full settled participation. An organization may talk about "countries in the system" without distinguishing applicants, observers, signatories, parties, associated territories, or members of an optional deeper club.

Those confusions are not cosmetic. They alter who can vote, who is bound, who may be reviewed, who owes money, what rights private actors can rely on, and whether the public is being told the truth about the state of integration.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **entry discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish participation statuses instead of calling everything "membership"

Shared regimes should keep separate at least these statuses:

1. observer or invited participant,
2. formal applicant or candidate,
3. signatory,
4. provisional applier,
5. party or member in force,
6. and participant in an optional deeper module or protocol.

Each status carries a different legal weight. The archive should reject press language that collapses them into one fuzzy phrase like "in the regime".

### 2. Declare who is even eligible to enter

Before a regime can admit anyone, it should say what kind of actor can enter.

That may be:

- states,
- customs territories with sufficient autonomous competence in the relevant field,
- international organizations,
- dependent territories through metropolitan extension,
- or some narrower category fixed by the parent text.

A regime without an explicit actor-class rule invites status fights that are really fights about constitutional subjecthood.

### 3. Separate open signature from later accession or negotiated admission

Some instruments are open for signature for a defined period. Others are closed for signature and permit only accession. Still others require a negotiated admission or accession decision on terms set by the existing members.

The archive should therefore ask three different questions at entry time:

- is the instrument open for signature,
- can later entrants accede as of right,
- or must admission terms be negotiated and approved by an existing body.

Those are different gates and should not be merged into one generic "join" verb.

### 4. Keep signature visibly below party status unless the instrument says otherwise

Signature can authenticate a text and indicate an intention to continue the treaty-making process. But where signature is subject to ratification, acceptance, or approval, signature alone does not yet establish consent to be bound.

The archive should therefore insist that every public register distinguish:

- signed but not yet bound,
- bound provisionally,
- and bound in force.

Otherwise signatory theatre becomes a standard method of overstating integration.

### 5. Use provisional application only through an explicit legal lane

Provisional application can be a useful bridge where formal domestic procedures are incomplete but urgent co-operation is needed.

But the archive should require any provisional status to declare:

- the legal basis for provisional application,
- the exact obligations treated as provisionally operative,
- the start date,
- the termination rule,
- and the transition path to full participation or clean exit.

A regime should never leave outsiders guessing whether provisional practice is a courtesy, a political promise, or a real temporary legal condition.

### 6. Publish the exact instrument that completed entry

For every participant, the regime should disclose the entry act that matters:

- signature,
- ratification,
- acceptance,
- approval,
- accession,
- notification of provisional application,
- deposit of a protocol instrument,
- or formal admission decision where the regime uses one.

That seems administrative, but it is one of the simplest ways to keep participation claims honest.

### 7. Separate regime-level entry into force from participant-level effectiveness

A regime may have one rule for the instrument as a whole to enter into force and another rule for when it becomes effective for each additional participant.

The archive should insist that the public be able to answer both questions:

- when did the regime itself become operative,
- and when did it become operative for this particular participant.

Threshold-based entry into force, additional waiting periods, and participant-specific effective dates should sit in one visible status register rather than in scattered depositary metadata.

### 8. Give each status an explicit rights-and-obligations bundle

Every participation rung should name what it allows and what it does not.

For example:

- observer status may allow attendance and document access but not voting;
- applicant status may trigger disclosure and negotiation duties but not settled rights;
- signatory status may carry a good-faith restraint against defeating the object and purpose without yet creating full party obligations;
- provisional application may activate only a defined subset or time-bounded set of obligations;
- full party or member status may activate finance, review, dispute, reporting, and voting rules;
- optional protocol status may add deeper obligations beyond the parent regime.

If the rights bundle is not published, status words alone will mislead.

### 9. Keep legal entry separate from implementation readiness

A government can be lawfully in a regime while still being late, partial, or weak in domestic implementation. The archive already addresses that landing problem in `665`.

This note adds the companion rule on the way in:

**do not use implementation weakness to obscure legal entry, and do not use legal entry to pretend implementation is finished.**

The regime should therefore maintain separate fields for:

- participation status,
- effective date,
- differentiated commitments,
- and implementation or compliance status.

### 10. Make deeper clubs and protocols visibly additional

A participant can be fully inside the parent regime while remaining outside an optional protocol, annex, or enhanced-cooperation ring.

The archive should insist that deeper participation be shown as an additional module rather than silently folded into base membership language. Otherwise the regime will either understate divergence or overstate common obligation.

### 11. Keep observer and candidate statuses bounded and reviewable

Observer status can be useful. So can candidacy. But neither should become a permanent constitutional grey zone.

The archive should therefore require such statuses to declare:

- who granted them,
- what participation rights they carry,
- whether the observer or candidate must begin accession or admission steps within a defined period,
- and how the status can expire, be renewed, or be withdrawn.

Without clocks, observers become decorative pseudo-members and candidacies become indefinite diplomatic scenery.

### 12. Maintain one participation register as the canonical public surface

A serious regime should let anyone answer, from one official register:

- who is eligible,
- who is observing,
- who has applied,
- who has signed,
- who provisionally applies,
- who is fully bound,
- what modules each participant has joined,
- what reservations or territorial limits exist,
- and the exact effective dates.

The archive should resist regimes that scatter this information across speeches, dashboards, outdated lists, and separate depositary notices.

## Typed participation ladder

| rung | what it is for | minimum public fields | anti-theater warning |
|---|---|---|---|
| observer / invited participant | access without full binding status | granting body, rights bundle, limits, expiry or review clock | calling attendance "membership" |
| applicant / candidate | formal path toward entry | request date, examining body, document set, milestones | indefinite candidacy with no real gatekeeping |
| signatory | authenticate text and preserve route to later consent where applicable | signature date, whether signature alone binds, next domestic step | pretending signature completed entry |
| provisional applier | bridge urgent co-operation before final consent | legal basis, scope, start date, termination rule | using temporary application as permanent limbo |
| party / member in force | full settled participation under the parent instrument | instrument type, deposit date, effective date, voting/finance/review bundle | unclear whether obligations are actually in force |
| optional deeper module participant | additional protocol or club inside the wider regime | parent status, extra instrument, extra obligations, effective date | bundling deeper obligations into base membership rhetoric |

## Failure modes

- **affiliation inflation**: observers, invitees, or signatories are described as if they were full parties.
- **signature theater**: governments announce that a rule has been "joined" when only the first procedural step has occurred.
- **provisional limbo**: temporary application runs for years without a visible completion or exit path.
- **dark entry thresholds**: nobody outside the secretariat can tell when the regime itself crossed into force.
- **module confusion**: the public cannot tell whether a participant is in the parent regime only or also in a deeper protocol or club.
- **implementation camouflage**: legal entry and operational readiness are collapsed to flatter the regime or the participant.

## Anti-theater tests

1. Can an outsider tell, from one register, whether a participant is an observer, applicant, signatory, provisional applier, full party, or deeper-module participant?
2. Is actor eligibility explicit, including any rule for organizations, territories, or autonomous customs areas?
3. Does the regime distinguish signature from consent to be bound?
4. Is provisional application visibly typed, scoped, and terminable?
5. Are regime-level entry into force and participant-level effectiveness shown separately?
6. Does each status have a defined rights-and-obligations bundle?
7. Are observer and candidate states bounded by review or expiry rather than left permanent?
8. Can the public identify the exact instrument or decision that completed entry?

## Closing synthesis

If a shared regime can say **who is around the table** but cannot say **who is eligible, who has applied, who has merely signed, who is provisionally operating, who is fully bound, what deeper modules exist, and when each status became effective**, then it still has **participation rhetoric without an honest entry constitution**.
