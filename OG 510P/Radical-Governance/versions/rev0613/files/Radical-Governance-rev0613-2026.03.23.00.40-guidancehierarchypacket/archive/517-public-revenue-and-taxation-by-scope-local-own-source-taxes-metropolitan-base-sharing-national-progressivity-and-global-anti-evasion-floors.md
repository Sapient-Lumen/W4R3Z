# 517 — Public revenue and taxation by scope: local own-source taxes, metropolitan base-sharing, national progressivity, and global anti-evasion floors

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should fund government by keeping visible own-source revenues tied to place and service at the local level; using metropolitan and regional revenue-sharing where commuters, land values, logistics, or extractive burdens cross borders; reserving progressive income taxation, broad consumption taxes, social insurance contributions, debt management, and equalizing transfers to the nation; and using the global layer for narrow anti-evasion, information-exchange, and minimum-tax rules that stop mobile capital from hollowing out democratic finance.

## Why this matters

A constitution that allocates powers without allocating revenue is mostly theater. The archive already says a great deal about subsidiarity, equalization, housing, markets, and utilities. What it still needs is one compact note on who should actually raise money, from which bases, and under what anti-race-to-the-bottom discipline.

Revenue governance fails in two common ways. One is **hollow localism**: municipalities are told they are self-governing while being denied any meaningful own-source revenue, leaving them dependent on grants, fines, favors, and developer bargaining. The other is **fragmented tax sovereignty**: every jurisdiction is invited to chase mobile capital or payrolls, producing predatory competition, opacity, and fiscal instability. Both mistakes corrode democracy because voters cannot tell who pays, who benefits, and who is quietly being played off against whom.

An ideal government therefore needs a scale-typed revenue constitution. It should keep some fiscal responsibility visible close to place, pool where metropolitan or regional spillovers are real, reserve redistribution and macro-stabilizing tax capacity to the nation, and keep the global layer narrow but real where avoidance, secrecy, and profit shifting would otherwise dissolve the tax base.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from fiscal legibility and democratic accountability, not just yield

A revenue constitution should ask whether people can see who raises which money for which public purposes, and whether the tax base matches the geography of the benefit or burden. High yield alone can hide coercive fines, opaque fees, extractive concessions, or dependency on one fragile tax base.

### 2. Give micro-local institutions signal roles, not sovereign tax empires

Neighborhood associations, village councils, tenant bodies, and community institutions often surface unpaid service needs, unfair charges, extractive informal fees, and local willingness to contribute to shared goods. They can help with priority setting, visibility, and trust. They should not become miniature customs states or fragmented tax sovereignties.

### 3. Treat municipalities as the home of visible own-source revenue tied to place

Municipal government is usually the right level for property-related taxes, land-value capture where lawful, user fees for genuinely local services, and transparent local charges that residents can see and contest. Those bases are relatively tied to place and can support accountability. Cities should not be forced to fund themselves through arbitrary fines, one-off asset sales, or constant pleading to the center.

### 4. Use metropolitan and regional sharing where the real tax base crosses borders

Commuter economies, ports, airports, logistics corridors, tourism zones, resource basins, and fast-growing commercial districts often generate costs and land values far beyond one municipality. The archive should therefore prefer metro or regional tax-base sharing, formula distribution, or jointly governed special revenues where fragmentation would otherwise let rich enclaves hoard gains while the wider system carries the burden.

### 5. Reserve progressive redistribution and broad mobile-base taxation to the nation

National government is usually the right level for progressive income taxation, broad consumption taxes, corporate income taxation, social insurance contributions, debt issuance frameworks, macro-stabilizing fiscal policy, and equalizing transfers. Those tools require larger pools, common citizenship, and defenses against territorial arbitrage that small jurisdictions usually cannot sustain alone.

### 6. Pair local revenue autonomy with equalization, not with abandonment

Local own-source revenue is valuable because it creates accountability and room for initiative. It becomes unjust when it is treated as a full substitute for equalization. The archive should therefore prefer systems where local revenue effort matters but universal service floors and territorial fairness do not depend on inherited property wealth or commuter luck.

### 7. Keep the global layer narrow: information exchange, anti-secrecy, and minimum-tax floors

The global layer should not set every domestic tax rate. Its proper role is narrower and still essential: enable information exchange, curb secrecy jurisdictions, reduce profit shifting, and put floors under destructive corporate tax competition. That helps preserve national democratic choice rather than replacing it.

### 8. Treat fines, fees, and resource rents as constitutionally dangerous when they replace a revenue system

A government financed by traffic tickets, punitive fees, gambling dependence, or opaque resource deals will often govern badly even if the formal tax code looks respectable. The archive should therefore treat revenue composition as a constitutional design issue, not just a budget spreadsheet.

## Guardrails

- Do not promise local self-government without meaningful own-source revenue.
- Do not assign highly mobile tax bases to tiny jurisdictions and then act surprised by tax competition.
- Do not let property wealth or one booming district determine equal citizenship across a territory.
- Do not finance ordinary government through punitive fines, coercive fees, or opaque extraction where taxes should do the work.
- Do not treat global anti-avoidance rules as an excuse for a world tax sovereign.

## Failure modes

- **grant dependency masquerading as autonomy**: subnational governments carry responsibility without fiscal room.
- **race-to-the-bottom federalism**: jurisdictions undercut one another on mobile tax bases until public capacity erodes.
- **postcode public finance**: local service quality tracks inherited land values more than equal citizenship.
- **fine-funded government**: enforcement becomes a revenue model.
- **tax-base exit constitutionalism**: multinational firms and wealthy actors route around democracy faster than states can legislate.

## Practical tests

A scale-matched revenue constitution passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Do municipalities have meaningful own-source revenues tied to place and service, rather than mainly fines and dependency?
2. Are metropolitan or regional sharing rules used where the real tax base and the real burden obviously cross municipal borders?
3. Does national government own the main progressive, redistributive, and macro-stabilizing tax instruments?
4. Is equalization strong enough that local autonomy does not become territorial abandonment?
5. Does the global layer concentrate on anti-evasion, information exchange, and minimum-tax floors rather than pretending to run domestic revenue policy?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can describe **its constitution and spending promises** but cannot show **which level raises visible own-source revenue, how cross-border tax bases are shared, who owns redistribution and stabilization, and how mobile capital is prevented from routing around the whole settlement**, it still has **government without a revenue constitution**.
