# 494 — Remedy stack by scope: local ombuds, administrative justice, constitutional review, and subsidiarity-safe access

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should give people remedies at the closest level that can actually cure the problem while preserving escalation paths upward: frontline complaint and ombuds channels for ordinary maladministration, administrative and ordinary courts for legal error, constitutional and federal review for rights and competence conflicts, and only subsidiary supranational or treaty review where domestic remedies have been exhausted or are unavailable.

## Why this matters

A competence map without a remedy map is decorative. People do not experience governance only through constitutions and election systems; they experience it through whether a wrong decision can be corrected in time, by a body they can reach, under rules they can understand.

Multi-level polities often fail here in two opposite ways. Some force small disputes upward into expensive, distant, overburdened courts. Others keep people trapped in local administrative mazes with no credible escalation path when local power hardens into arbitrariness. Federal systems add another layer of risk: disputes over rights or competences can become judicial bottlenecks if no one has standing, no ombuds route exists, or every remedy is too remote from the person affected.

The archive therefore needs one compact answer on remedies by scale. The right design is neither total localism nor total centralization. It is a stacked remedy system that solves most problems near the user while keeping a visible ladder for rights, legality, and constitutional balance.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start with the closest remedy that can actually fix the problem

People should not need a supreme court to correct a delayed permit, a missing benefit notice, a municipal service failure, or an inaccessible interface. The first remedy should usually be the smallest one capable of issuing a real fix: frontline reconsideration, caseworker correction, service complaint, inspection channel, or local review office.

### 2. Institutionalize ombuds channels for maladministration and rights-friction

Ombuds institutions are especially valuable where formal litigation is too expensive, slow, or intimidating for everyday injustice. The archive should prefer ombuds channels that can investigate, recommend correction, expose patterns, and escalate systemic issues, including at local and regional levels where many people actually meet the state.

### 3. Keep administrative justice separate enough to review the executive honestly

Administrative tribunals and courts should be able to review legality, reasons, process, evidence handling, and proportionality without collapsing into the executive they are reviewing. The remedy stack is not credible if agencies effectively judge themselves all the way up.

### 4. Preserve ordinary-court and constitutional-court pathways for rights and competence questions

Some disputes are not just service failures. They concern the legality of a norm, the constitutionality of an act, or the boundary between levels of government. For those questions, the polity needs routes through ordinary courts, preliminary references, constitutional complaint, or equivalent mechanisms so rights and competence conflicts do not depend on political grace alone.

### 5. Give ombuds institutions and courts standing routes that do not dead-end at local power

Where ombuds institutions exist, the archive should view it as desirable for them to be able to trigger higher-level review of unconstitutional or unlawful norms, directly or indirectly, when individuals cannot realistically do so alone. The point of the remedy ladder is not to admire subsidiarity; it is to keep it from becoming entrapment.

### 6. In federations, design the judiciary for clarity, not for decorative duplication

Federal systems vary. Some use integrated court systems, others dual or partly dual hierarchies, and others specialized constitutional tribunals. The archive should not fetishize one model. The real test is whether persons, municipalities, regions, and the center can identify:

- which court or body hears what,
- how rights claims move upward,
- how competence disputes are resolved,
- and how inconsistent jurisprudence is prevented.

### 7. Keep supranational and treaty remedies subsidiary, not performative

Regional human-rights courts, treaty bodies, and international complaint mechanisms matter most when domestic remedies fail, are unavailable, or are structurally biased. They should supplement, not replace, an effective domestic remedy stack. A polity that relies on distant international review for ordinary justice has already failed too low in the stack.

### 8. Make remedies reachable in time, language, cost, and format

A nominal remedy that cannot be reached because of filing fees, inaccessible procedure, missing legal aid, confusing forms, or excessive delay is not a real remedy. Multi-level justice must be reachable by ordinary people, not only by institutions and specialists.

### 9. Feed remedy outcomes back into system design

Complaints, ombuds findings, tribunal rulings, and constitutional judgments should change the competence map, service design, and administrative instructions. Otherwise the same pathology repeats at scale and the remedy system becomes a grievance museum.

## Guardrails

- Do not send ordinary local maladministration straight to apex courts.
- Do not trap people in local complaint loops with no independent escalation path.
- Do not let federal complexity make remedies unintelligible.
- Do not treat ombuds offices as decorative ethics mascots without real investigatory or escalation pathways.
- Do not rely on supranational remedies to substitute for an absent domestic rule-of-law stack.

## Failure modes

- **remedy vertigo**: the right forum is so hard to identify that the claimant gives up.
- **local entrapment**: the same authority that caused the problem controls every meaningful review step.
- **judicial overload by design**: minor matters are pushed upward because lower-tier remedies are too weak or absent.
- **federal maze justice**: multi-level court structures obscure rather than clarify the correction path.
- **rights without access**: constitutional rights exist on paper but cannot be invoked affordably or in time.

## Practical tests

A remedy stack passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can ordinary administrative wrongs usually be corrected close to the person affected?
2. Do ombuds and independent review bodies exist for people who cannot realistically litigate every grievance?
3. Are there clear routes for legal, constitutional, and competence disputes to move upward when needed?
4. In a federation, can persons and governments tell which body hears which dispute?
5. Are remedies reachable in practice, not only in theory, with tolerable cost, time, and procedural burden?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity says people have remedies but the only trustworthy correction path is **too far away, too expensive, too slow, or too obscure to use**, then it has not built a remedy stack; it has built **paper legality over practical helplessness**.
