# 630 — Rescoping authority for ideal governments of each scope: administration by law, constitutional bargains by consent, and no jurisdictional rewire by executive deal

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should treat **moving a function between scopes as a graded constitutional act**: ordinary implementation detail may be adjusted through ordinary law inside fixed competences, sectoral reallocations among existing tiers should travel through public legislation with consultation and impact proof, any move that alters the protected bargain of local or regional self-government should require the higher law and consent route appropriate to that bargain, and no executive compact, grant condition, crisis summit, or managerial workaround should be allowed to rewire who really governs what.

## Why this matters

The archive now says **what ideal governments by scope are** (`602`), **how to assign work across the stack** (`579`, `603`, `629`), **how to test whether a claimed tier is honest** (`628`), **how higher tiers should justify intervention** (`590`), **how transfers should be implemented** (`586`), and **how constitutional drift should be resisted** (`498`, `583`). What it still lacked was one compact doctrine for the institutional question that sits in the middle of all those notes: **who is actually allowed to move a function from one level to another, and by what legal lane?**

That gap matters because many multilevel constitutions fail less by formal doctrine than by **rescoping through the wrong instrument**. Ministries use grant conditions to dictate what law did not authorise. Executives sign co-operation deals that quietly create a new metropolitan government without a matching electorate or review route. Emergency coordination cells keep powers after the emergency because no ratification or handback rule was written. Legislatures narrow local or regional powers through ordinary implementation detail while insisting that the constitutional bargain has not changed. Each move may look technical in isolation, but the cumulative effect is jurisdictional rewiring without an honestly declared constitutional moment.

The archive therefore needs a simple rule: **the deeper the scope change, the stronger and more public the authorisation route must become**. Not every implementation tweak is a constitutional amendment. But neither is every competence shift a mere administrative convenience. A serious multilevel polity needs an authorisation ladder.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate administration from rescoping before choosing the legal lane

A polity should first ask whether the proposed change merely adjusts **how** an existing level carries out a power, or whether it changes **which level** may decide, finance, regulate, implement, or review the function.

That distinction matters because ordinary administration can often move within an existing competence frame. Rescoping cannot. Once the reform changes the primary deciding level, narrows a protected local or regional discretion, shifts a function from own competence to delegated task, alters a territorial electorate or representative chain, or converts co-operation into binding shared rule, it has crossed from administration into constitutional allocation.

### 2. Use an authorisation ladder in which legal depth matches constitutional depth

The archive should prefer a visible ladder:

- **operational adjustment inside a fixed competence**: ordinary legislation, regulation, or lawful administrative rule may refine process, standards, or workflow without changing the owning level;
- **delegation or implementation routing inside a preserved competence**: statute or clearly authorised delegated legislation may assign execution while preserving who owns the power, who funds it, and who answers for it;
- **sectoral reassignment among existing tiers**: framework law or equivalent public legislation should be required when financing, regulation, implementation, or monitoring is shifted between levels;
- **change to protected local or regional self-government**: constitutional amendment, organic law, territorial chamber assent, double majority, or other higher-law route should be required when the bargain itself changes;
- **creation, abolition, merger, or constitutional upgrade of a territorial tier**: a route strong enough to alter boundaries, electorates, representative chains, finance, and review must be used, not a side agreement among incumbents;
- **cross-border or treaty-bound reallocation**: domestic ratification and subnational consultation should be required when treaty commitments will materially alter the scope of local or regional self-government.

The ladder will vary by legal system. What should not vary is the principle that **deeper rewiring demands a more demanding and more public legal route**.

### 3. Ordinary law may steer execution, but it should not silently confiscate own competences

Legislatures need room to update sector law. Yet ordinary law becomes constitutionally dishonest when it claims to leave a lower tier formally intact while removing the discretion, finance, staffing room, or rulemaking space that made the competence real.

The archive should therefore treat the following as warning signs that a proposal is no longer a routine statutory update:

- local or regional initiative is replaced by mandatory upper-tier instruction;
- delegated-task logic replaces own-competence logic without saying so;
- grants or approvals become the real permission gate for routine lower-tier action;
- the lower tier keeps blame without effective control over standards, money, or timing;
- or the function remains nominally local or regional only because the center prefers to avoid the politics of openly taking it back.

Where those conditions hold, the polity should not pretend the reform is mere implementation detail.

### 4. Consultation is part of authorisation, not a courtesy after the decision

When a reform concerns the competences, essential interests, finances, boundaries, or institutional status of local or regional authorities, consultation should be part of the authorisation route itself.

That means consultation should be:

- early enough to affect the design,
- clear about the proposed legal lane,
- supplied with written reasons and expected consequences,
- open both to affected authorities and to their associations where relevant,
- and closed with a published explanation of what objections were rejected and why.

A scope change is already constitutionally suspect when the affected tier first learns of it after the decisive bargain has been made.

### 5. Executive compacts, grants, and memoranda may implement a settlement, but they should not substitute for one

Intergovernmental deals are often useful for sequencing, financing, and practical co-ordination. But they become abusive when they perform work that belongs to constitution or statute.

The archive should therefore reject:

- **government by grant condition** where central money becomes the hidden route for rewriting subnational competences;
- **government by memorandum** where co-operation agreements do the work of establishing a real territorial authority;
- **government by host-city deal** where one municipality effectively governs a wider territory without a corresponding legal upgrade;
- **government by regulator guidance** where technical instructions quietly narrow a protected discretion;
- and **government by platform default** where software architecture allocates power that law never openly reassigned.

Implementation instruments may organise an authorised settlement. They should not create the settlement that law refused to own.

### 6. Emergency rescoping should be temporary, reviewable, and ratified or unwound

Crises sometimes justify upward concentration, special command, or temporary simplification of ordinary multilevel routines. But emergency necessity does not abolish the authorisation ladder.

The archive should therefore prefer emergency scope changes that carry:

- a named trigger,
- the exact powers moved and to whom,
- a short ratification clock,
- a review route,
- service and rights protection during the temporary shift,
- and an explicit handback, renewal, or constitutional-upgrade decision point.

No polity should be allowed to convert provisional crisis command into the ordinary constitution simply by waiting.

### 7. Harder scope changes should travel with both a proof packet and a transfer pack

Once a reform materially changes who governs, it should carry two linked artifacts:

- a **proof packet** showing why the shift is necessary, proportionate, and territorially honest; and
- a **transfer pack** showing how staff, assets, records, contracts, liabilities, revenue, and review lanes will actually move.

Without the proof packet, rescoping lacks constitutional justification. Without the transfer pack, it lacks operational honesty.

### 8. Affected tiers and affected people both need recourse

A scope change is not honest if the only people allowed to challenge it are those who wrote it. Lower tiers whose protected powers are injured should have standing to seek review, and affected people should be able to challenge the concrete acts that flow from a defective rescoping.

Where the change may become irreversible quickly — boundary reform, forced merger, competence seizure, service cutover, election cancellation, or emergency takeover — interim relief should be thinkable.

### 9. Use territorial consent when the map, electorate, or constituent bargain is what is changing

Not every sector reform requires constituent consent. But reforms that alter the protected territorial bargain should not be settled by one distant majority acting alone.

The archive should therefore prefer stronger consent routes when a proposal:

- changes local or regional boundaries;
- creates or abolishes a metropolitan, regional, provincial, or state tier;
- materially narrows constitutionally or quasi-constitutionally protected self-government;
- changes the representative chain through which a territory is governed;
- or rewrites the shared-rule bargain in a federal, plurinational, or devolved system.

The exact mechanism can vary — referendum, territorial chamber assent, double majority, constituent-unit approval, or another hard-law route — but the principle is stable: **the deeper the territorial bargain, the less acceptable unilateral rewiring becomes**.

### 10. Publish one visible answer to the question: who had authority to move this function?

Every material scope change should leave behind one public record that names:

- the legal basis used,
- the class of rescoping involved,
- the consultation and consent route,
- the proof packet,
- the transfer pack,
- the review and redress lane,
- and the handback or later re-review condition if the move is temporary or contested.

That record should make it impossible for later actors to say the function “just ended up here somehow.”

## Authorisation ladder

| Class of change | Proper authorisation lane | What it must not be confused with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| process or workflow adjustment inside an unchanged competence | ordinary law, regulation, or lawful administrative rule | a hidden competence seizure |
| delegation of execution while competence ownership stays put | explicit statutory delegation with finance and accountability stated | informal managerial download |
| reassignment of financing / regulation / implementation / monitoring across existing tiers | public framework law or equivalent legislation, with consultation and proof | budget pressure dressed up as reform |
| alteration of protected self-government or shared-rule bargain | constitutional, organic, or otherwise entrenched route with the required consent mechanism | ordinary sector housekeeping |
| boundary, tier-creation, tier-abolition, or forced territorial merger | territorial-consent route plus hard law | executive map deal |
| emergency temporary upward shift | emergency law with trigger, ratification clock, review, and handback | permanent constitution by crisis residue |
| treaty or supranational commitment altering subnational competences | treaty ratification plus domestic consultation / implementation route | foreign-policy shortcut around the constitution |

## Guardrails

- Do not let grants, memoranda, procurement templates, or software defaults perform hidden constitutional work.
- Do not treat every lower-tier objection as veto, but do require the polity to use the correct legal lane and say why.
- Do not use ordinary law to hollow out entrenched local or regional powers while insisting nothing fundamental changed.
- Do not let emergency centralisation survive by inertia after the trigger event ends.
- Do not create a new territorial government through operator clubs, mayoral summits, or host-city residue.
- Do not move functions without also moving money, records, staff, and review routes.
- Do not alter boundaries or constituent bargains without a consent form proportionate to the depth of the change.

## Failure modes

- **jurisdictional rewiring by executive deal**: a real competence shift is carried by intergovernmental agreement rather than by the law required.
- **ordinary-law confiscation**: statute leaves a lower tier formally intact while draining the discretion that made the power meaningful.
- **grant coercion as rescoping**: financial dependency substitutes for lawful reassignment.
- **platform constitutionalism**: software architecture allocates authority that public law never openly assigned.
- **permanent emergency residue**: crisis concentration becomes the normal constitution because no ratification or handback rule was enforced.
- **territorial bargain without constituent consent**: deep federal, devolved, or metropolitan rewiring is settled unilaterally by the strongest tier.
- **implementation without authorisation**: records, staff, and workflows move even though the polity never honestly decided that the function should move.

## Practical tests

A polity is handling rescoping authority honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Can it distinguish operational adjustment from real rescoping?
2. Does it use a stronger legal lane as the depth of the scope change increases?
3. Are affected local or regional authorities consulted early enough to influence the design?
4. Does the reform state whether it changes own competence, delegated task status, boundary, finance, or representative chain?
5. Are proof packet and transfer pack both present when a function materially moves?
6. Can grants, memoranda, or software defaults be challenged if they are doing hidden constitutional work?
7. Is there a review and interim-relief route where harm may become irreversible quickly?
8. Does emergency rescoping expire, get ratified, or get unwound on a visible clock?
9. When the territorial or constituent bargain changes, is a proportionate consent mechanism used?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **which scope should govern a function** but cannot also say **which legal lane may move that function, when consultation becomes mandatory, when constituent or territorial consent is required, and how temporary shifts are ratified or unwound**, then it still has **scope doctrine without an honest law of rescoping authority**.
