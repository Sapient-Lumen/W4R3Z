# 582 — Capability congruence, minimum viable jurisdiction, intermunicipal backstops, and no subsidiarity by fiction

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should pair subsidiarity with **capability congruence**: public functions should sit at the closest level that can actually staff, finance, procure, supervise, and lawfully discharge them; when small jurisdictions cannot sustain that floor on their own, the constitution should prefer **intermunicipal shared-service backbones, regional technical corps, explicit fallback lanes, and only then carefully justified consolidation** rather than pretending every map unit is a fully capable government.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a strong note on **which functions belong at which level** (`579`), a doctrine of **mandate-finance congruence** (`581`), and multiple sector notes that already rely on shared centers, intermunicipal pooling, and regional specialist support. What it still lacked was one compact constitutional rule for a stubborn practical problem: **a polity can respect local identity while still lying about local capability**.

That lie is common. A municipality is told it owns planning, procurement, social care coordination, or environmental permitting, but it has too few professional staff, too little legal depth, too little data, or too little purchasing scale to perform the function honestly. A remote island or mountain municipality is expected to carry specialist services that only a larger pool can sustain. A metropolitan system relies on dozens of tiny governments that each preserve symbolic autonomy while none can reliably hire engineers, inspectors, analysts, or specialist case teams.

The result is a familiar pathology: **subsidiarity by fiction**. The constitution says power is close to the people; in practice the function is under-staffed, constantly improvised, quietly captured, or informally re-centralized through emergency calls, ministry discretion, consultants, and vendor dependence. Citizens then get the worst of both worlds: weak local democracy and weak operational competence.

A better rule is simple: **the right level is the closest capable level, with visible backstop and restoration path**. That rule protects local self-government from two enemies at once: romantic fragmentation that cannot perform, and lazy centralization that uses every small-unit weakness as an excuse to hoard power permanently.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish community identity from operating scale

The archive should not assume that because people identify with a village, ward, town, island, or municipality, every public function must be executed by a stand-alone organization at that same scale. Political belonging and administrative operating scale are related but not identical.

A serious constitution can keep local councils, wards, assemblies, or mayors while pooling payroll, procurement, engineering, licensing review, inspection support, call-center capacity, legal drafting, digital operations, or other specialist functions above the smallest unit. The design goal is to preserve **voice locally and capability where it is actually sustainable**.

### 2. Replace “most local level” with “closest capable level”

The subsidiarity instinct remains right: powers should not be moved upward without reason. But the archive should state the test more honestly. The real question is not whether a function can be named local on paper. The question is whether the designated level can actually perform it lawfully, safely, and repeatedly.

A function belongs at the **closest capable level** when the responsible institution can, on a continuing basis:

- recruit and retain enough competent staff,
- manage lawful finance and procurement,
- maintain minimum data and records discipline,
- resist obvious capture and single-person dependency,
- and meet service, rights, and continuity obligations without permanent improvisation.

When those conditions fail, constitutions should not keep calling the arrangement subsidiarity.

### 3. Prefer cooperative scale-building before abolition

When local capability is too thin, the archive should prefer **scale-building** before **identity-erasing abolition**. In many places the right first move is not merger or recentralization but durable intermunicipal co-operation: joint service entities, shared specialist teams, common utilities, pooled procurement, metropolitan or county technical bureaus, or regional service platforms.

This matters because some pathologies come from being too small to perform, while others come from destroying democratic reach in the name of efficiency. Shared structures can sometimes solve the first problem without creating the second.

### 4. Treat intermunicipal shared services as constitutional infrastructure, not ad hoc charity

A polity that relies on small municipalities, islands, remote settlements, or fragmented metro governments should not leave co-operation to occasional goodwill. Shared service provision should be treated as part of the constitutional operating system.

That means the legal system should clearly authorize forms of co-operation, specify governance options, define accountability and audit duties, allow pooled staffing and assets, and provide finance rules that do not punish collaboration. A function that predictably requires joint performance should not depend on annual improvisation or the personal chemistry of current mayors.

### 5. Pool high-skill, low-frequency, and capital-intensive functions above the smallest jurisdiction

The archive should assume that some capacities are structurally hard for small units to sustain alone. Examples often include:

- specialist permitting and engineering review,
- procurement and contract management,
- digital identity, cybersecurity, and registry operations,
- laboratories, inspectors, and advanced technical compliance,
- high-needs social, health, and care coordination,
- serious crime intelligence, anti-corruption investigation, and forensic support,
- actuarial, treasury, and debt-management disciplines,
- and emergency logistics or continuity planning.

Where the staffing bench is thin and the function is too important to fake, the default should be pooling, not prestige mini-bureaucracies.

### 6. Use capability diagnostics before forcing either merger or decentralization theater

The archive should prefer **published capability diagnostics** over slogan wars. Before granting, retaining, merging, or removing a function, governments should publish evidence on:

- service quality,
- accessibility and travel burden,
- staffing depth and vacancy risk,
- procurement and financial-control capacity,
- legal and records performance,
- resilience and continuity risk,
- and comparative cost at different scales.

That disciplines both camps. It stops centralizers from declaring local incapacity by stereotype, and it stops defenders of every existing municipality from pretending that any map unit is automatically viable for every duty.

### 7. Use consolidation rarely, transparently, and with democratic protection

Sometimes merger, county-wide administration, or other territorial consolidation really is justified. Extreme fragmentation can lock a polity into duplicated overhead, shallow staffing, and permanent dependence. The archive should therefore allow consolidation as a real tool — but not as the first, easiest, or only answer.

A good consolidation rule asks first whether shared services, base-sharing, or regional support can solve the problem. If not, consolidation may proceed, but only with:

- prior consultation of affected communities,
- published evidence on cost, quality, access, and capability,
- protection against partisan map engineering,
- continuity plans for staff, records, assets, and service routes,
- and preserved local voice channels inside the larger unit.

The goal is **capable government without documentary erasure of place**.

### 8. Pair pooled capacity with visible accountability and anti-fog rules

A danger of shared services is that everyone can start blaming everyone else. The archive should therefore require pooled or delegated arrangements to publish:

- who legally owns the function,
- who operates it day to day,
- who appoints and removes leadership,
- who pays by what formula,
- which service standards apply,
- which audit and appeal routes exist,
- and how a member government or citizen can trigger review.

Scale without accountability creates a different kind of fiction.

### 9. Build regional technical corps and fallback lanes for small-unit democracies

For many functions, the best answer is not a permanent transfer upward but a **regional or shared technical corps** that lets smaller governments remain politically real while borrowing specialist depth they cannot each maintain. This can include engineers, planners, procurement specialists, legal drafters, social-work support teams, digital operations staff, or mobile inspection units.

The archive should therefore prefer explicit fallback lanes: co-production, temporary trusteeship, shared technical bureaus, rotating support teams, and restoration criteria when a local authority regains capability. That keeps handback thinkable instead of turning every emergency support arrangement into quiet permanent centralization.

### 10. Admit that some territories need differentiated capability settlements

The archive already has a doctrine of principled territorial asymmetry (`580`). Capability design should follow the same logic. Small islands, archipelagos, mountain regions, sparse rural territories, border corridors, and fragmented metropolitan belts may need differentiated service platforms, remoteness weights, joint authorities, or special technical corps.

The key is that difference should be rule-bound and published. A capability settlement for a remote island chain should be legible as constitutional design, not disguised as permanent exception management.

## Guardrails

- Do not call a function local if the designated local unit cannot realistically staff or lawfully execute it.
- Do not treat every municipal map as a sacred operating scale for every public function.
- Do not force mergers before trying clear legal, fiscal, and institutional support for shared services where that is sufficient.
- Do not use small-unit weakness as a standing excuse for indefinite recentralization.
- Do not build joint entities so opaque that citizens cannot tell who governs, pays, or hears appeals.

## Failure modes

- **subsidiarity by fiction**: the law assigns local responsibility without local capability.
- **miniature prestige bureaucracies**: every small unit duplicates fragile specialist posts it cannot sustain.
- **co-operation by personal chemistry**: shared services depend on current office-holders rather than durable rules.
- **merger as map politics**: consolidation is used for party advantage or symbolic tidiness rather than published capability evidence.
- **joint-authority fog**: pooled institutions become too opaque for democratic correction.
- **permanent emergency tutelage**: temporary central rescue hardens into silent recentralization without handback.

## Practical tests

A polity is handling capability congruence honestly when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. For each important local or regional function, is there evidence that the assigned level can actually staff, procure, supervise, and continue the work?
2. Where that answer is no, is there a visible shared-service, regional-support, or fallback arrangement rather than quiet failure?
3. Are intermunicipal co-operation and pooled staffing legally simple enough to use without heroic improvisation?
4. Are citizens able to see who owns the function, who operates it, who pays, and where appeal or complaint goes?
5. Is consolidation used only after published evidence shows that lighter forms of scale-building are insufficient?
6. Do rescue or co-production arrangements include restoration criteria so higher-level support does not become invisible permanent takeover?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can say **which level is responsible** and even **how that level is financed**, but cannot also show **that the designated unit has minimum viable administrative, professional, and continuity capacity — or else a visible shared-service and fallback architecture that supplies it** — then it still has **subsidiarity by fiction rather than capability-congruent government**.
