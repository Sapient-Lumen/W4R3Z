# 503 — Intelligence by scope: no local secret police, judicial authorization, minimization, and multi-key oversight

## One-line thesis

An ideal multi-level polity should keep intelligence and surveillance narrowly scoped to genuine national-security functions under public law, forbid local secret-police formation and routine law-enforcement drift, require prior independent authorization and minimization for intrusive measures, and place the whole system under layered executive, legislative, judicial, privacy, and redress oversight so secrecy never matures into ungoverned power.

## Why this matters

The archive now has better answers for scope allocation, crisis, integrity, and the public sphere. Yet one of the darkest governance questions remains under-developed: **where should secret or highly intrusive state powers live, and how should they be prevented from colonizing the rest of government?**

Surveillance and intelligence are difficult because they deal in asymmetry. They often operate ex ante, in secret, with classified budgets, technical complexity, and genuine public-danger scenarios. Those same conditions make them unusually vulnerable to politicization, mission creep, rights violations, and the quiet migration of exceptional powers into everyday governance.

The archive should therefore prefer a strong negative rule: not every level of government should get a shadow service merely because it fears threats. The ideal stack uses the minimum number of intelligence institutions necessary, binds them to statute and narrow purpose, separates them from ordinary policing and prosecution where possible, and surrounds them with multiple keys of authorization, review, audit, and remedy.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Do not allow municipalities or regions to create autonomous secret-police cultures

Neighborhoods, cities, and most regions should not operate their own domestic intelligence services with covert powers, hidden files, and political-disruption capability. The lower the level, the greater the risk that secrecy fuses with local faction, patronage, or panic. Local government may gather ordinary risk information for public safety, but covert national-security powers should not be treated as a normal municipal toolkit.

### 2. Keep intelligence on a public statutory basis with purposes set by law, not by executive mood

The archive should prefer intelligence bodies whose mandates, powers, objectives, and limits are established by public law through normal legislation. “National security” should not be an elastic slogan that the executive expands at will. A lawful intelligence service is not simply one with patriotic intent; it is one whose jurisdiction is explicit enough that courts, legislatures, and the public can tell when it has crossed the line.

### 3. Separate intelligence from ordinary criminal investigation, arrest, and detention

The archive should strongly prefer institutions in which domestic intelligence bodies do not routinely perform general criminal investigation, arrest, or detention. Where the same body can secretly collect, openly investigate, seize, detain, and narrate the public case, abuse risk rises sharply. The cleaner design keeps intelligence for national-security assessment and warning, while police, prosecutors, and courts carry ordinary-law enforcement powers under ordinary procedural safeguards.

### 4. Require prior independent authorization for intrusive surveillance, with necessity and proportionality visible

Telephone interception, covert digital intrusion, location tracking, bulk or signals collection with individual impact, house searches tied to intelligence powers, and similar measures should not turn on administrative convenience alone. The archive should prefer prior judicial or equivalently independent authorization, necessity and proportionality tests, time limits, scope limits, and clear renewal thresholds.

### 5. Make minimization, retention, and dissemination first-class governance choices

Surveillance governance is not finished at collection. The archive should require minimization, role-based access, narrow dissemination rules, retention limits, logging, and deletion or review triggers. Otherwise every hard-won warrant rule is neutralized downstream by permanent storage, unchecked sharing, or vague future-use theories.

### 6. Use multiple oversight keys rather than one heroic committee

No single overseer can safely carry this domain alone. A sound intelligence constitution should divide functions across ministers or designated executives, specialized legislative committees with real budget access, independent judges or authorizers, inspectors general or commissioners, privacy and data-protection bodies where applicable, and courts or redress mechanisms that can hear complaints. Secrecy is precisely why oversight has to be redundant.

### 7. Preserve some route to notice, correction, and redress

Not every surveillance target can be notified immediately, but a system with no plausible route to later notice, access, correction, deletion, or challenge is drifting toward permanent impunity. The archive should prefer designs where affected people can eventually seek review, directly or through ombuds, privacy commissioners, special advocates, or courts, consistent with genuine security needs.

### 8. Keep the global layer to standards, rights baselines, and narrow cooperation rather than a transnational secret sovereign

The ideal global role is not a world intelligence state. It is a sparse layer of human-rights norms, privacy baselines, treaty obligations, and constrained cooperation across borders. Cross-border sharing should not become a laundering path by which one state acquires data or powers it could not lawfully obtain itself at home.

## Guardrails

- Do not normalize covert national-security powers at municipal or factional scale.
- Do not let “national security” become a portable excuse for general criminal policing or political monitoring.
- Do not allow intrusive surveillance without prior independent authorization except in tightly defined emergencies.
- Do not treat collection as the only danger; storage, sharing, and reuse are governance decisions too.
- Do not rely on one parliamentary committee or one minister as the sole guardian of a secret state.

## Failure modes

- **local secret police**: lower-tier governments build covert capability that fuses with local political rivalry.
- **mission creep by secrecy**: intelligence powers slide into routine policing, migration control, or dissent management.
- **warrant formalism**: authorization exists on paper but scope, renewal, and minimization are too weak to matter.
- **oversight monoculture**: one committee or executive office becomes the only official story about a hidden system.
- **share-first impunity**: data moves across agencies or borders faster than legality, necessity, or correction can follow.

## Practical tests

An intelligence constitution passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Are covert national-security powers concentrated in a small number of bodies with public legal mandates rather than scattered across local government?
2. Are intelligence, ordinary criminal investigation, and detention powers institutionally separated enough to reduce abuse risk?
3. Do intrusive surveillance measures require prior independent authorization, time limits, and necessity/proportionality analysis?
4. Are minimization, retention, dissemination, and deletion rules explicit enough that collection safeguards survive downstream?
5. Can legislatures, judges, privacy or oversight bodies, and affected individuals all reach the system through some real review path?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can explain **why secrecy is necessary** but cannot explain **which level may wield it, who authorizes it, who reviews it, how it shrinks, and how a person can ever challenge it**, then it has **an intelligence service without a constitutional leash**.
