# 610 — Accountability homes for shared territorial power: scrutiny, audit, disclosure, complaints, review, and no government by jurisdictional gap

## One-line thesis

Once shared metropolitan or regional power becomes real government, it needs an honest **accountability home** as well as a democratic, fiscal, and administrative one: its own scrutiny forum, named records-and-disclosure duties, internal control and audit proportionate to risk, external audit and integrity jurisdiction, complaint and review routes that ordinary people can actually find, and explicit accountability for operators and contractors so the territory is not really governed in the gaps between municipalities, agencies, and the state.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a tighter line on the threshold at which co-operation becomes real government, the democratic form that should follow, the fiscal constitution that should follow, and the administrative operating core that should follow. What it still lacked was the accountability analogue.

That gap matters because shared territorial government often fails in a recognisable way. A metropolitan or regional body gains real power over planning, transport, investment, resilience, debt, housing-supporting infrastructure, or pooled revenue. Yet when residents, journalists, auditors, courts, assembly members, or inspectors ask who can question it, obtain its records, audit its spending, investigate its conflicts, or hear complaints, the answers scatter. One matter is said to belong to a host municipality, another to a transport authority, another to a ministry, another to a contractor, and another to the shared body only “in principle”.

That is not accountability pluralism. It is **government by jurisdictional gap**. Real public power should not become harder to inspect merely because it is shared across a larger territory.

At the same time, the archive should not overbuild. A narrow shared service or bounded consortium does not need the whole apparatus of a territorial constitution. The point is not to put every co-ordinating table under a constitutional glass dome. The point is that once a body can govern a territory in a durable way, it should also be governable **as a body**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Treat accountability as more than elections, and more than upper-tier supervision

Elections matter, but they are not the whole accountability story. Nor is legality supervision by the state the whole story. A real accountability constitution also includes explanation, records, scrutiny, audit, complaint handling, remedies, integrity controls, and visible consequences for misconduct or failure.

The archive should therefore resist two evasions: pretending that periodic elections alone answer all questions once a shared tier is democratically anchored, and pretending that ministerial or prefectural supervision alone answers all questions once legality control exists. Shared territorial power needs a wider accountability stack.

### 2. Build the accountability framework around the territorial body itself, not only around its member governments

Where a body is still genuinely derivative and narrow, member-government accountability can do much of the work. But once the shared body becomes the place where consequential territorial bargains are made, its accountability chain should no longer depend entirely on the scrutiny arrangements of its component municipalities, counties, or ministries.

The archive should therefore prefer a visible accountability framework attached to the shared tier itself: who must report, who may question, who may inspect records, who may audit, who may investigate, who may hear complaints, and which remedies or sanctions apply.

### 3. Give the shared tier its own scrutiny forum and document trail

A territorial government should have a place where decisions are surfaced, questioned, recorded, and followed up in public. That usually means a real assembly or committee structure with agenda rights, questions, hearings, budget follow-up, audit follow-up, and durable minutes and vote records.

This is especially important where metropolitan or regional politics is tempted to live inside executive cartels, mayoral summits, agency boards, or ministerial compacts. Without a forum that can compel explanation and leave a searchable public trace, the territory may be politically governed without ever being institutionally questioned.

### 4. Attach records, disclosure, and open-meeting duties to the body and to those acting for it

Real shared territorial government should not be able to dodge openness by saying the records sit with a host municipality, a vendor, or a special-purpose operator. The archive should therefore prefer rules under which official documents linked to the shared body’s public functions remain reachable wherever they are held for that body.

That means named custody rules, publication duties, prompt request handling, reasons for refusal, and open-meeting or narrow-closure rules proportionate to the power exercised. It also means that the public should not have to decode an institutional maze before knowing where to ask.

### 5. Give the tier its own internal control, risk-management, and internal-audit spine once money and delivery are material

A shared territorial body with serious budgetary, procurement, debt, grant, investment, or operating responsibilities should not rely only on goodwill, ex post scandal, or occasional external inspections. The archive should therefore prefer an internal control and risk-management system proportionate to the scale and hazard of the work.

At a certain point, that also means internal audit: an organised capacity to test controls, inspect risk handling, and verify whether the institution is doing what its statutes, budgets, contracts, and public commitments say it is doing. This does not require a giant audit empire in every case. It does require more than a rotating spreadsheet and the assumption that somebody else must be checking.

### 6. Make external audit, legality review, and integrity jurisdiction explicit

A body that can tax, borrow, contract, build, or allocate should sit under a named external-audit and legality-review regime. The archive should therefore reject any settlement in which it is unclear whether a metropolitan or regional body is audited as a municipality, an agency, a company, a ministry, or not really at all.

The same is true for integrity rules. Conflict-of-interest controls, asset-declaration rules where applicable, procurement challenge routes, ethics investigation, and corruption-risk oversight should have named jurisdiction. A shared body should not become the zone where each watchdog assumes another watchdog is responsible.

### 7. Give residents and affected parties a complaint and remedy route that matches the actual territorial power

Ordinary people should be able to complain, appeal, or seek review without first mastering the constitutional sociology of the territory. Where the shared body makes decisions directly, there should be a direct route to complain to it, challenge it, or seek external review of its action or omission.

Depending on the system, that may mean an ombuds lane, administrative complaint lane, tribunal route, court route, or a layered combination. The institutional form can vary. The archive’s rule is narrower: **real territorial power should have a reachable remedy chain of its own**, even if that chain plugs into wider national institutions.

### 8. Keep operators, authorities, and contractors inside the accountability perimeter

Metropolitan and regional government often works through transport authorities, utilities, development agencies, concessionaires, shared-service operators, and private contractors. That is not inherently illegitimate. But the archive should reject outsourcing as an accountability laundering device.

Bodies exercising public functions for the territory should sit inside the accountability perimeter for records, audit, complaint handling, legality, integrity, and performance reporting to the extent the law permits and the public function requires. Otherwise the polity can become formally public but operationally unanswerable.

### 9. Publish accountability-upgrade triggers alongside democratic, fiscal, and administrative triggers

A polity should not wait for scandal before admitting that a shared body has outgrown borrowed accountability. The archive should therefore prefer visible upgrade triggers.

If a metropolitan or regional body acquires compulsory finance, debt authority, major procurement, broad planning powers, system-wide service authority, or a full territorial executive core, its accountability stack should be reviewed too. The review should ask whether the body now needs a stronger assembly scrutiny lane, its own disclosure rules and records protocols, a clearer audit regime, explicit integrity jurisdiction, and more legible complaint and review routes.

## Accountability-home matrix

| Condition | Borrowed / host accountability mostly fits | Mixed accountability with named shared controls fits | Full territorial accountability home fits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Institution type** | narrow consortium or bounded shared service | stronger co-ordination body with recurring territorial programmes | real metropolitan or regional government |
| **Scrutiny forum** | mainly through member governments | shared board or committee scrutiny plus member-government follow-up | own assembly / committee scrutiny with public records and follow-up powers |
| **Records / disclosure** | host body records with explicit service rules | mixed custody with named access and publication duties | territorial body has its own records, disclosure, and open-meeting regime |
| **Audit / controls** | host-government finance controls may suffice | shared controls plus explicit external-audit coverage | own internal-control and audit spine plus named external audit |
| **Complaints / remedies** | routed mainly through host or member governments | mixed routing with a visible shared front door | direct complaint, appeal, ombuds, or review lane for territorial action |
| **Main risk if misused** | residents fall into jurisdictional ricochet | blurred responsibility between hosts and shared body | overbuild if powers remain narrow and derivative |

## Failure modes

- **government by jurisdictional gap**: every oversight body assumes someone else is responsible.
- **audit orphan**: the territory spends, borrows, or procures at scale but sits under no clear audit spine.
- **disclosure dodge**: official documents are fragmented across hosts, agencies, or vendors so public access becomes guesswork.
- **committee darkness**: the real bargaining happens in bodies with weak records, weak questioning rights, or weak publicity.
- **operator veil**: authorities or contractors exercise public power for the territory while remaining functionally outside scrutiny and complaint lanes.
- **complaints ricochet**: residents are bounced among municipalities, operators, ministries, and the shared body without a real remedy.
- **integrity exceptionalism**: conflict, procurement, or ethics controls apply everywhere except the interstitial bodies where big territorial bargains occur.
- **review lag**: the democratic, fiscal, and administrative form upgrades, but the accountability stack remains stuck at consortium level.

## Practical tests

A shared territorial body has an honest accountability home when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is there a named forum that can question the territorial executive, follow up budgets and plans, and leave a durable public record?
2. Are the body’s official documents, meetings, and publication duties intelligible without forcing the public to decode host arrangements?
3. Is there a clear internal-control and risk-management system proportionate to its budget, delivery, and procurement exposure?
4. Is the body under a named external-audit, legality-review, and integrity jurisdiction?
5. Can residents and affected parties identify a direct complaint or review route for the body’s own actions or omissions?
6. Are operators, authorities, and contractors exercising territorial public functions kept inside the accountability perimeter?
7. If the body’s powers have deepened, has the accountability stack been upgraded as well?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared territorial body now governs real money, plans, services, or territory, but nobody can plainly say who may question it, audit it, obtain its records, complain about it, or review its acts, then it still lacks **an accountability home for shared territorial power** and is drifting into **government by jurisdictional gap**.
