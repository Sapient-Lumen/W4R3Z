# 675 — Shared-regime initiative and text-sponsorship ladders: agenda items, proposals, amendments, cosponsorship, withdrawal, financial screening, and no law by ghost draft

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed initiation constitution** that separates agenda items, draft texts, amendments, explanatory memoranda, cosponsorship, withdrawal, late-paper handling, and **budget, financial, or administrative implications review** — so law does not enter the system by ghost draft, ambush paper, or sponsorless secretariat residue.

## Why this matters

The archive now has notes on representation (`671`), decision rules (`672`), officer-and-secretariat power (`673`), and records/publicity (`674`).

But a regime can still hide real constitutional power one step earlier: in **who gets to start a matter, who owns a text, what supporting case must accompany it, and what screening happens before the floor ever sees it**.

Without a typed initiation constitution, three familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **ghost-draft government**: texts circulate, mutate, and harden even though nobody can say who actually initiated them or under what authority.

The second is **ambush-paper governance**: consequential drafts arrive too late, too thinly explained, or without their budget and administrative consequences, leaving members to choose between blind passage and public delay.

The third is **sponsor laundering**: a politically convenient draft is treated as if it were merely technical secretariat paper, or a staff-prepared paper is later narrated as if the membership itself initiated it.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **initiative and text-sponsorship discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Separate the agenda item from the draft text

An item on the agenda is not yet a proposed rule or decision.

The archive should require regimes to distinguish:

- agenda items,
- background or briefing papers,
- draft decisions or resolutions,
- amendments,
- procedural motions,
- and legally operative final texts.

A body should never have to infer whether “discussion of an item” quietly includes authority to adopt the text someone slipped under that item heading.

### 2. Publish who may initiate what

Not every actor should be able to initiate every object.

The regime should say, by text type, whether initiation may come from:

- a member or participant,
- a threshold of co-sponsors,
- the secretariat,
- a presiding officer or bureau,
- a subsidiary body,
- or an external actor whose proposal must first be adopted or endorsed internally.

If the right to initiate differs between agenda items, urgent supplements, amendments, and treaty-level revisions, that difference should be explicit.

### 3. Require a supporting statement or explanatory memorandum

A serious regime should not accept consequential text with no declared reason, scope, or effect.

The archive should therefore prefer a rule that each non-routine initiative carry a short support packet naming:

- the initiator,
- the problem addressed,
- the legal or constitutional basis,
- the intended effect,
- any urgency claim,
- and the expected fiscal, administrative, or implementation implications.

### 4. Bind initiation to writing, identifiers, and circulation clocks

Initiation should leave behind one stable text head.

The regime should require proposals and amendments to be submitted in writing, assigned a stable identifier, and circulated early enough to permit review.

Late papers may sometimes be unavoidable. That only means the regime needs a visible late-paper lane rather than pretending urgency erases notice.

### 5. Type sponsorship, cosponsorship, and sponsorship withdrawal

The archive should resist one blurred idea of “support”.

A regime should distinguish:

- formal sponsorship,
- co-sponsorship,
- political support without sponsorship,
- technical secretariat drafting support,
- and withdrawal of sponsorship before or after floor consideration.

The historical record should preserve who owned the text at each stage even if the sponsor list changed.

### 6. Screen fiscal and administrative implications before adoption, not after surprise passage

Some texts are cheap to adopt and expensive to execute.

The archive should prefer a rule that consequential initiatives receive visible fiscal, staffing, administrative, or implementation screening before the body adopts them.

This does not mean every proposal needs a full impact assessment. It means no body should legislate blind as to whether the text creates new mandates, recurring costs, reporting burdens, or compliance infrastructure that someone else will later have to fund.

### 7. Distinguish ordinary initiation from urgent supplementary initiation

Urgency is a constitutional lane, not a license to bypass initiation discipline.

The regime should therefore type:

- ordinary deadlines,
- supplementary agenda routes,
- urgent-item thresholds,
- who certifies urgency,
- and what reduced-but-not-zero notice floor still applies.

Otherwise every hard question becomes “urgent” exactly when notice would have constrained it.

### 8. Keep admissibility screening rule-bound and reviewable

Chairs, bureaus, and secretariats often screen items before the floor sees them.

That screening should rest on explicit tests such as scope, completeness, competence, language availability, duplicate status, and timing — not on unreviewable judgments about whether a proposal is politically convenient.

Where a text is refused, deferred, bundled, or returned for completion, the regime should leave a visible trace and an appeal or override lane.

### 9. Preserve authorship even where the secretariat helps draft

Shared regimes need professional drafting help. The archive should not treat that help as a reason to erase political ownership.

If the secretariat prepares consolidated text, redrafts clauses, or aligns language, the regime should still preserve:

- who initiated the matter,
- who requested the draft,
- what negotiating paper became the official draft,
- and who remained responsible for the proposal as it moved.

### 10. Publish one proposal-lifecycle map

A serious regime should have one public map showing, for each text type:

- who may initiate it,
- what minimum support packet is required,
- what time or notice rule applies,
- how fiscal and administrative implications are screened,
- whether co-sponsorship is possible,
- how amendments travel,
- and how withdrawal, deferral, bundling, or lapse is recorded.

If members must reconstruct initiation authority from custom, inbox archaeology, and who happened to send a tracked-change file, the initiation constitution has failed.

## Working matrix

| Initiation question | Honest rule | Why | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| who may place an item on the agenda | explicit initiator classes by text type | separates agenda access from ambient influence | assuming anyone with proximity can start law |
| what must accompany a proposal | explanatory memorandum plus basis and expected effect | makes the text legible before debate | sponsorless paper with no theory of action |
| when the text must be circulated | ordinary deadlines plus urgent supplement lane | prevents surprise government by late paper | treating all late paper as harmless |
| who owns the draft | stable sponsor and cosponsor trace even if secretariat assists | preserves political responsibility | sponsor laundering through staff drafting |
| whether implementation consequences are known | pre-adoption fiscal and administrative screening where material | stops symbolic passage with hidden downstream burden | discovering cost only after adoption |
| how a proposal changes or dies | explicit amendment, bundling, withdrawal, deferral, and lapse traces | makes lifecycle reviewable | letting text disappear into undocumented custom |

## Guardrails

- Do not let agenda discussion substitute for a typed proposal lane.
- Do not accept consequential text without a declared initiator and supporting statement.
- Do not treat secretariat drafting assistance as authorship erasure.
- Do not permit urgent or supplementary items to bypass all notice and implication review.
- Do not hide refusal, deferral, or bundling decisions inside officer discretion.
- Do not allow sponsor withdrawal to rewrite the historical ownership trail.

## Failure modes

- **ghost-draft government**: a text acquires operative force without a visible initiator or adoption path.
- **ambush-paper governance**: consequential drafts arrive too late for serious review.
- **sponsor laundering**: authorship is hidden inside secretariat or chair process.
- **budget-blind legislation**: bodies adopt obligations whose staffing or cost effects were never surfaced.
- **urgency abuse**: supplementary routes become the normal way to evade initiation discipline.
- **agenda monopolization**: only insiders with procedural access can place matters into the regime.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it distinguish agenda items from draft decisions and amendments?
2. Does it publish which actors may initiate which text types?
3. Must non-routine initiatives carry a supporting memorandum or statement?
4. Are written submission, identifiers, and circulation clocks visible?
5. Are sponsorship, cosponsorship, and sponsorship withdrawal typed separately?
6. Are fiscal and administrative implications surfaced before adoption where material?
7. Is there a bounded urgent or supplementary initiation lane rather than ambient exception?
8. Are refusals, deferrals, withdrawals, and lapses recorded somewhere reviewable?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can say **who voted and what text passed** but cannot say **who initiated the matter, what support packet accompanied it, what implications were surfaced, and how the draft moved from proposal to floor**, it still has **decisions without an honest initiation constitution**.
