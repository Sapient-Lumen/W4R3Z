# 667 — Differentiated commitments in shared regimes: reservations, opt-outs, derogations, transition periods, territorial application, review clocks, and no uniformity by false claim

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should maintain a **typed differentiation register** that distinguishes reservations, interpretive declarations, opt-outs, temporary derogations, transition periods, territorial applications, and enhanced-cooperation rings — with legal basis, scope, duration, review clock, and accession or withdrawal path — so a regime can use lawful variable geometry without lying that every member carries the same obligation.

## Why this matters

The archive now says more clearly how cross-border regimes should choose their depth (`663`), how burdens should be allocated (`664`), how commitments should land domestically (`665`), and how compliance should escalate (`666`). But one practical constitutional fact still needs a tighter object:

**shared regimes often do not bind every member in exactly the same way, on the same timetable, in the same territory, or at the same level of obligation.**

Sometimes that is lawful and desirable. Sometimes it is a camouflage device.

A reservation can preserve consent where the treaty allows it. A transition period can make convergence real instead of fictitious. A temporary derogation can prevent a brittle one-size-fits-all rule from breaking under emergency or capacity stress. An enhanced-cooperation ring can let a smaller club move further without pretending the whole wider polity already agreed.

But the same tools can also rot a regime from the inside when they become indefinite privilege, hidden carve-out, or public misdescription. If the public hears that a state "joined" or a government says a rule is "in force", but the real map of reservations, exclusions, territorial limits, or delayed application remains buried in depositary notices and ministry footnotes, the regime has already drifted into constitutional dishonesty.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **differentiated commitment discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Distinguish kinds of differentiation instead of calling every exception an "opt-out"

Not all departures from full common obligation are the same.

The archive should keep separate at least these types:

1. reservations or objections that modify legal effect where the treaty permits them,
2. interpretive declarations that explain a party's reading without formally changing the obligation,
3. explicit opt-outs or non-participation clauses built into the regime,
4. temporary derogations or waivers,
5. transition periods and staged implementation,
6. territorial extension or exclusion,
7. deeper participation clubs or enhanced-cooperation rings,
8. and non-application between particular parties where the legal order allows it.

Each has a different legitimacy test, time logic, and public explanation burden.

### 2. Preserve full common obligation as the default baseline

Differentiation should begin from an explicit common baseline rather than from an unspoken presumption that every member may bargain its own bespoke treaty.

The archive should therefore require every shared regime to say:

- what the full standard obligation is,
- what the lawful differentiation tools are,
- who can use them,
- and what conditions or limits attach.

Without a visible baseline, the regime stops being differentiated common law and becomes a fog of parallel bilateral deals.

### 3. Keep reservations narrow, text-linked, and treaty-compatible

Where reservations are allowed, they should be explicit, deposited through the proper legal lane, and tied to identified provisions or effects.

The archive should resist three common confusions:

- a reservation is not the same thing as a press release about political intention,
- an interpretive declaration is not the same thing as a lawful modification of obligation,
- and a reservation cannot do whatever the treaty's own compatibility rules forbid.

If the regime prohibits reservations, or if a purported reservation would destroy the core bargain, the answer is not to relabel the carve-out and move on.

### 4. Use derogations and waivers as temporary repair tools, not as parallel constitutions

Temporary derogation can be an honest instrument where immediate full conformity is impossible or where a short emergency exception is genuinely necessary.

But the archive should require every derogation to declare:

- purpose,
- legal basis,
- exact suspended or modified obligation,
- scope,
- duration,
- reporting duties,
- and the re-entry test.

A derogation without a clock or return condition is usually not a derogation. It is an undeclared second constitution.

### 5. Treat transition periods as convergence machinery, not soft permanent exemption

Staged implementation is often more honest than nominal simultaneity. Some members need time to legislate, build institutions, finance infrastructure, or retrain regulators before they can carry the common rule.

But a transition period should therefore name the convergence path: milestones, interim duties, support measures, review dates, and the condition for full ordinary application. The archive should reject "transitional" arrangements that are politically indefinite and administratively content-free.

### 6. Keep territorial application and exclusion visible

Many regimes do not apply in exactly the same way across every territory linked to a member. Territorial extensions, exclusions, dependencies, overseas units, and special-status zones can materially change who is bound and where.

The public register of a regime should therefore show not only **which members participate**, but also **which territories are in or out for each instrument or obligation class**. Otherwise map ambiguity becomes legal ambiguity.

### 7. Permit deeper clubs, but keep them open, typed, and accountable to the wider framework

Some regimes legitimately allow a subset of members to move further or faster. That can be a strength when the legal lane is visible and later participation remains possible.

The archive should insist that such deeper clubs say:

- what additional obligations apply only within the club,
- who may join later,
- what prior acts a later joiner must accept,
- what institutions decide for the club and for the wider regime,
- and how non-participants are protected from covert spillover.

Variable geometry should be governed pluralism, not constitutional fog.

### 8. Protect rights floors and common safety floors against carve-out abuse

Not every field can bear the same amount of differentiation.

The archive should expect tighter limits on reservations, opt-outs, or derogations where the regime protects:

- minimum rights floors,
- existential safety baselines,
- shared epidemiological or environmental alerting,
- or mutually trusted market and mobility infrastructures that fail when hidden divergence becomes too large.

Differentiation should be easiest where timing and sequencing vary, and hardest where hidden divergence destroys the common floor itself.

### 9. Publish review, withdrawal, and later-accession paths

Differentiation that can begin but not be reconsidered is poorly designed.

Every differentiated commitment should therefore carry one or more of the following:

- periodic review,
- expiration or sunset,
- a path to withdraw a reservation or declaration,
- a path to end a derogation,
- a path for a non-participant to join later,
- and where relevant a path to step back lawfully rather than by silent non-compliance.

A regime is more stable when it can show how differentiation narrows, widens, ends, or converts over time.

## Differentiation matrix

| Differentiation type | Honest use | Minimum public fields | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| reservation | preserve consent where treaty text allows limited modification | affected provision, legal effect, deposit date, objections if relevant, compatibility status | calling an incompatible carve-out a routine reservation |
| interpretive declaration | clarify reading without purporting to change obligation | text, scope, legal status, challenge route | using a "declaration" to smuggle in a reservation |
| explicit opt-out / non-participation clause | allow lawful non-participation in a bounded module | instrument or module, institutions that still bind, later-join path | saying a member is fully in when whole modules do not apply |
| temporary derogation / waiver | bridge emergency or short implementation difficulty | legal basis, reason, duration, conditions, reporting, re-entry test | open-ended exemption by habit |
| transition period | stage convergence toward the common rule | milestones, interim duties, support, review date, full-application trigger | permanent transition theater |
| territorial application / exclusion | reflect lawful geographic scope differences | covered territories, excluded territories, start and end dates, competent authority | hiding major territorial limits in depositary metadata alone |
| enhanced-cooperation / deeper club | let some members move further inside a wider framework | participating members, extra obligations, governance lane, accession rules, spillover guardrails | club government that quietly binds outsiders |

## Guardrails

- Do not use one word like "opt-out" for every form of differentiated commitment.
- Do not describe the regime publicly as uniform when reservations, carve-outs, or territorial exclusions materially alter the common rule.
- Do not permit derogations with no sunset, milestone, or re-entry condition.
- Do not let transition periods become inherited privilege.
- Do not let interpretive declarations silently modify the legal bargain.
- Do not allow deeper clubs to govern outsiders through infrastructure or market power without explicit authority.
- Do not hide territorial scope differences in annexes no ordinary governed person will ever see.

## Failure modes

- **uniformity by false claim**: public language says everyone accepted the same obligation when the real regime is deeply differentiated.
- **permanent temporary**: a derogation or transition period survives for years with no real convergence path.
- **reservation laundering**: an incompatible or politically embarrassing carve-out is relabeled as interpretation or implementation detail.
- **map fog**: the regime names members but not the territories to which the obligation really applies.
- **club capture**: a deeper participating ring quietly sets de facto standards for outsiders without the declared legal lane to do so.
- **one-way differentiation**: members can secure exemptions but there is no visible path back to ordinary full participation.

## Practical tests

A differentiated shared regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Has it named the full common baseline before listing any departures?
2. Does each reservation, declaration, opt-out, derogation, or transition tool have a distinct legal type?
3. Are scope, territory, duration, and review fields public for each differentiated commitment?
4. Are rights floors or safety floors protected against over-differentiation?
5. Can outsiders tell which members and territories are bound by which layer of obligation?
6. If a deeper club exists, are entry, later accession, and spillover limits visible?
7. Is there a public path to withdraw, expire, or convert differentiated status over time?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can say **who joined** but cannot say **what exactly each participant accepted, what they reserved or delayed, where the rule applies, how any exception ends, and how later convergence occurs**, it still has **membership language without an honest differentiation constitution**.
