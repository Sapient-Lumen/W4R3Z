# 685 — Shared-regime procurement constitutions: competition, thresholds, exceptions, award records, protest, debarment, and no regime by sole-source habit

## One-line thesis

Shared regimes should publish a **typed procurement constitution** that separates planning, solicitation methods, competition defaults, threshold rules, emergency and sole-source exceptions, award records, debriefing, complaint or protest lanes, vendor-sanctions logic, contract-change control, and exit capacity — so the regime is not governed by incumbent vendors, emergency habit, or off-book buyer discretion.

## Why this matters

The archive now has notes on participation, representation, decision rules, records, sessions, language, budgets, and workforce (`671`–`684`). That is enough to explain **who sits in the regime, how it decides, what it publishes, how it pays for itself, and how it staffs its secretariat**. It is still not enough to explain **how the regime buys the goods and services that often become its real operating skeleton**.

That gap matters because shared regimes often act through contracts before they act through doctrine.

The servers are hosted by a vendor. The inspection platform is operated by a contractor. The field logistics chain depends on framework agreements. The registry is maintained through cloud spend and integrators. Emergency procurement is invoked repeatedly until it becomes the real procurement constitution. A consulting roster stands in for hiring. An incumbent extension keeps recurring because the regime never typed when continuity risk justifies it and when it instead proves it has let exit capacity decay.

Without a typed procurement constitution, six familiar pathologies appear.

The first is **sole-source habit**: urgency, incumbent knowledge, or donor preference quietly replace competition as the normal lane.

The second is **threshold opacity**: no one can tell when the regime should use quotation, formal tender, restricted competition, direct award, or long-term agreement.

The third is **award invisibility**: the public sees a contract outcome but not the method, criteria, exception basis, or protest path that produced it.

The fourth is **vendor-governed continuity**: extensions, change orders, and support renewals become the real constitutional lane because the regime has no tested handoff or recompetition discipline.

The fifth is **sanctions confusion**: poor performance, integrity failure, exclusion, suspension, and cross-regime ineligibility all collapse into one vague blacklist rumor.

The sixth is **contract-afterlife government**: once a contract is signed, performance management, inspection, acceptance, amendment, and exit fall outside the visible governance spine.

This note therefore adds one compact doctrine for **shared-regime procurement discipline**.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Publish one visible buyer constitution

A serious regime should show in one place:

- who owns procurement policy,
- who approves methods and awards,
- which office conducts solicitations,
- which contracts need legal or financial clearance,
- what publication channels exist,
- and what complaint, sanction, and audit routes apply.

If outsiders must reconstruct procurement law from templates, ad hoc waivers, and vendor folklore, the regime is already buying through shadow constitution.

### 2. Type procurement methods by threshold, risk, and object

Not every purchase needs the same lane.

The regime should distinguish at minimum:

- low-value purchase or quotation,
- formal invitation to bid,
- request for proposal where quality and method matter,
- prequalified or roster-based competition where justified,
- framework or long-term agreement call-off,
- and direct or single-source award under named exceptions.

Thresholds do not need to be globally identical. They do need to be visible, reviewable, and linked to risk rather than habit.

### 3. Make competition the default and exceptions typed

Competition should be the presumptive procurement rule for consequential contracts.

A serious regime should then type its exception lanes separately: emergency need, genuine sole-capability, interoperability or standardization necessity, failed competition, limited pilot, security sensitivity, or bounded continuity bridge. Each should require a written basis, approval lane, and expiry or recompetition plan.

This is how the archive distinguishes honest exception from procurement by relationship.

### 4. Separate vendor qualification from award decision

A regime should distinguish whether a supplier is eligible or qualified to compete from whether that supplier should win a particular contract.

Registration, qualification, code-of-conduct acceptance, exclusion screening, and financial or technical capacity checks belong in one lane. Award against the published criteria belongs in another. Otherwise prequalification quietly becomes preferred-vendor government.

### 5. Publish criteria, record the basis, and offer debriefing

The regime should say before award:

- the method,
- the criteria and their weights or logic,
- mandatory requirements,
- any negotiation lane,
- and the authority to award.

After award it should preserve one award record naming the route, shortlisted or responsive field if applicable, winning basis, and any exception or best-value rationale. Unsuccessful bidders should have a bounded debriefing lane that improves future competition without exposing protected competitor data.

### 6. Provide one procurement complaint or protest lane

If a supplier believes the regime did not follow its own procurement rules, there should be one typed complaint or protest mechanism with standing rules, filing clocks, scope limits, review authority, and interim-protection logic where appropriate.

This lane is not a promise that every procurement dispute becomes litigation. It is a minimum constitutional check against ghost waiver, hidden criteria drift, and award by insider confidence.

### 7. Separate integrity sanctions from ordinary contract management

Not every bad vendor issue is the same.

The archive should therefore prefer separate lanes for:

- poor performance and corrective action,
- contract default and remedies,
- temporary suspension pending review,
- exclusion or debarment for fraud, corruption, collusion, coercion, abuse, or other integrity failure,
- and cross-recognition of sanctions where lawfully adopted.

Without that separation, regimes either over-punish ordinary underperformance or under-punish integrity breaches.

### 8. Constitutionalize change orders, extensions, and emergency bridges

A contract amendment is not a harmless administrative epilogue.

The regime should publish when extensions, quantity increases, scope changes, rate changes, and emergency bridge contracts may occur, what ceilings apply, who approves them, and when they trigger fresh competition. This is the main anti-incumbency discipline after award.

### 9. Keep inspection, acceptance, and payment inside the governance spine

Procurement legitimacy does not end at signature.

The regime should type:

- receiving and inspection,
- acceptance authority,
- invoice and payment controls,
- performance reporting,
- nonconformity escalation,
- and auditability of the whole file.

Otherwise the institution governs awards carefully and execution casually, which is how contract-afterlife government takes hold.

### 10. Make exit and handback a procurement question from the start

For consequential contracts, especially digital, logistics, registry, inspection, and field-support contracts, the regime should know before award:

- what data, tools, inventory, credentials, and documentation must be handed back,
- who may step in,
- how continuity is protected during transition,
- and what recompetition or off-ramp plan exists.

A regime that cannot leave a supplier does not fully govern the function it has contracted out.

## Working matrix

| Procurement object | Honest rule | Why | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| method selection | tie quotation, tender, proposal, framework, and direct award to named thresholds and risk tests | keeps buying lanes intelligible | method choice by convenience or sponsor preference |
| competition default | require competition unless a typed exception is justified in writing | protects value and legitimacy | habitual sole-sourcing |
| qualification vs award | screen eligibility separately from evaluation and award | prevents roster status from becoming entitlement | preferred-vendor government |
| debriefing and protest | provide bounded debriefing and one complaint lane | gives suppliers a rule-following check | awards that are final because no one can contest process failure |
| sanctions and performance | separate poor performance, default, suspension, and debarment | matches consequence to problem type | blacklist rumor or one-size punishment |
| amendments and exit | type extensions, change orders, handback, and recompetition triggers | stops continuity from becoming capture | permanent contract government by bridge extension |

## Guardrails

- Do not let emergency, continuity, or interoperability language erase the competition default.
- Do not let rosters, frameworks, or prequalification become hidden preferred-vendor constitutions.
- Do not let award criteria drift after solicitation without cancellation and honest restart where required.
- Do not treat debriefing as optional courtesy if the regime claims to buy by rule.
- Do not collapse poor performance, contract default, fraud, and debarment into one vague vendor-badness bucket.
- Do not let contract amendments or emergency bridge contracts grow into the real procurement constitution.
- Do not treat signature as the end of governance; inspection, acceptance, and handback belong inside the same discipline.

## Failure modes

- **regime by sole-source habit**: exception lanes silently become the default operating model.
- **threshold opacity**: buyers and outsiders cannot tell which method should have been used.
- **award invisibility**: contracts appear without a visible method, criteria, or exception basis.
- **vendor-governed continuity**: incumbent extensions replace exit planning and recompetition.
- **sanctions confusion**: integrity failure and ordinary poor performance are handled through the same blunt instrument.
- **contract-afterlife government**: post-award execution escapes the constitutional spine.

## Practical tests

A regime passes this note when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Does it publish one visible procurement constitution naming policy owner, buyer, approvers, and complaint routes?
2. Are procurement methods typed by threshold, object, and risk rather than left to buyer intuition?
3. Is competition the default for consequential contracts, with written and bounded exception lanes?
4. Are vendor qualification and contract award treated as separate decisions?
5. Are method, criteria, authority, and exception basis visible in the solicitation and award record?
6. Does the regime offer bounded debriefing and one procurement complaint or protest lane?
7. Are performance remedies, suspension, exclusion, and debarment kept in distinct lanes?
8. Are change orders, extensions, and emergency bridges typed with ceilings and recompetition triggers?
9. Are inspection, acceptance, invoice control, and performance reporting visibly governed?
10. Does the regime know in advance how data, tools, stock, and service continuity will be handed back at exit?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a shared regime can **commit common money to vendors** but cannot say **which buying lane applies, when competition may be displaced, how award reasons are recorded, how suppliers may complain, how sanctions differ from poor performance, and how exit will work**, it still has **procurement power without an honest procurement constitution**.
