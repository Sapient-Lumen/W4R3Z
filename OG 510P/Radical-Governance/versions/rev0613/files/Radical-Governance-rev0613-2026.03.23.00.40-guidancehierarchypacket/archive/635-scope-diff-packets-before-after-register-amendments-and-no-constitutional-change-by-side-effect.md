# 635 — Scope-diff packets, before/after register amendments, and no constitutional change by side effect

## One-line thesis

An ideal multilevel polity should require any legally or operationally material change to who governs what to ship with a public **scope-diff packet** against the canonical scope register, showing the before-and-after allocation, legal lane, consultation or consent route, jurisprudence impact, exception clocks, and review duties, so the stack cannot be rewired by side effect across statutes, grants, budgets, software, or emergency residue.

## Why this matters

The archive already says more clearly **how scope should be assigned** (`579`, `602`-`605`), **what proof upper-tier action owes before it narrows lower-tier room** (`590`), **when real drift should reopen review** (`632`), **where the live stack should be written down** (`633`), and **how competence rulings should become constitutional memory** (`634`).

What it still lacked was the change-discipline that connects all of those pieces. A polity can have a canonical scope register and still let real rescoping happen obliquely: one clause in a budget law, one grant condition, one shared platform default, one emergency order, one intergovernmental compact, one procurement standard, or one ministry circular at a time. Months later the stack has changed, but no one can point to one public before-and-after object saying **what moved, by which lane, under which authority, with what temporary or permanent status, and what now needs review or handback**.

That gap matters because multilevel constitutional drift is often incremental rather than theatrical. The archive should therefore prefer a standing doctrine of **scope-diff packets**. If a proposal or operational change would materially alter the live scope register, it should not be allowed to travel as a side effect hidden inside broader lawmaking, budgeting, procurement, digitisation, or crisis management.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Require a scope-diff packet whenever the live scope register would materially change

Not every policy choice changes the constitutional stack. But any instrument that materially alters the legal home, practical holder, finance path, territorial footprint, override lane, review route, or exception status of a major function should carry a scope-diff packet.

The packet requirement should attach at least to:

- bills, decrees, regulations, or constitutional amendments that reassign or narrow powers;
- grant schemes, funding conditions, or equalization rules that materially steer lower-tier discretion;
- emergency measures that suspend or centralize ordinary responsibility;
- intergovernmental compacts or authority-creation instruments that create new territorial power in practice;
- digital platforms, registry rules, or workflow mandates that silently move operational control;
- and budgetary or procurement reforms that effectively decide who governs a lane even if the formal legal text stays broad.

### 2. Make the packet a before-and-after map, not a recital of good intentions

A serious scope-diff packet should begin with a compact comparison between the current register entry and the proposed one. At minimum it should show:

- the function and lane or lanes affected;
- the current legal home and operational holder;
- the proposed legal home and operational holder;
- the territorial footprint before and after;
- the finance path before and after;
- whether the change is ordinary, emergency, delegated, provisional, experimental, or permanent;
- and which register fields would be updated if the change is adopted.

Without a before-and-after object, scope reform dissolves into prose and summary rhetoric.

### 3. Name the legal lane that authorises the change

A packet should not merely say that change is desirable. It should say **which legal lane is being used**: ordinary statute, constitutional amendment, territorial compact, delegated-legislation power, emergency clause, judicial order, or other named route.

This is how the note docks into `630`. A proposal that cannot say which lane lawfully permits the move is not ready for adoption, even if the substantive case sounds attractive.

### 4. Attach the consultation, consent, and challenge route in the same packet

Where local or regional self-government, constituent bargains, or territorial boundaries are affected, the packet should say:

- which authorities or associations must be consulted;
- whether consultation is advisory, mandatory, consent-based, or double-majoritarian;
- what challenge or review routes remain open during adoption;
- and what interim protections apply if the change is contested.

This stops consultation from becoming a separate forgotten annex and makes challenge rights visible before the stack is changed.

### 5. Link the packet to the scope-jurisprudence digest and live precedent state

Every material packet should say whether it:

- implements a ruling,
- departs from an existing line of jurisprudence,
- legislates around a ruling,
- or leaves a disputed point unresolved.

Where precedent is relevant, the packet should link to the digest entry and state whether the proposed change would update, narrow, supersede, or test that settlement.

This is how the note docks into `634`. The point is not to turn every packet into a legal essay. It is to stop institutions from pretending they are acting on clean constitutional ground when they are really stepping into live doctrinal terrain.

### 6. Force visibility about temporary status, handback, and review clocks

If a change is provisional, emergency-driven, pilot-based, capacity-repair based, or otherwise justified as temporary, the packet should include:

- the expiry or handback date,
- the conditions for extension,
- the evidence needed for continuation,
- the restoration lane,
- and the authority responsible for capability repair during the temporary hold.

This docks the packet into `631` and keeps temporary rescoping from hardening into constitutional fact by inertia.

### 7. Distinguish policy ambition from stack alteration

A proposal can be politically ambitious without changing which level truly governs. Conversely, a seemingly technical administrative reform can rewire the stack profoundly.

The packet should therefore answer a plain threshold question: **does this measure alter the scope register or only operate within it?**

If the answer is no, the proposal should say so and identify why. If the answer is yes, the packet should carry the full before-and-after map. This prevents both over-classifying ordinary policy as constitutional drama and under-classifying quiet rescoping as mere administration.

### 8. Keep one stable packet identifier that survives the whole change cycle

A scope-diff packet should not disappear once a bill is enacted or a platform is deployed. It should keep one stable identifier from proposal through adoption, challenge, amendment, implementation, review, and handback.

That identifier should link:

- the initiating proposal,
- the consultation log,
- the adopted legal text or executive act,
- the changed register entry,
- any digest entry created or updated,
- and the later review or reversal record.

Without a stable object, the public can see fragments of rescoping but not one constitutional change trace.

### 9. Prefer machine-readable diff fields alongside short prose

The archive should prefer packets that are readable by humans and updateable by systems. That means short prose plus structured fields for before/after values, status, dates, affected territories, legal lane, review route, and linked objects.

The point is not technocracy for its own sake. It is to make the live stack auditable, replayable, and mergeable across legislative, administrative, judicial, and public-information surfaces.

### 10. Reject adoption when the change is real but the packet is missing

A scope-diff packet only matters if it can block or slow sloppy change. The polity should therefore prefer a visible return lane when:

- the packet is missing,
- the before-and-after fields are incomplete,
- the legal lane is unclear,
- consultation or consent requirements are misstated,
- or temporary centralization lacks handback discipline.

Otherwise the packet becomes decorative and constitutional side effects continue as before.

## Guardrails

- Do not let broad policy rationales substitute for a before-and-after scope map.
- Do not treat budget, grant, procurement, or software changes as automatically non-constitutional.
- Do not separate consultation, consent, and review routes from the change object that needs them.
- Do not let emergency, pilot, or transition language excuse missing handback fields.
- Do not update the live scope register without preserving the prior state and the packet that justified the change.

## Failure modes

- **constitutional change by side effect**: the stack changes through laws, grants, code, or administrative practice without one visible before-and-after object.
- **register aftercare without change discipline**: the canonical register is updated after the fact, but the proposal never had to declare that it was changing the stack.
- **technical disguise**: platform defaults, data gateways, procurement standards, or reporting rules silently move real control while being narrated as mere implementation.
- **temporary without return lane**: emergency or pilot rescoping ships with no expiry, handback, or repair duties.
- **precedent evasion**: institutions step around live competence rulings because packets never have to name the doctrinal terrain they touch.

## Practical tests

A scope-diff discipline passes when it can answer yes to all of the following:

1. Is there a declared trigger for when a proposal must carry a scope-diff packet?
2. Does the packet show the relevant register entries before and after the change?
3. Does it identify the legal lane authorising the move and the consultation or consent route required?
4. Does it show whether the change is temporary, delegated, emergency-held, or permanent and name the relevant review or handback clocks?
5. Does it link to affected jurisprudence and to the live scope register update that would follow adoption?
6. Can the polity return, delay, or openly flag a proposal when the packet is missing or incomplete?

## Compression rule for the archive

If a polity can materially change **who really governs what** without publishing one stable before-and-after packet against the live scope register, then it is not merely updating policy. It is permitting **constitutional change by side effect**.
