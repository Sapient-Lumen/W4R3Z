# 546 — Public enterprises and state-owned enterprises: explicit rationales, separated roles, hard budget constraints, and open books

## One-line thesis

An ideal polity may own and operate enterprises where public ownership is genuinely justified — for natural monopolies, essential network infrastructure, strategic resilience, mission-critical finance, or other clearly stated public purposes — but it should publish why it owns them, separate ownership from regulation and policy-making, state public-service obligations explicitly, compensate them transparently, impose hard budget constraints, keep boards and books professional and visible, and review the portfolio often enough that public enterprise does not become a hiding place for patronage, off-balance-sheet debt, or anti-competitive favoritism.

## Why this matters

The archive now has stronger doctrines for governments by scope, intergovernmental machinery, special-purpose authorities, delegated public functions, and regulators. What it still needed was one compact note on the subset of public institutions that trade, borrow, employ, build, and hold assets like firms while remaining instruments of the state: public enterprises and state-owned enterprises.

These bodies matter because they often run the commanding systems of ordinary life. Electricity, rail, ports, housing finance, development banking, water, postal networks, hospitals, natural-resource firms, broadband backbones, and strategic industrial capacity are frequently carried by public corporations rather than by ministries. Sometimes that is exactly right. Sometimes it is where governments park political promises, hidden subsidies, liabilities, surplus employment, or discretionary power they do not want to defend in the budget and before the legislature.

That ambiguity creates recurring failures. One is market romanticism, where every public enterprise is treated as a relic even when the function is a natural monopoly, a foundational network, or a resilience instrument that markets alone undersupply. The other is ownership romanticism, where the state keeps firms for vague patriotic or political reasons, then excuses weak governance, soft bailouts, blurred mandates, and shadow fiscal operations as the price of public purpose.

An ideal government therefore needs a doctrine of **public enterprise constitutionalism**. The right answer is neither blanket privatization nor permanent portfolio accumulation. It is disciplined ownership: own only for named reasons; govern like an accountable owner rather than an improvised patron; keep competition, subsidy, and fiscal truth legible; and exit or redesign when the rationale no longer holds.

## Pattern pack

### 1. Start from a published ownership rationale, not inherited habit

A polity should not own firms merely because it inherited them, because ownership feels prestigious, or because no one wants to defend an exit decision. It should publish an ownership policy that explains why each category of enterprise remains public: natural-monopoly characteristics, universal-service obligations, system resilience, development-finance gaps, strategic infrastructure, mission-critical research capacity, or another defensible reason.

That rationale should be specific enough to falsify. If an enterprise could now be regulated, concessioned, mutualized, municipally owned, broken up, or returned to ordinary markets without sacrificing access, resilience, labour fairness, territorial inclusion, or public value, the state should say why continued ownership still beats those alternatives.

### 2. Separate the state’s roles as owner, regulator, policymaker, and purchaser

The state should not referee a market, write the sector strategy, buy services, and manage the enterprise as owner through one fused chain of command. Those roles should be institutionally separated enough that pricing, subsidy, licensing, procurement, and competition decisions can be reviewed without guessing which hat the state was wearing.

Where separation is impossible in small systems, the polity should at least require explicit role declarations, conflict-routing rules, published decisions, and external review paths. Public ownership is hardest to defend when the same officials can rewrite the rules, fund the firm, and declare its superior performance.

### 3. State public-service obligations explicitly and compensate them transparently

If a public enterprise is required to serve remote areas, maintain uneconomic routes, hold strategic reserves, cross-subsidize access, preserve surge capacity, or pursue other public-service obligations, those obligations should be written down, costed where feasible, and disclosed. They should not be hidden inside vague expectations that make every performance discussion incoherent.

The compensation method matters too. A polity should distinguish commercial activity from public-service obligation compensation, emergency support, and capital injections. Otherwise managers, workers, legislators, and the public cannot tell whether a firm is badly run, deliberately mission-bearing, or being used as a fiscal side pocket.

### 4. Impose hard budget constraints and disclose fiscal risk honestly

Public enterprises should not enjoy permanent ambiguity about who will rescue them. Governments may sometimes choose to support an enterprise for legitimate public reasons, but those decisions should be explicit, priced, and governed. Guarantees, on-lending, letters of comfort, arrears tolerance, directed lending, tax forbearance, and quasi-fiscal mandates should be treated as public exposures, not as costless administrative folklore.

An ideal polity should therefore monitor public enterprises as fiscal-risk transmitters. It should publish transfers, dividends, guarantees, major contingent liabilities, quasi-fiscal activities, and aggregate portfolio performance, and it should use that information in budget, debt, and resilience planning. No enterprise should become a warehouse for losses the finance ministry does not want to see until a bailout arrives.

### 5. Keep boards professional, bounded, and answerable

Public ownership does not excuse amateur governance. Boards should be selected for competence, integrity, and mission fit rather than party service or ministerial intimacy. They should have clear duties, conflict rules, auditable minutes, and authority that is real but not sovereign.

The state should usually set the mandate, ownership expectations, and public constraints; appoint a capable board through visible procedures; and then judge performance against published objectives. It should not micromanage every business decision and then blame “the board” for choices it never really allowed the enterprise to make.

### 6. Use transparency standards closer to listed-company discipline than to ministry opacity

Public enterprises should generally disclose enough that citizens, workers, users, creditors, competitors, regulators, and legislatures can understand what is happening. Annual reports, audited financial statements, ownership structures, material related-party transactions, major subsidies, board remuneration, public-service obligations, and aggregate portfolio reporting should be normal rather than heroic.

This matters especially when enterprises sit between public law and market activity. Ministry-style opacity plus corporate legal form is one of the easiest ways to hide risk, favouritism, and under-performance in plain sight.

### 7. Respect competition and competitive neutrality where enterprises operate in markets

A public enterprise should not win simply because the state tilts the field. Where it competes with private or cooperative providers, the polity should examine whether regulatory privileges, tax treatment, procurement design, state support, or vague public-service claims are distorting the market beyond what a legitimate public purpose requires.

This does not mean pretending all sectors are ordinary markets. It means being honest about when a public enterprise is carrying a mission and when it is simply receiving hidden advantage. If the state wants a social objective, it should name it, compensate it if appropriate, and constrain spillover favoritism.

### 8. Review the portfolio recurrently and make entry, redesign, and exit governable

An ownership policy should not be a museum label. Governments should review the portfolio regularly: what remains worth owning, what should be re-chartered, what should be devolved, what should be broken into infrastructure and service layers, what should become a regulated utility, and what should be wound down or sold.

Exit should not be treated as betrayal, and retention should not be treated as default virtue. The test is whether public ownership still solves a real problem better than the available alternatives under present conditions.

## Failure modes

- **portfolio patriotism**: the state treats ownership itself as proof of virtue and stops asking whether the rationale still holds.
- **role fusion**: the same political actors regulate, subsidize, procure from, and supervise the enterprise with no clean review route.
- **soft-budget theater**: everyone acts as though the enterprise is commercial while planning on rescue, arrears tolerance, or hidden guarantees.
- **quasi-fiscal laundering**: losses, subsidies, or social policy are pushed through enterprise balance sheets so they disappear from ordinary budget politics.
- **board patronage**: appointments reward loyalty first and competence second.
- **competitive-neutrality fog**: the enterprise’s public mission and market advantages are left deliberately indistinct.
- **permanent exception**: the firm is exempted from ordinary disclosure, audit, procurement, labour, or integrity discipline because it is “strategic.”

## Anti-theater tests

1. Can the polity explain in one page why this enterprise is publicly owned **instead of** regulated, concessioned, municipally held, mutualized, or left to an ordinary market?
2. Are public-service obligations written, costed where feasible, and separated from commercial performance?
3. Could an outside reviewer map all material transfers, guarantees, tax expenditures, arrears tolerance, and contingent liabilities between the enterprise and the state?
4. Is there a visible separation between ownership, regulation, sector policy, and purchasing authority?
5. Would the enterprise still look well governed if board appointments changed hands tomorrow and every related-party transaction became front-page news?
