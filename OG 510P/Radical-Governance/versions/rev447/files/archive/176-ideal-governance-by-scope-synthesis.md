# Ideal Governance by Scope (Synthesis)

**Purpose:** define what “ideal government” means **at each scope** (micro-local → global) without repeating the whole archive.

**Person served:** anyone trying to locate the *right* authority for a problem, understand what that authority owes them, and know what happens when it fails.

**From-below:** if an institution says “not our job,” this memo helps you prove where responsibility belongs, how it should coordinate, and what continuity you’re owed.

## What “ideal” means here

“Ideal” does **not** mean one constitution, one party system, or one cultural template.

It means each scope does the things it is uniquely suited to do, while the whole stack still delivers:
1. **rights floors that do not disappear by postcode,**
2. **services at the scale where people actually live and work,**
3. **redistribution and risk-pooling at the scale big enough to absorb shocks,**
4. **spillover control at the scale where externalities can actually be bound,**
5. **continuity when people cross borders or institutions fail.**

The OECD’s current multi-level governance framing is simple: policy works better when national, regional, and local levels coordinate well rather than govern in silos [BIB-OECD-MLG-HUB-2026]. The EU’s subsidiarity test is also useful outside the EU: intervene higher only where lower levels cannot solve the problem effectively and the higher level has clear advantages [BIB-EURLEX-SUBSIDIARITY-2024].

So the design problem is not “centralize or decentralize.” It is **assign, coordinate, and audit**.

---

## Six assignment tests (before deciding the scope)

Use these tests together. A scope assignment that fails several of them is usually bad design.

### 1. Local knowledge intensity
Does good performance depend on context that is easiest to see close to the ground: the building, block, neighborhood, clinic, school, stop, intake desk, river reach, or labor market?

High local knowledge intensity pushes authority **downward**.

### 2. Spillover radius
How far do the harms or benefits travel? If the effects cross municipal lines, labor markets, watersheds, grids, or borders, the coordinating authority must scale up enough to see and bind them.

High spillover pushes authority **upward or sideways into a compact body**.

### 3. Rights-floor sensitivity
Would local discretion predictably produce unequal access to rights, detention safeguards, elections, non-discrimination, or remedy?

High rights-floor sensitivity pushes core standards **upward**, even when implementation stays local.

### 4. Risk-pooling and redistribution need
Does the function require broad insurance pools, macro-stabilization, equalization, or redistribution between rich and poor places?

High pooling need pushes financing and backstop authority **upward**.

### 5. Interoperability and continuity burden
Do people need the same status, record, credential, or service path to survive institutional seams?

High continuity burden requires **joinable national or cross-jurisdiction rails**, even where frontline delivery stays local.

### 6. Catastrophic externality / irreversibility
Could failure create cascading, transboundary, or irreversible harm: pandemics, climate, nuclear, AI, financial contagion, large-scale cyber, biodiversity loss?

High irreversibility pushes monitoring, tripwires, and stop-rules **to the highest relevant scope**.

## Three permanent rules

### Rule A — Subsidiarity needs solidarity
Devolving responsibilities without money, staff, data, or fallback support is not subsidiarity; it is abandonment.

### Rule B — Local delivery still needs national rails
A humane local service can still fail if identity, records, benefits, procurement, or appeal systems are non-portable. Local discretion without interoperable rails becomes seam harm.

### Rule C — Escalation must be designed in advance
When a scope fails the capability or accountability test, authority must migrate temporarily upward with continuity duties and a restoration plan, not after a scandal.

## The scope ladder (who should do what)

### Micro-local (block / building / village / campus / frontline institution)

**Ideal role:** trust-building, detection, co-production, and immediate response where context matters most.

Best fit:
- mutual aid, commons stewardship, and high-context upkeep (`10-micro-local.md`, `11-commons-and-ecological-governance.md`)
- local participation, assembly, and short feedback loops
- early warning, intake, safeguarding, and community-embedded problem detection
- local emergency support and community-facing preparedness

Why here:
- local authorities and community actors are the closest institutional layer to people and are central to resilient communities [BIB-UNDRR-LOCAL-AUTHORITIES]
- community engagement works best when integrated into **national and local** emergency planning rather than bolted on during crisis [BIB-WHO-IHR-CE-16B]

**Guardrail:** micro-local bodies should never be the final arbiter of core rights floors, identity status, or coercive exceptions.

### Municipal (city / town / county-equivalent frontline state)

**Ideal role:** visible operator of everyday government.

Best fit:
- permitting, inspections, sanitation, streets, housing administration, local public health operations, school administration where applicable
- service access journeys people can actually visit and contest
- public records interfaces, complaints intake, and frontline case resolution
- neighborhood-level resilience, risk communication, and public space management

Why here:
- this is where most people experience the state as a service, queue, form, office, or enforcement contact
- quality, timely local services require responsibilities and resources to be aligned at the operating level [BIB-OECD-URBAN-POLICY-2024] [BIB-OECD-EPI-TOOLKIT-2025]

**Guardrail:** municipal government must not become a border wall between neighboring jurisdictions; portability and handoff continuity are mandatory (`109-portability-and-cross-jurisdiction-continuity.md`).

### Metropolitan / regional / bioregional (metro, corridor, labor market, watershed, grid area)

**Ideal role:** govern the spaces people actually move through, and the systems that ignore municipal borders.

Best fit:
- transport, commuting systems, housing markets, land-use coordination, air quality, watershed governance, grid coordination, regional hospitals, serious incident mutual aid
- place-based economic development where firms, workers, and infrastructure span multiple municipalities
- resilience planning across shared hazards and critical infrastructure networks

Why here:
- OECD urban policy work argues for adapting policy to the scale where people **live and work**, using functional urban area approaches rather than administrative borders [BIB-OECD-URBAN-POLICY-2024]
- around two-thirds of OECD metropolitan areas have a metropolitan governance authority, and fragmentation is associated with lower productivity; the observed productivity penalty is smaller where a metropolitan governance body exists [BIB-OECD-URBAN-POLICY-2024] [BIB-OECD-METRO-SURVEY-2014]

**Guardrail:** if the region can bind municipalities, it must also publish who is accountable, how budgets are set, and how a person contests cross-authority decisions.

### National

**Ideal role:** rights floor, redistribution, macro-capacity, and backbone infrastructure.

Best fit:
- constitutional rights floors, due process, elections framework, national integrity systems, competition and labor floors
- redistribution, equalization, social insurance, fiscal stabilization, and large-scale risk pooling
- civil service systems, major procurement norms, national public investment rules, national statistics
- national digital government and DPI stewardship: interoperable identity, payments, trusted data exchange, and minimum service continuity rails [BIB-WB-DIGITAL-GOV-2025] [BIB-UNDP-DPI-GOV-2025]

Why here:
- many public investment and fiscal capacities sit across levels, but national government remains the natural place for equalization, common standards, and backbone rules [BIB-OECD-EPI-TOOLKIT-2025]
- digital government increasingly depends on shared building blocks that must be governed for interoperability, resilience, and inclusion, not just procured piecemeal [BIB-WB-DIGITAL-GOV-2025] [BIB-UNDP-DPI-GOV-2025]

**Guardrail:** national government should standardize floors and rails, not swallow all local discretion. Over-centralization of high-context services is as damaging as fragmentation.

### Supranational / treaty-union / regional court space

**Ideal role:** bind cross-border problems no state can solve alone without creating remote unaccountable technocracy.

Best fit:
- mutual recognition, mobility, trade and standards with safety valves
- anti-corruption, beneficial ownership, sanctions coordination, cross-border evidence and enforcement cooperation
- shared courts or review bodies for common commitments
- shared infrastructure or market rules where unilateral action creates race-to-the-bottom incentives

Why here:
- subsidiarity does not mean “never coordinate above the nation”; it means do so where transnational aspects and clear comparative advantages exist [BIB-EURLEX-SUBSIDIARITY-2024]
- cross-border systems need common rules plus review, disclosure, and contestability, or they become legitimacy sinks

**Guardrail:** supranational authority should be narrow, reviewable, and explicit about what remains national or local.

### Global

**Ideal role:** catastrophic spillovers, planetary commons, and irreversible-risk coordination.

Best fit:
- climate, biodiversity, oceans, pandemics, nuclear risk, frontier-AI risk, major financial contagion, space traffic, and other transboundary catastrophic externalities
- common monitoring, disclosure, MRV, tripwires, and stop-rules
- treaty clubs and enforcement chokepoints where purely voluntary coordination predictably fails

Why here:
- disaster-risk and resilience governance already rely on national and local strategies nested inside international frameworks rather than isolated sovereign action [BIB-UNDRR-RISK-GOV-2025]
- the same logic applies to pandemics, biosecurity, and other high-irreversibility domains: local and national action matter, but alone they are insufficient

**Guardrail:** global institutions should mostly govern **shared risk and shared measurement**, not become generic world administrators.

## The healthiest stack

The best real-world pattern is usually:

- **local sensing and service delivery**
- **metro / regional coordination for shared systems**
- **national rights floors, equalization, and backbone rails**
- **supranational coordination for cross-border spillovers**
- **global monitoring and catastrophic-risk discipline**

That is **polycentric with hard floors**, not pure decentralization and not one-size-fits-all centralization.

---

## Anti-patterns (what to avoid)

### 1. Devolution without capacity
Giving lower levels duties without money, staff, data, or procurement leverage.

### 2. Localism for cross-border systems
Trying to solve watershed, commuter, grid, or epidemic problems municipality by municipality.

### 3. National standardization without local fit
Flattening high-context services into rigid national scripts that ignore place.

### 4. Digital centralization masquerading as modernization
Building monolithic digital stacks with no portability, no remedy path, and no steward/operator separation.

### 5. Crisis exceptionalism with no re-entry plan
Scaling authority upward during crisis and never shrinking it back.

## Failure migration rule (continuity when a scope fails)

When a scope cannot meet the three basic obligations — **capability**, **accountability**, and **spillover control** — authority should temporarily migrate upward or sideways into a compact body with a restoration plan.

Minimum requirements:
1. **Failure declaration** with evidence and explicit triggers.
2. **Immediate continuity duty** so the person does not fall into a seam.
3. **Remediation plan** with budget, staffing, and interface fixes.
4. **Return conditions** with measurable tests and review.

This is the archive’s answer to subsidiarity abuse: the lower level is primary **until it predictably stops being able to protect people**.

---

## Quick checklist (use in design reviews)

- Does the chosen scope match the function’s local knowledge, spillover radius, and risk-pooling needs?
- Are rights floors protected even if implementation is decentralized?
- Does the design use the scale where people actually live and work, rather than only formal borders?
- Are portability and interoperability rails in place so the person’s path survives seams?
- If the scope fails, is upward continuity already designed?

If not, the design is not ideal yet.
