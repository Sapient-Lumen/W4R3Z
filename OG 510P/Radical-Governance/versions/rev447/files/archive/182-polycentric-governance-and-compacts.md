# Polycentric Governance & Compacts (Overlapping Authority Without Deadlock)

**Goal:** enable multiple governing bodies (public + civic + cooperative + private-but-regulated) to share a problem-space **without** turning coordination into paralysis.

This is a *governance pattern*, not an ideology: polycentricity is a way to reduce single-point failure and improve fit-to-context while keeping rights, accountability, and spillovers controlled. See Ostrom’s framing of polycentric systems for complex public goods. [^ostrom2010]

---

## When polycentric beats hierarchy

Use polycentric architecture when:

- the problem has **strong local variation** but **shared constraints** (e.g., watersheds, housing, transport, safety)
- **failure costs** are high and you need redundancy (multiple providers, mutual aid)
- innovation rate is high and you want **many experiments** with clean evaluation
- the governance surface is already multi-actor (cities, agencies, NGOs, unions, platforms, utilities)

Do **not** polycentricize core rights adjudication, monopoly violence constraints, or the “final appeal” function (those need a clear apex). Polycentric systems still need a *constitutional* spine.

---

## The Compact: a minimal coordination contract

A **Compact** is the smallest thing you can sign that makes polycentric authority legible.

A compact has:

1. **Scope boundary** (what problem-space, geography, population, and duration)
2. **Interface obligations** (what each node must publish, deliver, and respect)
3. **Shared registries** (IDs, standards, and ledgers that make the system queryable)
4. **Conflict protocol** (how disputes are raised, mediated, arbitrated, and appealed)
5. **Spillover rules** (who pays for externalities; who can veto; escalation triggers)
6. **Exit & re-entry** (how parties leave without breaking continuity of care)

A compact is stronger if it includes:
- mutual aid + resource pooling clauses (see EMAC as an existence proof for cross-jurisdiction resource sharing under a legal wrapper). [^emac]
- data compatibility requirements (schema + publication cadence)
- sunset + renewal terms (see lifecycle doc patterns)

## Coordination primitives (use sparingly)

### 1) Shared registries (the system’s “spine”)
- **Service registry:** who does what, where, and under what SLA
- **Authority registry:** what each actor can decide, and their appeal chain
- **Intervention registry:** major programs/projects, owners, budgets, metrics
- **Incident registry:** failures, near-misses, responses, remediation status

### 2) Mutual aid + surge capacity
- pre-negotiated resource sharing (people, equipment, expertise)
- credential portability (licenses, background checks, security clearance where relevant)
- cost and liability rules

### 3) Dispute resolution ladder
- **local resolution** → **compact mediation** → **compact arbitration panel** → **constitutional / apex review**
- force **time-bounded** steps; escalation should be automatic when deadlines lapse

### 4) Multi-level evaluation
- local metrics + system-level metrics + external audit
- compact requires a shared definition of success + counterfactual tracking

## Anti-deadlock design rules

Deadlock is a *default failure mode* of polycentric systems. Reduce it with:

- **single accountable owner per interface** (many contributors, one accountable)
- **bounded veto:** veto requires a written harm claim + remedy proposal + time limit
- **“act then review” lanes** for emergencies and reversible interventions
- **budget rails:** pooled funds with pre-set triggers prevent bargaining collapse
- **escalation timers:** if no resolution in N days, authority migrates upward temporarily

Polycentricity is a cross-cutting layer that sits on top of:
- subsidiarity assignment
- interface obligations by scope
- integrity / anti-capture rails
- lifecycle / sunset logic

Think: *many nodes, one constitutional spine, shared registries, disciplined conflict protocol.*
