# 323 — International Reporting, Peer Review, and Domestic Follow-Through Rails

**Purpose:** stop international review cycles from becoming diplomatic theatre by forcing every recommendation, observation, and review finding into a visible domestic owner / action / evidence / follow-up path.

**Why this memo exists:** the archive now has a clean seam for deciding **what kind of international instrument exists at all** (`322`) and another for the point where a binding international agreement becomes — or does not become — domestic law (`321`). What it still lacked was the next operational layer: once a state is inside a treaty, review mechanism, or peer-review regime, how should reporting calendars, stakeholder submissions, recommendation tracking, official responses, and implementation follow-through actually work? Without that seam, readers had to stitch together global and supranational scope design (`50` / `60`), compact design (`19`), verification/compliance ladders (`81`), audit/follow-through (`231`), and assorted sector-specific review practices on their own.

**Evidence anchors:** OHCHR’s current material is the clearest public template for the reporting/follow-up side because it says the Universal Periodic Review is a periodic review of all 193 UN Member States, treats stakeholder participation as part of the process, explains that treaty bodies are committees of independent experts monitoring implementation of the core human-rights treaties, and documents simplified reporting, one- or two-year follow-up requests, and public follow-up materials [BIB-UN-UPR] [BIB-OHCHR-TREATY-BODIES-2026] [BIB-OHCHR-SRP-2026] [BIB-OHCHR-TB-FOLLOWUP-2026]. OHCHR’s current NMIRF guidance is the strongest institutional-design anchor because it describes standing national mechanisms that coordinate reporting, engage with international mechanisms, and track domestic follow-up and implementation over time [BIB-OHCHR-NMIRF-2026]. UNODC’s current UNCAC Implementation Review Mechanism is a good broader implementation anchor because it is explicitly a peer-review process built around self-assessment, peer review, country visits, executive summaries, country review reports, gap identification, and technical-assistance needs rather than pure naming-and-shaming [BIB-UNODC-UNCAC-IRM-2026]. OECD’s current DAC peer-review page is a good cross-domain learning anchor because it frames peer review as accountability and learning, uses recurring review cycles and mid-term progress tracking, and emphasizes that follow-up is stronger when diverse stakeholders are engaged [BIB-OECD-DAC-PEERREVIEWS-2026].

---

## Core claim

A safe international-review architecture does **not** end with ratification, accession, or signature.
It must answer five continuing questions publicly:
1. **What reporting or review cycle are we in?**
2. **What findings, recommendations, or observations were issued?**
3. **Which domestic institutions own each follow-up action?**
4. **What evidence shows progress, delay, disagreement, or non-implementation?**
5. **When is the next international check-in, and what happens if nothing moved?**

The archive should therefore stop treating international review as a foreign-affairs side activity. Once a polity accepts a reporting or peer-review regime, it should build a domestic follow-through system that is at least as legible as an ordinary internal audit or implementation plan.

The design target is simple:
- **one public ledger for international recommendations and review findings,**
- **one standing coordination mechanism rather than ad hoc scramble teams,**
- **clear owner-and-deadline assignment for every accepted or partially accepted action,**
- **explicit reasons where the state notes, rejects, contests, or cannot yet implement a recommendation,**
- **stakeholder input that can be seen alongside the official state narrative,**
- **and a no-reset rule so each review cycle starts from the actual prior record rather than a new press release.**

Use `323` when the question is:
- how a state should organize recurring treaty-body reports, peer reviews, or implementation-review cycles,
- how international recommendations should be classified, assigned, and tracked domestically,
- how to build a standing reporting / implementation mechanism instead of one-off reporting teams,
- how public dashboards, matrices, or recommendation trackers should work,
- how stakeholder submissions, national human-rights institutions, auditors, or civil society should fit into the cycle,

## The smallest good architecture

### 1. Review-cycle register (`RCR-*`)
Maintain one public register of each active international review / reporting obligation showing:
- mechanism name,
- legal or political basis,
- reporting / review cadence,
- current cycle stage,
- next submission / dialogue / follow-up deadline,
- responsible lead institution,
- and links to prior cycle outputs.

No ministry should need private inbox archaeology to answer “what is due next?”

### 2. Standing coordination mechanism (`SCM-*`)
Create a durable domestic coordination structure rather than an ad hoc task force that dissolves after each report.

Its minimum job is to:
- coordinate inputs across ministries and subnational bodies,
- maintain the master review calendar,
- standardize evidence and citation practice,
- keep a recommendation tracker,
- and preserve institutional memory across elections, reshuffles, and staff turnover.

Where the human-rights regime is the anchor, an NMIRF is the closest current public model. The archive generalizes the pattern beyond that domain.

### 3. Recommendation intake and typing sheet (`RIT-*`)
Every recommendation, concluding observation, executive-summary finding, or peer-review recommendation should be entered into one typed sheet that records:
- source mechanism,
- exact text or concise controlled summary,
- date issued,
- topic tags,
- whether it is legally binding, interpretive, recommendatory, or politically persuasive only,
- official state position: accept / partially accept / note / contest / already implemented / not applicable,
- and short reasons for that position.

Do not collapse treaty obligations and review recommendations into one undifferentiated pile.

### 4. Owner-and-action matrix (`OAM-*`)
For every item requiring follow-through, publish:
- lead institution,
- supporting institutions,
- relevant domestic rule / bill / budget / program / enforcement lane,
- target action,
- deadline or review date,
- implementation status,
- and dependencies.

If nobody owns a recommendation, the system is only simulating compliance.

### 5. Public recommendation tracker (`PRT-*`)
Expose a public dashboard or matrix that lets outsiders see:
- all active items,
- official position,
- owner,
- last update date,
- linked evidence,
- and whether the item is complete, in progress, stalled, disputed, or superseded.

This should not be a glossy progress page. It is an accountability surface.

### 6. Evidence-of-follow-through packet (`EFP-*`)
When the state claims implementation, it should cite the actual evidence:
- enacted law,
- regulation,
- budget line,
- court decision,
- program change,
- administrative instruction,
- training rollout,
- publication of data,
- or measured outcome.

A sentence saying “implemented” is never enough.

### 7. Stakeholder window and response note (`SRN-*`)
Publish a lane for stakeholder submissions and a short response note showing:
- what external critiques or corroborating inputs were received,
- whether the state disputes them,
- and whether they changed the official implementation status.

International review regimes are weakest when the domestic state file becomes a monopoly narrative.

### 8. No-reset carry-forward rule (`NCF-*`)
At the start of every new cycle, import unresolved items from the prior one and mark them explicitly as:
- still open,
- partially complete,
- replaced by newer recommendation,
- genuinely moot,
- or resolved with evidence.

Do not let deadline expiry erase unfinished obligations.

## Design rules that prevent the common failures

### A. Separate binding law from review pressure, but track both together
A treaty obligation, a treaty-body recommendation, a peer-review observation, and a political pledge are not the same thing.
The archive still wants them joined in one tracker — but typed clearly so no one mistakes moral pressure for self-executing law or uses non-binding recommendations as an excuse to ignore real obligations.

### B. Review cycles must have domestic owners, not only foreign-affairs editors
The lead ministry for international engagement may coordinate the file, but sector agencies, finance authorities, parliaments, and subnational operators must own the substantive actions.

### C. Public disagreement is better than fake closure
If the polity contests a recommendation, notes it without acceptance, or lacks present capacity, say so publicly and give reasons.
That is safer than quietly marking it “implemented” to clean the spreadsheet.

### D. Follow-up should cite domestic artifacts, not just promises
Implementation claims should point to specific laws, regulations, budgets, inspection reports, service changes, published metrics, or remediation actions.

### E. Stakeholder input is part of the architecture, not an optional extra
If civil society, unions, affected communities, NHRIs, auditors, or local authorities can see implementation failures, the system should have a structured way to receive and display that evidence.

### F. One recommendation tracker should feed multiple domestic lanes
The same international finding may require:
- a bill (`316`),
- delegated legislation (`317`),
- operational guidance change (`318`),
- budget action (`302`),
- service redesign (`47` / domain memos),
- or oversight follow-through (`231`).

The point of `323` is to keep the recommendation visible across those handoffs.

### G. Mid-cycle updates matter
Do not wait for the next full review to publish progress. Use interim updates, follow-up reports, and mid-cycle checkpoints.

### H. National coordination should survive elections and machinery-of-government changes
The archive prefers standing review infrastructure precisely because international obligations usually outlive cabinets, ministers, and organizational charts.

## Failure signatures

Watch for these recurring pathologies:
- **“We submitted the report, so the job is done.”** → reporting-without-follow-through failure.
- **Recommendations exist only in PDFs or meeting notes, with no domestic owner.** → orphan-recommendation failure.
- **Every cycle starts a fresh matrix and old unresolved items disappear.** → reset-by-calendar failure.
- **The public sees only the state narrative, not stakeholder or oversight evidence.** → monopoly-file failure.
- **Items are marked implemented with no law, budget, rule change, or operational evidence attached.** → assertion-as-compliance failure.
- **International recommendations are treated as either fully binding law or totally optional noise, with no typed middle ground.** → legal-status collapse.
- **Foreign affairs coordinates, but line ministries never internalize responsibility.** → diplomatic-silo failure.

## Scope-sensitive defaults

### National
- **Default:** one standing coordination mechanism plus a public recommendation tracker.
- **Prefer:** typed owner/deadline/evidence fields for every international finding.
- **Guardrail:** do not let treaty reporting live only inside the foreign ministry.

### Supranational / treaty-union
- **Default:** separate union-level findings, member-state findings, and mixed-responsibility items.
- **Prefer:** public matrices showing who owes what at which level and by what date.
- **Guardrail:** competence complexity must not become an excuse for no follow-through owner.

### Global / regime-complex
- **Default:** treat peer review, reporting, expert review, and implementation assistance as one visible cycle rather than disconnected diplomacy.
- **Prefer:** public carry-forward of unresolved items across COPs, review rounds, or implementation cycles.
- **Guardrail:** high-volume recommendation systems need typed triage or they collapse into symbolic compliance.

### Municipal / regional / functional authorities
- **Default:** only the recommendations that actually land at that layer should appear there, but they should still be linked back to the national review register.
- **Guardrail:** subnational implementers should never first learn of an international commitment when blamed for missing it.

## One-sentence design test

An international-review system is well designed when an ordinary outsider can tell, without diplomatic folklore, **what was recommended, who owns it domestically, what changed, what evidence proves that, and what will happen if it is still unfinished at the next review.**
