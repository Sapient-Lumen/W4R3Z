# 334 — Scope-Capacity Defaults: Stewards, Managers, Cadres, Secretariats, and No Hollow Shells

**Cross-stack note:** use `306-scope-design-ideal-government-and-reference-bundles-guide.md` for the canonical route across the scope-design family. This memo is the scale-to-operator / staffing-pattern front door; `14` / `176` explain cross-scope logic, `285` gives the default bundle language, `327` handles the scale-sensitive institutional form, `328` handles the scale-sensitive legitimacy channel, `329` handles the scale-sensitive tempo / time-horizon choice, `330` handles the scale-sensitive epistemic / evidence-mode choice, `331` handles the scale-sensitive accountability / review-mode choice, `332` handles the scale-sensitive coercion / direct-force-holding choice, `333` handles the scale-sensitive finance / revenue-model choice, and `09` / `199` / `297` handle the deeper merit-system / workforce / appointment cluster once the scale-fit capacity question has been identified.

**Purpose:** answer the design question that remains after scope assignment, bundle design, form choice, legitimacy choice, tempo choice, evidence choice, accountability choice, coercion choice, and finance choice: **what kind of operator and staffing pattern should government usually have at each scope?**

**Why this memo exists:** `14`, `176`, `285`, and `327-333` explain what each scale owns, what shape it should take, how it should be legitimated, what clock it should run on, what evidence it should mainly govern with, how it should be checked, which scales should directly hold force, and what finance model should dominate there. But the archive still needed one compact memo that says **scale should change not just the form of government, but the thickness, professionalization, and specialization of the operator that sits underneath it**.

**From-below:** this memo is a guardrail against hollow institutions. It helps stop systems from promising local self-government without staff, metro coordination without operators, regional backstop authority without cadres, national rights floors without competent civil service, or global commitments without the thin but real secretariats, verifiers, and financing teams that make them more than summit theater.

**Evidence anchors:** the European Charter of Local Self-Government is unusually explicit that local authorities need internal administrative structures suited to local needs and employee conditions that permit the recruitment of high-quality staff based on merit and competence, with training, remuneration, and career prospects [BIB-COE-LOCALSELF-ART6-1985]; OECD’s Recommendation on Public Service Leadership and Capability treats merit, professionalism, stewardship, and capability as system-level public-governance requirements [BIB-OECD-PSLC], while OECD’s *Public Employment and Management 2023* argues for more forward-looking, flexible, and resilient public services rather than static bureaucratic shells [BIB-OECD-PEM-2023]; OECD’s 2025 evidence on satisfaction with public administrative services reinforces that speed, ease, clarity, and staff interaction quality matter directly to people’s experience of government [BIB-OECD-GAAG-ADMIN-SATISFACTION-2025]; UN DESA’s principles of effective governance frame competence, integrity, participation, subsidiarity, and independent oversight as requirements for institutions at all levels [BIB-UN-CEPA-EFFECTIVE-GOV-2018], and its subnational application note stresses that leadership, technical and managerial capacity, resources, data, and fiscal support are persistent local and regional bottlenecks in multi-level systems [BIB-UN-CEPA-SUBNATIONAL-2022]; metropolitan and regional governance evidence still points toward bounded professional operators for shared systems rather than symbolic duplication of full state apparatuses [BIB-WB-METROGOV-2020] [BIB-OECD-REGIONAL-GOV-2022].

---

## Core claim

The ideal government at each scope is **not** just a different chamber or election rule.
It is also a different **operator**.

As scale changes, several capacity questions change with it:
- how much can safely be done by visible stewarding rather than a permanent bureaucracy,
- how much professional management is needed for daily service reliability,
- how much specialist depth is needed for law, finance, inspection, or infrastructure,
- how much independent verification capacity is needed,
- and how dangerous it would be if the institution existed mostly on paper.

So the archive’s rule is:
**lower scopes should default to stewards, conveners, and thin generalist operators; municipal and intermediate scales should default to professional managers and service cadres; regional and national scales should carry thicker administrative backstop and specialist capacity; supranational and global layers should usually gain capacity through bounded expert secretariats, compliance teams, scientific/technical support, and financing/verification machinery rather than giant generic bureaucracies.**

---

## The anti-hollow-shell test

Before creating or reforming a government at any scale, ask six questions:

1. **Operator test:** is there a named operator between elections, assemblies, or treaty summits, or only a symbolic office?
2. **Staffing test:** do the skills on hand match the discretion, coercion, infrastructure, or care duties assigned?
3. **Continuity test:** if two key people leave, does the institution still function?
4. **Backstop test:** if local capacity breaks, is there a higher-level or shared-service operator that can carry continuity without starting from zero?
5. **Handoff test:** can a case, record, or incident move across scopes without the person having to rebuild the file from memory?
6. **Theater test:** would this government still be legible if the title got smaller and the operator got stronger?

If the answer to several of these is “no”, the design is likely **symbolic devolution, prestige duplication, or institutional cosplay** rather than real government.

## Default capacity modes by scope

### 0. Household / association / cooperative

**Default capacity mode:** a **part-time steward, clerk, or treasurer layer**, not a standing bureaucracy.

Use:
- rotating or elected stewards,
- simple books and decision receipts,
- and lightweight mediation capacity.

Increase administrative thickness only when the body holds significant common assets, employs staff, or exercises durable delegated authority.

**Avoid:** hidden permanent officeholders, opaque control of common money, or pseudo-policing without outside review.

### 1. Micro-local (building / block / village / campus / frontline institution)

**Default capacity mode:** a **named steward plus convening capacity**, sometimes with tiny paid support, but not a freestanding coercive bureaucracy.

This scale is strongest when it can:
- notice,
- convene,
- keep a minimal ledger,
- route incidents upward fast,
- and maintain trust through visible presence.

Its operator should usually be:
- one steward or very small team,
- backed by rota / volunteer / civic participation for bounded tasks,
- and supported by municipal or county operators for anything that requires formal adjudication, professional inspection, emergency powers, or durable case management.

**Avoid:** dumping essential service obligations onto unpaid labor or treating neighborhood charisma as a substitute for trained operators.

### 2. Municipal (city / town / county-equivalent frontline state)

**Default capacity mode:** a **professional generalist operator with frontline service depth**.

Municipal government is where government becomes a queue, permit, inspector, navigator, maintenance crew, housing office, records clerk, benefits window, dispatcher, or caseworker. So the archive default is:
- professional executive management,
- stable frontline staffing,
- service navigation and interpretation support,
- records and case continuity,
- procurement / finance basics,
- and enough maintenance, inspection, and enforcement staff to keep promises real [BIB-OECD-GAAG-ADMIN-SATISFACTION-2025].

Small municipalities do **not** need to imitate a national ministry structure. But they do need enough operator depth that services do not depend on one heroic individual.

**Avoid:** volunteerized frontline government, vendor dependence without in-house specification capacity, or mayors without managers.

### 3. Intermediate local administration (county / prefecture pattern)

**Default capacity mode:** **standardized administrative cadres and shared-service backstops**.

This layer is often where the state removes local seams in:
- records,
- courts / tribunals,
- benefits administration,
- child and family services,
- public health operations,
- emergency coordination,
- and back-office support for smaller municipalities.

So the default operator here is not charismatic politics but:
- standardized service cadres,
- shared administrative systems,
- continuity teams,
- and cross-municipal handoff capacity.

**Avoid:** leaving this layer as a thin pass-through shell dependent on ad hoc bargaining, or letting it grow into an invisible political empire without clear service receipts.

### 4. Metropolitan / corridor / watershed / functional region

**Default capacity mode:** a **thin but professional network operator and compact secretariat**.

Metro and functional authorities usually need:
- planners,
- engineers or system managers,
- procurement / capital capability,
- compact administration,
- dispute-routing staff,
- and performance / asset data teams.

They usually do **not** need a duplicated all-purpose civil service. Their job is to run or coordinate shared systems whose externalities cross municipal borders [BIB-WB-METROGOV-2020].

The right design is often:
- thin headquarters,
- strong technical operator,
- high publication duties,
- and clear handoff contracts with member jurisdictions.

**Avoid:** building a “second city hall” for prestige, or conversely, assigning heavy infrastructure duties to a board with no real operating staff.

### 5. Regional (state / province / land / autonomous territory)

**Default capacity mode:** **sector backstop cadres with administrative-law, inspection, and territorial-planning depth**.

Regional government often needs thicker capacity than municipalities because it must:
- equalize uneven municipal capability,
- run scale-sensitive sectors,
- inspect lower-level performance,
- manage territorial infrastructure,
- and absorb spillovers across municipalities [BIB-OECD-REGIONAL-GOV-2022] [BIB-UN-CEPA-SUBNATIONAL-2022].

So the default operator here is usually:
- a professional regional civil service,
- sector-specialist teams,
- inspectorates and audit-adjacent review support,
- territorial planning and capital-delivery teams,
- and emergency / resilience backstop capacity.

**Avoid:** treating regions as either mere branch offices with no discretion or mini-sovereigns that overbuild symbolic ministries while underinvesting in municipal support.

### 6. National

**Default capacity mode:** a **merit civil service plus specialist backbone corps**.

National government is where the archive expects:
- ministries and policy directorates,
- treasury / budget / revenue / procurement depth,
- official statistics and census capability,
- regulatory and inspection capacity,
- administrative-justice support,
- backbone digital / interoperability teams,
- diplomatic and treaty machinery,
- and crisis-management / continuity institutions [BIB-OECD-PSLC] [BIB-OECD-PEM-2023].

This is the thickest general-purpose operator in the stack because it carries rights floors, redistribution, standards backbones, and crisis backstops. But thick does **not** mean unbounded. The archive default is still:
- merit over patronage,
- specialist depth where risk is high,
- and narrow political appointment zones around the elected executive rather than wholesale politicization.

**Avoid:** using patronage to replace competence, or centralizing every high-context service just because national staffing exists.

### 7. Supranational (union / treaty-union / shared-rule space)

**Default capacity mode:** a **bounded expert secretariat plus legal, review, standards, and implementation teams**.

This scale usually needs:
- treaty / legal administration,
- standards and policy coordination staff,
- compliance and monitoring teams,
- statistical comparability support,
- managed funding instruments,
- and narrow operational teams for shared-border or market systems.

It usually should **not** build a generic full-service state bureaucracy. Its operator should remain competence-bounded and legible to member governments and publics.

**Avoid:** pretending a supranational body can deliver every local service, or leaving it so understaffed that its commitments become paper-only.

### 8. Global / planetary

**Default capacity mode:** a **bounded secretariat and verification / finance / science support layer**, not a world administration.

The global layer is strongest when it has enough capacity for:
- scientific assessment,
- registry and reporting support,
- inspection or verification coordination where authorized,
- pooled-fund administration,
- incident warning and convening,
- and technical assistance for under-capacity jurisdictions [BIB-UN-PACT-FUTURE-2024] [BIB-UN-DFG] [BIB-UN-GDC].

Its operator should usually be:
- thin relative to its mandate,
- strong in evidence, verification, and finance administration,
- and weak in generic direct delivery except where a treaty or humanitarian mandate clearly requires field operations.

**Avoid:** summit bodies with no implementation staff, or fantasy world-state designs that ignore legitimacy and subsidiarity limits.

## Cross-cutting design rules

### A. The smaller the scale, the more continuity should come from named people and simple routines

Low scopes often fail from absenteeism, burnout, and missing handoff notes more than from constitutional defect. Their capacity design should prioritize steward presence, simple ledgers, and immediate escalation paths.

### B. The larger the scale, the more capacity should come from specialist depth, not just more hierarchy

Adding layers of approval without adding real expertise produces delay without competence.

### C. Coercion requires thicker professionalism than routine service coordination

Any scope that holds inspection, enforcement, detention, emergency force, or military powers needs stronger training, supervision, records, and independent review than a scope doing only coordination or service navigation.

### D. Shared-service arrangements are often safer than fake autonomy for very small units

Tiny municipalities or frontline institutions can preserve voice and visibility while pooling payroll, procurement, legal drafting, case systems, or inspection services upward or sideways.

### E. Volunteerism is not a rights floor

Volunteer and civic capacity can enrich local governance, but it should not quietly substitute for duties that require reliable staffing, lawful records, and continuity.

### F. Verification-heavy global or supranational systems need competence more than charisma

At distant scales, the most important operator qualities are often comparability, scientific credibility, legal discipline, and funding administration rather than mass personalized leadership.
