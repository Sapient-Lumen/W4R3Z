# 333 — Scope-Finance Defaults: Dues, Fees, Equalisation, Shared Taxes, and Burden-Sharing

**Cross-stack note:** use `306-scope-design-ideal-government-and-reference-bundles-guide.md` for the canonical route across the scope-design family. This memo is the scale-to-finance / revenue-model front door; `14` / `176` explain the cross-scope logic, `285` gives the default bundle language, `327` handles the scale-sensitive institutional-form default, `328` handles the scale-sensitive legitimacy-channel default, `329` handles the scale-sensitive tempo / time-horizon default, `330` handles the scale-sensitive epistemic / evidence-mode default, `331` handles the scale-sensitive accountability / review-mode default, `332` handles the scale-sensitive coercion / direct-force-holding default, `336` handles the scale-sensitive constituency / who-counts choice, and `18`, `07`, `135`, `194`, `217`, and `302` handle intergovernmental finance, budget design, taxation, fiscal federalism, transfers/equalisation, and the broader fiscal-routing family in general.

**Purpose:** answer the design question that remains after scope assignment, bundle design, institutional form, legitimacy choice, tempo choice, epistemic choice, accountability choice, and coercion choice: **what finance model should government at each scale mainly rely on?**

**Why this memo exists:** `14`, `176`, `285`, `327`, `328`, `329`, `330`, `331`, and `332` explain where functions belong, what minimum bundle a government needs, what shape it should take, how it should be legitimated, what tempo it should run on, what evidence it should mainly govern with, how it should be checked, and which scales should directly hold coercive powers. `18`, `07`, `135`, `194`, `217`, and `302` explain intergovernmental finance, budgeting, taxation, and equalisation in general. But the archive still lacked one compact memo answering the narrower practical question: **at a given scale, what should do most of the funding — dues, fees, local taxes, shared taxes, formula transfers/equalisation, pooled taxation and insurance, assessed contributions, or burden-sharing funds?**

**From-below:** this memo is a guardrail against three recurrent harms at once: symbolic localism that assigns duties without money, perverse finance that makes authorities live off fines or permit scarcity, and donor-style upper-tier funding that can disappear on a whim. A government is not “ideal” at its scale if its revenue model quietly pushes it to under-serve, over-punish, or beg for exceptions.

**Evidence anchors:** the OECD’s current decentralisation hub still defines decentralisation as the transfer of powers, responsibilities, **and resources** to elected subnational authorities, and stresses that balancing and sequencing those dimensions is a central design challenge [BIB-OECD-DECENTRALISATION-2026]. The OECD Fiscal Federalism Network says its Fiscal Decentralisation Database highlights the importance of local tax autonomy for fiscal accountability and performance [BIB-OECD-FISCAL-FEDERALISM-NETWORK-2026]. The European Charter of Local Self-Government says local authorities should have adequate own resources commensurate with their responsibilities, that part of those resources should derive from local taxes and charges, that financially weaker local authorities need equalisation, that local authorities should be consulted on redistributed resources, and that grants should, as far as possible, not be earmarked for specific projects [BIB-COE-LOCALSELF-ART9]. OECD’s 2025 work on intergovernmental fiscal transfers says transfers are crucial for addressing vertical and horizontal imbalances and promoting equitable service delivery, while design has to navigate trade-offs among equity, efficiency, transparency, and autonomy [BIB-OECD-IGFT-2025]. IMF’s 2025 work on decentralised fiscal crises says intergovernmental transfers can reduce crisis risk through interregional risk-sharing, while subnational fiscal rules and limits on local borrowing matter when deficits grow [BIB-IMF-DECENTRAL-FISCAL-CRISES-2025].

---

## Core claim

Every scale needs money, but **not every scale should rely on the same dominant revenue model**.

The archive default is:
- **the smaller and closer the scale, the more finance should come from visible small budgets, immobile tax bases, user charges for bounded local goods, and predictable general transfers rather than from fines or unstable grant-chasing;**
- **the more functional and cross-boundary the scale, the more finance should come from formula contributions, shared taxes, equalisation, and multi-year capital plans rather than from ad hoc bargaining;**
- **the more redistributive and macro-stabilising the scale, the more finance should come from broad tax bases, pooled insurance/social contributions, borrowing capacity, and explicit equalisation duties;**
- **the more treaty-based the scale, the more finance should come from assessed contributions, narrow own resources where lawfully conferred, and visible burden-sharing formulas rather than donor discretion or summit theatrics.**

So the question is never just “who pays?” It is:
**what revenue base fits the mobility of the tax base, the spillover radius of the function, the redistribution burden, and the lifespan of the assets being financed?**

---

## The anti-symbolic / anti-perverse-finance test

Before choosing a finance model, ask five questions:

1. **Base-fit test:** is the revenue base actually located at this scale, or can it leave overnight?
2. **Redistribution test:** does this function require equalisation or pooled insurance to keep rights from depending on local wealth?
3. **Predictability test:** can the operator plan staff and maintenance, or is the revenue stream too discretionary and volatile?
4. **Incentive test:** does the finance model reward service quality, or does it reward over-fining, permit scarcity, or strategic under-capacity?
5. **Risk test:** if this scale can borrow, are there visible limits, capital-only rules, and upper-tier crisis backstops?

If finance is too weak or too discretionary, the government becomes **symbolic**.
If finance depends on punishment, speculation, or one-off bargaining, the government becomes **perverse**.

## The archive’s finance defaults by scope

### 0. Household / association / cooperative

**Default finance mode:** **dues, shared contributions, and explicit common-fund rules**.

Use:
- dues,
- common maintenance contributions,
- and transparent shared-money rules for genuinely shared goods.

This scale should not imitate public finance. It should keep money simple enough that every member can understand how common funds are raised and spent.

**Avoid:** hidden quasi-taxation, punitive fee traps, or opaque steward control over common money.

### 1. Micro-local (building / block / village / campus / frontline institution)

**Default finance mode:** **micro-budgets, maintenance contributions, and delegated neighborhood grants — not punitive extraction**.

At micro-local scale, finance should lean mainly on:
- tiny shared budgets,
- upkeep funds,
- local dues or contributions,
- and delegated grants from the municipal layer for bounded neighborhood goods.

This layer should almost never finance itself through sanctions, exclusion rents, or “volunteerism” that quietly substitutes for duties the public stack actually owes.

**Avoid:** treating fines, informal social pressure, or unpaid labor as a substitute for real finance.

### 2. Municipal (city / town / county-equivalent frontline state)

**Default finance mode:** **immobile local bases plus predictable transfers and equalisation**.

Municipal government should lean mainly on:
- property and land-linked taxes,
- utility/service charges where the service is genuinely local and the charge is rights-compatible,
- local fees for bounded administrative acts,
- and predictable formula transfers plus equalisation for duties that local wealth cannot fairly support [BIB-COE-LOCALSELF-ART9] [BIB-OECD-IGFT-2025].

This is the everyday operating layer. It needs revenue that is visible, hard to hide, and compatible with maintaining streets, sanitation, local administration, and frontline services over time.

**Avoid:** funding ordinary government through policing fines, permit scarcity, sales of irreplaceable public assets, or grant-chasing that distorts service priorities.

### 3. Intermediate local administration (county / prefecture pattern)

**Default finance mode:** **shared taxes, delegated-administration funding, and formula backstop transfers**.

Intermediate local administration should lean mainly on:
- earmarked shares of broader taxes where law assigns cross-municipal service bundles,
- delegated-administration funding tied to the real cost of carrying state/regional functions,
- and formula transfers that stabilize uneven municipal capacity.

This is the layer where finance should remove seams, not create them. Money should follow the backstop function clearly enough that municipalities can see what is being funded here rather than locally.

**Avoid:** piling up opaque pass-through funds or leaving the unit dependent on ad hoc rescue bargaining every budget cycle.

### 4. Metropolitan / corridor / watershed / functional region

**Default finance mode:** **formula contributions, shared tax slices, user charges for network goods, and capital plans matched to long-lived assets**.

Metro and functional bodies should lean mainly on:
- member-jurisdiction contributions by formula,
- dedicated shares of broader taxes where the function is continuous,
- tolls/fares/charges for true network services where access equity is still protected,
- and multi-year capital plans for transit, water, and other shared systems.

This layer is the classic place for underfunding through bargaining. The archive default is therefore **formula first** and **project-grant dependence second**.

**Avoid:** building permanent region-scale obligations on one-off competitive grants or off-balance-sheet financial engineering.

### 5. Regional (state / province / land / autonomous territory)

**Default finance mode:** **broader tax handles, equalisation, and medium-term capital budgeting**.

Regional government should lean mainly on:
- broader bases than municipalities can safely hold,
- shared or own-source consumption/income/resource taxes where constitutionally assigned,
- transfer systems that equalise between rich and poor municipalities,
- and borrowing for capital investment within visible rules [BIB-COE-LOCALSELF-ART9] [BIB-IMF-DECENTRAL-FISCAL-CRISES-2025].

This is where the finance model must balance territorial autonomy against solidarity. It should be strong enough to backstop weaker local authorities without swallowing all local fiscal discretion.

**Avoid:** starving municipalities while regional governments hoard discretionary grant power.

### 6. National

**Default finance mode:** **broad taxation, pooled insurance, macro-stabilisation, and explicit equalisation obligations**.

National government is the archive’s main holder of:
- broad-based progressive taxes,
- social insurance and large risk pools,
- macro-fiscal stabilisation,
- sovereign borrowing,
- and the duty to finance equalisation and rights floors across territory [BIB-OECD-DECENTRALISATION-2026] [BIB-OECD-IGFT-2025].

This concentration is not a celebration of fiscal centralism. It is a recognition that redistribution, recession response, national guarantees, and crisis backstops require tax bases and balance-sheet capacity that smaller scales do not possess.

**Avoid:** offloading national obligations to property-poor localities, or using conditional transfers to micromanage every local operating choice.

### 7. Supranational (union / treaty-union / shared-rule space)

**Default finance mode:** **assessed contributions, narrow own resources where conferred, and bounded common borrowing for genuinely common goods**.

Supranational governance should usually lean on:
- member contributions by rule,
- narrow common revenues only where lawfully conferred and publicly scrutinised,
- investment instruments for shared infrastructure or crisis response where the mandate is clear,
- and burden-sharing formulas that are legible enough to survive distributional conflict.

Its safest finance posture is usually:
- enough own capacity to avoid permanent hostage-taking,
- but not so much unbounded fiscal autonomy that competence outgrows legitimacy.

**Avoid:** treaty unions that claim serious policy powers while surviving on unstable annual bargaining or opaque side deals.

### 8. Global / planetary

**Default finance mode:** **assessed contributions, formula burden-sharing, and pooled funds for under-capacity jurisdictions and global public goods**.

Global governance should mainly lean on:
- assessed contributions,
- treaty-based or club-based burden-sharing formulas,
- pooled climate, health, or risk funds,
- and, where politically possible, narrow levy-like mechanisms tied to genuine global externalities.

It should not mainly depend on donor whim if the task is truly planetary and continuous. Verification without predictable finance becomes performative.

**Avoid:** summit pledges that are politically dramatic but fiscally non-repeatable.

## Cross-cutting design rules

### A. Money should match mandates

No scale should be assigned durable duties without a commensurate finance path. “Do more with existing resources” is not a constitutional design principle.

### B. The smaller the scale, the more revenue should come from bases that cannot easily flee

Immobile bases and predictable general transfers are safer local foundations than highly mobile tax bases or speculative asset sales.

### C. Equalisation is not optional where rights or basic services would otherwise depend on local wealth

If the state promises a common floor, the finance model must help low-capacity places reach it. Equalisation exists to preserve autonomy **and** comparability, not to cancel either one [BIB-COE-LOCALSELF-ART9] [BIB-OECD-IGFT-2025].

### D. Grants should not quietly erase local discretion

Project earmarks can be useful, but the archive default is that routine local government should not be forced to live by discretionary, tightly earmarked grant contests when it is carrying continuous duties [BIB-COE-LOCALSELF-ART9].

### E. Borrowing should be tied to asset life and visible risk control

Borrowing is safest where it finances long-lived assets and sits inside transparent fiscal rules. It is dangerous when used to hide operating weakness or permanent structural gaps [BIB-IMF-DECENTRAL-FISCAL-CRISES-2025].

### F. Never let coercion fund the operator

Authorities that live off fines, forfeitures, or permit scarcity eventually govern to preserve those streams. Finance and enforcement should be separated wherever possible.
