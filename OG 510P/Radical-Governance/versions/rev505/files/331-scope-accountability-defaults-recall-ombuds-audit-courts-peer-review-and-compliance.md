# 331 — Scope-Accountability Defaults: Recall, Ombuds, Audit, Courts, Peer Review, and Compliance

**Cross-stack note:** use `306-scope-design-ideal-government-and-reference-bundles-guide.md` for the canonical route across the scope-design family. This memo is the scale-to-accountability / review-mode front door; `14` / `176` explain the cross-scope logic, `285` gives the default bundle language, `327` handles the scale-sensitive institutional-form default, `328` handles the scale-sensitive legitimacy-channel default, `329` handles the scale-sensitive tempo / time-horizon default, `330` handles the scale-sensitive epistemic / evidence-mode default, `333` handles the scale-sensitive finance / revenue-model default, `336` handles the scale-sensitive constituency / who-counts default, `08` and `172` handle complaint and ombuds design in general, `130` and `231` handle audit and inspection design, `156` handles courts and constitutional review, and `323` / `325` handle international peer review, reporting follow-through, adjudication, and compliance execution.

**Purpose:** answer the design question that remains after scope assignment, bundle design, institutional form, legitimacy choice, tempo choice, and epistemic choice: **what accountability and review mode should government at each scale mainly rely on?**

**Why this memo exists:** `14`, `176`, `285`, `327`, `328`, `329`, and `330` explain where functions belong, what minimum bundle a government needs, what shape it should take, how it should be legitimated, what tempo it should run on, and what kind of evidence it should mainly govern with. `08`, `172`, `130`, `231`, `156`, `323`, and `325` explain complaints, ombuds, audit, courts, peer review, and international adjudication in general. But the archive still lacked one compact memo answering the narrower practical question: **at a given scale, what should do most of the checking — visible recall and challenge, ombuds and administrative review, legislative scrutiny, audit, courts, peer review, or compliance tracking?**

**From-below:** this memo is a guardrail against two recurring harms at once: power that nobody nearby can challenge, and prestige review machinery that exists on paper but cannot correct the thing that harmed the person. It keeps review close enough to be usable, independent enough to be trusted, and proportionate enough not to turn every small-scale decision into constitutional theater.

**Evidence anchors:** OECD/SIGMA’s 2023 subnational-governance review says effective accountability mechanisms can improve local-government outcomes, that voting is only one part of local accountability, and that accountability frameworks plus administrative supervision matter [BIB-SIGMA-SUBNATIONAL-WBALKANS-2023]; the European Charter of Local Self-Government says supervision of local authorities must be grounded in law, normally target legality rather than expediency, and remain proportionate [BIB-COE-LOCALSELF-ART8]; OECD’s 2025 service-governance update says clear political accountability and administrative leadership are fundamental to delivering human-centred public administrative services [BIB-OECD-GAAG-SERVICE-ACCOUNTABILITY-2025]; INTOSAI still treats the Lima and Mexico Declarations as defining the prerequisites for independent and effective external government audit, with the Mexico Declaration identifying eight pillars of SAI independence [BIB-INTOSAI-P1] [BIB-INTOSAI-P10]; and OHCHR’s current materials still frame the UPR as a peer review of every UN member state every 4.5 years while NMIRFs are standing government structures for reporting, engagement, and domestic follow-through [BIB-UN-UPR] [BIB-OHCHR-NMIRF-2026].

---

## Core claim

Every scale needs accountability, but **not every scale should rely on the same dominant review machinery**.

The archive default is:
- **the smaller and closer the scale, the more accountability should come from visibility, challengeability, recallability, and an immediate upward complaint path;**
- **the more operational the scale, the more accountability should come from ombuds, administrative review, local audit, complaint handling, and council scrutiny;**
- **the more redistributive and constitutional the scale, the more accountability should come from legislatures, independent courts, supreme audit, public-accounts scrutiny, and integrity enforcement;**
- **the more cross-border and treaty-based the scale, the more accountability should come from peer review, compliance tracking, adjudication where accepted, and domestic follow-through mechanisms rather than from fake plebiscitary symmetry.**

So the question is never just “who watches this?” It is:
**what mode of review can actually see the failure, understand the mandate, issue a proportionate correction, and remain independent of the actor being checked?**

---

## The anti-shadow / anti-overkill test

Before choosing an accountability mode, ask five questions:

1. **Detection test:** who will notice the failure first — the affected person, a local representative, an ombuds intake, an auditor, a court, or a treaty-review body?
2. **Correction test:** which reviewer can actually change or halt the thing that went wrong rather than just describe it later?
3. **Independence test:** how far does the reviewer need to sit from the operator for the review to be trusted?
4. **Proportionality test:** is this mainly a legality check, a service-quality check, a budget/integrity check, or a rights/constitutionality check?
5. **Carry-forward test:** if this scale depends on another one to enforce the outcome, is there a visible follow-through mechanism rather than a symbolic finding?

If review is too weak or too near, it becomes **shadow impunity**.
If review is too distant or too heavy for the task, it becomes **performative overkill**.

## The archive’s accountability defaults by scope

### 0. Household / association / cooperative

**Default accountability mode:** **voice, rotation, simple ledgers, mediation, and exit**.

Use:
- visible meetings and common memory of decisions,
- basic money and membership records,
- rotation or easy replacement of stewards,
- and mediation before conflict hardens.

At this scale, the first review channel is usually the group itself. The point is to stop hidden control over money, membership, or sanctions before it becomes durable.

**Avoid:** creating mini-bureaucracies or quasi-courts where the real missing safeguard is simply visible process and the ability to leave.

### 1. Micro-local (building / block / village / campus / frontline institution)

**Default accountability mode:** **visible challenge, short-term recall or rotation, and a posted upward complaint path**.

At micro-local scale, accountability should lean mainly on:
- open meetings and inspectable minutes,
- quick challenge of steward decisions,
- short mandates and rotation,
- and a mandatory escalation route into municipal complaint or emergency systems when the issue exceeds local competence.

This is the scale where proximity is strongest, so the first check should usually be **visible contestation**, not remote professional review.

**Avoid:** informal domination that hides behind neighborliness, or local sanctions with no safe path out.

### 2. Municipal (city / town / county-equivalent frontline state)

**Default accountability mode:** **complaints handling, ombuds, administrative review, local audit, and council scrutiny**.

Municipal government should lean mainly on:
- complaint intake that can actually reopen or correct cases,
- ombuds or equivalent independent municipal redress,
- administrative tribunals or review officers for permits, benefits, enforcement, and service failures,
- local audit and procurement scrutiny,
- and council/public-accounts scrutiny over executive administration [BIB-SIGMA-SUBNATIONAL-WBALKANS-2023] [BIB-OECD-GAAG-SERVICE-ACCOUNTABILITY-2025].

This is the everyday operating layer. Its main failure mode is not absence of formal law, but everyday decisions that become hard to challenge because they are buried in queues, departments, or contractor chains.

**Avoid:** making local elections the only accountability story, or forcing every ordinary service error into general courts.

### 3. Metropolitan / corridor / watershed / functional region

**Default accountability mode:** **member-jurisdiction scrutiny, joint audit, bounded appeal, and competence review**.

Metro governance should lean mainly on:
- visible competence maps,
- board or assembly scrutiny by participating jurisdictions,
- joint audit and procurement review across authorities,
- complaint and appeal lanes for cross-jurisdiction service failures,
- and legality / mandate review when functional authorities drift into shadow general government.

This layer often fails through diffusion: many bodies, many budgets, and no single obvious checker. The accountability answer is therefore **shared scrutiny plus hard publication discipline**, not merely adding another symbolic executive.

**Avoid:** letting special districts or metro authorities accumulate practical power with weaker review than the municipalities they sit above.

### 4. Regional (state / province / land / autonomous territory)

**Default accountability mode:** **legislative scrutiny, regional ombuds/audit, legality review, and territorial-equity monitoring**.

Regional government should lean mainly on:
- assembly or parliamentary scrutiny of executive action,
- regional audit and inspection bodies,
- ombuds and administrative-justice pathways,
- legality / constitutional review where assigned,
- and monitoring of territorial inequality, transfer fairness, and standards compliance.

This is the scale where policy becomes more redistributive and less purely operational. Accountability must therefore see both **legality** and **territorial fairness**, not just service throughput.

**Avoid:** subnational presidentialization with thin review, or legality supervision that quietly slips into broad political expediency control over municipalities [BIB-COE-LOCALSELF-ART8].

### 5. National

**Default accountability mode:** **elections plus legislature, courts, supreme audit, administrative justice, and integrity enforcement**.

National government should lean mainly on:
- competitive elections and parliamentary confidence where relevant,
- legislative committees and public-accounts scrutiny,
- independent courts and administrative justice,
- supreme audit institutions with real independence,
- anti-corruption / integrity bodies,
- and complaint systems that can move ordinary administrative grievances without waiting for constitutional litigation [BIB-INTOSAI-P1] [BIB-INTOSAI-P10].

At national scale, no single checker is enough. The point is a **mesh** of political, legal, fiscal, and administrative review that can see both routine abuse and constitutional drift.

**Avoid:** collapsing accountability into leader-approval theater, or imagining that courts alone can replace legislative and audit scrutiny.

### 6. Supranational (union / treaty-union / shared-rule space)

**Default accountability mode:** **dual scrutiny, court/tribunal review, peer monitoring, and competence-bounded compliance**.

Supranational governance should lean mainly on:
- scrutiny by both member-state institutions and representative/common institutions,
- court or tribunal review where jurisdiction is conferred,
- peer monitoring of implementation,
- legality review around conferral, subsidiarity, and proportionality,
- and compliance processes that are visible about what can and cannot actually be enforced.

This layer is accountability-fragile because it is neither a municipality nor a full nation-state. Its review machinery must therefore be explicit about **who is checking, under what mandate, and by what consequence path**.

**Avoid:** copying national accountability symbolism without national accountability powers, or letting compliance drift into technocratic opacity.

### 7. Global / planetary

**Default accountability mode:** **reporting, verification, peer review, complaint/adjudication where accepted, and domestic follow-through mechanisms**.

Global governance should lean mainly on:
- treaty reporting and public methods,
- independent verification where feasible,
- recurring peer review,
- adjudication or complaints procedures where states have accepted them,
- domestic implementation and follow-through structures such as NMIRFs,
- and finance / compliance tracking that shows whether promises became action [BIB-UN-UPR] [BIB-OHCHR-NMIRF-2026].

This layer usually cannot rely on direct electoral correction. Its accountability strength comes instead from **repeatable review plus visible domestic carry-forward**, not from summit drama.

**Avoid:** one-shot declarations with no monitoring, or complaint bodies with no path into national follow-through.

## Cross-cutting design rules

### A. The first usable complaint lane should sit close to the person

Even when the decisive review happens higher up, the person should be able to start nearby. A distant perfect remedy is often functionally no remedy at all.

### B. Legality review and service correction are not the same thing

Courts and legality supervision matter, but many harms first need an ombuds, queue correction, billing fix, permit review, or audit intervention. Do not make constitutional review carry the whole accountability burden.

### C. The more money, coercion, or secrecy involved, the more independence the checker needs

Budget control, procurement, detention, surveillance, sanctions, and emergency power all require reviewers who are structurally farther from the operator than everyday service complaint handlers.

### D. Peer review needs domestic owners

At supranational and global scales, findings do not implement themselves. Every recommendation, compliance note, or adverse finding needs an identified domestic owner and a follow-through path.

### E. Accountability should reveal mandate mismatch early

One of the most valuable things review can do is show that a function sits at the wrong scale. Good accountability systems should therefore expose not just wrongdoing, but bad scope design.
