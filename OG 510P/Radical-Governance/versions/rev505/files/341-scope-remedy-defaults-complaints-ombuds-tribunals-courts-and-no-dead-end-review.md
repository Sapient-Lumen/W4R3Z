# 341 — Scope-Remedy Defaults: Complaints, Ombuds, Tribunals, Courts, and No Dead-End Review

**Cross-stack note:** use `306-scope-design-ideal-government-and-reference-bundles-guide.md` for the canonical route across the scope-design family. This memo is the scale-to-remedy / person-facing-review / no-dead-end-review front door; `14` / `176` explain the cross-scope logic, `285` gives the default bundle language, `331` handles the scale-sensitive accountability / review-mode choice in broader system terms, `71` handles the interface substrate, and `08` / `36` / `172` / `195` / `323` / `325` handle the deeper grievance, redress-registry, administrative-justice, dispute-resolution, international-reporting, and international-adjudication substrate once the scale-fit remedy question has been identified.

**Purpose:** answer the design question that remains after scope assignment, bundle design, form choice, legitimacy choice, decision-rule choice, selection choice, tenure choice, and office-status choice: **what should the primary remedy path at this scale usually be — immediate challenge and correction, complaint handling, ombuds review, administrative appeal, tribunal, court, peer-review compliance, treaty complaint, or some explicit ladder across them?**

**Why this memo exists:** `14`, `176`, `285`, and `327-340` explain what each scale owns, what shape it should take, how it should be legitimated, how it should decide, how offices should be filled and staffed, and what public surface it should expose. But the archive still needed one compact memo that says **scale should also change remedy design** — where a person starts, when fast correction beats litigation, when ombuds and administrative review should sit between frontline offices and courts, when compound bodies need a no-wrong-door routing layer, and when global review can only operate through reporting, individual communications, inspection, or domestic implementation rather than a fantasy world trial court.

**From-below:** this memo is a guardrail against two opposite failures. It helps stop small-scale governance from becoming intimate but unchallengeable. And it helps stop larger scales from offering only distant, expensive, court-first remedies that ordinary people cannot realistically use.

**Evidence anchors:** the European Charter of Local Self-Government explicitly protects local authorities’ right of recourse to a judicial remedy, which is a useful reminder that even local self-government cannot be left to politics without review [BIB-COE-LOCALSELF-ART11-1985]; Council of Europe ombuds standards emphasize institutions that are directly and easily accessible to everyone in respect of all public services, while the Venice Principles extend Ombudsman competence across public administration at all levels [BIB-COE-OMBUDSMAN-REC-2019] [BIB-COE-OMBUDSMAN-VENICE-2019]; OECD’s current administrative-justice and people-centred-justice work stresses that people seek redress not only through courts but through complaints, ombuds, mediation, and inquiries, and that effective systems use **no wrong door**, common intake, interoperable referrals, and warm handoffs [BIB-OECD-ADMINJUSTICE-2025] [BIB-OECD-PEOPLECENTRED-JUSTICE-TOOLKIT-2025]; the EU Charter’s Article 47 still anchors the right to an effective remedy and fair trial in Union law [BIB-EU-CHARTER-A47]; the European Ombudsman still provides the supranational maladministration complaint lane for EU institutions [BIB-EU-OMBUDS-GUIDE-COMPLAINTS]; and OHCHR still explains that international human-rights complaint procedures operate through individual communications only where states have accepted the relevant competence, which is the right constraint for the global layer [BIB-OHCHR-INDIVIDUAL-COMMUNICATIONS-2026] [BIB-OHCHR-ICCPR].

## Core claim

Every scale needs a **default remedy design**.

Not because every dispute should become a lawsuit, but because institutions become evasive, cruel, or theatrically “participatory” when they cannot say:
- where a person starts,
- who must try to fix the problem now,
- when the case must be escalated,
- whether filing pauses deadlines or enforcement,
- and which body can actually order correction, reversal, compensation, continuity, or restoration.

As scale changes, the fitting remedy path also changes:
- the closer, smaller, and more reversible the matter, the more remedy should begin with **visible challenge and fast correction** rather than formal litigation;
- the more service-dense the layer, the stronger the case for **complaint handling, ombuds review, and administrative appeal** before court;
- the more compound the authority, the more remedy should rely on **no-wrong-door intake, lead-case ownership, and enforceable routing** so the person is not bounced between governments;
- the thicker the coercion, status, money, or rights consequences, the more the system needs **tribunals, administrative courts, judicial review, interim protection, and restoration powers**;
- and the more distant and treaty-based the layer, the more remedy shifts toward **complaint/adjudication where accepted, inspection, reporting, peer pressure, compliance procedures, and domestic follow-through** instead of pretending there is a single global courthouse for everything.

So the archive’s rule is:
**fast correction first where the problem is local and reversible; ombuds and administrative review where service systems routinely harm people; tribunals and courts where rights, coercion, money, or status are at stake; no-wrong-door routing where several governments jointly govern; and treaty / compliance / complaint lanes where the relevant scale is supranational or global.**

## The no-dead-end-review test

Before creating or reforming any institution, ask seven questions:

1. **Entry test:** can a person tell where to start without legal expertise or insider knowledge?
2. **Ownership test:** once the complaint is lodged, does one body become responsible for routing and follow-through rather than forcing the person to shop around?
3. **Interim-protection test:** if delay would destroy housing, safety, liberty, income, status, or evidence, can someone pause the harm in time?
4. **Correction test:** can the first-line body actually fix obvious mistakes fast, or can it only apologize and forward?
5. **Escalation test:** when first-line review fails, is the next lane named, time-bounded, and reachable?
6. **Restoration test:** can the system reverse the act, restore access, pay compensation or back pay, and repair records — not just issue lessons learned?
7. **Seam test:** when the case crosses jurisdictions, are handoff and continuity duties explicit, or does the person effectively lose remedy at the border?

If several answers are “we assume the person will find the right office or lawyer,” the design is drifting toward **dead-end review, complaint theater, or court-only remedy that ordinary people cannot use**.

## Default remedy modes by scope

### 0. Household / association / cooperative

**Default remedy mode:** **internal complaint and correction first, then chartered mediation / appeal, then external law when assets, exclusion, coercion, or protected rights are in play.**

Use:
- direct challenge and logged correction for ordinary disputes,
- a named internal appeal or mediation step for recurring conflicts,
- and outside court / labor / housing / consumer / human-rights routes when the body is exercising powers it cannot just self-judge.

The rule here is not “keep everything private.” It is “small associations should solve what they can quickly, but never become unreviewable miniature sovereigns.”

### 1. Micro-local (building / block / village / campus / frontline institution)

**Default remedy mode:** **visible challenge plus municipal or parent-institution backstop.**

Use:
- posted steward complaint channels and quick correction for housekeeping, notice, queue, upkeep, or convening failures,
- automatic upward routing to the municipal, campus, landlord-regulator, or parent-institution complaint lane where the issue touches rights, sanctions, safety, housing, or exclusion,
- and no requirement that the harmed person guess the ownership map before someone takes responsibility.

The micro-local anti-pattern is intimate rule without external remedy.

### 2. Municipal (city / town)

**Default remedy mode:** **complaint handling + ombuds / administrative review + local tribunal or court escalation.**

Use:
- one intake front door for service complaints,
- frontline correction powers for obvious mistakes,
- ombuds or administrative review for fairness, delay, non-response, discretionary abuse, and recurring service failure,
- and tribunal / court lanes where permits, sanctions, benefits, housing, policing, custody, or protected rights are at stake.

Cities are where people most often meet the state, so municipal remedy should usually be **plain-language, local, and fast enough to matter before the harm hardens.**

### 2.5 Intermediate local administration (county / prefecture / province-lite backstop)

**Default remedy mode:** **single intake with mandated routing across county-scale services, backed by ombuds / administrative appeal and judicial review.**

Use:
- one county or prefecture complaint front door for cross-service problems,
- lead-case ownership when health, welfare, records, transport, and emergency services overlap,
- administrative appeal for standardized service determinations,
- and court escalation for legality, detention, major benefit, or rights-floor failures.

This layer often exists because fragmented municipal remedies break when a person’s real problem spans several service systems at once.

### 3. Metropolitan / corridor / watershed / functional region

**Default remedy mode:** **no-wrong-door routing + lead-case ownership + bounded venue for competence disputes.**

Use:
- a shared intake or mandated transfer rule across transport, water, planning, and regional-service operators,
- a named lead authority that must continue the case across member jurisdictions,
- complaint or ombuds review for shared-system operators,
- and a bounded tribunal / court / legality lane where the real issue is which member or compact body had authority.

The metro anti-pattern is making the person litigate the governance map before anyone fixes the service failure.

### 4. Regional (state / province / land)

**Default remedy mode:** **administrative justice mesh — regional ombuds, sector appeals, legality review, and administrative courts.**

Use:
- regional ombuds and inspectorate routes for service-system fairness and delay,
- sector-specific appeals for education, health, environment, licensing, or regional transfers,
- legality and constitutional review where regional law or delegated authority is contested,
- and interim protection when regional acts can cut off essential services or territorial rights.

At this scale, remedy must be able to correct both **individual cases** and **territorial-pattern failures**.

### 5. National

**Default remedy mode:** **ombuds + administrative justice + courts + restoration.**

Use:
- complaint and ombuds routes for maladministration and delay,
- administrative tribunals and courts for benefits, immigration, licensing, tax, procurement, detention, and other state determinations,
- judicial review and constitutional remedies where the legality or compatibility of state action is in question,
- and compensation / restoration / record-correction powers where the state has already injured someone.

The archive’s national rule is simple: **the general-purpose state must not be court-only, complaint-only, or apology-only.** It needs a layered remedy stack that ordinary people can enter and that can still end in binding correction.

### 6. Supranational (treaty union / shared-rule space)

**Default remedy mode:** **ombudsman, petition, administrative complaint, tribunal / court, and member-state follow-through.**

Use:
- maladministration complaint lanes for supranational institutions,
- petition or review mechanisms where directly representative bodies receive complaints,
- court / tribunal remedies where competences are conferred and justiciable,
- infringement or compliance procedures where member states fail to implement common obligations,
- and domestic implementation challenge paths where the practical harm is still delivered through national administrations.

This layer needs a sharper split than national systems do between **supranational maladministration**, **member-state implementation failure**, and **purely domestic acts wearing supranational clothing**.

### 7. Global (planetary / treaty-complex level)

**Default remedy mode:** **domestic implementation first, plus treaty complaint / inspection / reporting / peer-review / adjudication where accepted.**

Use:
- domestic administrative and judicial remedy as the first-line route where global norms are implemented nationally,
- treaty-body individual communications only where a state has accepted competence,
- international inspection, inquiry, reporting, and compliance procedures where direct individual remedy is not available,
- adjudication or special procedures where jurisdiction exists,
- and public follow-through obligations so global review is not severed from domestic correction.

The global anti-pattern is promising direct world-scale remedy that does not exist. The better design is to make the real ladder visible: **domestic correction, international complaint where available, and public compliance pressure where not.**

### Non-territorial & indigenous polities (cross-cutting)

**Default remedy mode:** **internal challenge plus compact-facing external review as delegated power grows.**

Use:
- internal complaint and appeal consistent with legitimate traditions,
- external review once the polity exercises coercion, essential services, major public funds, or delegated state power,
- and compact-defined venue rules so people do not fall through the seam between membership-based and territorial systems.

Do not flatten every polity into the same tribunal map. But do require an honest answer to **who reviews harm here if the internal authority is wrong**.

## Cross-scope defaults worth carrying everywhere

1. **No wrong door should be literal, not rhetorical.** A person should not lose remedy because they started with the wrong office.
2. **Complaint lanes need correction powers or enforceable escalations.** An inbox that cannot fix or compel is not remedy.
3. **Interim protection matters most where timing destroys rights.** Housing, safety, detention, status, benefits, and evidence cannot wait for leisurely review.
4. **Compound governance needs lead-case ownership.** Someone must carry the case across agencies and jurisdictions.
5. **Courts are essential but not sufficient.** Most people meet the state through services first, so ombuds, administrative review, and triage matter.
6. **International review must name its limits.** Global remedy often works through accepted jurisdiction, reporting, inspection, and domestic incorporation rather than universal direct enforceability.
7. **Restoration is part of remedy.** Reverse the act, reopen the file, fix the record, repay the loss, or reinstate the service — do not stop at explanation.
