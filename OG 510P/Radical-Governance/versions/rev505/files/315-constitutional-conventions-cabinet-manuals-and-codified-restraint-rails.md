# 315 — Constitutional Conventions, Cabinet Manuals, and Codified Restraint Rails

**Purpose:** make extra-legal but high-stakes constitutional norms explicit and auditable so a polity does not rely on insider folklore for caretaker restraint, formation practice, reserve-power handling, collective responsibility, or executive–civil-service boundaries.

**Why this memo exists:** the archive now covers transfer-of-power and caretaker practice (`308`), chamber and confidence design (`309`), cabinet / coalition / centre-of-government design (`310`), executive-form choice (`311`), party-system design (`312`), head-of-state powers (`313`), government formation (`314`), constitutional maintenance (`171` / `288`), and national / supranational defaults (`40` / `50`). What it still lacked was one compact seam memo for the layer that often sits **between** formal law and day-to-day constitutional operation: **which norms may live as conventions rather than statutes, how cabinet manuals and guidance should publish them, how a new government should endorse or amend them, and how crisis actors avoid turning “convention” into a secret constitution or an excuse for improvisation**.

**Evidence anchors:** International IDEA’s primer on government formation and removal is a good comparative anchor because it shows that formation, removal, and dissolution rules must fit together rather than leaving critical transitions to ad hoc improvisation [BIB-IDEA-GOV-FORMATION-PRIMER] [BIB-IDEA-DISSOLUTION-PRIMER]. New Zealand’s Cabinet Manual is one of the clearest contemporary models because it is published, described as an authoritative guide and primary authority on cabinet government, and endorsed at the first Cabinet meeting of a new government [BIB-NZ-CABINET-MANUAL-WEB-2023]. The New Zealand Governor-General’s reserve-powers guidance is equally useful because it insists on **clear and public statements** and on the Governor-General abiding by the outcome of the formation process rather than privately choosing the government [BIB-NZ-RESERVE-POWERS-2026]. Australia’s 2024 caretaker guidance is a useful caution because it explains the conventions, states that they are not legally binding or hard-and-fast rules, and still requires agencies to organize internal responsibility for applying them consistently [BIB-AU-CARETAKER-GUIDANCE-2024]. Canada’s 2025 caretaker guidance shows the same pattern in a different constitutional setting: operations continue, but specific classes of conduct during the writ period need published restraint rules [BIB-CANADA-CARETAKER-2025]. The UK Cabinet Manual remains a useful reference point because it makes the laws, rules, and conventions affecting the conduct and operation of government legible in one public place [BIB-UK-CABINET-MANUAL-2010].

## Core claim

Ideal governments should minimize the number of critical constitutional rules that exist only as private memory.

But no constitutional order can reduce everything to statute. Some rules must stay partly conventional because they concern:
- restraint rather than command,
- political responsibility rather than judicial enforceability,
- or flexible coordination among actors whose exact situations cannot all be pre-scripted.

The design problem is therefore **not** “abolish conventions.” It is:
**publish them, classify them, subordinate them to law, rehearse them before crisis, and never let a high-stakes constitutional power depend on insider ambiguity alone.**

A convention layer fails in five predictable ways:
1. **Secret constitution:** only insiders know the real operating rules.
2. **Selective invocation:** actors cite convention opportunistically when text is inconvenient.
3. **Hierarchy blur:** nobody can tell whether statute, standing orders, coalition terms, or manual guidance controls.
4. **Civil-service drift:** officials receive inconsistent signals during transitions or disputes.
5. **Crisis improvisation:** reserve powers, caretaker limits, or coalition carve-outs are “discovered” only when stakes are maximal.

The design target is simple:
- **public operating guidance,**
- **clear norm hierarchy,**
- **typed convention classes,**
- **start-of-term endorsement and amendment discipline,**
- **and after-action maintenance when conventions are actually stress-tested.**

## The smallest good architecture

### 1. Norm hierarchy note (`NHN-*`)
Publish one short hierarchy that makes clear the order of control:
- constitution / basic law,
- statute,
- standing orders / formal parliamentary rules,
- published cabinet manual or constitutional-operating guidance,
- published coalition or support agreements,
- and then only residual informal practice.

If two layers conflict, the higher one governs.

### 2. Convention register note (`CRN-*`)
For each high-stakes convention, publish:
- name,
- trigger,
- actors bound,
- what restraint or duty it creates,
- what evidence counts,
- what advice lane exists,
- and what public artifact should exist if the convention is invoked.

### 3. Start-of-term endorsement note (`SEN-*`)
At the first cabinet / executive-organizing meeting after installation, the incoming government should:
- endorse the operating manual,
- publish any deviations,
- state which coalition or confidence terms modify ordinary collective practice,
- and identify the office that maintains the manual.

### 4. Exceptional-use note (`EUN-*`)
If a convention is relied upon in a crisis or edge case, emit a short public note stating:
- which convention was used,
- why the trigger was met,
- what decision followed,
- and where fuller reasoning or later review will be recorded.

### 5. Maintenance and revision note (`MRN-*`)
After elections, caretaker periods, formation crises, reserve-power episodes, or major coalition innovations, review the guidance and publish:
- what held,
- what proved ambiguous,
- what was updated,
- and what remains legal text rather than manual guidance.

## Design rules

### A. Do not leave the hardest constitutional powers to convention alone
The more coercive or crisis-sensitive the power, the less it should depend on unwritten restraint.

Do **not** rely on convention alone for:
- emergency derogation triggers,
- election cancellation or unilateral timing changes,
- dismissal of a prime minister or government,
- dissolution powers,
- refusal of assent or veto override pathways,
- or long-term appointment powers during caretaker periods.

Use legal text or a tightly typed public rule first, and let convention operate only at the margin.

### B. Use conventions mainly for restraint, sequencing, and good-faith coordination
Conventions are most defensible where law would otherwise become too rigid or over-detailed.

Good convention candidates include:
- caretaker restraint,
- public-service impartiality around elections and government formation,
- collective-responsibility handling inside coalitions,
- consultation expectations among coalition partners,
- and the informational protocols that support neutral formation or transfer.

### C. Publish the convention before the crisis, not during it
If the first public explanation of a convention appears during a constitutional clash, the convention has already failed as a trust device.

A convention is much safer when:
- it has been published in advance,
- officials have trained against it,
- parties can organize around it before the fact,
- and outsiders can replay what the actors are claiming.

### D. Keep the manual public, but do not pretend it is supreme law
Cabinet manuals and analogous guidance should be public and easy to cite.

But they should also say clearly:
- what they are,
- what they are not,
- which parts summarize legal duties,
- which parts summarize conventional practice,
- and which parts simply give operational guidance.

A good manual reduces ambiguity; it does not silently rewrite the constitution.

### E. Make coalition or minority-government deviations explicit
In coalition, support, or confidence-and-supply settings, ordinary cabinet conventions may be modified.

If so, publish:
- where collective responsibility is preserved,
- where parties may differ publicly,
- what consultation steps apply before major decisions,
- and how those carve-outs interact with cabinet procedure and parliamentary confidence.

Do **not** let these modifications live only in leader-to-leader side understandings.

### F. Give civil servants and constitutional actors one shared reference point
Caretaker guidance, formation practice, and reserve-power handling become dangerous when ministers, civil servants, heads of state, parliamentary officers, and agencies are reading from different scripts.

The archive’s preference is:
- one public manual or linked public guidance set,
- one maintaining office,
- one advice path for edge cases,
- and clear internal responsibility for consistent application.

### G. Conventions should generate artifacts when they matter
The archive should not treat conventions as pure vibes.

When a convention is doing real constitutional work, generate a visible artifact such as:
- a caretaker notice,
- a formation support statement,
- a coalition protocol,
- a reserve-power explanation note,
- or a revision memo after the episode ends.

### H. Review conventions after stress, not just on anniversaries
The best time to refine constitutional operating guidance is after it has been tested.

At minimum, review after:
- a hung parliament,
- an early election,
- a major caretaker dispute,
- a novel coalition form,
- a reserve-power controversy,
- or a transfer failure that exposed ambiguity between political and administrative responsibility.

## Scope-sensitive defaults

### Micro-local / municipal
- **Default:** use formal charter and standing rules first; keep conventions thin.
- **Prefer:** short public protocols for council transition, acting authority, and meeting procedure.
- **Guardrail:** do not rely on unwritten elite custom where turnover is high and administrative capacity is thin.

### Metropolitan / regional
- **Default:** publish one short executive-operating protocol if coalitions, rotating leadership, or directly elected executives coexist with assemblies.
- **Prefer:** clear transition and acting-authority notes rather than thick constitutional folklore.
- **Guardrail:** if a metro or regional body is politically salient, do not leave coalition carve-outs or caretaker practice unwritten.

### National parliamentary
- **Default:** maintain a public cabinet manual or equivalent, endorsed by each incoming government and cross-linked to formation, caretaker, cabinet, and reserve-power practice.
- **Prefer:** public treatment of coalition modifications, caretaker conventions, and formation ascertainment rules.
- **Guardrail:** do not let reserve powers, dissolution practice, or collective-responsibility exceptions exist only as insider memory.

### National presidential or semi-presidential
- **Default:** rely more on legal text for appointment, dismissal, and emergency powers, but still publish transition, caretaker, and executive-procedure guidance where the law leaves operational gaps.
- **Prefer:** public transition manuals and typed inter-branch coordination rules.
- **Guardrail:** do not use vague “constitutional custom” to hide contested presidential discretion.

### Supranational / treaty-union
- **Default:** publish institutional operating protocols where multiple democratic channels, rotating presidencies, or summit–parliament sequences create ambiguity.
- **Prefer:** clear nomination / confirmation / caretaker notes and one public map of who advises whom.
- **Guardrail:** a union should not imitate Westminster-style convention density unless its political responsibility structure really matches it.

### Global
- **Default:** favor chartered procedures and transparent operating protocols over inherited constitutional convention.
- **Prefer:** publicly adopted procedural manuals for appointments, rotating chairs, crisis authority, and deadlock handling.
- **Guardrail:** do not assume a shared constitutional culture strong enough to carry unwritten norms across diverse political orders.

## Quick heuristics

### 1. If outsiders cannot cite the rule, the rule is too hidden
A usable convention should be easy to find, name, and replay.

### 2. If the power can topple a government, write more than a convention
The closer the issue is to dismissal, dissolution, emergency, or assent refusal, the less tolerance the archive has for tacit rules.

### 3. Manuals should be boring in tone and precise in hierarchy
A cabinet manual is safer when it reads like an operating reference rather than partisan constitutional theory.

### 4. Every coalition innovation should either enter the manual or sit beside it publicly
Hidden coalition carve-outs produce shadow constitutions.

### 5. When a convention is breached, do not answer only with outrage
Also answer with:
- a public explanation of the claimed rule,
- a recorded breach determination or dispute note,
- and a maintenance update to reduce future ambiguity.

## Routing boundaries

### `308` vs `315`
- `308` governs **what caretaker restraint, continuity, handover, and acting authority should actually require**.
- `315` governs **how those restraints and transfer conventions should be published, classified, endorsed, and maintained as public operating guidance**.

### `310` vs `315`
- `310` governs **cabinet structure, portfolio maps, cabinet committees, coalition operating terms, and centre-of-government coordination**.
- `315` governs **how collective responsibility, coalition carve-outs, and cabinet operating conventions should be codified and kept subordinate to law**.

### `311` vs `315`
- `311` governs **which executive form the polity should adopt**.
- `315` governs **how published manuals and conventions should operationalize that form once chosen, especially where law leaves coordination gaps**.

### `313` vs `315`
- `313` governs **which head-of-state acts exist and how reserve powers, countersignature, assent, and succession should be bounded**.
- `315` governs **how any residual conventional layer around those acts should be published so a head of state is not forced to improvise from private lore**.

### `314` vs `315`
- `314` governs **how a government is formed: investiture proofs, support disclosure, clocks, and deadlock ladders**.
- `315` governs **how the conventional guidance around ascertainment, caretaker restraint during bargaining, and coalition operating departures is made public and maintained**.

### `171` / `288` vs `315`
- `171` and `288` govern **constitutional maintenance, amendment discipline, and review observability**.
- `315` governs **the lower-level but still constitutional operating guidance that should be revised when practice exposes ambiguity without necessarily amending the constitution itself**.

## Minimum viable checklist

1. Is there a public hierarchy showing where manual guidance sits relative to law and standing orders?
2. Are the major conventions named, triggered, and tied to actors rather than left implicit?
3. Does each new government endorse or publicly amend the manual at the start of its term?
4. Are coalition or minority-government departures from ordinary cabinet practice published?
5. Are high-stakes powers kept in legal text or tightly typed public rules rather than left to tacit convention?
6. Is there one maintaining office and one advice path for edge cases?
7. Does a stress event generate a review and update rather than a return to silence?
