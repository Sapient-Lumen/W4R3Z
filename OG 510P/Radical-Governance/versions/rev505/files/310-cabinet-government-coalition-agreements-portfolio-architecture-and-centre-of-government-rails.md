# 310 — Cabinet Government, Coalition Agreements, Portfolio Architecture, and Centre-of-Government Rails

**Purpose:** make executive government legible after a governing majority or coalition exists, so cabinet formation, portfolio allocation, and cross-ministerial coordination do not dissolve into patronage fog, silent turf war, or informal rule by the leader’s office.

**Why this memo exists:** the archive now explains legitimacy generation (`21` / `307`), election administration (`56`), representative chambers and confidence rules (`309`), transfer-of-power and caretaker limits (`308`), lawmaking process (`215` / `300`), and state-capacity / appointment integrity (`09` / `297`). What it still needed was one compact seam memo for the next institutional layer: **how a government should actually be assembled and coordinated once it has the right to govern**. If the issue shifts from cabinet structure to how collective responsibility, coalition carve-outs, and cabinet practice should be codified in public operating guidance, route to `315-constitutional-conventions-cabinet-manuals-and-codified-restraint-rails.md`.

**Evidence anchors:** OECD’s current centre-of-government material describes the CoG as the support structure serving the highest level of the executive, helping heads of government and ministers make strategic, evidence-informed, and coherent decisions while coordinating players across the policy process [BIB-OECD-COG-NETWORK-2026]. OECD’s 2024 CoG compendium adds that CoGs sit at the political–administrative bridge and that clear roles, mandates, and spheres of influence are essential to avoid duplication and improve horizontal coordination [BIB-OECD-COG-COMPLEXITY-2024]. OECD’s 2025 policy-coherence chapter reports that 15 of 24 OECD countries with data have formal whole-of-government communication arrangements between ministries and departments, but only 3 of 24 report clear mandates to improve coherence and mitigate sectoral divergence [BIB-OECD-PCSD-COORDINATION-2025]. New Zealand’s current coalition operating guidance is a useful practice model: coalition parties are to work in good faith, follow a “no surprises” principle, and ensure consultation processes are in place across ministers and chief executives [BIB-NZ-COALITION-OPERATING-2024]. New Zealand’s Cabinet Office also publishes current rules for Cabinet committees and ministerial portfolios, including that committee structure and membership are decided by the Prime Minister in consultation with a coalition partner leader where relevant, and that portfolio directories should record the departments, agencies, votes, and legislation attached to each portfolio [BIB-NZ-CABINET-COMMITTEES-2024] [BIB-NZ-MINISTERIAL-PORTFOLIOS-2023]. The UK Ministerial Code remains a useful current reference for collective responsibility, the duty of ministers to Parliament, the obligation to consider impartial civil-service advice, and conflict management [BIB-UK-MINISTERIAL-CODE-2025].

## Core claim

Winning authority is not the same as structuring a governable executive.

Executive design fails in six predictable ways:
1. **Portfolio fog:** nobody can tell which minister owns which decision, agency, budget line, or law.
2. **Coalition opacity:** support agreements, carve-outs, and dispute rules are hidden until a crisis.
3. **Leader’s-office overrun:** the centre of government silently replaces ministries instead of coordinating them.
4. **Committee theater:** cabinet committees exist on paper but do not actually resolve cross-cutting conflicts.
5. **Civil-service politicisation drift:** ministers treat departments as faction property rather than professional capability.
6. **Accountability blur:** Parliament and the public cannot trace which minister, committee, or coalition term produced a decision.

The design target is simple:
- **public governing terms,**
- **clear portfolio maps,**
- **bounded collective responsibility,**
- **cabinet committees that do real work,**
- **and a centre of government that coordinates without swallowing the state.**

## The smallest good architecture

### 1. Governing agreement note (`GAN-*`)
If government depends on a coalition, support agreement, or structured minority arrangement, publish:
- participating parties or blocs,
- confidence and supply terms,
- collective priorities,
- issues reserved for separate party positions / conscience votes / “agree to disagree” treatment,
- consultation rules,
- dispute-escalation path,
- and review / update triggers.

### 2. Portfolio map (`PFM-*`)
For each ministerial portfolio, publish:
- minister and any junior / associate minister,
- departments, agencies, public bodies, and SOEs attached,
- vote / budget responsibilities,
- legislation administered,
- formal delegations,
- cabinet committee memberships,
- and the person-facing services or high-risk powers linked to the portfolio.

### 3. Cabinet committee charter set (`CCC-*`)
For each committee, publish:
- remit,
- chair,
- membership rule,
- attendance rule for officials,
- decision status (recommendation / delegated decision / escalation only),
- and how minutes, decisions, and follow-through are tracked.

### 4. Collective responsibility and disagreement protocol (`CRP-*`)
Publish the default rule for:
- when collective responsibility applies,
- when it is explicitly relaxed,
- how dissent is registered,
- what ministers may say publicly,
- and what happens if a minister cannot continue under the agreed line.

### 5. Centre-of-government mandate note (`CGM-*`)
The CoG should publish:
- its formal functions,
- which cross-government priorities it monitors,
- how it resolves cross-ministerial disputes,
- which matters must come through it,
- and what remains the responsibility of line ministries, the cabinet office, the treasury / finance ministry, and the civil service / public service commission.

## Design rules

### A. Cabinet is a coordination engine, not a prize table
Do not treat ministries as spoils to be distributed without regard to governability. Portfolio design should follow problem domains, delivery lines, and accountability routes.

### B. Portfolio boundaries should be public and auditable
A person should be able to tell which minister owns:
- the law,
- the budget,
- the department,
- the regulator or operator,
- and the appeal / oversight path.

If those do not line up cleanly, publish the split explicitly rather than letting it hide in convention.

### C. Coalition terms should be public enough to be governable
Not every negotiating detail must be public, but the governing terms that affect legislation, budget deals, consultation rights, carve-outs, and stability should be.

At minimum, the public should be able to see:
- what binds the parties,
- what does not,
- who must be consulted,
- and how disputes are meant to be settled before they become constitutional events.

### D. Cabinet committees should carry the real load
Full cabinet should not be the first place serious cross-government conflict is surfaced.

Use committees for:
- economic and fiscal coordination,
- legislation and parliamentary business,
- appointments and integrity-sensitive decisions,
- social / domestic cross-cutting policy,
- security / crisis matters,
- and strategy / implementation review.

But keep the map sparse enough that committees clarify rather than multiply confusion.

### E. The centre of government should coordinate, not become a shadow ministry
The CoG is justified when it:
- aligns priorities,
- checks coherence,
- arbitrates cross-ministry conflict,
- monitors implementation of agreed priorities,
- and protects decision quality.

The CoG fails when it:
- duplicates line-ministry analysis without necessity,
- centralizes routine portfolio work for political convenience,
- bypasses published committee routes,
- or turns every issue into a leader’s-office intervention.

### F. Ministers govern politically; civil services execute professionally
Ministers are entitled to set direction within law. They are not entitled to erase merit systems, suppress candid advice, or treat public agencies as faction property.

Keep visible:
- ministerial direction,
- civil-service advice routes,
- conflict recusal,
- and the difference between political office staff and career officials.

### G. Junior, associate, and delegated ministers need written boundaries
If multiple ministers touch one field, publish who has primary authority, what is delegated, and which decisions require consultation or joint sign-off.

### H. Every cabinet needs a parliamentary-business operating lane
A government that cannot translate priorities into a sequenced parliamentary and budget program will either drift or improvise. The cabinet / committee map should therefore connect clearly to:
- legislative planning,
- budget sequencing,
- consultation duties,
- and response duties to committees, auditors, and Parliament.

## Scope-sensitive defaults

### Municipal / micro-local
Do **not** imitate a full national cabinet if the jurisdiction is too small. Prefer:
- a directly accountable mayor or council leader,
- a small executive committee,
- a professional city manager / chief executive,
- and explicit portfolio ownership for planning, finance, public works, and safety.

### Metropolitan / regional
Use a cabinet-style executive only if the authority carries real cross-sector coordination burdens. Otherwise, strong committee government with a professional executive may be enough.

### National
This is the main zone for `310`.
A national government usually needs:
- a prime minister / president and cabinet,
- a published portfolio directory,
- cabinet committees,
- a cabinet office / CoG function,
- written coalition or support terms where no single party governs alone,
- and a clean interface to Parliament, budget processes, and the civil service.

### Supranational / treaty-union
Do not assume “cabinet government” transfers directly. Still, the same design questions recur:
- who coordinates across sectoral bodies,
- what counts as collective responsibility,
- which competences are genuinely shared,
- and how member-polity consultation and executive steering are made legible.

### Global
Prefer issue-specific executive secretariats and bounded steering bodies over an imitation of national cabinet government.

---

## Design rules for ideal governments by scope

1. **Do not form portfolios faster than you can explain them.**
2. **Do not centralize coordination so much that ministries lose responsibility.**
3. **Do not hide coalition government behind private understandings that shape public outcomes.**
4. **Do not let junior-minister and delegated-authority arrangements become invisible power channels.**
5. **Do make cabinet committees the place where cross-government issues are resolved before crisis.**
6. **Do keep the political executive and the professional state connected but distinct.**

## Confusion boundaries

### `310` vs `309`
- `309` governs **how representative chambers, committees, opposition rights, and confidence architecture are designed**.
- `310` governs **how the executive team formed from that confidence basis is assembled and coordinated**.

### `310` vs `308`
- `308` governs **transfer, caretaker restraint, acting authority, and continuity while power is changing hands**.
- `310` governs **the standing operating design once a government is formed and expected to govern**.

### `310` vs `311`
- `311` governs **the constitutional choice of executive form and head-of-state / head-of-government structure**.
- `310` governs **the cabinet, portfolio, committee, and centre-of-government operating layer inside that form once it exists**.

### `310` vs `297` / `09` / `199`
- `297`, `09`, and `199` govern **state capacity, merit systems, public-service capability, and depoliticisation**.
- `310` governs **the political executive layer that should sit above, coordinate with, and not corrode that capability**.

### `310` vs `215`
- `215` governs **legislative process, drafting, amendments, and enactment packets**.
- `310` governs **the executive-side portfolio, committee, and coordination architecture that produces and sequences those decisions**.

### `310` vs `40`
- `40` is the **broad national-scope memo**.
- `310` is the **cabinet / portfolio / CoG specialization** for one core part of national executive design.

## Minimal evaluation questions

1. Can a person or parliamentarian tell which minister owns a law, budget line, agency, and service pathway?
2. If coalition or minority support matters, are the governing terms visible enough to understand how decisions are being made?
3. Do cabinet committees have clear remits and membership, or are they ornamental?
4. Is the centre of government coordinating cross-cutting issues, or informally replacing ministries?
5. Are junior-minister and delegated-authority arrangements published and legible?
6. Can Parliament and the public see how collective responsibility, dissent, and ministerial accountability are supposed to work?
