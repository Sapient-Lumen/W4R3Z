# 319 — Statute-Book Maintenance, Consolidation, Codification, Repeal, Restatement, and Revision-Bill Rails

**Purpose:** keep enacted law usable over time so a polity does not let its statute book decay into patchwork amendments, zombie provisions, and unofficial reconstructions that only insiders can navigate.

**Why this memo exists:** the archive now covers legislative drafting (`215`), bill finalization and official publication (`316`), delegated legislation (`317`), quasi-law guidance (`318`), legal legibility (`25`), the rulebook substrate (`39`), and change control / release discipline (`118` / `208`). What it still lacked was one compact seam memo for **what happens after laws accumulate**: **when to consolidate, restate, codify, repeal spent provisions, use revision bills, publish authoritative current texts, and keep the statute book coherent without smuggling substantive reform through a “tidying up” lane**.

**Evidence anchors:** New Zealand’s Legislation Act remains one of the clearest current official anchors because it frames high-quality legislation as legislation that is easy to find, use, and understand, provides tools for modernising and simplifying legislation and keeping it up to date, gives legal status to official versions, and provides a revision-bill process with certification and an old-law interpretation bridge [BIB-NZ-LEGISLATION-ACT-2019-2026]. The current New Zealand Legislation Amendment Bill is also useful as a live maintenance-side signal because it explicitly says secondary legislation can be hard to find, seeks a single point of access, expands revision-bill powers, and enables editorial changes to keep legislation up to date and correct errors [BIB-NZ-LEGISLATION-AMENDMENT-BILL-2026]. The Law Commission’s current law-reform material remains a compact accessibility anchor because it treats consolidation and repeal as core tools for making statute law clearer, more concise, accessible, and less misleading [BIB-UK-LAWCOM-LAW-REFORMS-2026] [BIB-UK-LAWCOM-REPEALS-2026]. UK parliamentary procedure also remains a useful design anchor because Erskine May and the House of Lords Standing Orders still distinguish consolidation bills and statute law revision bills as special categories reviewed through a dedicated Joint Committee path [BIB-UK-ERSKINE-CONSOLIDATION-BILLS-2026] [BIB-UK-HL-STANDING-ORDERS-CONSOLIDATION-2026]. Wales’s current accessibility programme adds a modern operational example because the 2025 Welsh legislation statute and current planning-law consolidation work explicitly join publication, repeal, consolidation, bilingual accessibility, explanatory notes, and tables of origins / destinations [BIB-WALES-LPPR-ACT-2025] [BIB-WALES-PLANNING-CONSOLIDATION-2025].

---

## Core claim

A lawmaking system fails **even when new laws are formally valid** if old laws become too fragmented to read, too cluttered to trust, or too stale to use.

The post-enactment statute-book layer is where six predictable failures appear:
1. **Patchwork law:** users must reconstruct current law from many amendment Acts and scattered instruments.
2. **Zombie law:** spent, obsolete, or Wales-only / local-only leftovers remain on the books and mislead readers.
3. **Shadow consolidation:** unofficial publishers, advisers, or internal teams become the practical source of “current law” because no authoritative current text exists.
4. **Hidden policy drift:** substantive changes are smuggled into consolidation, revision, or repeal packages that are supposed to be technical.
5. **Broken provenance:** readers cannot trace where renumbered or relocated provisions came from.
6. **Maintenance starvation:** legislators keep adding law but never budget for codification, repeal, or restatement.

The design target is simple:
- **authoritative current text,**
- **routine repeal of dead law,**
- **typed maintenance lanes distinct from policy reform,**
- **clear provenance across renumbering and restructuring,**
- **and a certified path for revision work that improves form without disguising new policy.**

## The smallest good architecture

### 1. Statute-book maintenance programme (`SBM-*`)
Maintain one rolling public programme that identifies:
- high-amendment clusters that should be consolidated,
- obsolete or spent provisions ready for repeal,
- domains needing codification or restatement,
- maintenance priorities by user pain, litigation burden, or administrative confusion,
- and the office responsible for delivery.

### 2. Maintenance-lane classification note (`MLC-*`)
Before starting work, classify the project as one of:
- **consolidation** — reorganize and restate existing law without substantive change,
- **revision / restatement** — modernize language, structure, numbering, or drafting style with only tightly bounded technical changes,
- **codification** — bring a body of law into a more coherent whole, which may or may not include deliberate reform,
- **repeal / disapplication** — remove spent, obsolete, or no-longer-useful law,
- **policy reform** — make actual substantive choices.

If the project is really policy reform, do not disguise it as maintenance.

### 3. No-policy-change certificate (`NPC-*`)
For consolidation, repeal, and revision packages, publish one short note stating:
- whether the package changes legal effect,
- what categories of minor change are permitted,
- who certified the package,
- and what should happen if reviewers detect a substantive change.

### 4. Provenance tables (`PROV-*`)
Where provisions are restructured, renumbered, or split, publish:
- table of origins,
- table of destinations,
- list of repealed provisions,
- and a note on where users should cite the new text.

### 5. Official current-text chain (`OCT-*`)
For each maintained law body, provide:
- as-enacted text,
- current official consolidation,
- date-stamped prior consolidations,
- amendment history,
- and any editorial changes log.

### 6. Dead-law review packet (`DLR-*`)
For repeal projects, publish one compact packet explaining:
- why the provision is spent, obsolete, superseded, or otherwise not of practical utility,
- whether any savings, transitional, or local effects survive,
- whether courts or administrators still rely on it,
- and what replacement or tombstone note remains.

## Design rules

### A. Treat legal accessibility as a maintenance duty, not a one-time publication event
Publishing a law once is not enough.

A good legal system keeps the statute book readable **after** years of amendments, repeals, commencements, and local carve-outs. Maintenance is therefore part of rule-of-law infrastructure, not a cosmetic project.

### B. Separate maintenance from policy change
The archive accepts technical maintenance lanes only if they stay visibly technical.

Consolidation, repeal, and revision work should not become a low-friction way to:
- expand powers,
- narrow rights,
- alter sanctions,
- change review standards,
- or insert new policy choices that deserve ordinary political scrutiny.

Where genuine reform is needed, open a real reform bill or companion bill.

### C. Consolidate when amendment drift defeats ordinary comprehension
If a law has been amended so many times that normal users must reconstruct it from multiple texts, the system should prefer consolidation.

Consolidation is especially indicated when:
- users routinely cite unofficial compilations,
- frontline agencies work from private “clean copies”,
- section numbering has become hostile to navigation,
- or litigation and administration repeatedly trip over amendment history rather than merits.

### D. Repeal spent and obsolete law on purpose, not by neglect
Spent transitional clauses, dead institutional references, superseded procedural regimes, and obsolete local or emergency provisions should not simply linger forever.

Dead law wastes time, misleads users, and raises compliance cost. A healthy system runs periodic repeal work and leaves visible tombstones or provenance notes where readers need them.

### E. Preserve both history and present usability
The archive rejects the false choice between:
- preserving the as-enacted text,
- and giving people a usable current text.

A good system publishes both:
- the historical text for provenance and review,
- and the official current consolidation for practical use.

Users should not have to choose between authenticity and usability.

### F. Revision and editorial powers must be bounded and logged
Editorial and revision powers can be valuable because they:
- correct obvious errors,
- modernize drafting style,
- improve consistency,
- and reduce navigational friction.

But they become dangerous if they can silently alter legal effect. Therefore every maintenance lane should specify:
- what kinds of changes are allowed,
- who certifies them,
- how they are logged,
- and how doubtful changes are escalated out of the technical lane.

### G. Where structure changes, provenance must stay legible
When a consolidation or restatement moves provisions around, the system should publish origins / destinations tables and keep stable searchable crosswalks.

Renumbering without provenance is not simplification; it is memory loss.

### H. Codification is justified only if it lowers complexity for real users
The archive is not impressed by codification theatre.

A code or restatement is worthwhile only if it:
- reduces navigation burden,
- clarifies scope,
- improves official accessibility,
- and preserves or openly debates any real policy changes.

A giant code that ordinary users still cannot navigate is merely a prettier maze.

### I. Statute-book maintenance should follow user pain, not prestige alone
Prioritize maintenance where fragmentation causes:
- high enforcement volume,
- recurring procedural error,
- chronic litigation over interpretation or commencement,
- known local / devolved divergence confusion,
- or heavy use by non-specialists.

The best maintenance candidate is often not the most famous law, but the most routinely misread one.

---

## Anti-patterns to reject

Reject designs where:
- governments promise “accessibility” but publish no authoritative current text,
- agencies privately maintain working consolidations while the public gets amendment scraps,
- repeal bills hide substantive policy choices,
- revision or restatement exercises quietly alter legal effect,
- renumbered or relocated provisions appear with no origins / destinations tables,
- obsolete emergency, transitional, or local provisions remain in force-looking form for years,
- or a codification project becomes so ambitious that maintenance never ships at all.

## One-line design test

**If ordinary users need a private expert’s homemade clean copy to know the current law, statute-book maintenance has already failed.**
