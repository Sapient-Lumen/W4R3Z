# 332 — Scope-Coercion Defaults: Policing, Detention, Emergency Force, and No-Shadow Militias

**Cross-stack note:** use `306-scope-design-ideal-government-and-reference-bundles-guide.md` for the canonical route across the scope-design family. This memo is the scale-to-coercion / direct-force-holding front door; `14` / `176` explain the cross-scope logic, `285` gives the default bundle language, `327` handles the scale-sensitive institutional-form default, `328` handles the scale-sensitive legitimacy-channel default, `329` handles the scale-sensitive tempo / time-horizon default, `330` handles the scale-sensitive epistemic / evidence-mode default, `331` handles the scale-sensitive accountability / review-mode default, `333` handles the scale-sensitive finance / revenue-model default, `336` handles the scale-sensitive constituency / who-counts default, `05` is the coercion and public-safety front door, `112` handles emergency-power override discipline, `116` handles the portable force / detention protocol, `233` handles policing operations, `232` handles custody and corrections, `222` handles war powers and external force, and `292` is the coercion-family router.

**Purpose:** answer the design question that remains after scope assignment, bundle design, institutional form, legitimacy choice, tempo choice, evidence choice, and accountability choice: **which scales should directly hold coercive powers at all, and which should mostly not?**

**Why this memo exists:** `14`, `176`, `285`, `327`, `328`, `329`, `330`, and `331` explain where functions belong, what minimum bundle a government needs, what shape it should take, how it should be legitimated, what tempo it should run on, what evidence it should mainly govern with, and how it should mainly be checked. `05`, `112`, `116`, `233`, `232`, and `222` explain how coercive power, emergency override, detention, policing, custody, and war authority should be governed in general. But the archive still lacked one compact memo answering the narrower practical question: **at a given scale, should government hold no direct force at all, only civil enforcement, ordinary policing/custody, specialized emergency force, or military power?**

**From-below:** this memo is a guardrail against two recurring harms at once: tiny units pretending to be sovereign because they can threaten or exclude, and distant prestige institutions accumulating force because nobody wrote a clear coercion ceiling. It keeps direct force close enough to remain understandable, professional enough to remain bounded, and centralized enough to prevent militia drift and duplicate armed bureaucracies.

**Evidence anchors:** the European Charter of Local Self-Government says public responsibilities should generally be exercised by authorities closest to the citizen, while allowing higher-level allocation where the nature of the task or efficiency requires it, and it also limits supervision of local authorities to law- and constitution-focused review with proportionality discipline [BIB-COE-LOCALSELF-ART8]; OHCHR’s Code of Conduct for Law Enforcement Officials says force may be used only when strictly necessary and to the extent required for duty, with firearms treated as an extreme measure [BIB-UN-BPUFF] [BIB-OHCHR-LAW-ENFORCEMENT]; UNODC’s current police-reform framing still treats police and other law-enforcement bodies as human-rights-based institutions whose authority to use force must remain accountable [BIB-UNODC-POLICE-REFORM-2026]; OPCAT defines deprivation of liberty broadly and requires visiting access to all places where persons are or may be deprived of liberty [BIB-OPCAT] [BIB-UN-SPT-NPM]; and current UN peacekeeping guidance still treats peacekeeping as consent-based and non-enforcement by default, with force limited to self-defence and defence of the mandate rather than open-ended global police power [BIB-UN-PEACEKEEPING-PRINCIPLES-2026] [BIB-UNCHARTER-CH7].

---

## Core claim

Direct coercive power should **not** be distributed evenly across the ladder.

The archive default is:
- **the smaller and more intimate the scale, the less direct force it should usually hold;**
- **the more routine and person-facing the task, the more coercion should lean toward civilian enforcement, municipal policing, and short, reviewable custody rather than prestige force structures;**
- **the more specialized, exceptional, or war-like the power, the more it should sit at regional or national levels with clearer law, tighter command, and stronger independent review;**
- **the more cross-border the scale, the more coercion should shift away from direct policing and toward standards, warrants, sanctions, inspections, verification, and tightly bounded cooperative deployment.**

So the question is never just “who can use force?” It is:
**what scale can carry the training, legality, accountability, custody safeguards, and democratic control that make direct coercion even barely defensible?**

---

## The anti-militia / anti-duplication test

Before giving a scale direct coercive powers, ask six questions:

1. **Necessity test:** is direct force actually necessary here, or would civil enforcement, mediation, inspection, or upstream prevention do the job?
2. **Professionalism test:** can this scale sustain trained staffing, supervision, reporting, and 24/7 continuity rather than improvisation?
3. **Custody test:** if this scale can detain, who monitors every place of detention and every short-term hold?
4. **Command test:** when force escalates, is there a clear lawful chain of command rather than overlapping armed actors?
5. **Review test:** can a harmed person reach an actually independent complaint, court, ombuds, inspector, or serious-incident pathway?
6. **Duplication test:** does a new coercive unit solve a real gap, or does it merely create another badge, vehicle fleet, and command culture?

If the answer to these is weak, do **not** create a new armed authority. Publish a narrower coercion ceiling instead.

## The archive’s coercion defaults by scope

### 0. Household / association / cooperative

**Default coercion mode:** **none**.

Internal governance at this scale may set membership rules, shared-use norms, and mediation paths, but it should not hold public-style coercive powers. It may not lawfully create its own detention, armed enforcement, or exclusion from essential services without a higher-scope legal basis and review path.

Use:
- mediation,
- visible house rules,
- rotation of stewards,
- and easy upward complaint or exit where rights are at risk.

**Avoid:** quasi-sovereign household or association power hiding behind “private rules”.

### 1. Micro-local (building / block / neighborhood / campus / frontline institution)

**Default coercion mode:** **no direct public force; prevention, steward presence, and immediate escalation instead**.

Micro-local units should usually hold:
- watchfulness,
- communication and de-escalation roles,
- commons stewardship,
- non-coercive first response,
- and clear referral into municipal emergency, health, or safety services.

They should **not** usually hold:
- armed patrol authority,
- detention power,
- independent search/seizure powers,
- or discretionary punitive exclusion from necessities.

This is where the archive is most anti-militia. Small-scale legitimacy does **not** justify neighborhood armed sovereignty.

**Avoid:** “community safety” bodies that drift into intimidation, stop/search, or custody without professional safeguards.

### 2. Municipal (city / town / county-equivalent frontline state)

**Default coercion mode:** **civil enforcement first, ordinary civilian policing where assigned, and only bounded short-term custody**.

Municipal government is the archive’s usual frontline holder of everyday coercive authority because it sits close enough to daily harms to remain visible, yet large enough to professionalize reporting, supervision, and independent review.

Municipal scale may hold:
- code and civil enforcement,
- frontline policing,
- dispatch and emergency response,
- and short, reviewable detention/holding where the full custody safeguards exist.

It should generally **not** hold:
- military / war powers,
- open-ended intelligence authority,
- or prestige paramilitary structures for ordinary civic problems.

The design preference here is **civilianization where possible**: not every local compliance task should route through armed officers.

**Avoid:** turning every municipal problem into an armed one, or using municipal fragmentation as an excuse for unreviewable force.

### 3. Intermediate local administration (county / prefecture pattern)

**Default coercion mode:** **specialized backstop roles, not prestige duplication of general force**.

Intermediate local administration may hold:
- courts and sheriff-like legal process roles where constitutionally assigned,
- jail/custody administration,
- inspection and enforcement support,
- and emergency support or coordination functions.

But its safest default is **standardization and backstop**, not a new independent armed identity. Use it to stabilize custody, transport, inspection, records, and cross-municipal service gaps — not to multiply general-purpose coercive actors.

**Avoid:** creating county/prefecture armed brands whose real function is symbolic status rather than a concrete operational gap.

### 4. Metropolitan / corridor / watershed / functional region

**Default coercion mode:** **very limited direct force; coordination, inspection, and shared support by default**.

Metro and functional bodies should usually lean on:
- shared dispatch or mutual-aid protocols,
- major-incident coordination,
- inspectorates,
- compliance audits,
- and narrow operational roles tied to a network system (for example, transit or port safety) only where law is explicit.

They should usually **not** default to:
- general street policing,
- independent broad arrest powers,
- or self-justifying metro armed bureaucracies.

This is the scale most prone to “armed authority by sprawl”. If a metro body needs direct force, its mandate must stay narrow, joint-governed, and publication-heavy.

**Avoid:** solving coordination failures by creating a shadow general government with guns.

### 5. Regional (state / province / land / autonomous territory)

**Default coercion mode:** **specialized coercive powers, emergency command, and large custody systems where constitutionally assigned**.

Regional governments may appropriately hold:
- inspectorates and regulatory enforcement,
- specialized investigative or public-order support,
- prisons/corrections where systems assign them there,
- emergency management command,
- and reserve / guard-like powers where constitutionally bounded.

This scale is often large enough to professionalize major corrections, inspection, and emergency response, but still close enough to territory-specific conditions to avoid national overreach.

It should still avoid swallowing every ordinary frontline coercive task by default if municipal capacity exists.

**Avoid:** using regional scale to centralize routine local coercion merely for prestige or party control.

### 6. National

**Default coercion mode:** **highest coercive powers, strongest lawmaking responsibility, and ultimate command of military force**.

National government is the archive’s usual holder of:
- the criminal-law framework,
- border and customs force,
- national policing where structured that way,
- intelligence authorities,
- military / war powers,
- nationwide emergency override powers,
- and national custody baselines, standards, and inspection obligations.

This concentration is not a celebration of centralization. It is a recognition that the highest-coercion powers require the clearest law, strongest command discipline, and broadest constitutional scrutiny.

National scale should still avoid absorbing ordinary neighborhood, municipal, or network-governance tasks that can be handled below it without rights erosion.

**Avoid:** turning national government into the default operator of every low-level enforcement problem.

### 7. Supranational (union / treaty-union / shared-rule space)

**Default coercion mode:** **narrow, treaty-confined, and heavily reviewable**.

Supranational governance should usually hold:
- standards and warrant/cooperation frameworks,
- sanctions and compliance tools,
- cross-border inspection or monitoring roles,
- and very limited operational force only where treaties clearly confer it and review bodies can police the boundaries.

Its safest coercive posture is usually:
- enabling and constraining member-state action,
- not replacing member states with a full shared police sovereign.

**Avoid:** importing national coercive symbolism into a union whose mandate is actually conferral-limited and review-dependent.

### 8. Global / planetary

**Default coercion mode:** **almost never direct general policing; verification, sanctions, inspections, and consent-based peacekeeping by default**.

Global governance should mainly hold:
- standards,
- reporting and verification,
- sanctions/listings with review paths,
- inspection where treaty consent exists,
- and peacekeeping or protective deployment only under clearly bounded international authority.

It should **not** default to a world police power. Even UN peacekeeping remains consent-based and non-enforcement by default, with force treated as exceptional and mandate-bounded rather than open-ended global sovereignty [BIB-UN-PEACEKEEPING-PRINCIPLES-2026] [BIB-UNCHARTER-CH7].

**Avoid:** treating the global layer as if every collective-action failure justifies direct armed authority.

## Cross-cutting design rules

### A. Coercion ceilings should be explicit public artifacts

Every unit in the competence ledger should publish not just what it owns, but its **coercion ceiling**: none, civil enforcement only, ordinary policing/custody, specialized emergency force, or military power.

### B. Detention authority always drags oversight behind it

If a scale can deprive liberty, it must also carry:
- custody receipts,
- legal access,
- health and safety floors,
- independent visits,
- complaint channels,
- and serious-incident review.

No detention without inspection.

### C. Emergency powers do not justify creating permanent armed layers by stealth

Temporary emergency command can be necessary. Permanent coercive bureaucracy hidden inside an emergency carve-out is not.

### D. Prefer civil and administrative enforcement where the task allows it

Many rule-enforcement tasks do not need armed officers. The archive default is to keep armed force for tasks that truly require it, and to civilly enforce the rest where safety permits.

### E. Cross-border scales should usually coerce through law, not patrol

As scale increases beyond the nation-state, direct armed authority usually weakens while warrants, sanctions, inspections, monitoring, and compliance tools become more appropriate.

### F. The closer the scale is to lived community life, the stronger the anti-retaliation rule must be

Small units can coerce socially even when they lack formal force. So the lower the scale, the stronger the archive’s presumption against delegated direct force.

- `332` answers **where detention/custody should usually sit by scale**.

### `332` vs `112` / `222`
- `332` gives the **cross-scale coercion ceiling defaults**.

- `285` answers **what minimum bundle the government at that scale should contain**.
- `332` isolates the narrower question of **whether direct force belongs in that bundle at all, and at what ceiling**.
