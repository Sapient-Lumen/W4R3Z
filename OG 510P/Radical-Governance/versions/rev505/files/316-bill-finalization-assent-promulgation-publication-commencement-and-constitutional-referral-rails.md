# 316 — Bill Finalization, Assent, Promulgation, Publication, Commencement, and Constitutional-Referral Rails

**Purpose:** make the final stage of lawmaking explicit and auditable so a polity does not let bills become binding through clerical opacity, hidden veto points, or publication limbo.

**Why this memo exists:** the archive already covers legislative drafting and amendment traceability (`215`), head-of-state design (`313`), constitutional review (`156` / `205`), constitutional conventions (`315`), records and publication integrity (`31` / `53`), and constitutional change discipline (`58` / `206`). What it still lacked was one compact seam memo for the narrow but high-stakes interval **between final parliamentary passage and a law actually being in force**: **how the enrolled text is certified, whether assent or reconsideration exists, when constitutional referral is allowed, how promulgation and official publication work, when commencement occurs, and how corrections are handled without smuggling in silent rewrites**.

**Evidence anchors:** International IDEA’s current constitutional primers remain useful here because they treat legislative powers, assent, and vetoes as design choices that shape whether a head of state is a reviewer, a delay point, or a rival political actor [BIB-IDEA-PRES-LEG-POWERS-2017] [BIB-IDEA-PRES-VETO-2017]. OSCE/ODIHR’s 2024 democratic-lawmaking guidance is also a strong whole-cycle anchor because it treats preparing, discussing, adopting, implementing, and evaluating laws as one democratic process rather than disconnected stages [BIB-OSCE-DEMOCRATIC-LAWMAKING-2024]. The Venice Commission’s Rule of Law Checklist still supplies the clearest compact baseline for the publication side: laws should be published before entering into force and be easily accessible [BIB-VENICE-RULE-OF-LAW-CHECKLIST-2016]. Germany’s Basic Law remains a useful official example of typed countersignature, certification, promulgation, and default commencement rules [BIB-GERMANY-BASIC-LAW-2026]. France’s Constitution remains a useful official example of limited presidential reconsideration and pre-promulgation constitutional referral [BIB-FR-CONSTITUTION-ART10-2026] [BIB-FR-CONSTITUTION-ART61-2026]. New Zealand’s Legislation Act remains a strong publication-side anchor because it frames high-quality legislation as legislation that is easy to find, use, and understand, and gives official legal status to published official versions [BIB-NZ-LEGISLATION-ACT-2019-2026].

---

## Core claim

A legislature has **not** finished its job when the last vote ends.

The last stage between passage and legal force is where five predictable failures appear:
1. **Ghost final text:** the public cannot tell which exact text was finally adopted.
2. **Shadow veto:** assent or signature powers exist, but with unclear grounds, clocks, or override rules.
3. **Referral limbo:** constitutional review before promulgation is possible, but no one knows who may trigger it, on what grounds, or with what deadline.
4. **Publication opacity:** a law is nominally enacted, but the public cannot easily find the authoritative version or know when it became binding.
5. **Commencement ambush:** a law takes effect immediately or in fragments without operational readiness, notice, or traceable transition logic.

The design target is simple:
- **one final enrolled text,**
- **typed assent / return / referral powers,**
- **official publication before force,**
- **explicit commencement rules,**
- **and no silent correction path that rewrites meaning after passage.**

## The smallest good architecture

### 1. Final enrolled-text note (`FEN-*`)
Before assent, referral, or promulgation, publish one final text object stating:
- the exact text passed,
- the vote / chamber event(s) that finalized it,
- the authoritative language version(s),
- who certified the enrolled text,
- and the stable citation / rulebook identifier that later official versions will carry.

### 2. Assent / return / referral note (`ARR-*`)
Publish one typed rule stating:
- whether assent is formal, discretionary, return-based, or veto-based,
- who may request reconsideration,
- who may trigger constitutional referral before promulgation,
- what deadline applies to assent, return, or referral,
- what happens if the deadline expires,
- and whether a returned or reviewed bill suspends promulgation automatically.

### 3. Official-publication note (`OPN-*`)
For every enacted law, publish:
- the promulgation event,
- the official publication channel,
- the publication timestamp,
- the authoritative text and any authorized official translations,
- and the link between the enacted text, the official version, and later consolidations.

### 4. Commencement map (`COM-*`)
Every law should state:
- whether it takes effect on publication, on a fixed future date, by section, or by commencement order,
- whether transitional and savings clauses apply,
- what dependent systems or subordinate legislation must exist first,
- and what happens if the commencement preconditions are not met on time.

### 5. Rectification and republication note (`RRN-*`)
Publish one narrow correction protocol stating:
- which errors can be corrected without new legislative action,
- who authorizes the correction,
- how the correction is displayed against the prior text,
- whether the correction changes legal effect,
- and when republication or legislative re-passage is required instead of quiet editing.

## Design rules

### A. One law, one final text, before anyone signs anything
A polity should not let the public guess which document is the law that actually passed.

The archive’s preference is:
- one final enrolled text,
- certified before assent or promulgation,
- with stable identifiers,
- and with later official versions clearly linked back to that enrolled text.

Do **not** let ministries, parliamentary offices, or gazette publishers create silently divergent “working finals”.

### B. Assent powers must be typed, bounded, and clocked
If assent exists, the constitution should say what kind of act it is.

Possible types include:
- **formal assent** on advice,
- **return for reconsideration** once, with reasons,
- **suspensive veto** with an override rule,
- or a stronger veto in systems that openly choose a more powerful presidency.

Bad design is not merely “too much presidential power”; it is **unclear power**.

The final stage becomes dangerous when:
- the grounds for return are unspecified,
- no deadline applies,
- the override path is unclear,
- or a nominally ceremonial office can create indefinite delay.

### C. Constitutional referral before promulgation can be useful, but only if it is narrow and anti-limbo
Pre-promulgation review may help prevent obviously unconstitutional text from entering into force.

But it is safe only when the system states:
- who may refer,
- what kinds of bill or constitutional question qualify,
- the review deadline,
- whether urgency shortens that deadline,
- and whether serial or abusive referrals are blocked.

The archive prefers **constitutional referral as a typed checkpoint, not as a general political appeal lane after losing the vote**.

### D. No law should bind the public before official publication and practical accessibility
Promulgation without accessible publication is not a sufficient rule-of-law condition.

A healthy default is:
- official publication before force,
- one authoritative publication channel,
- free public access,
- stable citations,
- and clear indication of whether the text shown is enrolled, as enacted, or consolidated.

Digital-first publication is fine, but only if the system treats official electronic versions as authoritative and preserves durable access.

### E. Commencement should be explicit, legible, and operationally honest
A law should not take effect by surprise.

Good defaults include:
- explicit effective dates,
- section-level commencement only where necessary,
- phased commencement tied to published readiness conditions,
- and visible transition rules for people already inside the system.

Immediate commencement is sometimes justified, but the archive treats it as an exception that should be explained, not a lazy default.

### F. Commencement orders must not become a shadow legislature
Delegated commencement is sometimes useful for operational sequencing.

But do **not** let the executive use commencement orders to:
- indefinitely suppress a law it dislikes,
- selectively activate politically convenient sections only,
- or hide operational unreadiness that should have blocked passage upstream.

If commencement is delegated, publish:
- the maximum delay,
- reporting duties while sections remain dormant,
- and the fallback rule if the executive misses the deadline.

### G. Official versions and consolidations matter because people need the law they can actually use
People often need both:
- the **as-enacted** text for legal traceability,
- and the **consolidated** text for current practical use.

The archive’s preference is:
- authoritative official versions,
- clearly dated consolidations,
- machine-readable publication metadata,
- and visible notices distinguishing legal text from navigation aids, editorial notes, or convenience markup.

### H. Corrections must be narrow, visible, and replayable
Small typographical fixes may be necessary.

But the correction path should never become a quiet second lawmaking channel.

Use narrow rectification only for:
- clerical errors,
- citation or numbering mistakes,
- obvious publication defects,
- or cross-reference repair that does not change meaning.

If legal effect changes, use new legislative or constitutionally authorized corrective action instead.

### I. Emergency publication fallback should exist, but remain temporary and auditable
A state can lose presses, networks, or normal gazette operations during war, disaster, or state collapse.

The system should still have a typed fallback for promulgation and publication in those conditions.

But any emergency fallback should:
- be temporary,
- preserve later republication in the normal official channel,
- and leave a replayable record of what was announced, when, and by whom.

## Scope-sensitive defaults

### Micro-local / municipal
- **Default:** no ceremonial assent layer; use council passage plus clerk / recorder certification and one official publication channel.
- **Prefer:** one public ordinance portal with as-enacted and current versions.
- **Guardrail:** do not let mayoral signature become an untyped hidden veto unless the charter openly creates one.

### Metropolitan / regional
- **Default:** formal executive signature plus clerk / secretariat certification, with one regional gazette or equivalent publication site.
- **Prefer:** explicit commencement tables where multiple municipalities or agencies must implement the law.
- **Guardrail:** avoid fragmented publication across constituent units when the law is region-wide.

### National parliamentary
- **Default:** formal or narrowly review-based assent, limited reconsideration if used, optional pre-promulgation constitutional referral with fixed clocks, and official publication before force.
- **Prefer:** one official gazette, one enrolled text, one consolidation path, and explicit transitional provisions.
- **Guardrail:** do not let a nominally neutral head of state create indefinite delay after parliament has finally spoken.

### National presidential or semi-presidential
- **Default:** if a president has return or veto power, specify grounds, clocks, override threshold, and publication duty after override or expiry.
- **Prefer:** strong enrolled-text certification and a clear distinction between political veto, constitutional referral, and mere promulgation duty.
- **Guardrail:** do not collapse political disagreement, constitutional review, and publication control into one under-specified presidential power.

### Supranational / treaty-union
- **Default:** publish one authoritative adoption act plus one stable official-publication channel and clear rules on direct effect vs transposition.
- **Prefer:** multilingual official versions and commencement notes that distinguish immediate applicability from member-unit implementation duties.
- **Guardrail:** do not let multi-language or multi-body complexity obscure which text is authoritative and when it binds. If the issue is the wider treaty-domestication seam rather than the final domestic publication stage of an implementing measure, route to `321-treaty-ratification-domestic-effect-reservations-and-implementation-rails.md`.

### Global
- **Default:** favor chartered promulgation and publication procedures over political-assent theater.
- **Prefer:** one official multilingual publication ledger with cryptographic integrity and durable public access.
- **Guardrail:** do not assume a shared constitutional culture strong enough to carry the final stage through informal convention alone.

## Failure signatures

Watch for these recurring pathologies:
- **“The law passed, but nobody can find the real text.”** → enrolled-text / official-version failure.
- **“The president never signed, returned, or explained.”** → unclocked assent / shadow-veto failure.
- **“Everyone says the court might review it, but no one knows who can refer it or when.”** → referral-limbo failure.
- **“The law exists, but agencies say it is not really live yet.”** → commencement-opacity failure.
- **“The gazette text changed after publication, but there is no correction note.”** → silent-rectification failure.

## Confusion boundaries

### `316` vs `215`
- `215` governs **bill packets, amendment traceability, hearings, anti-bundling, and final vote readiness before passage**.
- `316` governs **what happens after the final vote: enrolled text, assent, referral, promulgation, publication, commencement, and rectification**.

### `316` vs `313`
- `313` governs **whether a head of state has assent, veto, return, or reserve powers at all and how that office should be bounded**.
- `316` governs **how those final-stage powers are sequenced, clocked, published, and connected to the law’s actual entry into force**.

### `316` vs `156` / `205`
- `156` and `205` govern **constitutional-review architecture, precedent visibility, compliance, and judicial observability generally**.
- `316` governs **the narrow checkpoint where constitutional referral may happen before promulgation and before the law enters into force**.

### `316` vs `31` / `53`
- `31` and `53` govern **records, archival memory, release integrity, and tamper-evident publication across government generally**.
- `316` governs **the specific lawmaking-finalization artifacts that make one enacted law authentic, findable, and time-stamped**.

### `316` vs `58` / `206`
- `58` and `206` govern **constitutional amendment and foundational-change discipline**.
- `316` governs **the ordinary final stage for laws once their substantive adoption path is complete**, while still supplying publication and commencement patterns that constitutional change may borrow.

## Minimal evaluation questions

1. Is there one unambiguous enrolled text for the law that actually passed?
2. Can a reader tell whether assent is formal, suspensive, return-based, or veto-based — and with what deadline?
3. If pre-promulgation constitutional review exists, who may trigger it, on what grounds, and within what clock?
4. Is the law officially published before it binds the public?
5. Can a user distinguish as-enacted text, official version, and current consolidation?
6. Are effective dates, phased commencement, and transition rules explicit?
7. Is there a narrow, visible, non-abusive correction path?

If the answer to three or more is “no”, the polity has not really designed the final stage by which bills become law; it has merely inherited one.
