# 339 — Scope-Tenure Defaults: Short Terms, Medium Cycles, Staggering, and No Open-Ended Office

**Cross-stack note:** use `306-scope-design-ideal-government-and-reference-bundles-guide.md` for the canonical route across the scope-design family. This memo is the scale-to-tenure / term-design front door; `14` / `176` explain the cross-scope logic, `285` gives the default bundle language, `327` handles the scale-sensitive institutional form, `328` handles the scale-sensitive legitimacy channel, `337` handles the scale-sensitive decision-rule / threshold-choice choice, `338` handles the scale-sensitive selection / office-filling choice, and `113` / `78` / `228` / `229` / `308` / `314` handle the deeper appointments / acting / board / agency-independence / continuity / formation substrate once the scale-fit tenure question has been identified.

**Purpose:** answer the design question that remains after scope assignment, bundle design, form choice, legitimacy choice, decision-rule choice, and selection choice: **how long should offices at this scale usually last, how renewable should they be, and when is staggering safer than synchronized turnover?**

**Why this memo exists:** `14`, `176`, `285`, and `327-338` explain what each scale owns, what shape it should take, how it should be legitimated, how it should decide, and how offices should be filled. But the archive still needed one compact memo that says **scale should also change tenure design** — short or medium terms, renewable or non-renewable appointments, staggered or synchronized turnover, and different anti-entrenchment rules for chambers, singular executives, delegates, and bounded expert offices.

**From-below:** this memo is a guardrail against tenure abuse in both directions. It helps stop tiny local roles from hardening into mini-fiefdoms through endless incumbency, and it helps stop complex institutions from burning away memory and continuity through prestige churn, synchronized replacement, or endless acting appointments.

**Evidence anchors:** the Council of Europe still anchors local self-government in councils or assemblies freely elected by secret ballot rather than perpetual officeholding [BIB-COE-LOCALSELF-ART3-2026]; the Venice Commission’s 2019 report still says term limits for members of parliament are not generally recommended, and if introduced they should usually allow at least two consecutive terms and gradual renewal for continuity, while term limits appear more justifiable for directly elected sub-national and local executives than for indirectly elected ones [BIB-VENICE-TERM-LIMITS-LOCAL-2019]; the Venice Commission’s 2018 report still treats presidential term limits as a normal democratic safeguard and rejects any freestanding human right to re-election [BIB-VENICE-TERM-LIMITS-PRESIDENTS-2018]; EU treaty law still uses five-year cycles both for the directly elected European Parliament and for the Commission [BIB-EURLEX-TEU-ART14-2026] [BIB-EURLEX-TEU-ART17-TERM-2026]; the UN’s own Secretary-General materials still describe a five-year term pattern, with no Secretary-General so far serving more than two terms even though the Charter itself does not hard-code a cap [BIB-UN-SG-ROLE-2026] [BIB-UN-GA-RES-11I-SG-TERM-1946]; and WHO basic rules still give the Director-General and Regional Directors five-year terms renewable once only, which is a useful current public-law anchor for bounded expert-secretariat leadership [BIB-WHO-BASICDOCS-DG-TERM-2021].

---

## Core claim

Every scale needs a **default tenure design**.

Not because one term length solves every problem, but because institutions become brittle or captured when they cannot say how long authority lasts, how renewal works, whether several offices should turn over together or in sequence, and when incumbency should be interrupted to prevent office from becoming personal property.

As scale changes, the fitting tenure pattern also changes:
- the smaller and more person-near the office, the more tenure can lean on **short cycles, rotation, and easy replacement**;
- the more general-purpose and publicly political the office, the more tenure should be **fixed, legible, and long enough to govern a real cycle**;
- the more collegial, technical, or continuity-sensitive the body, the more tenure should lean on **staggering rather than synchronized churn**;
- the more singular and mandate-concentrating the executive office, the more tenure should lean on **anti-entrenchment discipline and clearer outer bounds**;
- and the more expert, adjudicative, review, or secretariat-like the office, the more tenure should be **bounded, reviewable, and insulated from arbitrary midstream replacement**.

So the archive’s rule is:
**short and rotating where offices are tiny and local; medium fixed cycles where democratic authorization must stay live; staggered continuity where whole bodies must not turn over at once; stronger anti-entrenchment for singular executives than for representative chambers; and bounded renewable terms for appointed expert and secretariat offices.**

---

## The anti-entrenchment / anti-churn test

Before creating or reforming any office, ask seven questions:

1. **Concentration test:** is this a singular office that can accumulate personal mandate or patronage, or a collegiate seat inside a larger body?
2. **Continuity test:** would replacing everyone at once create dangerous memory loss, implementation breaks, or capture opportunities for the appointing coalition?
3. **Learning-curve test:** how long does it take an officeholder to become competent enough for the office to stop being theater?
4. **Reauthorization test:** how often should the relevant public be able to revisit the mandate under normal conditions?
5. **Stacking test:** could one election, cabinet, or majority capture the whole institution simply by replacing everyone quickly?
6. **Entrenchment test:** does indefinite re-eligibility risk personalism, dynasty, or machine lock-in?
7. **Acting-overhang test:** if an office falls vacant, can the system prevent “temporary” officeholders from staying indefinitely without full authorization?

If the answer to several of these is “we have not specified it” or “the same term will do for every office,” the design is likely suffering from **incumbency drift, synchronized churn, or open-ended office**.

## Default tenure modes by scope

### 0. Household / association / cooperative

**Default tenure mode:** **short, reviewable terms with easy rotation; no office should quietly become permanent property.**

Use:
- short cycles tied to a meeting, budget, or seasonal work rhythm,
- renewable terms for treasurer / secretary / steward roles where continuity matters,
- and informal or formal rest norms where a chair or convener role is otherwise becoming a status throne.

This scale should usually optimize for participation, trust, and easy correction rather than career incumbency.

### 1. Micro-local (building / block / village / campus / frontline institution)

**Default tenure mode:** **short terms, usually renewable but interruptible, with strong rotation pressure for tiny steward roles.**

Use:
- one-year or similarly short cycles as the default shape,
- non-consecutive service or rotation where the role is mostly convening and maintenance,
- and immediate replacement / recall paths where the role remains close to everyday shared life.

The main danger here is not instability from short terms but the quiet emergence of the person who is “always in charge.”

### 2. Municipal (city / town)

**Default tenure mode:** **medium fixed electoral cycles for representative offices, indefinite merit tenure for administration, and staggered terms for boards that must survive elections.**

Use:
- representative terms long enough to own a budget cycle, service changes, and medium-horizon local capital choices,
- mayoral or executive terms matched to the actual city constitution rather than copied for prestige,
- indefinite professional tenure with discipline and review for managers, clerks, inspectors, and service operators,
- and staggered terms for planning boards, utilities boards, election bodies, and other bodies that should not fully turn over with one local wave.

The municipal anti-pattern is electing or replacing everything at once and then pretending continuity failures are nobody’s responsibility.

### 2.5 Intermediate local administration (county / prefecture / province-lite backstop)

**Default tenure mode:** **medium terms synchronized enough to coordinate municipalities, but staggered where the unit carries continuity-critical shared administration.**

Use:
- terms tied to the regional or municipal coordination cycle where the body mainly aggregates member localities,
- delegated tenure that expires with the parent office where delegates sit by virtue of another elected mandate,
- and staggered appointed terms for technical backstop boards or review bodies.

Because this layer often exists to stabilize seams, its tenure design should resist both local political whiplash and invisible permanence.

### 3. Metropolitan / corridor / watershed / functional region

**Default tenure mode:** **delegated tenure linked to originating offices, plus staggered terms for compact-critical technical or oversight seats.**

Use:
- delegated seats that track the member-jurisdiction office from which they derive legitimacy,
- compact terms long enough to sustain network planning and capital sequencing,
- and staggered renewal for regulatory, technical, or multi-municipal oversight members so one coalition cannot recompose the whole metro operator at once.

This scale usually needs continuity more than personal mandate. The danger is synchronized bargaining churn, not insufficient electoral drama.

### 4. Regional (state / province / land)

**Default tenure mode:** **medium fixed terms for representative institutions, confidence-linked tenure for parliamentary executives, and longer or staggered terms for review / oversight offices.**

Use:
- representative terms long enough to legislate, budget, and plan territorially,
- no artificial term caps for parliamentary chief executives whose tenure is already bounded by confidence, unless a particular constitutional context strongly justifies them,
- more plausible outer limits for directly elected regional executives where mandate concentration is high,
- and longer, non-coincident, or staggered terms for auditors, constitutional review bodies, electoral commissions, and similar institutions.

This scale often mixes politics and backstop administration, so it needs both live democratic renewal and institutional memory.

### 5. National

**Default tenure mode:** **medium fixed legislative cycles, confidence-bounded parliamentary tenure where applicable, stronger anti-entrenchment for singular executives, and long staggered terms for independence-sensitive bodies.**

Use:
- regular medium electoral cycles for the representative chamber,
- no default term caps for members of parliament or confidence-dependent prime ministers simply because they may be re-elected,
- firmer outer limits for directly elected presidents or similar singular executive offices,
- and longer, staggered, or partially non-renewable terms for judges, regulators, auditors, and guardians of the constitutional or integrity stack.

The archive’s national rule follows the main comparative logic: **term limits are a sharper remedy for concentrated executive office than for representative chambers.**

### 6. Supranational (treaty union / shared-rule space)

**Default tenure mode:** **fixed medium cycles for directly representative institutions, bounded multi-year terms for commissions or secretariats, and staggered tenure for courts, regulators, and review bodies.**

Use:
- fixed cycles for directly elected chambers,
- commission / college terms long enough to implement across a multi-year policy and budget horizon,
- delegate tenure for member-state representatives tied to domestic office or national appointment rules,
- and staggered or partially renewed terms for adjudicative and specialist institutions so they cannot be swept by one political moment.

This layer usually needs predictable cycles more than dramatic recall. Its core problem is institutional synchronization across several publics, not permanent campaign politics.

### 7. Global (planetary / treaty-complex level)

**Default tenure mode:** **bounded multi-year terms for secretariat and panel leadership, delegation tenure tied to member-state or treaty cycles, and visible non-permanent composition where legitimacy would otherwise freeze.**

Use:
- fixed multi-year terms for secretariat heads,
- one-renewal or similarly bounded renewal norms for leadership of global bodies where personal entrenchment would corrode legitimacy,
- membership rotation or staggered turnover for committees, expert panels, and review bodies,
- and delegate mandates that are legible about whether they reflect a state office, a treaty cycle, or a conference-specific appointment.

At this scale the danger is not only authoritarian entrenchment, but also elite permanence hidden inside diplomatic or expert continuity.

### Non-territorial & indigenous polities (cross-cutting)

**Default tenure mode:** **terms should follow the polity’s own legitimate traditions, but duration, renewal, and removal must still be legible where public power affects outsiders, rights, or public resources.**

Use:
- traditional or customary tenure where it remains reviewable and legible,
- explicit publication of how office begins, ends, renews, and transfers where the polity exercises delegated public power,
- and stronger continuity / anti-entrenchment safeguards where coercion, custody, major funds, or cross-government compacts are involved.

Do not flatten every legitimate polity into one electoral clock. But do require that outsiders can tell when authority ends, who may renew it, and how challenge or replacement works.

## Cross-scope defaults worth carrying everywhere

1. **Selection and tenure must be designed together.** A directly elected executive, a delegated board seat, and an appointed regulator do not need the same renewal logic.
2. **Representative chambers need voter choice and continuity more than strict caps.** Hard term limits are usually a worse default for legislators than for singular executives.
3. **Singular executives need thicker anti-entrenchment rails.** The more a single office can accumulate mandate, patronage, and symbolic ownership, the more bounded the tenure should be.
4. **Continuity-sensitive collegial bodies should usually be staggered.** Whole-body replacement is often an avoidable capture vector.
5. **Acting authority must be harder-bounded than ordinary tenure.** Temporary office should not become a shadow route to indefinite rule.
6. **Long expert or oversight terms need removal discipline and publication, not mystique.** Long tenure is justifiable only where independence or continuity really requires it.
7. **The farther the institution is from ordinary people, the stronger the case for explicit renewal and anti-open-ended-office rules.**
