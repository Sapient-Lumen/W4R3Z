# Watershed & Water Governance Compacts (River‑Basin Institutions as a Cross‑Scope Pattern)

**Person served:** someone whose life depends on a river basin / aquifer system that crosses municipal and regional lines, and who needs **allocations + quality + drought response** to be legible, contestable, and durable.

**Thesis:** water is the archetypal **functional region** problem: hydrology ignores political borders. “Ideal” governance here is **polycentric** (local operations, basin‑level budgets, national backstops, cross‑border treaties) with **compact‑grade clauses**: clear budgets, monitoring, dispute channels, enforcement, and upgrade paths.

## 1) Scope fit

Water governance is rarely owned by a single tier:
- **Local:** distribution, leakage, customer service standards, local pollution enforcement.
- **Regional / basin:** withdrawals, ecological flows, reservoir operations, drought allocation, basin‑wide pollution loads.
- **National:** rights framework, environmental baselines, financing, courts, interstate arbitration.
- **Transboundary / global:** treaty cooperation obligations, notification/monitoring coordination, emergency assistance.

The Water Framework Directive’s river‑basin planning logic and “no deterioration / good status” target illustrate the **basin‑as‑unit** approach. [BIB-EU-WFD-HUB]

## 2) Compact clauses (minimum viable basin constitution)

A basin compact SHOULD include:

### A) Water budget & allocation rules
- **Hydrological budget**: surface + groundwater (including recharge) with explicit uncertainty bands.
- **Priority rules** under scarcity (human needs floors, ecological minimums, then proportional cuts).
- **Ecological flow floor**: explicit, not a leftover.
- **Trading / transfers** only with anti‑harm constraints (no third‑party harm; no ecosystem collapse).

### B) Monitoring, data, and “receipts”
- Metering/measurement standards, shared registries for withdrawals and discharge permits.
- Independent monitoring with publication defaults (data interfaces: `115-...`, `70-...`).
- **Receipts**: allocations and exceptions must be traceable to rules and triggers.

OECD’s water governance principles emphasize clarity of roles, capacity, integrity, transparency, and stakeholder engagement—useful as a checklist for the basin authority’s “operating system.” [BIB-OECD-WATER-PRINCIPLES-2015]

### C) Drought / flood triggers and adaptive management
- Pre‑agreed **trigger rubrics** (reservoir levels, snowpack indices, groundwater drawdown, salinity thresholds).
- A **staged response ladder**: voluntary conservation → mandatory rationing → emergency reallocation.
- **Revision discipline**: learning loops with revision logs (`03-...`, `104-...`, `102-...`).

### D) Enforcement and anti‑capture
- A small number of enforceable “hard rails” (minimum flows; illegal extraction penalties; pollution caps).
- Conflict‑of‑interest and revolving‑door discipline (`79-...`, `163-...`).
- Procurement and contracting integrity for major works (`110-...`, `162-...`).

### E) Dispute resolution & escalation
- Internal adjudication + external appeal path.
- Multi‑party disputes go to an agreed forum (court / arbitration panel).
- Explicit **standing** rules for downstream communities and ecosystems (guardianship patterns: `11-...`, `08-...`).

### F) Finance
- Stable operating finance (fees/levies), plus investment finance for resilience.
- Explicit equalization / affordability protections (household lifelines; cross‑district transfers) (`18-...`, `169-...`).

## 3) Transboundary cooperation (international basins)

For rivers/aquifers crossing national borders, compacts should align with widely used international water law principles:
- **Equitable and reasonable utilization / participation** and related factor balancing. [BIB-UN-WATERCOURSES-1997]
- **No significant transboundary harm** as an operational constraint alongside equitable use. [BIB-UN-WATERCOURSES-1997]
- **Cooperation, joint monitoring, and joint bodies** (river commissions) as durable institutions. [BIB-UNECE-WATERCONV-TEXT] [BIB-UNECE-JOINTBODIES-2018]

Treaty design in practice often fails at **monitoring + enforcement + finance**; treat these as first‑class clauses, not annexes.

## 4) Implementation template (what to build)

### Basin authority “minimum stack”
1. **Basin register**: rights/permits, meters, withdrawals, discharges.
2. **Budget docket**: annual water budget + ecological flow decision, published.
3. **Trigger docket**: drought/flood staging + public dashboards.
4. **Exception ledger**: any derogation with sunset, reason codes, and review (`45-...`, `52-...`).
5. **Grievance & remedy interface**: fast path for downstream harms (`08-...`, `76-...`).

### Red flags
- “Plans” without binding allocations or enforceable monitoring.
- Upstream domination without downstream standing.
- Groundwater excluded (the classic failure mode).
- Data opacity (metering gaps; private telemetry without public audit hooks).
