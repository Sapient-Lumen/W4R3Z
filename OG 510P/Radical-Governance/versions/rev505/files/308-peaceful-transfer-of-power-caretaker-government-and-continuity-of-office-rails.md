# 308 — Peaceful Transfer of Power, Caretaker Government, and Continuity of Office Rails

**Purpose:** make the transfer-of-power seam auditable so elections, resignations, no-confidence events, dissolutions, coalition negotiations, incapacity, death, or temporary vacancy do not create either a power vacuum or a last-minute capture sprint.

**Why this memo exists:** the archive already covers legitimacy (`21`, `307`), election administration (`56`), appointment / acting / removal integrity (`113`, `228`), constitutional maintenance (`171`, `288`), and emergency powers (`165`, `186`, `286`). What it still needed was a compact operating memo for the seam between those families: **who may keep governing, on what terms, with what limits, and with what public handover artifacts while authority is changing hands**.

**Evidence anchors:** International IDEA’s primer on government formation and removal shows that constitutions need explicit rules for forming and dismissing governments and often include caretaker provisions to avoid a vacuum [BIB-IDEA-GOV-FORMATION-PRIMER]. New Zealand’s Cabinet Manual and 2023 caretaker circular make the convention operational by distinguishing normal authority from caretaker restraint and by requiring special handling of significant decisions during uncertainty [BIB-NZ-CABINET-MANUAL-2023] [BIB-NZ-CARETAKER-2023]. New Zealand also publishes separate rules for what information may be provided during coalition talks and to an incoming government, which is a useful model for handover discipline [BIB-NZ-TRANSITIONS-INFO-2023]. Canada’s 2025 caretaker guidance explicitly narrows government business during the writ period and sets rules for communications, contracts, grants, and appointments [BIB-CANADA-CARETAKER-2025]. The UK Cabinet Office’s civil-service election guidance, updated on 2 March 2026, remains a clean reference for pre-election restraint and impartial administrative conduct [BIB-UK-CIVILSERVICE-ELECTION-GUIDANCE-2026]. OECD’s 2024 centre-of-government compendium also treats management of transitions between outgoing and incoming governments as a core CoG practice rather than an improvised side task [BIB-OECD-COG-COMPLEXITY-2024].

---

## Core claim

A legitimate election is **not enough**. An ideal government also needs a legitimate **handover protocol**.

A transfer-of-power seam fails in four predictable ways:
1. **Power vacuum:** nobody can clearly authorize urgent routine action.
2. **Caretaker abuse:** an outgoing authority uses the transition window to lock in appointments, contracts, pardons, records changes, or policy facts on the ground.
3. **Administrative sabotage or amnesia:** the incoming authority receives partial, slanted, or non-joinable information.
4. **Service cliff:** ordinary people lose access to essential services because political transition and administrative continuity were not separated.

The design target is simple:
- **no vacuum,**
- **no midnight capture,**
- **no secret handover,**
- **no service interruption,**
- **no silent acting-authority drift.**

## When to use this memo

Use `308` when the question is:
- how peaceful transfer of power should work after an election,
- what a caretaker government may or may not do,
- how long acting authority may persist,
- how incoming officials should receive briefings and records,
- how to handle resignation, incapacity, death, coalition deadlock, or no-confidence without improvisation,
- or how to separate **political turnover** from **service continuity**.

This memo is the **transfer / caretaker / continuity seam**. If the issue shifts from the substance of caretaker restraint to how those conventions should be published, classified, endorsed, and maintained in a cabinet manual or similar public guidance, route to `315-constitutional-conventions-cabinet-manuals-and-codified-restraint-rails.md`.

## Minimal public artifact set

Keep the interface small and replayable.

### 1. Transition Trigger Notice (`TTN-*`)
Publish immediately when a trigger occurs:
- election called,
- certified result changes governing authority,
- no-confidence vote,
- resignation / death / incapacity of a chief office-holder,
- dissolution, coalition deadlock, or constitutional vacancy.

The notice states:
- legal trigger,
- effective dates,
- who currently holds interim authority,
- whether caretaker rules are active,
- what continuity floor remains in force,
- and where subsequent transition records will be published.

### 2. Caretaker Scope Notice (`CSN-*`)
If caretaker mode is active, publish:
- what decisions remain routine and permitted,
- what decisions require deferral,
- what decisions require cross-party consultation or a necessity finding,
- what communications restrictions apply,
- what appointment / contracting / grant restrictions apply,
- and which official can issue an exception receipt.

### 3. Reserved Decision Log (`RDL-*`)
A public list of decisions that were:
- deferred to the incoming authority,
- taken during caretaker mode because delay would cause material harm,
- or escalated because legality / urgency was contested.

Every non-routine caretaker decision must emit a reasoned receipt with:
- necessity claim,
- harm of waiting,
- alternatives considered,
- consultation performed,
- review lane,
- and sunset / revisit trigger where relevant.

### 4. Acting Authority & Vacancy Receipt (`AVR-*`)
Whenever a constitutional or high-discretion office is temporarily filled, publish:
- why the office is vacant,
- who is acting,
- the source of acting authority,
- start date,
- powers allowed / withheld,
- term limit or expiry condition,
- incompatibilities / recusal constraints,
- and the path to permanent replacement.

### 5. Incoming Government Briefing Pack (`IGB-*`)
The administrative state must publish a handover index for the incoming authority with:
- statutory mandates,
- time-critical decisions pending in the next 30 / 90 / 180 days,
- fiscal commitments and contingent liabilities,
- ongoing litigation and treaty / compact obligations,
- integrity, security, safety, and outage risks,
- unresolved redress / oversight deadlines,
- and records pointers rather than narrative spin.

### 6. Continuity Floor Notice (`CFN-*`)
A person-facing service notice stating which essential functions continue without interruption, how to access them, and what fallback path exists if leadership turnover disrupts a channel.

### 7. Transfer Completion Receipt (`TCR-*`)
When the transfer is complete, publish:
- who now holds authority,
- what caretaker restrictions ended,
- what reserved decisions remain open,
- whether any records or systems were found incomplete,
- and the post-transition audit window.

## Caretaker rules (the compact version)

### A. Routine administration continues
Caretaker mode is **not** state paralysis. Ordinary service delivery, emergency response inside existing authority, payroll, benefits, maintenance, and legally required administration continue.

### B. Major discretionary change is constrained
During caretaker periods, outgoing authorities should not normally:
- make major irreversible policy changes,
- sign unusual long-term contracts,
- make partisan or difficult-to-reverse appointments,
- alter baseline data / records / publication practices,
- restructure agencies,
- or grant exceptional favors, immunities, or irreversible disposals.

### C. Exceptions require a higher bar
A caretaker decision outside routine administration needs a public necessity case:
- delay would cause serious public harm, legal breach, or safety failure,
- the step is proportionate,
- the least-locking option was chosen,
- consultation was attempted where constitutionally possible,
- and the decision is reviewable after transfer.

### D. Records freeze with controlled continuation
During transfer, records, audit logs, and release schedules must not silently disappear. Any emergency redaction, access narrowing, or data migration change needs a receipted authority chain and later review.

### E. Acting authority is bounded
No office should remain in “temporary” hands indefinitely. Acting roles need:
- clear legal source,
- limited duration,
- limited mission,
- non-renewal or explicit renewal receipts,
- and public visibility of vacancy duration.

## Design rules for ideal governments

### 1. Separate political mandate from service continuity
People should not lose food assistance, health access, permits already in process, emergency help, or appeal rights because coalition talks or succession politics are unresolved.

### 2. Publish the transition clock
The archive already treats time bounds as a governance interface. Transfer-of-power seams need the same treatment: trigger date, certification date, caretaker start, expected handover date, and review checkpoints.

### 3. Keep incoming briefings factual and joinable
The handover pack should be mostly pointers into stable registers (`31`, `34`, `35`, `39`, `47`, `48`, `55`) rather than an un-auditable political memo.

### 4. Treat vacancy and acting abuse as integrity incidents
An acting appointment can be a covert way to avoid scrutiny, quorum rules, or confirmation. Prolonged acting status, repeated renewals, or vacancy manipulation should trigger automatic review.

### 5. Distinguish urgent necessity from partisan acceleration
The danger period is when outgoing authorities claim urgency for choices that are really ideological, patronage-based, or lock-in oriented. The default answer should be **defer unless necessity is receipted**.

### 6. Audit the transfer after completion
A post-transfer integrity review should check:
- whether caretaker limits were respected,
- whether any records went missing,
- whether appointments / contracts / grants spiked abnormally,
- whether service performance dipped,
- and whether incoming briefings were materially complete.

## Confusion boundaries

### `308` vs `56`
- `56` is about **running elections**.
- `308` is about **handing over authority after or around them**.

### `308` vs `309`
- `309` designs **how representative chambers, committee systems, opposition rights, and confidence rules should work in steady state**.
- `308` governs **what happens once authority is actually changing hands and caretaker / continuity discipline is required**.

### `308` vs `310`
- `308` governs **transfer, caretaker restraint, acting authority, and continuity while power is changing hands**.
- `310` governs **cabinet formation after confidence exists, coalition terms, ministerial portfolios, and centre-of-government coordination once the executive is in office**.

### `308` vs `313`
- `308` governs **the transfer seam itself**.
- `313` governs **the standing design of the head-of-state office that may become central during death, resignation, incapacity, regency, or vacancy**.

### `308` vs `113` / `228`
- `113` and `228` govern **appointment and removal integrity generally**.
- `308` governs the **special risks created when those powers are exercised during a transfer seam**.

### `308` vs `171` / `206`
- `171` and `206` govern **constitutional change and maintenance**.
- `308` governs **continuity and caretaker practice while authority is in flux even without formal constitutional amendment**.

### `308` vs `165` / `186`
- emergency memos answer **how extraordinary powers are lawfully activated and exited**;
- `308` answers **how ordinary democratic continuity is preserved when leadership changes or a vacancy opens**.

A real crisis may require both families.

## Minimum tests

1. Can an ordinary person tell **who currently has authority** and under what limits?
2. Is there a public line between **routine administration** and **major discretionary change** during caretaker periods?
3. Do acting roles have clear expiry, limited powers, and public receipts?
4. Can an incoming authority identify urgent liabilities and deadlines without depending on insider loyalty?
5. Can the public audit whether contracts, appointments, pardons, or records changes spiked during transition?
6. Do essential services continue even if coalition talks, certification disputes, or succession fights drag on?

If the answer to any of these is “no”, the transfer-of-power seam is not yet democratically safe.
