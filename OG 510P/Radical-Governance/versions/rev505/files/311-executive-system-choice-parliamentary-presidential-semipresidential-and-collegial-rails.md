# 311 — Executive-System Choice: Parliamentary, Presidential, Semi-Presidential, and Collegial Rails

**Purpose:** make the choice of executive system explicit and auditable so a polity does not drift into personalism, deadlock, or hidden reserve-power politics by accident.

**Why this memo exists:** the archive already covers legitimacy architecture (`21` / `307`), electoral-system choice (`147`), representative chambers and confidence rules (`309`), transfer-of-power seams (`308`), cabinet / portfolio / centre-of-government design (`310`), state-capacity neighbors (`297`), and broad national / supranational scope design (`40` / `50`). What it still lacked was one compact seam memo for the executive-form choice itself: **when to use parliamentary, presidential, semi-presidential, non-executive-presidential, or collegial designs; how head-of-state and head-of-government roles should relate; and how dismissal, dissolution, reserve powers, and emergency authority should be bounded so the rest of the stack does not quietly collapse into one person or one office**.

**Evidence anchors:** International IDEA’s primer on government formation and removal is still the clearest compact reminder that these rules determine who gains, holds, and loses executive power, and that the stakes are therefore high [BIB-IDEA-GOV-FORMATION-PRIMER]. International IDEA’s primer on electing presidents is equally useful because it stresses that presidential-election rules shape long-run access to power and that **form should follow function** [BIB-IDEA-ELECTING-PRESIDENTS-2019]. Its primer on non-executive presidents is a strong design aid for parliamentary republics because it explains both the continuity advantages of separating head of state from head of government and the risks created by unclear discretionary powers [BIB-IDEA-NONEXEC-PRES-2017]. Its 2022 primer on removal of presidents is the right compact reference for the problem of interrupting a popular mandate without normalizing coups-by-procedure [BIB-IDEA-REMOVAL-PRES-2022]. OECD’s 2024 centre-of-government compendium is also useful here: clear roles, mandates, and spheres of influence remain essential if the executive form is not to blur political and administrative responsibility [BIB-OECD-COG-STEERING-2024]. For the rare collegial-executive path, Switzerland remains a bounded contemporary reference: seven equal members, a rotating presidency, and collective defence of joint decisions rather than a singular executive mandate [BIB-CH-FEDERAL-COUNCIL-COLLEGIAL-2025].

---

## Core claim

Executive form is not constitutional branding.

It determines:
- who may claim a democratic mandate,
- who can dismiss whom,
- whether executive crisis is resolved inside parliament or through plebiscitary confrontation,
- whether the state has a non-partisan continuity node,
- and whether emergency authority quietly becomes a substitute constitution.

The archive’s answer is therefore not “parliamentary good” or “presidential good”. It is:
**choose the form that matches the job, publish the responsibility map, and narrow every escape hatch that would let constitutional ambiguity become personalized power.**

## When to use this memo

Use `311` when the question is:
- whether a polity should be parliamentary, presidential, semi-presidential, or collegial,
- whether head of state and head of government should be separated,
- whether a president should be executive, non-executive, or absent,
- how dissolution, impeachment, incapacity, and constructive no-confidence should relate,
- how directly elected executives should be constrained,
- or how to avoid dual-executive ambiguity in national or supranational design.

This memo is the **executive-form / head-of-state–head-of-government seam**. If the issue shifts from choosing an executive form to how its operating norms should be published in a cabinet manual or equivalent constitutional guidance, route to `315-constitutional-conventions-cabinet-manuals-and-codified-restraint-rails.md`. ## The smallest good architecture

### 1. Executive-form note (`EFN-*`)
For the polity as a whole, publish or entrench:
- whether it is parliamentary, presidential, semi-presidential, or collegial,
- whether head of state and head of government are the same office,
- how each office is selected,
- term length and re-eligibility,
- and the powers that are ordinary, reserve, ceremonial, or emergency-only.

### 2. Responsibility matrix (`ERM-*`)
Make the following explicit:
- who appoints the head of government,
- who appoints and dismisses ministers,
- who can dismiss a government,
- who can dissolve the legislature,
- who can refuse signature, veto legislation, issue decrees, or return bills,
- and which acts require countersignature, legislative approval, judicial review, or publication.

### 3. Continuity and crisis note (`CCN-*`)
Publish:
- acting succession,
- incapacity procedures,
- caretaker limits and the handoff to `308`,
- emergency and war-power interfaces to `186` / `222`,
- and which offices remain politically neutral when government formation is unresolved.

### 4. Cabinet / CoG interface note (`ECI-*`)
The executive form must connect cleanly to:
- cabinet formation,
- portfolio maps,
- collective responsibility,
- ministerial direction,
- the centre of government,
- and the boundary between political executives and the professional state.

That operating layer belongs with `310`, but the executive form must make it possible.

### 5. Removal and dissolution protocol (`RDP-*`)
Do not leave crisis to improvisation.
Publish or entrench:
- no-confidence rule,
- constructive no-confidence rule where used,
- impeachment / removal thresholds,
- incapacity findings,
- dissolution triggers and limits,
- and the role of courts or other independent bodies where constitutional misconduct is alleged.

## Design rules

### A. Form should follow function
Start by deciding what democratic task the executive must perform.

Possible aims include:
- **responsible government** that can be continuously dismissed by a legislature,
- **a separately elected chief executive** with a distinct national mandate,
- **a non-partisan continuity node** above day-to-day party conflict,
- **a power-sharing executive** in a deeply plural or consociational setting,
- or **an anti-personalist collegial form** where the design goal is to disperse executive symbolism and authority.

Choose the office structure that fits that task. Do not begin with prestige, imitation, or leader preference.

### B. Parliamentary systems are strongest when the goal is responsible government
Use parliamentary design when the main objective is to keep executive authority answerable to a representative chamber that can replace it without regime rupture.

This is especially useful when:
- coalition formation is likely,
- executive–legislative cooperation must remain routine,
- confidence and budgeting need to stay inside parliament,
- and a non-executive head of state can provide continuity without becoming a rival mandate.

But parliamentary systems still need discipline: unstable investiture rules, ornamental opposition rights, or ambiguous dissolution powers can turn “responsible government” into recurring crisis. That is why `309` and `310` remain separate neighbors.

### C. Presidential systems need real counterweights, not just a direct election
Use a presidential design only when a separately elected chief executive is intentional and the constitution can survive a divided mandate.

That means:
- fixed terms with clearly limited exceptions,
- a legislature that is not structurally subordinate,
- narrow and reviewable decree / emergency / appointment powers,
- clear budget and confirmation rules,
- and removal procedures that are demanding enough to prevent routine sabotage but usable enough to address genuine incapacity or grave abuse.

A presidential election is not a shortcut around party fragmentation or weak legislatures. It is a decision to create an independent executive mandate that must then be heavily bounded.

### D. If semi-presidential, prefer premier-presidential over president-parliamentary where the goal is shared power without executive domination
The crucial distinction is not the label “semi-presidential”; it is **who the prime minister is really answerable to**.

- In **premier-presidential** systems, executive leadership and policymaking are primarily in the hands of a prime minister and government chosen by, and responsible to, parliament.
- In **president-parliamentary** systems, the prime minister is in practice chosen mainly by, and responsible mainly to, the president.

If the goal is to preserve parliamentary responsibility while keeping a president with bounded political functions, premier-presidential design is usually the safer path. President-parliamentary design is far more likely to create a dominant presidency plus a subordinate cabinet with blurry accountability.

### E. Non-executive presidents should stay visibly non-executive
A parliamentary republic may benefit from a non-executive president who embodies continuity, constitutional authority, and political neutrality while the prime minister and cabinet carry policymaking responsibility.

But this only works if:
- reserve powers are explicit and narrow,
- conventions are either written down or backed by a strong constitutional culture,
- the mode of selection fits the desired neutrality,
- and direct election is not used casually for an office expected to remain above party competition.

If the design goal is an impartial constitutional guardian, indirect election or supermajority rules can be better aligned with that purpose than a direct mass campaign.

### F. Collegial executives are rare but useful where anti-personalism and structured power-sharing are the real goals
A collegial executive is not just “a cabinet with more members”. It is a system in which executive authority is intentionally vested in a group of equal-status officeholders, often with a rotating presidency and a strong norm of collective defence of decisions.

Use it only when:
- plural inclusion is a first-order design goal,
- no single-person executive mandate is required,
- cabinet-style joint decision-making is credible,
- and the political system can tolerate slower but more consensual executive formation.

Do not use it where the polity expects one leader to campaign, govern, and be clearly removable as an individual.

### G. Dissolution and removal rules must match the executive form
- **Parliamentary systems** need clear investiture, no-confidence, and dissolution rules, with constructive no-confidence where fragmentation risk is high.
- **Presidential systems** need a removal path for incapacity and grave wrongdoing that does not turn every impasse into pseudo-impeachment.
- **Semi-presidential systems** need both sides of the matrix stated clearly: who can dismiss the government, who can dissolve parliament, and what the president may do during deadlock.
- **Collegial systems** need vacancy, rotation, and intra-college deadlock rules.

Never let constitutions answer these questions with silence, folklore, or one-off crisis improvisation.

### H. Do not smuggle emergency and war powers into executive-system choice
A constitution sometimes pretends to be parliamentary, presidential, or collegial in normal times while quietly becoming a personalist executive order in crisis.

Do not allow that.

Emergency, derogation, and external-force powers should remain visibly bounded by:
- trigger conditions,
- publication duties,
- legislative ratification,
- judicial review where applicable,
- sunset and renewal discipline,
- and continuity rules that do not let crisis become a pretext for altering the executive form.

### I. Smaller and larger scales should resist prestige imitation
- Small municipalities do not need faux presidencies.
- Treaty unions do not need symbolic presidentialization by default.
- Global institutions rarely benefit from single-person executive personalization.

A constitution should not import the most theatrical model available when the real need is legible coordination, bounded authority, and continuity.

## Scope-sensitive defaults

### Micro-local / municipal
Default to:
- council + professional manager,
- or a directly elected mayor / executive with clear delegation, recall, acting, and publication duties.

Do **not** create a ceremonial-but-mischief-capable presidency at small scale.

### Metropolitan / regional
Use a directly elected executive only when the region genuinely functions as a polity with a clear mandate story.
Otherwise, council-executive or committee-government models with a strong professional administration often fit better.

### National
This is the main zone for `311`.
A national constitution must decide whether it wants:
- parliamentary responsibility,
- a separately elected executive mandate,
- a bounded dual executive,
- a non-executive continuity node,
- or, rarely, a collegial executive.

Each path must then be connected explicitly to `309`, `310`, `308`, `297`, `186`, and `222`.

### Supranational / treaty-union
Default against personalized presidentialization.
Most unions need:
- dual-legitimacy chambers or councils,
- a commission / secretariat / collegial executive or similarly bounded steering body,
- and transparent rules for who proposes, who coordinates, and who can be removed.

If a union ever creates a directly personalized executive mandate, the democratic thickness, competence clarity, and removal rules must be correspondingly stronger.

### Global
Prefer:
- secretariats,
- rotating presidencies or chairs,
- collegial steering bodies,
- and explicit limits on who can speak or act for the whole system.

Default against a “world president” model.

## Confusion boundaries

### `311` vs `147`
- `147` chooses **how representation is translated into seats or direct electoral outcomes**.
- `311` chooses **what kind of executive mandate and office structure those outcomes are meant to produce**.

### `311` vs `309`
- `309` governs **how representative chambers, committee systems, opposition rights, and confidence rules are built**.
- `311` governs **which executive form those chamber rules are supporting or constraining**.

### `311` vs `310`
- `310` governs **cabinet formation, coalition terms, portfolios, cabinet committees, collective responsibility, and centre-of-government coordination once the executive form is chosen**.
- `311` governs **the prior constitutional choice about what the executive actually is**.

### `311` vs `313`
- `311` chooses **the executive form overall**.
- `313` chooses **how a distinct head-of-state office should be typed, bounded, and succeeded within that form**.

### `311` vs `308`
- `308` governs **transition, caretaker restraint, acting authority, and continuity while power is changing hands**.
- `311` governs **the standing constitutional structure that determines who can hold that power in the first place**.

### `311` vs `40` / `50`
- `40` and `50` are the **broad national- and supranational-scope memos**.
- `311` is the **executive-form specialization** for one crucial institutional choice inside those scopes.

## Minimal evaluation questions

1. Does the executive form match the actual democratic task: responsible government, separate executive mandate, non-partisan continuity, bounded dual executive, or collegial power-sharing?
2. Can a reader tell who appoints, dismisses, replaces, or temporarily acts for whom?
3. Are dissolution, incapacity, removal, and caretaker rules explicit rather than folkloric?
4. If there is a directly elected president, are powers proportionate to the claimed mandate and counterweights visibly real?
5. If there is a non-executive president, are reserve powers narrow enough to prevent shadow government?
6. If the system is semi-presidential, is the prime minister genuinely responsible to parliament, or mainly to the president—and is that what the constitution actually intends?
7. Does the system degrade safely during deadlock, illness, resignation, emergency, or war without rewriting itself around one officeholder?
