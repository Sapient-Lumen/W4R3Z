# 343 — Scope-Participation Defaults: Open Meetings, Public Comment, Participatory Budgeting, Deliberative Panels, and No Spectator Governance

**Cross-stack note:** use `306-scope-design-ideal-government-and-reference-bundles-guide.md` for the canonical route across the scope-design family. This memo is the scale-to-participation / between-elections voice / no-spectator-governance front door; `14` / `176` explain the cross-scope logic, `21` / `180` handle broader legitimacy and deliberative-engine design, `103` gives the quick cards, `285` handles bundle composition, `328` handles dominant legitimacy channels, `336` handles who counts, `337` handles decision thresholds, `341` handles remedy lanes, and `41` / `88` / `143` / `159` / `307` handle deeper participation, sortition, and deliberative-system design once the participation question has been identified.

**Purpose:** answer the design question that remains after scope assignment, bundle design, legitimacy choice, constituency choice, and decision-rule choice: **how should people actually get into the process at this scale between elections and court cases — open meeting, public comment, participatory budgeting, standing advisory board, representative deliberative process, petition or initiative, treaty-observer lane, or some explicit mix?**

**Why this memo exists:** `14`, `176`, `285`, and `327-342` already explain what each scale owns, what shape it should take, who counts there, how it should decide, how offices are filled, how long authority lasts, what remedy path exists, and what map fits. But the archive still needed one compact memo that says **scale should also change participation mode** — when neighbor voice should be face-to-face and immediate, when municipalities should combine comment with co-design and participatory budgeting, when metro systems need cross-jurisdiction mini-publics rather than town-hall theater, when national and regional systems need hearings plus representative deliberation for long-horizon tradeoffs, and when supranational or global bodies should expose bounded public-input channels without pretending to be permanently plebiscitary world parliaments.

**From-below:** this memo is a guardrail against two opposite failures. It helps stop governments from treating people as spectators who only reappear on election day. And it helps stop institutions from staging fake participation exercises that generate comments, applause, or "engagement" without any disclosed effect on the decision.

**Evidence anchors:** the Council of Europe’s Additional Protocol on the right to participate in the affairs of a local authority still frames participation as a right and highlights facilitation, inclusiveness, petitioning, and openness in local decision-making [BIB-COE-LOCAL-PARTICIPATION-PROTOCOL-2009]; OECD’s 2022 citizen-participation guidelines still distinguish multiple methods — including open meetings, public consultations, participatory budgeting, civic monitoring, and representative deliberative processes — and stress that participation should happen only where there is real room to influence decisions [BIB-OECD-CITIZEN-PARTICIPATION-2022] [BIB-OECD-8WAYS-2021]; the European Commission still treats the European Citizens’ Initiative as a formal way for EU citizens to ask the Commission to propose new legislation, with a million signatures across at least seven member states needed for consideration [BIB-EU-ECI-2026]; and the UN’s HLPF materials still describe Major Groups and Other Stakeholders as the main channels through which broad participation is facilitated in UN sustainable-development activities while retaining the forum’s intergovernmental character [BIB-UN-HLPF-MGOS-2025].

## Core claim

Every scale needs a **default participation design**.

Not because every level needs the same amount of public input, but because authority becomes performative, brittle, or captured when it cannot say:
- who can show up or be recruited,
- when participation happens in the decision cycle,
- what kind of input is actually being asked for,
- how officials must respond, publish, or route that input,
- and when participation should be broad, representative, expert-supported, stakeholder-facing, or tightly bounded.

As scale changes, the fitting participation mode also changes:
- the nearer and smaller the scale, the more participation should be **open, face-to-face, and immediately legible**;
- the more service-heavy the scale, the more participation should become **comment, co-design, monitoring, and budgeting input close to operations**;
- the more compound the public and geography, the more participation should use **cross-jurisdiction assemblies, stakeholder boards, and representative mini-publics** instead of one-city theater;
- the thicker the lawmaking, redistribution, or identity stakes, the more participation should combine **hearings, petitions, committee-facing consultation, and occasional representative deliberation** rather than permanent plebiscite;
- the more treaty-based the authority, the more participation should become **formal initiative, consultation, observer, and scrutiny channels** with honest limits on who finally decides;
- and the more global the forum, the more participation should emphasize **visibility, accredited voice, affected-public documentation, and pressure on state-led decisions** rather than pretending a world referendum exists.

So the archive’s rule is:
**let people in early enough to matter, choose a participation mode that fits the scale, and publish how the input changed or failed to change the decision.**

## The no-spectator-governance test

Before adopting a participation design, ask seven questions:

1. **Decision-stage test:** does participation happen early enough to influence framing, options, or implementation rather than only ratify a finished choice?
2. **Audience-fit test:** are the right people being invited — open public, affected users, a representative mini-public, stakeholders, member governments, or some explicit combination?
3. **Method-fit test:** does the participation method fit the problem — quick meeting, consultation, budget vote, civic monitoring, petition, citizens’ assembly, or observer lane — rather than repeating the institution’s favorite ritual?
4. **Response test:** must the authority publish what it heard, what it changed, and what it refused?
5. **Access test:** are language, timing, childcare, disability access, travel burden, digital access, and reimbursement being handled rather than quietly filtering for the already organized?
6. **Escalation test:** if participation reveals harm, exclusion, or cross-boundary conflict, is there a route into formal remedy, committee scrutiny, or administrative action?
7. **Anti-theater test:** is the institution sharing power, or merely staging attention around a decision that has already been closed?

If several answers are "people can speak but nothing has to move," the design is drifting toward **spectator governance, consultation theater, or deliberation without consequence.**

## Default participation modes by scope

### 0. Household / association / cooperative

**Default participation mode:** **open member meeting first, with simple petition and agenda rights.**

Use:
- open meeting and agenda access for ordinary governance,
- direct member proposals for bylaw, budget, or common-property decisions,
- and representative deliberation only when the membership is too large for everyone to work directly.

### 1. Micro-local (building / block / village / campus / frontline institution)

**Default participation mode:** **open assembly, steward availability, visible agenda posting, and fast challenge rights.**

Use:
- face-to-face meetings or accessible hybrid equivalents,
- low-friction issue logging and repair requests,
- and short-loop voice before the problem hardens into formal complaint or exclusion.

### 2. Municipal (city / town)

**Default participation mode:** **public comment plus service co-design plus participatory budgeting where discretionary local spending is real.**

Use:
- public hearings and consultations on rules, plans, and capital decisions,
- participatory budgeting or neighborhood fund allocation for visible local priorities,
- service-user panels, civic monitoring, and co-design where delivery quality matters,
- and representative deliberative processes for dense local tradeoffs such as housing, street allocation, resilience, or siting.

### 2.5 Intermediate local administration (county / prefecture / province-lite backstop)

**Default participation mode:** **county-scale hearings, service-user advisory channels, and routed participation across fragmented municipal systems.**

Use:
- one intake for participation on shared services and countywide plans,
- targeted panels for health, welfare, emergency, and transport users,
- and representative mini-publics where several municipalities must share burden or scarce capacity.

### 3. Metropolitan / corridor / watershed / functional region

**Default participation mode:** **cross-jurisdiction participation — not one-core-city theater.**

Use:
- representative mini-publics or citizens’ panels drawn across the real metro / corridor footprint,
- stakeholder and user boards for transport, utilities, basin, or air-quality systems,
- and published participation crosswalks so residents, commuters, and regular users can see where to intervene.

### 4. Regional (state / province / land)

**Default participation mode:** **committee hearings, territorial consultation, and deliberative processes for long-horizon allocation questions.**

Use:
- hearings and comment on regional statutes, plans, and transfer rules,
- structured consultation with municipalities, indigenous governments, and sectoral bodies,
- and representative deliberative processes for constitutionalized regional tradeoffs such as land, water, infrastructure, and territorial equality.

### 5. National

**Default participation mode:** **public consultation and petition as routine; representative deliberation for long-horizon, high-conflict, or low-party-trust questions.**

Use:
- committee hearings, public consultations, petitions, and consultation portals for ordinary policy development,
- representative deliberative processes or citizens’ assemblies for constitutional, electoral, climate, bioethics, or other multi-cycle questions,
- and civic monitoring where national service systems or integrity risks need public verification.

### 6. Supranational (union / confederation / treaty bloc)

**Default participation mode:** **formal initiative, petition, consultation, and selected representative panels under dual-legitimacy conditions.**

Use:
- citizen initiative or petition channels where treaties provide them,
- open consultations and hearing rights on common rules,
- representative citizens’ panels or mini-publics where public input can inform union-wide agenda setting,
- and strong publication explaining what remains with member states and what is truly being decided jointly.

### 7. Global (planetary / treaty-complex level)

**Default participation mode:** **observer, stakeholder, affected-public, and expert-supported input around state-led decisions.**

Use:
- accredited civil-society, labor, indigenous, youth, science, and affected-community channels,
- written submissions, interventions, side events, and implementation monitoring,
- and public reporting or complaint lanes where treaty machinery allows.

Do **not** pretend this is a directly elected world legislature when it is not.

### Non-territorial & indigenous polities (cross-cutting)

**Default participation mode:** **membership-rooted participation with explicit interfaces for affected non-members and neighboring governments.**

Use:
- internal member forums for internal authority,
- compact-facing consultation where territorial spillovers or shared services are involved,
- and culturally appropriate deliberation that still publishes interfaces outsiders must navigate.

## Cross-scope defaults worth carrying everywhere

1. **Participation must be tied to a decision point.** Listening after closure is theater.
2. **Match openness to consequence.** Open meetings fit tiny and local questions; representative mini-publics fit high-conflict, long-horizon, or overload-prone questions.
3. **Publish the response.** Inputs should generate receipts, response memos, revised drafts, or explicit refusal reasons.
4. **Do not confuse participation with remedy.** Voice helps shape decisions; remedy corrects harm after or during administration.
5. **Do not confuse participation with legitimacy.** Elections, delegation, and treaty consent may authorize the institution even when participation is the ongoing input mode.
6. **Use compensation and access support where participation would otherwise skew wealthy, urban, expert, or already organized.**
7. **Where several publics are bound together, recruit across the real footprint.** Metro, regional, supranational, and global participation fail when the participating public is narrower than the governed one.
