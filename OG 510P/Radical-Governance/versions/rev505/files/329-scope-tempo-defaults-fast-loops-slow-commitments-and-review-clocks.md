# 329 — Scope-Tempo Defaults: Fast Loops, Slow Commitments, and Review Clocks

**Cross-stack note:** use `306-scope-design-ideal-government-and-reference-bundles-guide.md` for the canonical route across the scope-design family. This memo is the scale-to-tempo / time-horizon front door; `14` / `176` explain the cross-scope logic, `285` gives the default bundle language, `327` handles the scale-sensitive institutional-form default, `328` handles the scale-sensitive legitimacy-channel default, `330` handles the scale-sensitive epistemic / evidence-mode default, `331` handles the scale-sensitive accountability / review-mode default, `332` handles the scale-sensitive coercion / direct-force-holding default, `333` handles the scale-sensitive finance / revenue-model default, and `104` handles control-loop design in general, `108` handles service time budgets, `74` handles sunsetting and deprecation, `122` handles future-protection triggers, and `323` / `324` handle international review and treaty-lifecycle clocks.

**Purpose:** answer the design question that remains after scope assignment, bundle design, institutional form, and legitimacy choice: **what tempo should government at each scale usually run on?**

**Why this memo exists:** `14`, `176`, `285`, `327`, and `328` explain where functions belong, what the minimum bundle is, what shape government should take, and what legitimacy story it can honestly sustain. But they do not fully answer the next practical question: whether a given scale should default to immediate repair loops, annual bargains, medium-term implementation cycles, decade-spanning planning horizons, or verification-and-review clocks. The archive needed one compact memo that says **scale should change not only what government does, what it looks like, and how it is legitimated, but also how fast it should move and how long its commitments should run**.

**From-below:** this memo is a guardrail against two common harms at once: frantic short-termism that tramples people with constant churn, and distant slow-motion governance that leaves everyday harms waiting in line. It helps keep urgent relief fast, high-blast-radius commitments slow enough to inspect, and long-horizon obligations real enough to outlast one news cycle.

**Evidence anchors:** local self-government is strongest when nearby democratic bodies can actually act and be monitored as the first level of democracy [BIB-COE-LOCAL-SELF-GOV-2026]; community-based resilience depends on local and area-based capacities that can detect, adapt, and recover through repeated short loops rather than remote episodic interventions [BIB-UNDP-CBRB-2025]; deliberative processes are especially useful for long-term, value-laden trade-offs and are disproportionately institutionalised at local and regional levels, where they can outlast single electoral moments [BIB-OECD-GAAG-DELIB-2025]; regional development policy is explicitly a long-term, cross-sectoral, multi-level policy and OECD now frames strategic planning as a cycle spanning assessment/design, implementation/monitoring, and evaluation/learning [BIB-OECD-STRATEGIC-PLANNING-2026] [BIB-OECD-REGIONAL-IMPLEMENTATION-2025]; metropolitan governance is often justified by long-horizon infrastructure, land-use, and service externalities that cannot be governed as if each municipality were temporally self-contained [BIB-WB-METROGOV-2020]; current UN-Habitat strategy language still treats long-term urban and territorial planning as a core implementation backbone [BIB-UNHABITAT-SP-2026-2029-2025]; and current multilateral reform language still pushes global governance toward recurring review, stocktake, verification, and future-oriented duties rather than permanent emergency improvisation or one-shot summit theater [BIB-UN-PACT-FUTURE-2024] [BIB-UN-DFG].

---

## Core claim

The ideal government at each scope is **not** just a larger or smaller copy of the same political clock.

As scale changes, several temporal things change with it:
- how quickly harm is first seen,
- how reversible the decision usually is,
- how wide the spillovers travel,
- how long assets and commitments last,
- and how often ordinary democratic correction can realistically happen.

So the archive’s rule is:
**lower scopes should default to faster sensing, response, and repair loops; middle scopes should combine fast operations with slower planning and finance cycles; higher scopes should move slowly on irreversible commitments but run fixed review, verification, and emergency clocks so slowness does not become drift.**

---

## The anti-frenzy / anti-freeze test

Before choosing a decision cadence, ask five questions:

1. **Irreversibility test:** how hard is this decision to unwind once taken?
2. **Blast-radius test:** if it is wrong, how far do the harms travel?
3. **Detection test:** where will failure be noticed first — by neighbors, frontline staff, city operators, or distant reviewers?
4. **Asset-life test:** are we deciding today for a queue, a school year, a bridge, a watershed, or a generation?
5. **Correction-path test:** if this decision later proves wrong, is there a scheduled review path or only political improvisation?

If the cadence is faster than the system’s ability to inspect and reverse, it is frenzy.
If the cadence is slower than the harm people are enduring, it is freeze.

## Default temporal modes by scope

### 0. Household / association / cooperative

**Default tempo:** **immediate upkeep, short meetings, short mandates, easy exit**.

Use:
- quick recurring check-ins,
- near-term money visibility,
- and short review windows for delegated roles.

This scale should not pretend to govern through elaborate terms, distant strategy documents, or slow constitutional choreography. Its virtue is immediate correction and low-cost exit.

**Avoid:** letting minor offices become durable mini-regimes simply because no one set a rotation or review clock.

### 1. Micro-local (building / block / village / campus / frontline institution)

**Default tempo:** **continuous sensing and rapid repair**, with **frequent visible convening** and **very fast escalation upward when local capacity is exceeded**.

Why this fits:
- problem detection is close to the ground,
- maintenance and trust failures compound quickly when ignored,
- and legitimacy depends heavily on short feedback loops rather than long electoral narratives [BIB-UNDP-CBRB-2025] [BIB-COE-LOCAL-SELF-GOV-2026].

Use:
- recurring open meetings or issue reviews,
- short stewardship terms,
- immediate logging of small decisions,
- and fixed handoff clocks to municipal or emergency services.

**Avoid:** using micro-local bodies for slowly reviewed coercive decisions, or forcing neighborhood harms to wait for distant annual cycles.

### 2. Municipal (city / town / county-equivalent frontline state)

**Default tempo:** **fast daily service loops + annual budget cycles + multi-year maintenance and capital planning**.

Municipal government is the frontline operator. Its time pattern should therefore be layered:
- **day-to-day** for complaints, permits, inspections, and service recovery,
- **seasonal / annual** for staffing and budget bargaining,
- **multi-year** for asset maintenance, resilience, housing, and land-use adjustments,
- and **special long-horizon windows** for contested place-shaping decisions [BIB-COE-LOCAL-SELF-GOV-2026] [BIB-OECD-GAAG-DELIB-2025] [BIB-UNHABITAT-SP-2026-2029-2025].

Municipalities should not force every issue into the same annual political clock. A pothole, a permit queue, a bus network redesign, and a waterfront retreat plan do not belong on one tempo.

**Avoid:** annual-budget government swallowing maintenance cycles, climate adaptation, or housing supply decisions that need steadier review and less performative churn.

### 3. Metropolitan / corridor / watershed / functional region

**Default tempo:** **slow planning, steady operator cadence, and fixed compact-review windows**.

Metropolitan governance sits between fast municipal operations and slower regional/state planning. Its best temporal fit is usually:
- rapid operational coordination for network incidents,
- scheduled budget and performance reviews for shared services,
- and long-horizon planning for land use, transport, utilities, basins, and corridor assets [BIB-WB-METROGOV-2020] [BIB-OECD-URBAN-POLICY-2024].

Because metro systems cross boundaries, they should rely less on constant renegotiation and more on published review intervals, escalation triggers, and no-reset continuity rules.

**Avoid:** rebuilding metropolitan bargains from scratch every election or, conversely, letting shared authorities drift for decades without competence and burden-sharing review.

### 4. Regional (state / province / land / autonomous territory)

**Default tempo:** **electoral and legislative cycles at the surface, medium-term implementation cycles underneath, and long-horizon territorial review above them**.

At regional scale, the government is large enough to make durable commitments but still close enough to places that timing errors are visible. The archive default is therefore:
- ordinary electoral and legislative cycles,
- medium-term programme, equalisation, and implementation frameworks,
- recurring evaluation-and-learning windows,
- and long-horizon review for infrastructure, watershed, energy, industrial-transition, and spatial-equity decisions [BIB-OECD-STRATEGIC-PLANNING-2026] [BIB-OECD-REGIONAL-IMPLEMENTATION-2025] [BIB-OECD-RDP-TOOLKIT-2026].

Deliberative add-ons are especially useful here because regional trade-offs are often spatially uneven and politically tempting to defer.

**Avoid:** treating the region as either a permanently campaigning arena or a purely technocratic plan machine with no periodic democratic reopening.

### 5. National

**Default tempo:** **multiple stacked clocks rather than one master cycle**.

National government must hold together several incompatible rhythms at once:
- short democratic cycles for leadership change,
- annual budgets,
- medium-term fiscal and administrative planning,
- slower legal and institutional maintenance,
- and very long horizons for debt, ecological constraint, critical infrastructure, and future obligations [BIB-OECD-STRATEGIC-PLANNING-2026] [BIB-UN-DFG].

The archive default is therefore not “go fast” or “go slow” but **separate the clocks**:
- keep relief, administration, and correction fast,
- keep law and constitutional change slower and more inspectable,
- and attach long-horizon review triggers where a normal electoral cycle will predictably underweight future harm.

**Avoid:** pretending a single election victory resets every long-lived obligation, or pretending long-term plans may float free of contestable democratic review.

### 6. Supranational / treaty union / regional court space

**Default tempo:** **slow conferral, scheduled review, and disciplined implementation windows**.

Supranational governance usually cannot rely on continuous high-trust democratic correction the way local or national politics sometimes can. It therefore needs:
- slower competence change,
- explicit transposition / implementation windows,
- fixed peer-review and compliance cycles,
- and narrow emergency fast lanes with strong sunset and review design [BIB-OECD-MLG-HUB-2026] [BIB-OECD-RDP-TOOLKIT-2026].

This means stability is valuable, but only when paired with visible review clocks and public follow-through.

**Avoid:** competence creep by slow accretion without explicit review, or summit-driven improvisation that bypasses publication, domestic handoff, and legal clarity.

### 7. Global / planetary

**Default tempo:** **slow commitment formation, recurring stocktake and verification, and fast warning / response only for tightly defined emergencies**.

Global governance works best when it accepts its real temporal strengths and weaknesses. It is often bad at rapid ordinary consensus, but it can still be good at:
- periodic stocktakes,
- treaty reporting and peer review,
- scientific and statistical verification,
- future-risk signaling,
- and ring-fenced response mechanisms for clearly bounded crises [BIB-UN-PACT-FUTURE-2024] [BIB-UN-DFG].

The archive default is therefore not permanent global emergency governance and not leisurely summit theater. It is **scheduled planetary review plus bounded rapid response when tripwires fire**.

**Avoid:** pretending the global layer can legitimately improvise permanent rule by communique, or pretending century-scale threats can wait for purely voluntary episodic attention.

## Five rules that travel across the ladder

### A. Fast relief, slow irreversible commitments

People should not wait for basic relief, continuity, or error correction.
But highly irreversible decisions — constitutional change, megaproj­ects, cross-border obligations, mass data infrastructures, ecological loss — should move more slowly and with more review than everyday operations.

### B. The longer the asset or obligation lasts, the more the decision needs a horizon wider than one budget cycle

Queues, payroll, and maintenance can live inside annual rhythms.
Transit corridors, grids, housing supply, demographic adaptation, climate resilience, and treaty commitments cannot.

### C. Distant authority needs scheduled review because ordinary democratic correction is weaker there

At local scale, people notice quickly.
At national scale, elections still do some correction work.
At supranational and global scale, legitimacy depends much more on fixed review, reporting, and verification clocks.

### D. Emergency tempo must degrade back to ordinary tempo automatically

Fast crisis powers are sometimes necessary.
They become illegitimate when they have no precommitted sunset, renewal, publication, and after-action clocks.

### E. Tempo should be layered, not flattened

Every scale needs more than one clock.
The design question is not “what is the one right cadence?” but “which decisions belong on which cadence, and how do those cadences hand off cleanly?”
