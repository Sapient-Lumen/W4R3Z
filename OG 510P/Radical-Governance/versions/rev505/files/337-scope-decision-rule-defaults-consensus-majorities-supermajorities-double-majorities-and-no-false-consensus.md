# 337 — Scope-Decision-Rule Defaults: Consensus, Majorities, Supermajorities, Double Majorities, and No False Consensus

**Cross-stack note:** use `306-scope-design-ideal-government-and-reference-bundles-guide.md` for the canonical route across the scope-design family. This memo is the scale-to-decision-rule / threshold-choice front door; `14` / `176` explain the cross-scope logic, `285` gives the default bundle language, `327` handles the scale-sensitive institutional form, `328` handles the scale-sensitive legitimacy channel, `329` handles the scale-sensitive tempo / time-horizon choice, `331` handles the scale-sensitive accountability / review-mode choice, `336` handles the scale-sensitive constituency / who-counts choice, and `111` / `124` / `141` / `309` / `314` / `321` handle the deeper binding, entrenchment, mandate, chamber, formation, and treaty-threshold substrate once the scale-fit decision-rule question has been identified.

**Purpose:** answer the design question that remains after scope assignment, bundle design, form choice, legitimacy choice, tempo choice, accountability choice, and constituency choice: **what decision rule should usually dominate at this scale?**

**Why this memo exists:** `14`, `176`, `285`, and `327-336` explain what each scale owns, what shape it should take, how it should be legitimated, what clock it should run on, how it should be checked, and who should count there. But the archive still needed one compact memo that says **scale should also change how a body decides** — consensus-seeking, ordinary majority, absolute majority, supermajority, concurrent majority, double majority, or treaty-consent with narrow fallback voting.

**From-below:** this memo is a guardrail against threshold abuse in both directions. It helps stop small bodies from weaponizing “consensus” so a tiny insider bloc can veto everything, and it helps stop large or compound polities from pretending a bare single-majority vote is enough where several publics, territories, or constitutional commitments are actually at stake.

**Evidence anchors:** the ICA still states that primary cooperatives are democratically controlled by members with equal voting rights — one member, one vote — which is the cleanest current anchor for member-polity decision equality [BIB-ICA-COOP-IDENTITY-2026]; the Council of Europe’s current good-governance benchmark still says decisions should reflect the will of the majority while safeguarding the rights and legitimate interests of the minority, while preserving conditions where consensus can be reached in the interests of the community as a whole [BIB-COE-12-GDG-2026]; the Venice Commission still stresses that qualified majorities aim to ensure broad agreement in parliament and should be paired with anti-deadlock mechanisms rather than romanticized into permanent paralysis [BIB-VENICE-QUALIFIED-MAJORITIES-2023]; the Council’s current qualified-majority rule remains the clearest positive-law example of a **compound-public decision rule** that requires both a majority of states and a majority of population [BIB-CONSILIUM-QMV-2026], while treaty revision remains unanimity-and-ratification territory [BIB-EURLEX-TREATY-REVISION-2026]; Article 18 of the UN Charter still distinguishes between important questions decided by a two-thirds majority and other questions decided by a majority of members present and voting [BIB-UN-CHARTER]; and UNFCCC guidance still records consensus-centered COP practice because draft rule 42 remains unresolved [BIB-UNFCCC-COP-CONSENSUS-2026].

## Core claim

Every scale needs a **default decision rule**.

Not because one threshold solves every question, but because institutions become manipulable when they cannot say which matters should pass by ordinary majority, which require a thicker coalition, which require concurrent approval across several publics, and which should be handled by consensus-seeking only.

As scale changes, the fitting decision rule also changes:
- lower and closer scales should usually lean on **consensus-seeking plus reversible-majority fallback**,
- ordinary territorial governments should usually lean on **simple or absolute majorities for ordinary business**,
- high-commitment or identity-changing questions should usually lean on **supermajorities, delays, or second-look rules**,
- compound or multi-jurisdiction scales should usually lean on **concurrent or double majorities**,
- and the farthest treaty and global layers should usually lean on **consensus for conferral-sensitive commitments, with clearly bounded fallback voting or club pathways where unanimity would otherwise become paralysis theater**.

So the archive’s rule is:
**the more reversible and person-near the question, the lower the threshold can usually be; the more entrenched, compound, or sovereignty-touching the question, the more the decision rule should prove a broader coalition than a bare single majority.**

---

## The anti-false-consensus / anti-threshold-gaming test

Before creating or reforming a government at any scale, ask seven questions:

1. **Reversibility test:** is this an ordinary reversible operating decision, or an entrenched / identity-shaping one?
2. **Minority-rights test:** can a bare majority here predictably override a rights floor, expel a protected minority, or lock in unreviewable harms?
3. **Compound-public test:** does this body govern several jurisdictions, peoples, or territorially unequal populations such that one aggregate majority would be politically dishonest?
4. **Deadlock-cost test:** what happens if one actor or one small bloc can stop action indefinitely?
5. **Entrenchment test:** are you using an ordinary majority for constitutional or quasi-constitutional change, or using a supermajority for day-to-day management just to empower incumbents?
6. **Transparency test:** can outsiders tell which rule applies to which category of decision, or is the threshold movable at will?
7. **Scale-fit test:** are you borrowing unanimity, plebiscites, or presidential veto drama from a different scale for prestige reasons rather than because the public structure actually demands it?

If the answer to several of these is “we will decide case by case” or “we hope for consensus”, the design is likely suffering from **threshold ambiguity, veto theater, or fake consensus**.

## Default decision-rule modes by scope

### 0. Household / association / cooperative

**Default decision-rule mode:** **consensus-seeking for ordinary matters, majority fallback for reversible operations, and qualified majorities for exclusion or lock-in decisions**.

Use:
- consensus or near-consensus for routine shared-life coordination where the cost of revisiting decisions is low,
- **one member, one vote** as the clean default where the body is a primary cooperative or other member polity built on equal membership standing,
- ordinary majority for budgets, schedules, or ordinary steward choices,
- and a higher threshold for expulsion, irreversible asset disposal, merger, dissolution, or durable delegation of coercive authority.

**Avoid:** both forced consensus that lets one insider block everything and bare-majority expulsion / asset seizure with no exit or appeal lane.

### 1. Micro-local (building / block / village / campus / frontline institution)

**Default decision-rule mode:** **visible consensus-seeking plus simple-majority fallback for reversible local choices**.

This scale works best when it:
- tries to surface objections and minority harms before voting,
- uses a simple majority or similarly legible fallback for routine maintenance, meeting decisions, and rotating roles,
- and reserves qualified thresholds for common-asset disposal, durable rules, exclusion from shared essentials, or delegation into municipal enforcement.

At this scale the public is small and concrete, so the main danger is not insufficient thickness but **informal vetoes, hidden cliques, and fake unanimity**.

### 2. Municipal (city / town)

**Default decision-rule mode:** **ordinary council majorities for ordinary law and operating business; higher thresholds only for charter-like or irreversible moves**.

Use:
- ordinary majority for ordinary legislation, by-laws, procurement approval, and service design,
- absolute or recorded majorities where budget passage, executive appointment, or no-confidence-type consequences justify clearer proof,
- and supermajority / second-look rules for annexation, boundary change, municipal charter rewrite, sale of irreplaceable public assets, exceptional debt exposure, or rights-sensitive emergency extensions.

The municipal anti-pattern is **trying to run everyday service government through constant supermajority politics** or, in the other direction, using bare majorities for quasi-constitutional city rewrites.

### 2.5 Intermediate local administration (county / prefecture / province-lite backstop)

**Default decision-rule mode:** **ordinary majority for administration and coordination, with qualified thresholds for redistribution or municipal-status changes**.

Use:
- ordinary majority for shared-service administration, delegated oversight, and regional backstop choices,
- and qualified thresholds for forced local mergers, redistribution formula rewrites, or decisions that permanently reallocate competence away from municipalities.

Where this scale is mostly deconcentrated administration, do not pretend it needs thick constitutional decision drama. Where it has real political authority, do not hide structural change in ordinary operating votes.

### 3. Metropolitan / corridor / watershed / functional region

**Default decision-rule mode:** **concurrent or double majorities for structurally important choices; weighted or ordinary majorities only for already-delegated routine operations**.

This is the first scale where one public is usually **compound**:
- municipalities want standing as member jurisdictions,
- populations want weight proportionate to how many people are actually governed,
- and users / commuters often experience the system regardless of where they vote.

So the usual default is:
- **ordinary or weighted majority** for routine operations *once* a compact and budget formula already exist,
- **double / concurrent majority** for mandate changes, major budgets, new revenue powers, binding plans, or admission / exit,
- and **unanimity only for narrow conferral-like questions** such as founding compacts, accession, dissolution, or changes that materially alter core member obligations.

The anti-pattern here is either **state-like bare-population majoritarianism that crushes smaller jurisdictions** or **unanimity-by-default that prevents any metro system from operating at all**.

### 4. Regional (state / province / land)

**Default decision-rule mode:** **ordinary legislative majority for ordinary law; thicker thresholds for territorial settlement, constitutional status, or municipal-order redesign**.

Use:
- ordinary majority for ordinary legislation and budgets,
- stronger thresholds for electoral-system redesign, constitutional or autonomy-statute revision, municipal-boundary restructuring at scale, or equalization-rule entrenchment,
- and territorial concurrence where the region is genuinely federal, plurinational, or built from strongly protected constituent communities.

Regions often need more decision thickness than cities but less than nation-states. The fit depends on whether the region is mainly an administrative territory or a constitutionally meaningful polity.

### 5. National

**Default decision-rule mode:** **ordinary majority for ordinary governing; supermajorities, delays, or dual decisions for constitutional and regime-shaping change**.

Use:
- ordinary legislative majority for ordinary lawmaking,
- confidence / investiture rules that prove an actual governing majority without turning every vote into a constitutional crisis,
- supermajority, referendum, territorial concurrence, time delay, or double-decision rules for constitutional amendment and other regime-shaping changes,
- and specially typed thresholds for treaty ratification, emergency renewal, impeachment / removal, or judicial appointments where the archive’s narrower memos require them.

At the national scale, the central warning is: **do not confuse ordinary politics with constituent power, and do not use excessive supermajorities to let durable minorities veto all repair**.

### 6. Supranational (treaty union / shared-rule space)

**Default decision-rule mode:** **double or qualified majorities for ordinary shared-rule decisions; unanimity reserved for conferral-sensitive core changes**.

This scale usually governs a **compound polity**. It is rarely honest to decide everything either by one-state-one-vote or by one-aggregate-population majority alone.

Use:
- parliamentary-style majority within directly representative chambers where such chambers exist,
- qualified or double majorities for ordinary shared-rule legislation or implementation decisions,
- blocking-minority rules that prevent domination but do not turn every small coalition into a permanent veto point,
- and unanimity or treaty-ratification procedures only for accession, withdrawal, competence expansion, core revenue / borrowing shifts, or other moves that materially re-found the union.

The anti-pattern is **veto romanticism**: treating each member’s consent as equally necessary for every ordinary policy question even after a shared-rule system has already been created.

### 7. Global (planetary / treaty-complex level)

**Default decision-rule mode:** **consensus-seeking for universal legitimacy-sensitive frameworks, with explicit majorities or club pathways where universal unanimity would otherwise disable action**.

At the global scale there is no single thick demos, so decision rules must respect state consent *and* avoid empowering one-party paralysis over clearly shared risks.

Use:
- consensus-seeking for foundational frameworks, admission, treaty creation, and politically fragile universal commitments,
- Charter- or treaty-specified voting thresholds for bodies that already have them,
- supermajority or formula-based rules for finance, review, procedural reconsideration, or amendment where the governing text already provides them,
- and club / coalition / treaty-article pathways for implementation where waiting for total unanimity would make collective protection impossible.

The anti-pattern is either **fake world majoritarianism** or **permanent single-party veto over urgent shared-risk governance**.

### Non-territorial & indigenous polities (cross-cutting)

**Default decision-rule mode:** **membership-fit majorities bounded by compact obligations, minority protections, and external rights floors**.

Use:
- the decision rule that fits the polity’s own legitimate traditions where those traditions remain reviewable and non-coercive,
- stronger thresholds for changes to membership, land/commons status, intergovernmental compacts, or delegation of public force,
- and explicit compact-facing concurrence rules where decisions also bind neighboring or partner jurisdictions.

Do not flatten every legitimate polity into a parliamentary-majority model. But do require that outsiders can tell which threshold applies to which class of decision and how review works when the decision affects protected rights or shared public authority.

## Cross-scope defaults worth carrying everywhere

1. **Ordinary reversible matters should not require constitutional thresholds.**
2. **Entrenched or identity-shaping matters should not pass as if they were ordinary operations.**
3. **Compound publics usually need concurrent or double-majority logic somewhere in the design.**
4. **Consensus is only honest when objections are surfaced, minority concerns are recorded, and the body is not structurally hostage to silent veto.**
5. **Unanimity should be narrow: conferral, admission, withdrawal, dissolution, or genuinely sovereign exposure — not the whole of everyday governing.**
