# 313 — Head-of-State Design: Non-Executive Presidents, Constitutional Monarchs, Reserve Powers, and Succession Rails

**Purpose:** make the head-of-state layer explicit and auditable so a polity does not hide constitutional arbitration, dissolution leverage, assent delay, or succession improvisation inside ceremony.

**Why this memo exists:** the archive now covers legitimacy architecture (`21` / `307`), chamber and confidence design (`309`), transfer-of-power and caretaker practice (`308`), cabinet / centre-of-government design (`310`), executive-form choice (`311`), party-system design (`312`), and national / supranational scope defaults (`40` / `50`). What it still lacked was one compact seam memo for the office that often sits **between** those layers: **whether a polity should have a distinct head of state at all; whether that office should be a non-executive president, constitutional monarch, or rotating / collective substitute; which acts are ceremonial, formal-on-advice, review, or reserve; how countersignature, assent, appointment, and dissolution should work; and how vacancy, incapacity, regency, or succession should be handled without either a vacuum or a shadow executive**.

**Evidence anchors:** International IDEA’s primer on non-executive presidents remains the clearest compact guide to the parliamentary-republic path because it explains both the continuity advantages of a separate head of state and the dangers created by unclear discretionary authority [BIB-IDEA-NONEXEC-PRES-2017]. Its primer on constitutional monarchs is equally useful because it treats monarchy as a constitutional office with representative and civic roles, not an unbounded sovereign residue [BIB-IDEA-CONST-MONARCHS-2016]. IDEA’s dissolution primer matters here because dissolution is one of the classic pressure points where head-of-state discretion, prime-ministerial advice, and constitutional deadlock can become indistinct [BIB-IDEA-DISSOLUTION-PRIMER]. Its primers on presidential legislative powers and vetoes are also helpful guardrails for systems where a head of state has more than merely ceremonial functions: agenda-setting, assent, and veto powers shape whether the office is a referee, a delay point, or a rival lawmaker [BIB-IDEA-PRES-LEG-POWERS-2017] [BIB-IDEA-PRES-VETO-2017]. OECD’s 2024 centre-of-government compendium is a useful neighboring reminder that constitutional dignity is not an excuse for role ambiguity: mandate clarity and responsibility mapping still matter at the political apex [BIB-OECD-COG-STEERING-2024].

## Core claim

A head of state should not be a constitutional ghost.

If the office exists, it must be legible whether it is:
- a civic symbol,
- a continuity node,
- a constitutional referee of last resort,
- a directly mandated political actor,
- or only a ceremonial wrapper around decisions made elsewhere.

The archive’s answer is therefore not “republic good” or “monarchy bad”, nor the reverse. It is:
**match the selection method and powers to the real job, classify every head-of-state act by type, keep reserve powers narrow and reviewable, and make succession / incapacity / regency rules boring enough that no crisis can be won by improvisation.**

If the issue shifts from which acts exist to how any residual conventional layer around them should be published and maintained, route to `315-constitutional-conventions-cabinet-manuals-and-codified-restraint-rails.md`. If the issue shifts from whether assent or return powers exist to how the final enrolled text is certified, promulgated, published, commenced, or referred for constitutional review before entry into force, route to `316-bill-finalization-assent-promulgation-publication-commencement-and-constitutional-referral-rails.md`.

## The smallest good architecture

### 1. Head-of-state role charter (`HRC-*`)
For the polity as a whole, publish or entrench:
- whether the office exists,
- whether it is monarchical, presidential, rotating, or collective,
- whether it is executive, non-executive, or nearly entirely ceremonial,
- how it is selected,
- the term or succession rule,
- and which constitutional goods the office is meant to serve: symbolism, continuity, arbitration, representation abroad, or emergency continuity.

### 2. Act-classification and countersignature matrix (`ACM-*`)
Classify every head-of-state act into one of four buckets:
- **ceremonial / representational,**
- **formal-on-advice,**
- **review / return / referral,**
- **reserve / last-resort.**

For each act, publish:
- who initiates it,
- whether countersignature is required,
- whether reasons must be given,
- publication deadlines,
- and whether a court, chamber, or other body can review the act or refusal.

### 3. Government-formation and dissolution protocol (`GDP-*`)
Make explicit:
- who invites a leader to form a government,
- what evidence of parliamentary viability is required,
- when advice to dissolve must be followed,
- when it may be delayed or refused,
- whether a caretaker prime minister may request dissolution,
- and what happens if no government can be formed by the deadline.

This is where hidden reserve powers most often metastasize.

### 4. Assent, return, veto, and message interface (`ARV-*`)
State clearly:
- whether bills require assent,
- whether assent may only be formal,
- whether a bill may be returned once for reconsideration,
- whether any veto is suspensive or absolute,
- what override threshold applies,
- and whether the head of state may send formal messages, propose legislation, or request constitutional review.

Never leave the legislative interface implied.

### 5. Succession, incapacity, regency, and vacancy note (`SIV-*`)
Publish or entrench:
- temporary incapacity tests,
- who certifies incapacity,
- who acts during temporary absence,
- vacancy triggers,
- regency or presidential-commission rules where relevant,
- the maximum duration of acting status,
- and the election / appointment timetable for a permanent replacement.

### 6. Household, funding, and neutrality note (`HFN-*`)
If the office includes a court, household, or ceremonial establishment, make visible:
- public funding,
- staffing and procurement rules,
- gift / honor / patronage rules,
- political-neutrality obligations,
- and the line between civic ceremony and party activity.

If the office is hereditary, this note matters even more, not less.

## Design rules

### A. Decide first whether a distinct head of state is actually needed
Some systems do not need one.

A small municipality, a compact regional authority, or a treaty secretariat often needs a clear chair or executive, not a second apex office.

Use a distinct head of state only when the polity genuinely needs one or more of these functions:
- continuity above cabinet turnover,
- a constitutional referee of last resort,
- non-partisan civic representation,
- dignified appointment / accreditation functions,
- or a symbolic office that helps separate state continuity from day-to-day government.

Do not create an ornamental office with enough legal fog to become mischievous.

### B. Match the selection method to the intended neutrality of the office
Selection method is not cosmetic.

- A **direct election** creates a visible democratic mandate and therefore invites political activity.
- An **indirect election or supermajority parliamentary election** better fits a non-executive office expected to stand above party competition.
- A **hereditary office** can only fit democratic constitutionalism if the constitution aggressively limits personal discretion and treats the office as public, bounded, and law-governed.

Do not directly elect a head of state and then pretend the office has no independent political claim at all.

### C. Make countersignature the default for non-executive offices
Where the head of state is not intended to govern, most state acts should be valid only on ministerial or governmental advice, with countersignature and publication.

That does three things:
- keeps democratic responsibility with the government,
- prevents ceremonial offices from quietly exercising policy discretion,
- and makes it easier to audit who actually decided.

If an act is exempt from countersignature, name it explicitly and explain why.

### D. Keep reserve powers narrow, typed, and last-resort
Reserve powers are sometimes unavoidable.
They are also one of the easiest doors through which a symbolic office becomes a constitutional wildcard.

If reserve powers exist at all, tie them to a short list such as:
- inviting a government to form when parliamentary arithmetic is uncertain,
- refusing a dissolution request in a narrowly defined abuse case,
- dismissing a government only after a constitutionally specified and publicly provable loss of lawful authority,
- or ensuring continuity during vacancy / incapacity where no cabinet can act.

Do not draft reserve powers as open-textured authority to “protect the constitution” without procedure, thresholds, or reasons.

### E. Treat dissolution as a constitutional safety valve, not a personal weapon
A head of state should not be free to dissolve parliament at pleasure.

For each system, publish:
- who may request dissolution,
- whether a head of state may refuse,
- the maximum number of refusals,
- blackout periods near recent elections,
- caretaker constraints once dissolution occurs,
- and the timetable for the next election.

Dissolution design belongs partly with `309`, but this memo governs the head-of-state side of that seam.

### F. Assent and veto powers must fit the intended constitutional role
For a non-executive head of state, the cleanest default is often:
- formal assent,
- or at most a one-time suspensive return or referral.

Where a president has real political power, veto or legislative-message powers may be part of the design — but then the constitution must admit that the office is not merely ceremonial.

Do not give a supposedly neutral head of state a potent, under-specified veto and then call the office apolitical.

### G. Separate constitutional refereeing from ordinary policymaking
A healthy head-of-state office may have limited refereeing functions — for example, ensuring that the person invited to form government can probably command confidence, or sending a constitutionally doubtful bill for review.

That does **not** justify ordinary agenda control, routine policy bargaining, or standing parallel authority over ministers.

If the office speaks often on contested policy, the constitution has probably misclassified the office.

### H. Succession and incapacity rules must be boring under stress
The best succession design is dull, quick, and hard to game.

Good defaults include:
- objective vacancy triggers,
- temporary acting arrangements that do not change the office’s substantive powers,
- short clocks for replacement,
- published incapacity procedures,
- and no major appointments, dissolutions, pardons, or policy shocks by an acting holder unless necessity is documented.

A crisis should not become a ladder into stronger personal powers for whoever happens to be next in line.

### I. Monarchy, if retained, must be constitutional all the way down
A constitutional monarch can perform continuity and representational roles, but only if the office is treated as fully constitutional rather than mystically residual.

That means:
- public financing rules,
- political neutrality,
- clear succession law,
- explicit regency and incapacity rules,
- no hidden prerogative reservoir,
- and published limits on advisory, assent, dissolution, and appointment powers.

The test is not tradition alone. The test is whether the office can be audited like any other constitutional node.

### J. Non-executive presidencies work best when the office is visibly above cabinet politics
A parliamentary republic can benefit from a non-executive president where the polity wants:
- a civic representative chosen by a broader coalition than the cabinet majority,
- a bounded constitutional referee,
- or a substitute for monarchy without merging everything into the prime minister’s office.

But that only works if:
- direct election does not overclaim political mandate,
- reserve powers remain narrow,
- and investiture, confidence, dissolution, and caretaker practice are already cleanly specified elsewhere.

### K. Large unions and global bodies should resist theatrical head-of-state imitation
Most supranational and global institutions do not need a quasi-monarchical or pseudo-presidential figure.

Prefer:
- rotating presidencies,
- chairs,
- collegiate bodies,
- or bounded secretariat heads,

unless the institution truly has the democratic thickness and competence density of a polity. A symbolic apex office without a real demos usually produces confusion, not legitimacy.

## Scope-sensitive defaults

### Micro-local / municipal
Do **not** create a separate head of state.
Use a chair, mayor, speaker, or manager with explicit delegation and acting rules.

### Metropolitan / regional
Rarely justify a distinct head of state.
If continuity is needed, use a speaker, council president, governor, or rotating chair with typed formal powers rather than an invented ceremonial presidency.

### National
This is the main zone for `313`.
A national constitution should decide whether:
- the prime minister / president alone already provides enough apex clarity,
- a non-executive president is useful for continuity and neutral representation,
- a constitutional monarch is retained as a fully bounded office,
- or the polity prefers no separate head of state at all.

### Supranational / treaty-union
Prefer a rotating chair, council president, parliamentary presiding officer, or collegial executive rather than a dramatized head-of-state office.
If a union develops one, powers should remain typed, narrow, and heavily published.

### Global
Default against a global head-of-state model.
Prefer chairs, rotating presidencies, or secretariat leadership with explicit limits.

## Confusion boundaries

### `313` vs `311`
- `311` chooses **the executive form overall**: parliamentary, presidential, semi-presidential, non-executive, or collegial.
- `313` chooses **how a distinct head-of-state office should actually be bounded, typed, selected, and succeeded once that broader form is known**.

### `313` vs `309`
- `309` governs **chambers, confidence, investiture, and dissolution architecture generally**.
- `313` governs **the head-of-state side of invitation, assent, dissolution, and reserve-power design**.

### `313` vs `316`
- `313` governs **whether a head-of-state office should have assent, return, veto, or reserve powers at all, and how those powers fit the office’s claim to neutrality or mandate**.
- `316` governs **the final lawmaking sequence in which those powers may be exercised: enrolled text, constitutional referral, promulgation, publication, commencement, and rectification**.

### `313` vs `308`
- `308` governs **transfer-of-power, caretaker restraint, acting authority, and continuity when offices change hands**.
- `313` governs **the standing constitutional design of the head-of-state office, including vacancy, regency, incapacity, and succession rules**.

### `313` vs `310`
- `310` governs **cabinet formation, portfolios, cabinet committees, and centre-of-government coordination**.
- `313` governs **the distinct apex office that may appoint, accredit, invite, or formally act on the advice of that cabinet**.

### `313` vs `147`
- `147` governs **how people vote and how mandates are translated**.
- `313` governs **what a head-of-state office may claim, and how its powers should be calibrated to that claim**.

## Minimal evaluation questions

1. Does the polity actually need a distinct head of state, or is the office mostly inherited symbolism plus hidden leverage?
2. Does the office’s selection method match the neutrality or political mandate the constitution expects from it?
3. Can a reader tell which acts are ceremonial, on advice, review-only, or genuine reserve powers?
4. Are assent, return, veto, and legislative-message powers proportionate to the office’s intended role?
5. Are dissolution, invitation-to-form-government, and refusal-of-advice powers narrow enough to prevent shadow executive politics?
6. Are succession, regency, incapacity, and vacancy rules explicit enough that crisis does not create improvisational authority?
7. Is the office funded, staffed, and audited as a public constitutional node rather than an untouchable court or personal household?
