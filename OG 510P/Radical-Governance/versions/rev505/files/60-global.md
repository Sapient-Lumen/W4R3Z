**Purpose:** make cross-border governance obligations and claims traceable so global coordination doesn’t erase responsibility.

**Person served:** A person harmed by cross‑border externalities (climate, pandemics, financial shocks) who needs accountability that doesn’t disappear at borders.

**From-below:** This maps planetary‑scale obligations so global rules don’t escape accountability just because no one state ‘owns’ them.

## Scope card (one-screen)
- **Typical scale / unit types:** planetary regime complex (treaties + institutions + measurement + finance).
- **Owns (and nothing else):** global public goods and existential risks (climate, biodiversity/oceans, pandemic preparedness, atrocity prevention, systemic stability).
- **Does not own:** general domestic governance; routine service delivery; coercive enforcement without legitimacy.
- **MVG (minimum viable government):** treaties with clear mandates; independent measurement/verification; dispute resolution; ring-fenced finance; compliance ladders.
- **Interfaces:** publish commitments + metrics + verification; open registers for obligations and outcomes (`70/71`, `81`).
- **Person-facing invariants:** where this scope issues rights/service determinations, require **comprehension-tested receipts/notices**, **no-wrong-door** routing, and **safe remedy** (incl. representation) (`98-persons-path-and-accessibility-invariants.md`, `08-...`, `36-...`).
- **Top failure modes:** coercion without legitimacy, under-enforcement, geopolitics, forum-shopping (TM-3, TM-5, TM-8).

This memo treats “global government” as a **layered system**: treaties + institutions + measurement + finance + compliance. The goal is not a unitary world state, but *effective governance of global public goods and existential risks*.

**Current anchor (2024):** at the UN **Summit of the Future** (22 Sep 2024), states adopted the **Pact for the Future** and its annexes (the **Global Digital Compact** and **Declaration on Future Generations**). Treat these as a recent reference point for what “modernized multilateralism” is currently willing to say on paper (implementation remains the real test). See [BIB-UN-PACT-FUTURE-2024], [BIB-UN-GDC], [BIB-UN-DFG].

## What the global level must own (and nothing else)
- prevention/management of catastrophic war and mass violence
- pandemic preparedness and cross-border outbreak response
- climate stabilization + biodiversity and ocean commons
- systemic financial stability (crisis coordination)
- baseline human rights norms and accountability for mass atrocities
- global technical standards where interoperability is essential (see `27-standards-and-technical-governance.md`)

## Minimum Viable Government (MVG)

**Baseline:** global governance is a **stack of compacts** (treaties/regimes) plus measurement, finance, and remedy—not a unitary world state.

Minimums:
- compacts with measurable obligations + reporting, filed in registers with stable IDs (`IOP-1`; `19-compacts-and-cooperative-governance.md`).
- mobility/refugee cooperation as rights-floor compacts: publish party commitments, burden-sharing finance, and verification; require that status/return/custody actions that the regime governs remain receipt-based (`DRR-*` + `AL-*` lanes) and that aggregate flows/processing/custody metrics publish as `REL-*` with methods (anchors: [BIB-UN-GCM-2018], [BIB-IOM-WMR-2024]; see `67-migration-and-mobility-governance.md`).
- independent measurement/verification where feasible, with public methods + revision logs (`OPEN-4/5`; see `26-epistemic-infrastructure-and-public-knowledge.md`).
- ring-fenced public-goods finance with consolidated accounts + audit (`CAP-2/3`, `ACC-1`; see `07-fiscal-and-budgetary-governance.md`).
- compliance tools that preserve due process (peer review, conditional finance, dispute settlement, market-access hooks) (`LAW-5`; see `08-remedy-and-grievance.md`).
- a structured people’s channel (advisory but recurring, with duty-to-respond triggers) (`DEC-2`) and logged in `ENG-*` (see `41-public-participation-and-deliberation-register.md`).
- ADS governance for high-stakes global regimes (sanctions/listings, aid targeting): system registers + human review + appeal (`IOP-5`; see `06-digital-and-algorithmic-governance.md`).

## Interfaces (how global regimes bind without a world state)

Global regimes work when obligations become **legible domestic commitments** and **measurable corrections**.

- **Obligation mapping:** each party maps treaty/compact duties into domestic `RULE-*` and competence-ledger entries (who is responsible for what), with a public “obligation-to-rule” crosswalk as a `REL-*` release. Treat this as a typed treaty-domestication artifact rather than a loose implementation memo (see `321-treaty-ratification-domestic-effect-reservations-and-implementation-rails.md`).
- **Receipts and remedy:** where regimes touch people (mobility controls, sanctions/listings, aid eligibility), actions remain receipt-based (`DRR-*`) with discoverable appeal lanes (`AL-*`) and due-process guarantees (`08-...`, `36-...`). Apply the **Person’s Path** constraint: comprehension-tested notices, no‑wrong‑door routing, safe/representative filing, and offline channels where connectivity/literacy is constrained (`98-persons-path-and-accessibility-invariants.md`).
- **Measurement/verification:** publish MRV baselines and revisions as `REL-*` with methods + change logs; dispute over methods is handled as a governed process, not a “data fight” (`26-...`, `51-...`; see `81-verification-inspection-and-compliance-ladders.md`).
- **Finance and conditionality:** public-goods finance is joinable through `TRF-*` transfers and audited accounts; conditional finance uses explicit criteria and preserves appealability (`35-...`).
- **Compact discipline:** major regimes are treated as `CMP-*` compacts with enforceable dispute paths and upgrade/exit rules (`19-...`).

## Ideal institutional architecture (modular)
### 1) Global Charter Layer (legitimacy + constraints)
- Compact rights and procedural commitments emphasizing participation, non-discrimination, due process, transparency, and remedy.
- Human rights anchor: see [BIB-UDHR].
- Recent reform agenda anchor: see [BIB-UN-PFTF] (with [BIB-UN-GDC] + [BIB-UN-DFG]).

### 2) Global Public Goods Compacts (topic-specific operating agreements)
Each compact includes:
- clear obligations + targets + reporting cadence
- inspection/verification (where relevant)
- graduated sanctions and incentives
- dispute resolution and appeal pathways
- funding mechanism tied to benefits/harms

Example anchors (illustrative):
- **Pandemic governance:** International Health Regulations (IHR, 2005) + 2024 amendments (entered into force **19 Sep 2025** for most States Parties, with opt‑out procedures) — see [BIB-WHO-IHR-TEXT-2025]; [BIB-WHO-IHR-AMEND-EIF-2025]; [BIB-WHO-IHR-AMEND-QA].
- **Pandemic agreement:** WHO Pandemic Agreement (WHA78.1, adopted **20 May 2025**): see [BIB-WHO-PA-2025].
 - Domestic implementation pattern: `57-public-health-and-biosecurity-governance.md` (REL/DRR/EMR spine).
- **Climate governance:** Paris Agreement (global goals + transparency framework): see [BIB-PARIS]. For mitigation pipeline design (baselines → targets → instruments → MRV → correction), see `65-energy-and-decarbonization-governance.md` (anchors: [BIB-IPCC-AR6-WG3-2022], [BIB-UNFCCC-NDCREG]). For adaptation + disaster-risk governance as a joinable baselines/triggers/recovery pipeline, see `63-climate-adaptation-and-disaster-risk-governance.md` (anchors: [BIB-UNDRR-SENDAI-2015], [BIB-UNFCCC-GGA]).
- **Biodiversity governance:** Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (CBD COP15 Decision 15/4): see [BIB-CBD-GBF].
- **Digital cooperation:** Global Digital Compact (2024): see [BIB-UN-GDC].

### 2.1) Compliance mechanisms (use the smallest that works)
Global regimes rarely have police power. They work by combining *measurement + incentives + process*:

- **Peer review** (learning + pressure): e.g., UN Human Rights Council Universal Periodic Review (UPR): see [BIB-UN-UPR].
- **Verification/inspection** (where feasible): independent monitoring linked to measurable obligations.
- **Conditional finance**: funds tied to verified performance (avoid discretionary patronage; preserve appeal paths).
- **Dispute settlement**: arbitration/courts with authorized remedies (canonical template: see [BIB-WTO-DSU]).
- **Market-access hooks**: trade/technology restrictions against non-participants can raise participation incentives (illustrative: Montreal Protocol Article 4 trade with non-parties: see [BIB-MONTREAL-A4]).
- **Transparency + “name-and-explain”**: public scoreboards with evidence packs and duty-to-respond.
- **Domestic incorporation**: require parties to name the domestic implementing agency, budget line, and remedy path.

Hard constraint: compliance tools MUST preserve **due process** (especially for listings/sanctions) and offer a credible path to compliance and review (`08-...`). A concrete due-process pattern exists in the UN Security Council Ombudsperson delisting mechanism: see [BIB-UNSC-OMB] and [BIB-UNSC-OMB-PROC].

### 3) Global Measurement & Early Warning (trust infrastructure)
- Multi-stakeholder, auditable measurement bodies; publish comparable indicators.
- Use small anchor sets (see `03-metrics-and-evidence.md`) rather than indicator explosions.

### 4) Global Finance (capability infrastructure)
Ring-fenced funds for:
- pandemic preparedness,
- adaptation and loss/damage,
- peacebuilding and refugees,
- rapid disaster response.
Funding principles: ability-to-pay; polluter-pays where measurable; strong audit + anti-fraud.

### 5) Peace & Security (hardest constraint)
- Maintain collective security mechanisms with tight legal constraints and transparency.
- Today’s enforcement anchor is UN Charter Chapter VII authority (threats to peace; measures): see [BIB-UNCHARTER-CH7].
- Incremental improvements (where politically attainable):
 - clearer thresholds for action,
 - stronger due process for sanctions listings,
 - transparent deliberation norms,
 - standing investigatory capacity,
 - veto restraint norms in mass-atrocity contexts.

### 6) Legitimacy adjuncts (keep them real)
Global regimes are legitimacy-fragile. Add *advisory* channels that create transparency and review triggers without pretending to be a world parliament:

- **People’s channel:** a recurring, stratified Global Citizens’ Assembly (`DEC-2`) that issues public advisory opinions on compacts/crises; hosts evidence packs; triggers duty-to-respond; and logs proceedings in `ENG-*`.

**Routing note:** use `325-international-adjudication-individual-communications-jurisdiction-interim-measures-and-compliance-rails.md` when the question is not global-governance scope design as such, but which international court, tribunal, or complaint body can hear a dispute or complaint, what consent or admissibility basis applies, what interim protection exists, and how compliance with outcomes is tracked afterward.
- **Future generations function:** a small office/commission that can trigger review of compacts and emergency measures, publish future-impact statements, and demand duty-to-respond (`DEC-6`). Norm anchor: see [BIB-UN-DFG].

## Top failure modes + countermeasures
- **Opt-outs / reservations / non-participation:** design for partial coverage: transparent party lists, interoperability baselines, and “coalition compacts” that stay open to later accession.
- **Weak compliance:** verification + conditional finance + graduated sanctions (compact design).
- **Legitimacy gaps:** structured people’s channel + transparency + long-horizon review (`DEC-2`, `DEC-6`, `OPEN-1`).
- **Great-power veto paralysis:** narrower mandates + coalition compacts where universalism fails (polycentricity).

## Success metrics (minimal set)
Use metric IDs from `03-metrics-and-evidence.md` packs where possible; keep ≤10 total.
- outbreak detection-to-action time (global health response queue) [CAD-2] + IHR core capacity rubric (local add-on)
- emissions trajectory relative to targets (and monitoring/verification coverage) [ECO-2] (+ monitoring lag where measurable) [ECO-1]
- compliance rates with reporting/inspection regimes (local add-on; define per regime)
- reduction in major interstate conflict and mass atrocities (scope-appropriate serious harm rate) [SAC-6]
- legitimacy: participation breadth in global processes [LRR-1] + trust/fairness [LRR-2] + transparency coverage [LRR-8]

## Named tensions (design must surface these)
- Sovereignty/self‑determination vs governing global public goods and existential risk.
- Universality of norms vs plural legitimate institutional forms (avoid “one true bureaucracy”).
- Enforcement teeth vs legitimacy/due‑process (compliance tools can become coercion).
- Transparency vs diplomacy/security/retaliation risks (especially for vulnerable actors).
- Burden‑sharing justice vs geopolitical leverage (finance conditionality can become domination).
