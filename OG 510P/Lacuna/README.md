# Lacuna

## A world should not become true by accident

Lacuna is built around a familiar storytelling difficulty: a character’s claim, a player’s attempt, a narrator’s commitment and the hidden explanation of an event are not the same thing. If a system silently merges them, it can make a mystery unfair or a fictional world incoherent without noticing where the change occurred.

The supplied Python/SQLite kernel treats those distinctions as durable state. Several explanations may remain possible; a planner’s preference is not automatically accepted history. The prose is worth reading even before asking whether you want to operate the software.

## Where the kernel ends

Lacuna does not itself call a model, write the plot or choose which possible world wins. Its records and checks can discipline a surrounding storyteller, but they do not prove narrative quality, semantic completeness or a model’s forgotten context.

That limit makes the division of labor interesting. A world can have a strict memory of what was accepted while remaining open to invention. Preserving uncertainty is not the same as refusing to commit; the question is when commitment becomes warranted and what it changes.

The player-facing source also notes that durable operation may retain exact player text and private planning artifacts. Reading this public source initiates no such storage or play session. The supplied release’s MIT license and retained evidence remain with it; no runtime test was performed for this introduction.
