# AN HOUR OF CEILING

My mother answered every video call
the way you'd answer a hot pan: at arm's length,
aimed at God. Sundays I got the ceiling fan
on low, her good lamp, the upper third
of a framed photo of, I think, me.

Am I on? she'd ask the light fixture.
She talked to her own little face in the corner
the whole time, the way you'd talk to a baby
in a backpack — turned slightly away, fond,
convinced it could hear.

She muted herself for the best part
of every story. I watched her mouth the ending
of something about her sister and a ladder,
then laugh, then slap a table I couldn't see.
I gave her a thumbs up. We were both lying a little.

I used to flip my camera and show her
the yard, the new fence, her grandson's left ear.
She showed me, in nine years of Sundays,
one cat (partial), four strangers' weddings,
and that fan turning on low like it had somewhere to be.

It's still the most of anything I have of her.
Hours of it, if I added it up, and one Sunday I will:
her ceiling, her voice coming up from under the frame
like a neighbor through a floor, mid-story, unmuted
for once: you should have seen it. You should have been there.

PROCESS NOTE. Drill 105 (06/09): the funny poem that turns out to be the saddest, where the joke structure must BE the grief structure — and here it is literally: the running gag (nine years of calls in which the camera never finds her) is, after the unstated death, the exact shape of the loss (he never sees her; now he can't). Comic timing as line-break timing throughout: "aimed at God," "(partial)," "her grandson's left ear" are placed where a stand-up would place them, because precision in the laugh is what licenses precision in the turn. The volta is one tense ("It's still the most of anything I have of her") — death never named, no scene at a bedside, no lesson. The ending hands the last words to her own catchphrase, which the poem has converted, without comment, into the accusation grief always files: you should have been there. Traps: tech-nostalgia syrup and the comfort reflex — no reconciliation is staged, and the fan keeps its joke ("like it had somewhere to be") even inside the elegy, because she would have. Banlist check: the one mid-poem dash is enumerative, not the em-dash epiphany; "still" is load-bearing syntax, not line-final pathos.
