# RING-DOWN
Bryant Pond, Maine — October 11, 1983

The calls went through her living room for thirty-one years.
Number, please. One long and one short gets you 22 ring 11.
Two longs, the Averills. The fire whistle she rang herself.

She could hear it when your batteries went low —
your voice would thin out on the line like late ice,
and she'd tell the repairman, sounds like 31 needs batteries,

and 31 would find him in the dooryard by noon.
Try getting that from a tone. Try telling a tone
the chicken's boiling over and you have to run.

In '81 they sold. A committee formed: Don't Yank the Crank.
The committee lost. Committees of the previous thing
always lose. The oak phones went to subscribers, three dollars each,

a crank, a cradle, a box of bells, cheaper than the fee
for one month of the future. On the last day
the daughters worked the board, who had worked it as girls,

and the last call went south to a Portland hospital,
to a lineman too sick to be in the room,
and when he answered, that was that. The mouths

of four hundred houses, give or take, went modern overnight —
the histories disagree on the count, four-thirty-one,
four-eighty-one, and nobody now to ask. You used to just

crank twice and ask.

PROCESS NOTE. Documentary contract (04): town, date, ring-code convention, the battery diagnosis by voice, the committee's name, the $3 phones, the daughters at the board, and the final call to a hospitalized employee are all source-verified. The customer count conflicts across sources (431 vs. 481) — per the fact-gate, the conflict is kept IN the poem rather than silently resolved, and becomes the ending's engine: the loss of the count is the loss of the operator, the person you could just ask. Traps named in advance: lonely-keeper kitsch and nostalgia syrup (seed 1); countered by procedure, prices, and the dry aphorism about committees. Banlist check: "histories disagree" is information, not elegy-perfume. No consolation; the last line is a capability, gone.
