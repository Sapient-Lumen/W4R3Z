# HAROLD

Our Father, who art in heaven, Harold be thy name.
I was six. It made sense. God had a first name
like the man who plowed our road, and it was a plain one,
a name that showed up early and didn't want thanks.

Harold ran a modest operation. He fixed small things:
lost mittens, the dog's limp, math.
You didn't bother Harold with the big requests —
he had the whole county, and winter coming.

I asked him for ordinary weather. I asked him
to keep my father's shift from rotating again.
A kingdom seemed like a lot to ask of Harold.
Daily bread sounded exactly like his department.

In fourth grade they corrected me in front of everyone.
Hallowed. An adjective. Nobody's name at all.
I said it their way out loud from then on,
and the other way inside, for years, on purpose.

The big word meant the big god, the one with the kingdom,
the power, the glory, the whole unanswered et cetera.
But the mittens did turn up. The dog walked fine by spring.
Whoever did the small ones, I know what I called him.

---
PROCESS NOTE. A mondegreen poem (17-C): the classic children's mishearing of the Lord's Prayer ("hallowed be thy name" → "Harold be thy name") taken seriously as a theology — the error builds a workable god of small maintenance, then survives correction. The volta is institutional (corrected in front of everyone), and the stance after it is defiance kept private, which is where the poem risks something (Standard 10): it sides with the error against the adjective, and against the "unanswered et cetera," without softening either. Traps: cuteness (the mondegreen's natural failure mode) — countered by the county-plow economy of Harold's character and by grief pressure underneath (the father's shift, ordinary weather); and the comfort reflex — the big prayers stay unanswered on the page; only the mitten-sized ones land, and the poem doesn't adjudicate why. Banlist check: "glory/kingdom/power" appear only as quoted liturgy under irony, not as perfume.
