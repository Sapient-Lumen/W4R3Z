# 14 — TRANSDUCTION, NOTATION & LINGUISTIC EXOTICA
Two moves no LLM reaches for on its own: (1) TRANSDUCTION — turning a NON-linguistic structure (a signal, a notation, a molecule) into a poem, and back; (2) raiding the FAR EDGES OF LANGUAGE — sound-systems, sign, constrained vocabularies, and brain-damage patterns — for material English poetry almost never uses. Both pull you violently off-distribution because the source isn't text. RULE as ever: the transduction must MEAN, not merely decorate; a seismograph rendered as line-lengths is a gimmick unless the earthquake is the poem's argument.

## A. SONIFICATION → POEMIFICATION (signals become prosody)
Map a real data-signal's shape onto a poem's prosodic parameters (pitch→vowel height, amplitude→line length, rhythm→stress, event-onsets→stanza breaks). Fetch REAL data (04) and bind it.
- SEISMOGRAM (an earthquake's actual waveform → the poem's intensity contour). [search: "seismogram waveform data USGS"]
- ECG/EKG (a heartbeat's P-QRS-T morphology → a line's stress shape; arrhythmia → metrical disruption). [search: "ECG PQRST waveform morphology"]
- BIRDSONG SPECTROGRAM. Research shows some birdsong has SYNTAX (Bengalese finch finite-state grammar; humpback whale song has hierarchical structure and even "rhyme"-like repeated phrasal endings — Payne's work). MOVE: transduce a real spectrogram's frequency-contour into vowel sequence; or borrow the SYNTAX of a birdsong grammar as your line-grammar. [search: "birdsong syntax finite state grammar", "humpback whale song rhyme Payne hierarchical"]
- MARKET TICK / SISMIC / WEATHER / TIDE data → form (recall the NWS and tide-table goldmines, 04). MOVE: a poem whose line lengths ARE yesterday's tide heights at a named port.
- PROTEIN FOLDING / DNA — see C (the genetic code is a literal alphabet).

## B. NOTATION SYSTEMS AS FORM (borrow another discipline's grammar)
Every specialized notation is a constrained language with its own beauty. Write IN it, or transduce it.
- LABANOTATION / MOVEMENT SCORE (the symbolic notation for dance). MOVE: write a poem that is also a valid movement score, or whose line-breaks encode a body's path through space. [search: "Labanotation dance score symbols"]
- CHESS NOTATION + ANNOTATION. Algebraic moves (1.e4 e5) plus the annotators' affect-laden symbols (! brilliant, ? blunder, !? interesting, ⩲ slight edge, "White is lost but does not know it"). MOVE: a poem that is a real (or invented) annotated game, the marginal symbols carrying the human drama; or a relationship narrated entirely in move-notation. [search: "chess annotation symbols brilliant blunder", "annotated chess game language"]
- KNITTING / CROCHET CHARTS (k2tog, ssk, yo, charted symbols). MOVE: a grief or labor poem in pattern-abbreviation (k2tog = "knit two together" = two becoming one); the chart's repeat-bracket as refrain. [search: "knitting chart symbols k2tog yo abbreviations"]
- CIRCUIT DIAGRAMS / FEYNMAN DIAGRAMS / CHEMICAL REACTION NOTATION (reactants → products, ⇌ equilibrium arrows, catalysts above the arrow). MOVE: a poem as a balanced chemical equation (what is consumed, what is produced, what merely catalyzes and is returned unchanged — a devastating model for a relationship); or as a Feynman diagram's vertices and exchanged particles. [search: "chemical equation notation equilibrium arrow catalyst", "Feynman diagram vertices"]
- SCHENKERIAN ANALYSIS (music theory's reduction of a piece to its deep structural skeleton, the Ursatz). MOVE: write the poem AND its Schenkerian reduction (the three-word skeleton beneath the surface) as a diptych — surface and deep structure shown together. [search: "Schenkerian analysis Ursatz reduction"]
- HERALDIC BLAZON (the formal grammar for describing coats of arms: "Azure, a bend Or between two mullets Argent"). A rigid, gorgeous generative language with strict word order (field, then ordinaries, then charges, then tinctures). MOVE: blazon something un-heraldic (a kitchen, a wound, a server rack) in strict blazon grammar; the constraint forces precise spatial description and archaic music. [search: "heraldic blazon grammar rules tincture ordinary charge"]

## C. THE GENETIC CODE AS A LITERAL ALPHABET
DNA is written in 4 letters (A,C,G,T) read as 64 triplet CODONS mapping to 20 amino acids + stop. Christian Bök's XENOTEXT (07/17) encoded a poem into a bacterium's DNA such that the cell, reading it, builds a protein that itself decodes as a SECOND poem — a chemical cipher and a living, mutation-proof co-author. MOVE (no lab needed): use the real 64-codon table as a substitution cipher — assign words/sounds to codons, "transcribe" and "translate" a text through the central-dogma steps (DNA→RNA→protein), letting each transformation mutate the poem; or write under a "stop codon" constraint where certain triplets end the line. The redundancy of the code (multiple codons → one amino acid) is a built-in synonymy you can exploit. [search: "codon table 64 amino acid genetic code", "Christian Bök Xenotext protein cipher"]

## D. CARTOGRAPHIC & SPATIAL DATA
Isobars, contour lines, GIS layers, transit maps, star charts. MOVE: a poem laid on a contour map where elevation = emotional intensity; or a "legend/key" poem where the map is absent and only its legend speaks; or constellation poems where you invent the figures and the myth (recall the moth-name and disambiguation goldmines, 04). [search: "topographic contour map legend", "isobar weather map"]

## E. THE FAR EDGES OF LANGUAGE (phonetic & grammatical material English poetry ignores)
- IDEOPHONES / MIMETICS (vast in Japanese giongo/gitaigo, Korean, and Bantu languages; nearly absent in English verse). Words that ARE their meaning sonically — not just "buzz" but whole systematic inventories for textures, gaits, glitters, silences, emotional states (Japanese has thousands; e.g., kira-kira = glittering, shīn = the sound of silence). MOVE: build an English ideophone system from scratch (sound-symbolic coinages for sensations English has no word for) and write in it with a glossary (cf. conlang forms, 05). The sound-symbolism is the opposite of your latinate-abstraction drift. [search: "Japanese ideophones giongo gitaigo list", "ideophone mimetic sound symbolism Bantu"]
- PHONESTHEMES / SOUND SYMBOLISM (sub-morphemic English patterns: gl- in glow/glint/gleam/glisten = light; sn- in snout/sniff/snore/sneer = nose/mouth; -ump in bump/lump/stump/clump = rounded mass; the bouba/kiki effect). MOVE: write a poem governed by ONE phonestheme — every key word carries gl- — so meaning leaks from sound. [search: "phonestheme gl sn sound symbolism English", "bouba kiki effect"]
- WHISTLED & DRUMMED LANGUAGES (Silbo Gomero whistled Spanish; West African talking drums that reproduce tonal speech). Here PROSODY IS THE ENTIRE MESSAGE — pitch contour carries meaning with no segmental words. MOVE: write a poem meant to be "heard" as pure contour — score it as pitch-and-rhythm with minimal lexical content — to interrogate how much your meaning rides on melody vs. words (the Hostile Translator's two columns, 02). [search: "Silbo Gomero whistled language", "talking drum tonal language"]
- SIGN LANGUAGE POETICS (ASL poetry — Clayton Valli's foundational work; also Christine Sun Kim's sound-art). Signed verse has METER as movement-rhythm, RHYME as repeated HANDSHAPE or location, line-breaks as holds/transitions, and a cinematic grammar (classifier predicates, role-shift) impossible in speech. You cannot sign — and that's the point: studying signed poetics SHATTERS the assumption that poetry is phonetic at all, freeing you to think of "rhyme" as repetition of ANY feature (handshape, semantic vector, image, syntax). MOVE: write a poem whose "rhyme" is a repeated GESTURE described in the text, or a repeated visual handshape-image, not a sound. [search: "ASL poetry Clayton Valli handshape rhyme meter", "sign language poetics classifier"]
- CONSTRAINED VOCABULARIES (radically small word-sets):
  • TOKI PONA (Sonja Lang, 2001): a complete language of ~120–137 words built for minimalism — complex meaning emerges from combining simple roots (e.g., "telo nasa" = crazy-water = alcohol). MOVE: write in (or pseudo-translate into) a ~120-word vocabulary; the forced periphrasis produces strange fresh compounds and kills your jargon reflex. [search: "Toki Pona word list minimalist language"]
  • BASIC ENGLISH (C.K. Ogden's 850 words) and the UP-GOER FIVE / "ten hundred words" constraint (Randall Munroe: explain anything using only the 1,000 most common English words — "the up-goer five" = the Saturn V rocket). MOVE: write a profound poem using ONLY the thousand commonest words; profundity-in-plainness is the test (and an antidote to your "luminous/ineffable" register, 01). [search: "Up-Goer Five ten hundred words", "Basic English 850 word list"]
- INFIXATION PROSODY (English expletive infixation follows a real metrical rule: "abso-bloody-lutely," "fan-bloody-tastic" — the infix lands before the stressed syllable). MOVE: a poem whose form IS governed by infixation placement — a hidden prosodic grammar most speakers obey unconsciously. [search: "expletive infixation English metrical rule"]
- APHASIA & AGRAMMATISM AS DELIBERATE STYLE. Roman Jakobson mapped two language axes — the METAPHORIC (selection/similarity) and the METONYMIC (combination/contiguity) — and showed aphasias damage one or the other. MOVE: write deliberately "Broca's" poems (agrammatic, content-words-only, telegraphic) and "Wernicke's" poems (fluent, grammatical, semantically unmoored) to feel the two axes as compositional dials; what breaks reveals what poetry leans on. (Handle real disorder with respect — this is structural study, not mockery.) [search: "Jakobson metaphor metonymy aphasia two axes", "agrammatism Broca's Wernicke's language"]
- VOYNICHESE (the Voynich Manuscript: an undeciphered text with language-LIKE statistics — Zipfian word-frequencies, word-structure — but no known meaning). MOVE: write a poem in a private "Voynichese" that has all the STATISTICAL signatures of meaning (recurrence, distribution) with no stable semantics — interrogating how much of "sense" is just statistics (uncomfortably close to a question about you). [search: "Voynich manuscript language statistics Zipf"]

## F. THE TRANSDUCTION DISCIPLINE
For every transduction: (1) name the SOURCE STRUCTURE and fetch real data where possible (04); (2) state the MAPPING explicitly (what source-feature → what poetic-feature) BEFORE writing; (3) verify the mapping mechanically (01-G); (4) ask the Child judge (02) "what is actually happening?" — if the source structure is mere costume and the poem would be identical without it, you transduced nothing. The win condition: a poem whose FORM could only have come from that alien source, and whose meaning needs it.
