\begin{abstract}
Monitoring an anonymous overlay creates its own witness channel: public alarms, release times, budget beacons, and later incident disclosures can reveal hidden control-plane state.
We retain the measurement-firewall pattern---rich telemetry stays closed-view and only explicitly budgeted outputs cross the boundary---but repair the alert accounting.
A detector's false-alarm and miss targets imply only a \emph{necessary lower bound} on any symmetric max-divergence guarantee; they are not a privacy certificate and do not prove that an alert fits under a cap.
We give the missing two-sided bound, an explicit counterexample to the old sufficiency reading, and a constructive randomized-response wrapper whose upper guarantee and fixed-slot composition can actually be checked.
We then reduce the Control-Plane Privacy Card (CPPC) to the fields needed to distinguish a target, a mechanism, an upper guarantee, a release schedule, and a threat window.
No deployment CPPC is claimed in this archive.
\end{abstract}

\section{Measurement firewall}

Let $X$ denote rich internal monitoring state and let $Y$ denote the entire public monitoring transcript.
The measurement firewall is a policy and mechanism boundary:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item internal detectors may inspect $X$;
\item every public bit, timestamp, omission, retry, beacon, commitment, and later reveal is included in $Y$;
\item an accountant is applied to the mechanism that generates $Y$, not merely to the detector's semantic payload;
\item publication halts before an unbudgeted state-dependent output occurs.
\end{enumerate}
A fixed schedule avoids an extra data-dependent timing channel, but schedule discipline alone is not a privacy guarantee.
Coarsening a release that already has a valid stability guarantee is post-processing; coarsening an unprotected raw report does not manufacture such a guarantee.\cite{dworkroth2014foundations,nist_sp800_226}

\section{Alert channels: necessity is not sufficiency}

Let $\sigma\in\{0,1\}$ index two adjacent control-plane states and let $B\in\{0,1\}$ be the complete public alarm output for one declared slot.
Write
\[
a=\Pr[B=1\mid\sigma=0],\qquad b=\Pr[B=0\mid\sigma=1].
\]
Thus $a$ is the false-alarm probability and $b$ is the miss probability.
State~1's stability definition is symmetric: both likelihood-ratio directions must hold for every event.\cite{series:state1}

\begin{corollary}[Two-sided necessary alert tax]
\label{cor:alert-tax}
If the alarm channel is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ stable in the bit convention of State~1, then
\[
1-b\le 2^\varepsilon a+\delta,
\qquad
1-a\le 2^\varepsilon b+\delta.
\]
Consequently, if only performance targets $a\le\alpha$ and $b\le\beta$ are known, then
\[
\varepsilon\ge
\max\!\left\{
\log_2\!\frac{1-\beta-\delta}{\alpha},
\log_2\!\frac{1-\alpha-\delta}{\beta}
\right\},
\tag{1}
\]
whenever the displayed numerators are positive.  A positive numerator divided by a zero denominator means the lower bound is infinite.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Apply the two stability inequalities to $\{B=1\}$ and $\{B=0\}$.
Then use $1-b\ge1-\beta$, $a\le\alpha$, $1-a\ge1-\alpha$, and $b\le\beta$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{The non-substitution rule.}
Equation~(1) is a \emph{necessary} lower bound on any valid upper guarantee.
The fact that the lower bound is below a policy cap does not show that a mechanism satisfying the cap exists, and it does not measure realized privacy loss.
For example,
\[
\Pr[B=1\mid\sigma=0]=0,
\qquad
\Pr[B=1\mid\sigma=1]=0.9
\]
meets the targets $\alpha=0.02$ and $\beta=0.10$, yet admits no finite pure-stability parameter and no $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ guarantee with $\delta<0.9$.
The old inference ``$\log_2(45)\approx5.49<6$, therefore the alert fits under a six-bit cap'' was therefore invalid.

\section{A constructive alert wrapper}

A cap requires an upper guarantee from a named mechanism.
The simplest auditable construction for one bit is symmetric randomized response.\cite{warner1965randomizedresponse}

\begin{theorem}[Randomized-response upper guarantee]
\label{thm:rr}
Let $B$ be any internal alarm bit, possibly randomized and state-dependent, and publish
\[
\widetilde B=B\oplus N,
\qquad N\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(q),
\qquad 0<q\le\tfrac12,
\]
with $N$ independent of all state and detector randomness.
Then the channel from $\sigma$ to $\widetilde B$ is pure stable with
\[
\varepsilon_{\rm RR}=\log_2\!\frac{1-q}{q}.
\tag{2}
\]
The bound is tight when the internal bit is deterministic and opposite in the two states.
If the internal detector has false-alarm and miss probabilities $a_0,b_0$, the public errors are
\[
a=q+(1-2q)a_0,
\qquad
b=q+(1-2q)b_0.
\tag{3}
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For either public output $y$, its probability under any state lies in $[q,1-q]$.
Hence every likelihood ratio in either direction is at most $(1-q)/q$.
Equation~(3) follows by conditioning on whether $N$ flips the internal bit.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Fixed-slot composition]
If exactly $r$ predeclared slots each emit an RR-protected state-dependent bit satisfying Theorem~\ref{thm:rr}, then basic adaptive composition gives at most
\[
r\log_2\!\frac{1-q}{q}
\]
bits.
A data-dependent choice to emit, omit, advance, or delay a slot is an additional public channel and is not covered by this count unless it is included in the mechanism proof.
\end{corollary}

\paragraph{Consistent toy drill.}
For $q=0.1$, Equation~(2) gives
\[
\varepsilon_{\rm RR}=\log_2 9=3.169925001\text{ bits}.
\]
If the internal toy detector is perfect, Equation~(3) gives public errors $a=b=0.1$, matching the lower bound exactly.
A six-bit cap therefore certifies one such fixed-slot release.  For two slots, basic composition supplies the generic certificate
$2\log_2 9=6.339850003$ bits, so this card cannot certify two releases under a six-bit cap without a tighter source-bound joint analysis.
The number $6.339850003$ is an upper bound for the declared mechanism class, not a claim that every concrete two-release detector has exactly that loss.
This conclusion is valid because it uses a mechanism upper bound, not because a performance lower bound happened to be smaller than six.
Also, $q=0.1$ can never achieve a public false-alarm rate below $0.1$; the previous pairing with a $0.02$ target was internally inconsistent.

\begin{corollary}[Cap inversion for a generic fixed-slot RR certificate]
\label{cor:rr-cap-inversion}
For $r$ fixed RR-protected slots and a pure-stability cap $C$ bits, the basic-composition certificate fits whenever
\[
q\ge q_{\min}(C,r):=\frac{1}{1+2^{C/r}}.
\tag{4}
\]
With $C=6$ and $r=2$, this requires $q\ge1/9$; for a perfect internal detector the public false-alarm and miss probabilities are therefore at least $1/9$.
Equation~(4) designs a mechanism whose generic certificate fits the cap.  It does not prove that a smaller-$q$ implementation lacks a tighter joint proof.
\end{corollary}

\section{Delay, commitments, and threat windows}

\begin{proposition}[Delay is not eventual-observer amplification]
If an observer eventually receives the same plaintext report $Y$, changing only its release time does not improve the stability of the eventual transcript.
Delay can change a time-bounded threat model, but then the CPPC must name that threat window and treat any later plaintext reveal as a new release.
\end{proposition}

A fixed delay can still be operationally useful: it removes event-time precision from the declared window and can support incident response before disclosure.
It must not be described as reducing eventual information by itself.
Likewise, a bare hash of a low-entropy alert transcript is not a hiding commitment: an observer may enumerate candidate transcripts.
Use a commitment scheme with an explicit hiding property, randomness, domain separation, and metadata policy when commitment confidentiality is needed.\cite{pedersen1991distributedprovers}
Opening the commitment later releases the plaintext and must be accounted at that time.

\section{Control-Plane Privacy Cards}

A CPPC is a signed, diffable declaration of the public monitoring channel.
It is not a proof of implementation correctness.
At minimum it records:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item claim scope: \texttt{exposure\_nf\_id}, \texttt{tw\_id}, and any \texttt{state\_decl\_id};
\item adjacency, observer, and the complete public transcript, including timing, omissions, beacons, commitments, and later reveals;
\item the named release mechanism and parameters, not merely false-alarm/miss targets;
\item a source-bound upper guarantee $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ and a separate field for any necessary utility lower bound;
\item fixed release slots, maximum state-dependent release count, and behavior after budget exhaustion;
\item utility evidence for $a_0,b_0$ and the resulting public $a,b$;
\item composition/accountant rule, cap, units, and expiry;
\item integrity metadata and an explicit evidence status.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Budget-consistency audit.}
From public artifacts an auditor can check the card signature and log inclusion, compare the declared fixed slots with observed releases, recompute Equation~(2), count state-dependent outputs, and verify that any beacon is static or derived only from already public data.
The auditor generally cannot infer whether internal telemetry was complete, whether the detector had the stated $a_0,b_0$, or whether undeclared channels existed without reproducibility, attestation, or runtime evidence.\cite{series:evalnotary,series:opB,rfc9162_ct,newman2022sigstore}

\section{Related work}

Differential privacy and its hypothesis-testing interpretation supply the two-sided stability language used above.\cite{dworkroth2014foundations,balle2020rdp_testing,nist_sp800_226}
Odometers and filters address adaptive composition, but they still require each released mechanism and its stopping behavior to be inside the accounting model.\cite{rogers2016odometers,lecuuyer2021practical}
Continual-observation mechanisms are preferable to ad hoc event-triggered disclosure when a stream must be public.\cite{dwork2010continual,chan2010continual,jain2023priceContinual,cohen2024continual_lowerbounds}
Confidence sequences and e-values help operators monitor closed-view streams without invalid optional-stopping inference; they do not automatically privatize a public alarm.\cite{howard2021confseq,ramdas2024evalues,wang2022ebh}
CPPCs borrow the disclosure and provenance posture of model cards, datasheets, SBOMs, and transparency logs.\cite{mitchell2019modelcards,gebru2021datasheets,spdx_overview,rfc9162_ct}

\section{Takeaways}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item Performance targets impose necessary privacy-loss lower bounds, not sufficient privacy certificates.
\item A cap needs a named mechanism with an upper guarantee; randomized response provides one simple binary example.
\item Release count and timing are part of the public transcript.  Fixed slots compose; data-dependent omissions or trigger times require their own proof.
\item Delay changes a declared threat window but does not reduce eventual plaintext leakage.  A commitment must actually be hiding, and its opening is a separate release.
\item A CPPC is useful only when it separates targets, mechanism parameters, upper guarantees, utility evidence, release count, and evidence status.
\item The card in this note is illustrative and publication-ineligible; external hostile review and a real deployment packet remain missing.
\end{itemize}
