# 074 — Continuity, non-churn, and durable attachment

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must treat continuity, non-churn, and durable attachment as morally important rather than as administrative convenience or private sentiment, because people cannot reliably belong, heal, learn, remember, trust, or plan when homes, schools, carers, clinicians, records, routes, routines, and institutions keep resetting them to zero.

## Why this matters

The archive already protects home, care continuity, companionship, childhood, dependable institutions, public memory, and humane pace. But it still lacks a direct canon rule for **serial restart itself**: the condition in which people are repeatedly moved, reassigned, re-documented, re-explained, re-evaluated, re-onboarded, or reattached under systems that treat churn as normal background administration.

That omission matters because many injuries arrive not as one spectacular loss but as accumulated interruption. A child can be technically schooled while cycling through teachers, placements, and classmates often enough that attachment and learning keep fraying. A patient can be formally covered while repeating the same story to new clinicians, losing medication continuity at each transfer, and never becoming known over time. A tenant, migrant, foster youth, unhoused person, disabled person, prisoner leaving custody, elder in long-term care, or worker on unstable schedules can be kept in motion just enough that documents, relationships, routines, and trust never thicken into a livable world.

The opposite mistake is also possible. Continuity can be romanticized into entrapment, inherited hierarchy, forced family endurance, stagnant institutions, or the demand to stay loyal to harmful places and relations simply because they are familiar. The archive therefore needs a middle rule: protect continuity where it lets lives become inhabitable, but pair it with revisability, safe exit, and justified change.

## Core claims

### 1. Continuity is often a constitutive good, not merely a means

Many human goods become real only over time: being known by name, trusting a clinician, learning a neighborhood, growing into a craft, maintaining friendship, mourning in place, recognizing familiar routes, or inhabiting a room or routine long enough that it starts to hold memory. A world of endless handoff and reset is not neutral movement; it is often a direct loss.

### 2. Churn redistributes burden downward

People with money, status, and spare capacity can survive turnover by buying continuity: private records support, the same doctor, the same school district, legal help, cloud backup, better housing, quieter offices, or time to re-explain everything. Others pay in repetition, delay, mistrust, broken treatment, lost paperwork, and social thinness.

### 3. Institutional continuity matters even when no intimate bond is at stake

Continuity is not only about family or close relationships. It also matters when the same teacher, interpreter, librarian, caseworker, care team, route, public office, or record system stays intelligible long enough that ordinary users do not have to start over every time they seek help.

### 4. Durable attachment should be distinguished from coercive staying

The archive should defend durable attachment without treating all persistence as good. Some relations, jobs, institutions, and places should be easy to leave. The relevant claim is not that people must remain where they began, but that a humane order should not force needless turnover or make stability itself a luxury.

### 5. Good handoff is morally different from serial rupture

Change is sometimes necessary: children grow, people move, clinicians rotate, organizations reorganize, and unsafe situations require exit. The question is whether transitions preserve memory, trust, records, obligations, and recognition well enough that change remains navigable rather than becoming identity-stripping disruption. `archive/094-handoff-justice-discharge-discipline-and-no-transfer-into-nothing.md` now sharpens the point that a humane handoff needs the next step to be actually live, not merely assigned on paper.

### 6. Predictable worlds support agency

People act better when some parts of life are stable enough to learn. Stable routes, familiar staff, repeated schedules, enduring community institutions, and non-chaotic records reduce fear and cognitive load. They make voice, accountability, and planning more realistic because people know where to return and whom to ask.

### 7. The moral core is inhabitable continuity, not frozen order

A good order should not maximize stasis. It should secure enough durable attachment, continuity of recognition, and non-chaotic transition that lives can gather history without forcing anyone to remain under domination, neglect, or inherited script.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask whether people can remain known across time without constant self-reconstruction

When evaluating homes, schools, clinics, care systems, workplaces, welfare offices, migration systems, shelters, detention, and digital services, ask whether people must repeatedly prove, narrate, translate, or rebuild themselves from scratch, or whether memory and recognition travel with them in humane ways.

### 2. Prefer stable contact points and continuity-preserving handoff

A humane order should value the same teacher where possible, the same clinician where appropriate, durable case relationships, stable records, intelligible transfers, and named responsibility rather than anonymous queue logic, rotating indifference, or permanent onboarding.

### 3. Count needless displacement and avoidable turnover as direct losses

If schools churn children, care systems rotate staff beyond functional necessity, shelters or institutions keep moving people without reason, or public systems redesign themselves so often that ordinary users lose competence and trust, the archive should count that as a direct degradation rather than as neutral flexibility.

### 4. Make continuity portable rather than gated by wealth

Continuity should not depend on buying into the right zip code, private provider network, or stable enclave. Good systems carry memory, entitlements, relationships, and usable records across movement where movement is necessary.

### 5. Pair continuity with exit, correction, and revision

The relevant aim is not forced staying. It is a world in which people can build durable attachments and histories, leave harmful situations, and undergo necessary transition without being socially or administratively erased.

## Failure modes

- **serial-restart life**: people repeatedly reapply, re-explain, and re-prove themselves because systems do not carry memory forward.
- **teacher-or-caseworker roulette**: children, patients, or dependent adults are cycled through staff so often that trust and continuity never accumulate.
- **temporary-by-default dwelling**: shelters, camps, placements, dormitories, and rented rooms keep people indoors but not stably situated.
- **handoff without memory**: transfer occurs, but records, context, and obligations do not travel well enough to protect the person undergoing change.
- **premium continuity**: the affluent buy the same doctor, school, neighborhood, caregiver, or legal advisor while everyone else lives inside turnover.
- **continuity as entrapment**: appeals to stability are used to trap people inside abusive families, dead-end institutions, stagnant hierarchies, or unsafe locations.
- **interface churn governance**: portals, procedures, route maps, office names, and service pathways change so often that ordinary learned competence never sticks.

## Guardrails

- Do not romanticize continuity into forced staying, family coercion, inherited hierarchy, or institutional stagnation.
- Do not assume every change is harmful; some movement, reorganization, and exit are necessary and just.
- Do not treat continuity as valuable only in intimate life; public and institutional continuity matter too.
- Do not mistake digitized records alone for continuity when users still have to retell, resend, or reprove everything in practice.
- Do not let efficiency rhetoric, staffing models, or optimization language hide avoidable churn imposed mainly on those with the least buffer.
- Do not reserve durable attachment for people rich enough to buy their way out of turnover.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order lets people build histories with places, persons, and institutions, so necessary change does not continually reset life back to zero.
