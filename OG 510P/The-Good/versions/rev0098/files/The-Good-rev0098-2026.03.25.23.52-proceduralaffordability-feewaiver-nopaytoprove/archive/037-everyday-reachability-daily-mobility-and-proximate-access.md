# 037 — Everyday reachability, daily mobility, and proximate access

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must treat everyday reachability, daily mobility, and proximate access as morally central rather than as mere transport engineering, because work, school, care, friendship, culture, and civic life do not genuinely exist for people when ordinary destinations can be reached only through dangerous, exhausting, unaffordable, inaccessible, or car-dependent journeys.

## Why this matters

The archive is now strong on dwelling, accessibility, time sovereignty, affirmative flourishing, movement across borders, and non-ordeal access through institutions. But it still lacks a direct rule for the **ordinary spatial path** between these goods. That gap matters because a person can be formally housed, enrolled, entitled, and legally free while still being stranded by distance, hostile streets, broken sidewalks, inaccessible stations, unreliable service, unsafe crossings, fare burden, or the practical requirement to own and operate a car.

A good order should therefore ask not only whether valued destinations exist, but whether people can reach them within humane bounds of time, cost, safety, energy, and dignity. The issue is not mobility for its own sake. It is whether everyday life is spatially arranged so people can actually get to school, clinics, work, care, parks, libraries, shops, and one another without sacrificing health, income, autonomy, or belonging on the way.

## Core claims

### 1. Opportunity is a reachability question, not only an infrastructure question

Road length, station count, lane speed, or project spend are not the right moral bottom line. The more basic question is what people can **actually reach** from where they live, at what cost, and under what conditions.

### 2. Proximity is morally important because it protects time, care, and ordinary spontaneity

When daily necessities and common goods are near enough to reach easily, people retain more usable time, more energy for care and friendship, more room for play and civic participation, and less dependence on expensive or exhausting travel. Proximity is therefore not only a convenience gain. It can be a direct protection for agency and ordinary life.

### 3. Daily mobility systems can dominate before they obviously exclude

A person may technically be able to travel and still be governed by the terms of movement: long waits, unsafe roads, repeated transfers, fare burden, exposure to harassment, inaccessible vehicles or stops, punishing heat, missing sidewalks, surveillance-heavy enforcement, or the standing assumption that anyone without a car must absorb extraordinary friction. These are not merely transport annoyances. They are ways a social order shifts the cost of access onto the already burdened.

### 4. Public space is part of access, not an ornamental extra

A good order does not only connect homes to paid destinations. It also preserves parks, squares, sidewalks, libraries, courts, community facilities, and other low-threshold spaces where people can rest, gather, play, encounter difference, and belong without having to buy admission. Everyday reachability therefore includes access to the public realm itself.

### 5. Planning should connect people to opportunities, not only move vehicles efficiently

The archive should favor accessibility thinking over throughput fetishism. Faster traffic can coexist with worse human access if jobs, care, and public life are pushed farther away, crossings become hostile, or mobility depends on private vehicle ownership. The relevant design question is whether land use, housing, public services, and transport are arranged to make ordinary opportunity more reachable.

### 6. Physical and digital reach should be read together without being collapsed

Digital access can reduce some travel burdens and widen communication, but it cannot fully replace bodily presence, public space, embodied care, or the need to reach schools, clinics, shops, workplaces, and one another. A good order should therefore reject both digital substitution optimism and the idea that connectivity alone solves spatial exclusion.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask what ordinary life can be reached within humane bounds

When evaluating cities, regions, towns, campuses, housing policy, transport systems, or digital service substitution, ask what people can reach within reasonable time, cost, safety, accessibility, and reliability constraints from where they actually live.

### 2. Prefer proximity plus integrated mobility

Favor mixed and connected neighborhoods, safe walking and wheeling, reliable and legible public transport, fare structures that do not punish poverty, and public-space networks that let people move through ordinary life without constant danger, heroic planning, or car dependence.

### 3. Treat service deserts, dangerous access, and forced car dependence as value losses

If people can reach work, school, health care, food, culture, or social life only by absorbing extreme travel time, dangerous road conditions, inaccessible routes, or compulsory vehicle ownership, the archive should count that as a direct loss to time, equality, health, and agency rather than as neutral background geography.

### 4. Read schools, care, work, and public space together

The archive should judge everyday reachability across domains at once. A place that is good for commuters but bad for caregivers, children, older people, disabled people, or non-drivers is not well-ordered merely because some employment center is connected to some road network.

## Failure modes

- **throughput fetish**: systems optimize vehicle speed, road capacity, or commuter flow while ordinary people lose access to nearby life.
- **forced car dependence**: the standing design assumption is that serious participation requires private vehicle ownership and the costs that come with it.
- **service-desert normalization**: long travel for food, care, school, or culture is treated as natural rather than as an access failure.
- **dangerous-access tax**: children, older people, disabled people, low-income households, and non-drivers absorb more danger, heat, distance, harassment, and fatigue simply to do ordinary things.
- **public-space starvation**: parks, libraries, plazas, sidewalks, and low-threshold gathering places are underprovided, unsafe, or effectively unreachable.
- **digital-substitution alibi**: institutions use apps, portals, or remote service rhetoric to excuse the decay of physical access where bodily presence still matters.

## Guardrails

- Do not romanticize density, urbanity, or transit-oriented design if they are delivered through overcrowding, displacement, or exclusion.
- Do not assume the same access pattern fits every rural, suburban, urban, or remote context; the standard is humane reachability, not one settlement template.
- Do not treat all travel reduction as good; chosen mobility, exploration, and long-distance ties can be genuine goods.
- Do not let accessibility language ignore safety from violence, traffic injury, or environmental exposure along the route itself.
- Do not let climate or efficiency rhetoric excuse designs that strand caregivers, children, older persons, or disabled travelers.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order makes ordinary life reachable on humane terms, rather than placing valued destinations behind dangerous, exhausting, or dependency-producing journeys.
