# 089 — Presumptive permission, bounded prior approval, and non-licensed ordinary life

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must not organize ordinary life as a standing request for permission, because people cannot flourish when harmless movement, gathering, repair, caregiving, small-scale making, public presence, and everyday initiative begin only after sponsor approval, gatekeeper signoff, or discretionary licensing; prior approval has legitimate but bounded jurisdiction where serious prospective risks are concrete and hard to repair after the fact, but a good order should begin most ordinary action from **presumptive permission**, clear public rules, and reviewable accountability rather than from default authorization.

## Why this matters

The archive is already strong on non-domination, administrative dignity, fair hearing, public integrity, public realm, association, hospitality, and ordinary legitimacy. But it still lacks a direct canon rule against a recurrent deformation: **approval dependence**.

That deformation appears when ordinary people must ask permission to gather peacefully, to remain in shared space, to start tiny livelihoods, to alter a dwelling in minor ways, to use common facilities, to host a meeting, to bring a helper, to carry ordinary tools, to keep documents usable across systems, or simply to continue an activity that is harmless unless and until a specific problem can be shown. It also appears when systems preserve legal openness in theory while building choke points through sponsor signatures, remote permit portals, scarce appointments, opaque approvals, or discretionary waivers that insiders can obtain more easily than everyone else.

In such settings people do not merely face occasional coordination rules. They learn to live by advance appeasement. They moderate dissent, avoid improvisation, seek brokers, flatter officeholders, and treat everyday initiative as something granted from above rather than ordinarily theirs. A salient theory of the good therefore needs a cleaner rule. Some permits are justified. Some licensing is necessary. Some prior review is indispensable where prospective harms are grave, difficult to reverse, or impossible to correct after the fact. But a good order should reserve prior approval for such bounded cases instead of making authorization the background grammar of ordinary life.

## Core claims

### 1. Prior approval is not a neutral administrative detail

To require advance permission is to place action under another’s discretionary control before anything has gone wrong. Even when approval is often granted, the requirement itself changes the social meaning of action. It teaches people that everyday agency is conditional.

### 2. A rule-of-law order should presume liberty for ordinary persons and justification for official restraint

The archive should treat it as morally important that ordinary people may usually act unless a clear public rule specifically forbids or conditions the act, while officials should need articulated legal authority to stop, deny, or condition them. This asymmetry helps keep public power from quietly expanding through ad hoc gatekeeping.

### 3. Notification is morally thinner than authorization

Some activities require coordination: a large march, road use, dangerous construction, highly hazardous production, or technically sensitive environmental discharge. But the need for coordination does not automatically justify a regime in which an authority may simply say yes or no at broad discretion. In many cases notification, registration, inspection, or ex post enforcement can protect the relevant public interest without turning the activity into a privilege.

### 4. Prior approval has legitimate but bounded jurisdiction

A good order may require strong ex ante checks where risks are serious, prospective, and not realistically reparable later: major pollution, dangerous building works, high-risk medicine, explosives, large hazardous operations, or genuinely exceptional security-sensitive settings. The point is not that all licensing is domination. The point is that authorization should answer to concrete risk, not bureaucratic habit, institutional convenience, or status policing.

### 5. Approval dependence redistributes power upward and outward

People with money, lawyers, consultants, insider contacts, polished documents, employer backing, or family reputation generally navigate approvals more easily. Everyone else pays in delay, lost chances, fear of technical error, brokerage costs, and strategic silence. What presents itself as neutral governance often functions as a selective tax on initiative.

### 6. Cheap digital clearance can scale permission dependence without making it more just

Apps, portals, QR codes, automated screening, geofenced access, database checks, and digital permit flows can make approval systems faster for administrators while still leaving ordinary life too conditional. Lower processing cost is not itself a moral reason to expand the number of things that require preclearance.

### 7. Ex post accountability often protects the public better than universal preclearance

Where conduct can be governed by clear rules, monitoring, complaint, repair, and proportionate sanction after actual misuse, the archive should hesitate before endorsing universal advance permission. A society that tries to prevent every possible misuse before people may act often produces more dependency, corruption opportunity, and civic thinning than one that lets ordinary action proceed under fair rules.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask whether the activity really needs ex ante permission

When evaluating assemblies, neighborhood use, schools, libraries, public services, small enterprise, housing adjustments, common-space rules, civic events, local trade, and digital access systems, ask whether the activity could instead be governed by general rules, notification, inspection, and ex post accountability.

### 2. Demand a concrete risk case for prior approval

Where a permit or license is proposed, require a clear account of the harm being prevented, why later enforcement would be inadequate, and why the chosen approval burden is proportionate to that risk.

### 3. Keep unavoidable approval systems low-friction, non-rent-seeking, and reviewable

Where permits are genuinely needed, they should be clear, timely, affordable, portable where possible, transparent in criteria, limited in discretion, and backed by accessible appeal and human fallback. A permit regime that mainly manufactures delay, brokerage, or favor-trading has already become morally suspect.

### 4. Prefer facilitative notification to discretionary permission where coordination is the issue

If the real need is preparation, routing, safety planning, or recordkeeping, the archive should generally favor notification or simple registration over a system that turns the activity into a privilege to be granted or withheld.

### 5. Count permit scarcity, sponsor dependency, and broker dependence as substantive harms

If people can exercise a right or start an ordinary project only through hard-to-get appointments, discretionary letters, sponsor signatures, unofficial fixers, or opaque local indulgence, the archive should count that as a direct loss of equal standing, not merely as regrettable red tape.

## What this changes in the archive

### 1. It sharpens association and assembly by drawing a brighter line between facilitation and permission

`archive/044-association-assembly-and-organized-counterpower.md` already rejects permit choke points and retaliation. This note adds the cleaner general rule: coordination needs do not by themselves justify treating peaceful collective action as something that exists only after official leave is granted.

### 2. It thickens public realm by opposing permission-to-remain life

`archive/056-public-realm-common-world-and-low-threshold-shared-life.md` already protects low-threshold shared presence. This note adds that public space is also degraded when harmless staying, meeting, vending, resting, or using common facilities is quietly redescribed as a revocable license available mainly to the documented, connected, or commercially legible.

### 3. It clarifies administrative burden by naming approval dependence as one of bureaucracy’s deepest harms

`archive/036-administrative-burden-procedural-dignity-and-non-ordeal-access.md` already protects people against repeated proof, queues, and humiliation. This note adds that many ordeals exist because institutions start from the assumption that action must first be cleared rather than ordinarily allowed.

### 4. It strengthens legibility and hospitality by refusing brokered ordinary life

`archive/061-legibility-wayfinding-and-navigable-complexity.md` already resists maze access, and `archive/065-hospitality-welcome-and-low-friction-arrival.md` already protects humane first thresholds. This note adds that orientation failure is often permission failure in disguise: people cannot tell which acts require approval, which office actually matters, or whether a requirement is lawful, optional, or merely customary. A humane order should not force newcomers or ordinary users to hire guides simply to discover whether they are allowed to proceed.

### 5. It gives public integrity a cleaner anti-favor test

`archive/047-public-integrity-anti-corruption-and-non-kleptocratic-rule.md` already rejects bribery, patronage, and insider access. This note adds that sprawling approval systems are often fertile soil for petty corruption, sponsor politics, and informal toll-taking even when the rulebook looks orderly on paper.

## Failure modes

- **permit choke point**: ordinary action survives in theory but is throttled by inaccessible or discretionary approvals.
- **approval creep**: more and more harmless acts are pulled into licensing, preclearance, or gatekeeper signoff without a fresh risk case.
- **brokered ordinary life**: insiders, fixers, landlords, employers, or politically connected intermediaries become necessary to do basic things.
- **notification as disguised permission**: a notice regime is treated in practice as a request that can be stalled, denied, or selectively enforced.
- **digital preclearance sprawl**: portal design, QR access, appointment scarcity, or database matching quietly turns daily life into constant pre-authorization.
- **favor governance**: permits, letters, and exceptions become channels for patronage, bribes, selective mercy, or status filtering.
- **safety pretext inflation**: vague invocations of order, image, or security are used to justify expansive approval systems for low-risk activity.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat all licensing or permitting as morally defective; some activities create serious prospective harms that genuinely require prior review.
- Do not let presumptive permission become a slogan for abandoning labor, accessibility, public-health, or environmental protections.
- Do not confuse notification with permission; the former may coordinate and facilitate while the latter may convert a right or ordinary liberty into a privilege.
- Do not insist on ex post enforcement alone where harms are highly irreversible or where victims cannot realistically protect themselves after the fact.
- Do not let “low-friction” rhetoric erase the rights of neighbors, workers, disabled users, or ecosystems affected by real risks.
- Do not forget that discretion can be reduced not only by abolishing approval, but also by clear criteria, portability, appeals, sunset review, and anti-corruption checks where approval remains justified.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order does not make ordinary harmless life into a standing request for permission; it reserves prior approval for bounded high-risk cases and otherwise governs through clear rules, facilitative notice where needed, and reviewable accountability.
