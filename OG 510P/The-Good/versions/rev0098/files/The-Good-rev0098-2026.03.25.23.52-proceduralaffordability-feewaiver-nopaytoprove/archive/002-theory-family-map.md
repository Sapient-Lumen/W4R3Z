# 002 — Theory-family map

## One-line thesis

The archive should treat the major theory families of the good as **lenses with partial jurisdiction**, not as rival empires that must each explain everything alone.

## Why this matters

Philosophical arguments about the good often fail by overreach. Hedonic theories catch something real about suffering and enjoyment, but flatten agency and meaning. Desire theories honor the subject’s standpoint, but inherit distorted preferences. Objective-list views capture goods people can fail to recognize, but risk paternalism. Virtue, capability, care, contractualist, perfectionist, and republican approaches all reveal something load-bearing, but none should be presumed complete merely because it is elegant.

A strong archive needs a map of what each family sees clearly, what it tends to miss, and where hybridization is not confusion but progress.

## Working family map

### 1. Hedonic and experiential views

These foreground pleasure, suffering, affect, and lived felt quality. Their strength is moral seriousness about pain and joy. Their weakness is under-reading agency, meaning, truth, and degraded preferences.

### 2. Desire-satisfaction and preference-based views

These foreground what agents actually want or would want under better conditions. Their strength is respect for the first-person standpoint. Their weakness is that desire can be manipulated, adaptive, ignorant, or self-destructive.

### 3. Objective-list views

These hold that some goods matter whether or not they are currently desired: friendship, knowledge, achievement, health, autonomy, and so on. Their strength is that they can criticize damaged preference sets. Their weakness is the risk of authoritative list-making without adequate justification.

### 4. Virtue and eudaimonist views

These ask what kind of being one should become and what a flourishing life looks like when character, judgment, and practice are central. Their strength is integrative depth. Their weakness is that they can become vague on institutions and distributive conflict.

### 5. Consequentialist views

These ask what outcomes are best and how action should answer to those outcomes. Their strength is seriousness about aggregate effects and impartial scope. Their weakness is pressure toward over-aggregation and insufficient respect for status constraints.

### 6. Deontic and contractualist views

These ask which principles, permissions, duties, and forms of mutual justification can govern us without one person merely using another. Their strength is respect for standing and constraint. Their weakness is occasional thinness about the positive shape of flourishing.

### 7. Capability views

These shift focus from resources or mental states to what persons are actually able to do and be. Their strength is sensitivity to freedom, diversity of conversion factors, and the difference between nominal and real opportunity. Their weakness is the difficulty of choosing and ranking capabilities.

### 8. Care and relational views

These foreground dependency, vulnerability, care work, attachment, and the moral salience of relationships. Their strength is realism about how beings actually survive and develop. Their weakness is possible under-specification of public rules and limits.

### 9. Republican and public-reason views

These foreground freedom from domination, fair standing, and institutions that can justify common rules to those bound by them. Their strength is institutional seriousness. Their weakness is that they can under-describe intimate and existential goods.

### 10. Meaning and perfectionist views

These foreground significance, excellence, achievement, and objectively worthwhile forms of life. Their strength is aspiration beyond comfort. Their weakness is risk of ranking persons by elite ideals or imposing controversial pictures of human excellence.

## Working use

The archive should treat these families as a **diagnostic ensemble**:

- when a proposal looks efficient, ask what deontic or domination costs it hides;
- when a proposal looks principled, ask what suffering it neglects;
- when a proposal looks freedom-enhancing, ask whether people can actually convert opportunity into life;
- when a proposal looks humane, ask whether it can scale institutionally;
- when a proposal looks uplifting, ask whether it mistakes one culture’s ideal for universal authority.

## Guardrails

- Do not force a premature winner among theory families.
- Do not let hybridization become incoherence by failing to say which problem each borrowed piece solves.
- Do not mistake familiar classroom triads for the full moral landscape.
- Do not treat theoretical disagreement as a reason to stop making judgments.

## Compression rule for the archive

The archive should use theory families the way a navigator uses instruments: none is sovereign, but a reading is more trustworthy when several instruments converge and their failure modes are known.
