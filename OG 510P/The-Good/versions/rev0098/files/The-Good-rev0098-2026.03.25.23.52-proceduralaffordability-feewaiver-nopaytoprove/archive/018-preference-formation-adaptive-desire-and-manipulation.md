# 018 — Preference formation: adaptive desire and manipulation

## One-line thesis

A theory of the good should treat expressed preferences as **morally important evidence but not as sovereign verdicts** when they are formed under deprivation, domination, humiliation, ignorance, or manipulative choice architecture.

## Why this matters

The archive already rejects simple preference-satisfaction as a complete theory of the good, but it still needs a disciplined rule for how to use preference without either worshipping it or casually overriding it. That missing rule matters because many damaged worlds teach people not merely to endure deprivation, but to want less, expect less, hide injury, or mistake engineered steering for self-direction. A good theory should therefore ask not only **what someone chooses**, but also **how the wanting was formed**.

## Core distinctions

### 1. Preference has presumptive standing

What people say they want matters. First-person reports often reveal pain, attachment, aspiration, fear, and local knowledge that institutions or theorists cannot simply infer from outside. The archive should therefore begin from a rebuttable presumption that stated preference has moral relevance.

### 2. Preference is not self-authenticating

A reported desire can still be shaped by coercive dependence, propaganda, humiliation, addictive design, narrowed horizons, or long adaptation to the unavailable. The archive should not treat “she chose it” or “they no longer ask for more” as decisive proof that a condition serves their good.

### 3. Adaptive preference is different from reflective endorsement

Some preferences become smaller because circumstances are harsh rather than because the person has found a deeper or freer good. The archive should distinguish **reflective revision** from **adaptive resignation**. The difference is not whether a preference is unusual or low-status, but whether it emerged under conditions that support informed, non-humiliated, non-captive agency.

### 4. Manipulation is different from persuasion

Not every influence is illegitimate. Education, argument, example, and invitation are normal parts of social life. The archive’s concern is influence that bypasses, distorts, or exploits deliberation through trickery, pressure, opacity, or engineered vulnerability rather than through reasons the target can actually assess.

## A working audit for preference-sensitive judgment

### 1. Look for deformation signals

Treat the following as warning lights rather than automatic disqualifiers:

- persistent deprivation or scarcity that has trained expectations downward;
- coercive dependency on employers, partners, landlords, platforms, or states;
- informational blockage, propaganda, or systematic deception;
- humiliating norms that make self-erasure look virtuous or inevitable;
- addictive or manipulative interface design that steers choice without clear awareness;
- option sets so truncated that “choice” mainly records captivity.

### 2. Repair conditions before overruling preference

When possible, improve the choice environment before declaring a person mistaken about their own good. Expand exit options, raise material floors, reduce fear of retaliation, disclose relevant facts, slow down pressured decisions, and let people answer again under less captive conditions.

### 3. Use the least domineering correction that works

Because anti-manipulation can itself become paternal domination, the archive should prefer remedies that restore agency rather than substitute permanently for it: friction, disclosure, cooling-off periods, default reversal, protected alternatives, and public oversight before coercive override.

### 4. Protect children and systematically targeted populations more strongly

Where developmental vulnerability, cognitive asymmetry, or mass-targeted behavioral engineering is strong, institutions should accept a heavier protective burden. The point is not moral infantilization of everyone, but recognition that some environments are designed precisely to exploit immature or burdened agency.

### 5. Keep self-report in the loop

Do not let the critique of adaptive preference become a license to dismiss the testimony of the poor, disabled, subordinated, or culturally unfamiliar. The archive should repeatedly return to situated reports after conditions improve, since outsiders often misread resilience, attachment, or chosen sacrifice as mere false consciousness.

## What this changes in the archive

### 1. It narrows the gap between welfare and agency

The archive can now say more clearly why a contented but manipulated life is still morally damaged, and why a person’s reported wants remain evidentially important even when they are not final arbiters.

### 2. It disciplines capability talk

Real opportunity is not enough if aspirations themselves have been deformed by fear, stigma, or engineered dependence. A capability frame therefore needs a preference-formation audit, not just a resource count.

### 3. It sharpens institutional evaluation

Labor markets, schools, families, media systems, and digital platforms should be judged partly by whether they cultivate reflective agency or profit from shrinking it.

## Guardrails

- Do not infer false consciousness merely because someone chooses differently from elite observers.
- Do not treat all nudges, pedagogy, or norms as manipulation.
- Do not let anti-manipulation policy become a pretext for permanent guardianship.
- Do not treat preference reports under oppression as worthless; treat them as context-sensitive evidence.

## Practical tests

A stronger theory of the good should explain why the following are all morally different:

1. choosing a simple life from stable conviction;
2. renouncing aspiration because every path upward is blocked;
3. changing one’s mind after honest argument;
4. clicking “agree” after deceptive interface steering;
5. accepting dangerous work because every alternative is worse;
6. reporting satisfaction after conditions improve and exit becomes real.

## Compression rule for the archive

Respect what people say they want, but never forget to ask whether the wanting itself was formed under conditions fit for freedom.
