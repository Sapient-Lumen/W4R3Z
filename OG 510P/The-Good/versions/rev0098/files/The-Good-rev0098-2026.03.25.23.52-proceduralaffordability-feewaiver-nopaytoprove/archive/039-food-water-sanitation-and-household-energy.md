# 039 — Food, water, sanitation, and household energy

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must treat regular access to adequate food, safe water, sanitation, hygiene, and basic household energy as morally central rather than as mere poverty residue or infrastructure background, because no life goes well when eating, drinking, washing, cooking, keeping warm or cool, and maintaining a minimally habitable household depend on chronic insecurity, contamination, dirty fuel, shutoff risk, or emergency improvisation.

## Why this matters

The archive already protects health, dwelling, livelihood, administrative dignity, and everyday reachability. But it still lacks a direct rule for the **daily material floor** that lets people remain fed, hydrated, clean, and basically habitable at home. That gap matters because many injuries arrive not first as spectacular violence, but as hunger, unsafe water, missing toilets, hygiene deprivation, spoiled food, cold or overheated rooms, unlit homes, uncharged devices, disconnected utilities, and cooking arrangements that poison the air or consume hours of labor.

A good order should therefore ask not only whether people occasionally receive aid or technically face a stocked market, but whether they can rely on a material floor stable enough to support ordinary life. The relevant question is not luxury consumption. It is whether households can secure adequate nourishment, drink and use safe water, maintain dignified sanitation and hygiene, and sustain basic household energy for cooking, light, temperature safety, communication, and domestic continuity.

## Core claims

### 1. The material floor is not exhausted by income or emergency relief

Cash matters, but it is not identical with being fed, hydrated, or safely housed in daily practice. A household can receive income and still be unable to afford food, water, fuel, or utility arrears under price shocks, debt, monopolized provision, or local service absence. Likewise, emergency handouts, bottled water, or temporary vouchers should not be mistaken for durable material security.

### 2. Adequate food is more than calorie delivery

A good order should care whether people have regular access to food that is sufficient, safe, and compatible with health, development, and dignity. The point is not gourmet abundance or paternal nutritional micromanagement. It is that hunger, chronic undernourishment, forced dietary degradation, and permanent anxiety over the next meal are direct injuries to bodily life, agency, learning, care, and social participation.

### 3. Water, sanitation, and hygiene are dignity-bearing household goods

Safe water and sanitation are not merely public-health inputs. They shape privacy, menstrual dignity, child development, school attendance, caregiving burden, disease exposure, and whether ordinary domestic life can proceed without shame or danger. A theory of the good should therefore treat contamination, open defecation, inaccessible toilets, unaffordable service, and inability to wash as direct degradations rather than as regrettable technical deficits.

### 4. Basic household energy is part of the good, not a luxury afterthought

Electricity and clean household energy support cooking, refrigeration, heating or cooling, lighting, communication, study, medication storage, and the ordinary rhythms of home life. Dirty-fuel dependence, recurrent outages, unsafe heating, or utility shutoff are not merely engineering inconveniences. They thin health, time, learning, care, and safety at once.

### 5. Reliability and affordability matter as much as nominal connection

A house with a meter but frequent disconnection, unaffordable arrears, contaminated pipes, unsafe toilets, or a cooking setup that fills the room with smoke does not genuinely possess the relevant goods. The archive should therefore judge continuity, safety, and affordability, not only formal service coverage.

### 6. Material security should be universal enough to support refusal of domination

People who can keep food in the kitchen, water running, the toilet usable, and the lights or clean stove on are less easily ruled through wage desperation, intimate dependence, administrative fear, or landlord and creditor pressure. The daily material floor therefore belongs not only with health but with agency and non-domination.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask whether households can maintain ordinary bodily and domestic life without crisis improvisation

When evaluating institutions, ask whether people can reliably eat, drink, wash, use a toilet, store food and medicine, cook safely, keep a home lit, maintain safe temperatures, and avoid dangerous domestic exposure without debt spirals, repeated emergency aid, or degrading workarounds.

### 2. Treat hunger, contamination, hygiene deprivation, and shutoff risk as direct value losses

If people skip meals, ration water, lose refrigeration, avoid washing, rely on toxic or dirty fuels, or live under recurring disconnection threats, the archive should count that as a direct loss to health, dignity, time, learning, and agency rather than as unfortunate background poverty.

### 3. Prefer durable floor guarantees over crisis-only rescue

Favor systems that stabilize access through affordability protection, arrears prevention, humane debt treatment, service continuity, public-health safeguards, school and neighborhood provisioning, and ordinary rights of use rather than waiting until malnutrition, dehydration, disease, or household collapse becomes visible.

### 4. Track the household terms of access, not only aggregate supply

The archive should ask who can actually use services at home and on what terms: whether women and children spend hours collecting fuel or water, whether disabled or older people can safely use facilities, whether tenants or migrants can activate service without document traps, and whether heat, outages, contamination, or price spikes burden some groups systematically more than others.

## What this changes in the archive

### 1. It thickens health and dwelling by naming the daily material floor directly

`archive/038-health-mental-well-being-and-care-continuity.md` already treats food, water, and environmental conditions as determinants of health; this note adds that they matter not only as determinants but as goods whose absence directly makes a life go badly. `archive/029-home-dwelling-place-and-non-displacement.md` protects dwelling; this note adds that people do not really dwell when they cannot keep a household fed, washed, lit, safely cooked, and temperature-safe.

### 2. It sharpens livelihood, administration, and reachability

`archive/032-livelihood-meaningful-work-and-non-servility.md` argues that people need a material floor strong enough to refuse degrading labor; this note specifies what that floor must often include. `archive/036-administrative-burden-procedural-dignity-and-non-ordeal-access.md` gains a clearer utility and benefit reading when shutoff prevention, food support, or water access depend on impossible procedures. `archive/037-everyday-reachability-daily-mobility-and-proximate-access.md` gains a sharper account of why food deserts, water hauling, and fuel collection are not merely travel issues but material-floor failures.

### 3. It keeps the archive from mistaking markets or emergency aid for subsistence security

A society can have stocked supermarkets, tanker deliveries, grid expansion, or food charities and still leave households hungry, dehydrated, smoke-exposed, or one missed payment away from domestic breakdown. This note therefore gives the archive a cleaner way to separate visible supply from durable access.

## Failure modes

- **hunger amid plenty**: food exists in aggregate, but low income, inflation, debt, conflict, exclusion, or distance keep households from adequate diets.
- **water and sanitation precarity**: people face contamination, missing toilets, unsafe collection routes, open defecation, menstrual indignity, or unaffordable service even where nominal systems exist.
- **energy poverty**: households endure unsafe temperatures, darkness, spoiled food, missed schoolwork, or disconnection despite being formally connected.
- **dirty-fuel lock-in**: people cook with biomass, coal, or kerosene because clean alternatives are absent or unaffordable, absorbing smoke exposure, time loss, and danger.
- **crisis-only subsistence**: systems tolerate chronic deprivation until it appears as famine, dehydration, hospitalization, or disaster.
- **utility rule by arrears**: landlords, providers, or states exercise power through shutoff threats, prepaid scarcity, punitive fees, or impossible debt terms.
- **commodity minimalism**: policy counts calories delivered, liters connected, or hookups installed while ignoring adequacy, reliability, dignity, safety, and household burden.

## Guardrails

- Do not collapse the good into consumption volume, appliance accumulation, or ever-rising throughput.
- Do not treat cheap calories as enough when diets are unsafe, developmentally thin, or secured only by sacrificing other necessities.
- Do not assume a formal connection, market supply, or relief program is sufficient evidence that the material floor is secure.
- Do not let climate, utility, or anti-fraud policy quietly normalize shutoffs, dirty fuel, unsafe water, or hygiene deprivation for the poor.
- Do not reduce the issue to private household responsibility when prices, infrastructure, land use, conflict, and public systems are structuring access.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order keeps ordinary households fed, watered, clean, and basically powered without making survival depend on smoke, scarcity, humiliation, or recurring crisis.
