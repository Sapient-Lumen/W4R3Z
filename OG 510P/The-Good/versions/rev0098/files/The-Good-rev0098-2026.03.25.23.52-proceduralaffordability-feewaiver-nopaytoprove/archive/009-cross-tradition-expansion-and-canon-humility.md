# 009 — Cross-tradition expansion and canon humility

## One-line thesis

A serious archive on The Good should widen its canon beyond a narrow Anglophone frame, while remaining careful not to **flatten distinct traditions into one flattering consensus**.

## Why this matters

Theories of the good are often distorted by archive selection before argument even begins. If the archive only samples a familiar set of twentieth-century analytic disputes, it may overemphasize autonomy, preference, and rule-formulation while underweighting community, role, ritual, interdependence, discipline of desire, and forms of moral attention developed elsewhere.

Cross-tradition expansion is therefore not decoration. It is error correction.

## Working contributions to keep live

### 1. African ethical traditions

These often foreground humanism, welfare, and a communitarian ethos in which the good of persons is deeply entangled with the good of social belonging and mutual support.

### 2. Chinese ethical traditions

These bring relational role-ethics, cultivated character, ritual form, and harmony without simple individualist reduction. They are useful checks on models that imagine selves as morally complete before relationship.

### 3. Buddhist traditions

These sharpen moral attention to suffering, attachment, interdependence, discipline, compassion, and the ways distorted cognition contributes to harm.

## What expansion should not do

### 1. It should not pretend sameness where there is deep difference

Traditions differ about selfhood, virtue, liberation, authority, ritual, metaphysics, and the place of public institutions. These differences matter.

### 2. It should not use non-Western material as moral garnish

The archive should not harvest a few attractive terms while preserving the same old conceptual hierarchy underneath.

### 3. It should not romanticize community

Communitarian goods are real, but community can dominate as well as sustain. Cross-tradition work should enlarge criticism, not suspend it.

## Working use in the archive

Cross-tradition materials are especially valuable when the archive is asking:

- whether autonomy has been pictured too atomistically;
- whether suffering has been under-theorized;
- whether care and role obligations have been treated as merely private;
- whether public reason has been imagined too abstractly from embodied social practice;
- whether the good life must always be framed first as individual choice.

## Guardrails

- Do not flatten traditions into interchangeable “global wisdom.”
- Do not let expansion become token citation without conceptual uptake.
- Do not protect harmful hierarchy by calling it tradition.
- Do not assume convergence where the archive has not yet done the hard comparative work.

## Compression rule for the archive

A wider canon should make the archive less provincial and more demanding, not merely more ornamental.
