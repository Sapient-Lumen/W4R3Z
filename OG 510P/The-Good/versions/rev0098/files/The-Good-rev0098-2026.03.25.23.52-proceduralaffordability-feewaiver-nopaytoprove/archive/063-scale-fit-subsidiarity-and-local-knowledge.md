# 063 — Scale-fit, subsidiarity, and local knowledge

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must treat scale-fit, subsidiarity, and local knowledge as morally central rather than as mere administrative style, because lives go badly when decisions are made too far away to perceive context, too low to coordinate shared problems, or in systems that harvest local knowledge without sharing real power.

## Why this matters

The archive already protects voice, organized counterpower, collective self-determination, public integrity, legibility, maintenance, and everyday reachability. But it still lacks a direct canon rule for **where authority should sit**: which questions should be decided near the people and places they shape, which require wider coordination, and how different levels of rule should remain answerable to one another.

That gap matters because scale is not morally neutral. A school policy set from far away can flatten neighborhood reality, language needs, disability access, and caring arrangements into a single dashboard logic. A housing or climate rule left only to hyperlocal veto can entrench segregation, exclusion, or ecological sabotage while pretending to honor local freedom. Development schemes can extract community knowledge through workshops, surveys, translators, or "engagement" and still reserve final design, budget, and timing for distant ministries, donors, or platforms. In those settings, people are asked to supply context without being allowed to govern it.

A good order should therefore ask not only whether a decision is well-intentioned or formally participatory, but whether its **scale fits the problem**, whether locally rooted judgment has real authority where it should, whether higher levels supply floors and coordination where they must, and whether no level is allowed to hide domination behind either central efficiency or romantic localism.

## Core claims

### 1. Scale is a moral question, not just an efficiency question

Where authority sits affects dignity, error rates, intelligibility, cultural survival, ecological fit, response speed, and who can realistically contest a decision. Jurisdiction is therefore not merely technical plumbing. It helps determine whether people are governed with them, over them, or around them.

### 2. Subsidiarity means "as close as compatible," not "local no matter what"

Many decisions should be made near those most affected because local actors understand terrain, language, need variation, social trust, and implementation reality that distant systems routinely miss. But some goods require larger-scale coordination: equal-rights floors, redistribution, watershed or air-quality protection, macro-risk management, infectious-disease response, anti-corruption safeguards, migration status, and protection against local caste power or exclusion. A good order should therefore prefer the most local level **compatible with rights, coordination, and non-domination**, not the smallest unit by reflex.

### 3. Local knowledge should carry design power, not just advisory burden

Communities, workers, caregivers, tenants, patients, Indigenous peoples, disabled people, migrants, and other affected groups often know where bottlenecks, harms, informal routes, and place-specific supports actually are. The archive should treat this knowledge as more than a data source to be extracted and translated upward. If local knowledge repeatedly shapes diagnosis while outsiders retain budget, timing, veto, and narrative control, participation becomes a mined input rather than shared rule.

### 4. Higher-level rule is sometimes what protects the local from domination

A town, family network, village authority, school board, platform moderator circle, or neighborhood association can itself be a site of capture, caste, corruption, or exclusion. National and translocal institutions can be necessary to secure remedy, enforce floors, redistribute resources, protect minorities, and restrain local oligarchies. The archive should therefore reject the fantasy that the local is innocent by scale alone.

### 5. Good governance is often nested and polycentric

Many problems do not live at one level. Housing, water, schooling, disability access, policing, labor standards, food systems, evacuation planning, and climate adaptation often require layered design: neighborhood knowledge, municipal administration, regional coordination, national standards, and sometimes international obligation. A good order should therefore favor nested arrangements with clear roles, answerability, and routes of escalation rather than both central monocontrol and chaotic fragmentation.

### 6. Unfunded devolution is not subsidiarity

Pushing duties downward without money, staff, data access, legal authority, translation support, or repair capacity is often abandonment disguised as empowerment. Likewise, leaving local actors to improvise around austerity, climate damage, migration pressure, or care-system overload is not self-government. It is burden dumping.

### 7. Place-blind uniformity and parochial veto are twin failure modes

One danger is map-blind administration: identical rules across radically different terrains, infrastructures, histories, and cultures. The opposite danger is giving every local incumbent a veto over housing, access, environmental transition, minority presence, or public equality. The archive should therefore treat both distant flattening and hyperlocal exclusion as moral failures of scale-fit.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask where the problem actually lives

When evaluating schools, clinics, transport, land use, conservation, public records, disability access, labor standards, migration systems, platform governance, or infrastructure maintenance, ask which parts of the problem are intimate, neighborhood, municipal, regional, national, or transnational. Treat any design that answers every layer at one scale as suspect.

### 2. Prefer the nearest answerable level that can still protect floors and coordinate spillovers

Favor local authorship where context, trust, language, geography, or habitability are decisive, but keep wider floors, fiscal solidarity, anti-corruption checks, and escalation paths strong enough that people are not trapped inside local neglect or dominance.

### 3. Count extraction of local knowledge without shared authority as a direct loss

If communities are surveyed, consulted, translated, mapped, or asked for lived expertise while budgets, criteria, and binding decisions remain elsewhere, the archive should count that as a direct degradation of agency and public justification rather than as near-miss participation.

### 4. Judge devolution by means, not rhetoric

A transfer of responsibility counts as good only when lower levels receive the money, staffing, legal authority, and reviewable relation to higher levels needed to act on humane terms.

### 5. Keep routes upward and downward visible

Good nested rule lets people know where to decide locally, where to appeal upward, where coordination happens, and who answers for failure. A system with many levels but no intelligible responsibility chain is not scale-fit; it is complexity dumping.

### 1. It thickens voice and shared rule beyond participation volume alone

`archive/022-voice-participation-and-co-authorship.md` already protects meaningful say and uptake. This note adds that co-authorship is also about **jurisdictional fit**: people need not only opportunities to speak, but meaningful authority at the level where their knowledge and stakes actually live.

### 2. It disciplines collective self-determination without isolating it

`archive/051-collective-self-determination-territorial-authority-and-non-colonial-rule.md` already protects peoples and territorially rooted communities against consultation-only rule. This note generalizes the lesson beyond settler-colonial and territorial contexts: scale-fit matters across schools, care systems, housing, climate adaptation, transport, archives, and local public administration.

### 3. It sharpens legibility and maintenance by adding a jurisdiction layer

`archive/061-legibility-wayfinding-and-navigable-complexity.md` and `archive/058-maintenance-repair-and-ordinary-stewardship.md` already protect understandable paths and durable upkeep. This note adds that confusion and neglect often follow from scale mismatch: people cannot tell who owns the problem, and repairs fail because authority, budget, and knowledge are split across levels without answerable fit.

### 4. It keeps public integrity from collapsing into centralization reflex

`archive/047-public-integrity-anti-corruption-and-non-kleptocratic-rule.md` already protects against bribery, patronage, and capture. This note adds that both remote central systems and local incumbents can capture rule, and that anti-corruption sometimes requires devolved transparency just as much as centralized audit or prosecution.

## Failure modes

- **consultation-without-power**: communities are heard, mapped, or translated, but final authority remains elsewhere.
- **map-blind uniformity**: one rule is imposed across radically different terrains, cultures, risks, and infrastructures.
- **parochial domination**: local control becomes a shield for caste power, exclusion, patronage, or minority suppression.
- **unfunded devolution**: responsibility is pushed downward without resources, staff, or legal authority.
- **scale mismatch**: a problem with watershed, labor-chain, migration, or regional spillover is left to tiny units that cannot coordinate it.
- **jurisdictional blur**: many layers exist, but no one can tell who decides, who pays, or who must repair failure.
- **local-knowledge extraction**: lived expertise is collected as design input while outsiders keep veto, credit, and control.

## Guardrails

- Do not romanticize the local; intimacy with place can coexist with patriarchy, caste, corruption, landlord power, or exclusion.
- Do not let subsidiarity language excuse abandonment by higher levels that still control money, law, or data.
- Do not let national standards erase real local variation in language, disability access, ecology, mobility, or dwelling conditions.
- Do not confuse decentralization with democracy; power can be devolved directly into unanswerable local elites.
- Do not mistake endless multi-level architecture for good governance if responsibility chains remain opaque to ordinary people.
- Do not let higher-level coordination become a pretext for treating communities as sources of friction rather than as co-authors of shared rule.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order puts decisions as near to affected life as it can without abandoning rights floors, shared coordination, or protection against local domination, and it does not mine local knowledge while keeping power elsewhere.
