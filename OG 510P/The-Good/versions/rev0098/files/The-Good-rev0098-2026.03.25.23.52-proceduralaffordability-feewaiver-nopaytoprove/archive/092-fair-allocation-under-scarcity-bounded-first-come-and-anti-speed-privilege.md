# 092 — Fair allocation under scarcity, bounded first-come, and anti-speed privilege

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** should treat **allocation under scarcity** as a first-class moral design problem: when beds, appointments, seats, permits, school places, hearing dates, shelter spaces, or other limited public goods must be distributed, a humane order should not let speed, queue endurance, digital reflex, money, or broker access quietly decide what ought instead to turn on need, transparent criteria, equal standing, and fair lottery or rotation where claims are roughly equal.

## Why this matters

The archive is already strong on distribution, administrative dignity, legibility, humane pace, presumptive permission, cumulative burden, and anti-hostage design. But it still lacks a direct canon rule for a recurring deformation: **scarcity allocated by hustle**.

That deformation appears when vaccine appointments go first to those with fastest internet or the most flexible workday, when school places or clinic slots reward parents who know the portal refresh ritual, when housing or shelter access depends on physically enduring long lines, when hearing dates or permits are brokered through insiders, when price buys the fast lane for what is supposed to remain public, and when institutions defend these results as neutral because no one was formally excluded. In such settings the scarcity is real, but the allocator is misdescribed.

A good order therefore needs a cleaner rule. It may have to ration some goods. It may have to triage, queue, rotate, prioritize, or lottery. But it should not pretend that **first-come, fastest-click, longest-wait, or most-connected** is a morally innocent default.

## Core claims

### 1. Scarcity allocation is a moral choice, not mere logistics

Whenever a good cannot presently be given to everyone at once, the selection rule matters morally. A queue, a waitlist, a portal drop, a referral gate, a lottery, a priority score, or a brokered shortcut is not just operations. It is part of what the institution thinks counts.

### 2. First-come-first-served is often speed privilege in procedural costume

Some low-stakes flows can be served in order of arrival without deep injustice. But many scarce goods are not low stakes. In those settings first-come-first-served often sorts by schedule control, transport access, local familiarity, language fluency, device quality, broadband stability, bodily stamina, and the ability to monitor for openings. The archive should therefore treat first-come rules as presumptively bounded rather than as obviously neutral.

### 3. Queue endurance is not a fair measure of claim strength

Standing in line, sleeping outside an office, refreshing a portal at midnight, repeatedly calling a full voicemail box, or travelling across town for a maybe-slot may display desperation, but it does not by itself show greater desert, need, or civic worth. Systems that force people to prove seriousness through waiting often reward the buffered and punish the exhausted.

### 4. Need, urgency, and floor protection should outrank speed where the stakes are basic

For floor-adjacent goods such as urgent care, shelter, basic income support, disability accommodation, school continuity, medicine access, documentation repair, and emergency legal protection, the archive should generally prefer triage by need, risk, vulnerability, and time-sensitivity over race-like allocation by arrival speed or procedural aggression.

### 5. Where claims are roughly equal, lottery or rotation may be fairer than contest

Not every scarce good has a morally salient urgency gradient. Some public opportunities are best allocated through fair lottery, transparent rotation, or other anti-favoring rules rather than through queue races, scarcity theater, or informal broker capture. Equal claim does not require identical outcome every time, but it often does require a selection method that does not covertly reward advantage.

### 6. Money and brokerage should not quietly become the real allocator of public scarcity

When a nominally public or floor-adjacent good is rationed by VIP lanes, paid expediter markets, concierge access, fixer knowledge, or connection-based slot trading, scarcity has been redescribed as a privilege auction. That move is especially suspect where the good protects standing, safety, or basic continuity.

### 7. Waiting burdens belong inside the justice of the allocation itself

The archive should not ask only who eventually gets the slot. It should also ask who had to spend hours, wages, travel, childcare, phone credit, physical endurance, panic bandwidth, or repeated disclosure to stay in contention. Queue design and waitlist maintenance are part of distribution, not afterthoughts.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Distinguish urgency triage from ordinary queueing

When harms from delay differ sharply, use need- and risk-sensitive prioritization rather than pretending all claims are equal. When claims are roughly equal, do not smuggle urgency back in through vague discretion or insider favor.

### 2. Bound first-come rules to genuinely low-stakes, low-burden contexts

If missing the first opening can destabilize housing, treatment, schooling, income, papers, or legal standing, a good order should hesitate before letting access turn on refresh speed, line endurance, or lucky timing.

### 3. Prefer transparent criteria, fair lottery, or rotation over hustle contests

Where supply is limited and not all can be served at once, publish the rule, make it reviewable, and choose an allocator that does not simply reward the most buffered user. Sometimes that means explicit triage. Sometimes it means lottery. Sometimes it means rotation, quota, or staged release. The point is not one universal formula but refusal of covert advantage sorting.

### 4. Count premium lanes, broker markets, and insider shortcuts as substantive failures

A system should not congratulate itself on formal openness while ordinary people can only access scarce goods through paid expediters, professionalized queue monitoring, politically connected referrals, or private backup purchases.

### 5. Expand capacity where scarcity is manufactured or maintained by design neglect

Not all scarcity is tragic. Some of it is produced by understaffing, underbuilding, fragmented scheduling, poor maintenance, or needless approval bottlenecks. The archive should therefore ask not only how goods are rationed, but whether the scarcity itself is being tolerated for reasons incompatible with equal standing.

## Failure modes

- **midnight portal race**: scarce goods are released in ways that reward constant vigilance, fast devices, and flexible schedules.
- **queue endurance test**: people prove seriousness by waiting in unsafe, unpaid, or physically punishing conditions.
- **premium fast lane**: price or private concierge service buys earlier access to floor-adjacent goods.
- **brokered slot capture**: intermediaries, fixers, employers, landlords, or politically connected actors become the real path to scarce public goods.
- **first-come alibi**: institutions defend obviously unequal outcomes as neutral because the rule looked simple on paper.
- **urgency blindness**: basic need, vulnerability, or risk of irreversible loss is flattened into undifferentiated queue order.
- **manufactured scarcity**: preventable undercapacity, maintenance neglect, or needless bottlenecks are treated as natural shortage and then rationed through unfair selection rules.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat every queue as unjust; some low-stakes services can be handled in order of arrival without serious distortion.
- Do not replace transparent rules with opaque discretion merely by criticizing first-come-first-served.
- Do not use lottery where urgency, vulnerability, or harm from delay clearly differs.
- Do not let triage collapse into moralized deservingness, respectability sorting, or sponsor influence.
- Do not let anti-speed-privilege language excuse failure to expand capacity where scarcity is avoidable.
- Do not assume online release, app alerts, or digital waitlist tools are neutral merely because everyone can theoretically log in.

## Compression rule for the archive

When scarce goods must be allocated, do not let speed, stamina, money, or insider access decide what should turn on need, transparent fairness, and equal standing.
