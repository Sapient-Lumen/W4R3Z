# 091 — Compound dependency, anti-hostage access, and decoupled essentials

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** should resist **compound dependency**: no single employer, sponsor, landlord, household authority, platform, or office should be able to control too many floor goods at once, because when work, papers, housing, care, money, communication, or safety are bundled under one discretionary gatekeeper, exit, complaint, refusal, and ordinary life revision become dangerously expensive.

## Why this matters

The archive is already strong on non-domination, economic security, anti-ordeal access, portable autonomy, perpetual provisionality, presumptive permission, and voice without reprisal. But it still lacks a direct canon rule against a recurrent deformation: **hostage architecture**.

That deformation appears when one job controls not only wages but residence status, housing, health access, and social belonging; when one partner or family authority controls money, transport, documents, devices, and the practical possibility of leaving; when one landlord or shelter operator effectively controls address proof, school continuity, benefits usability, and the ability to remain in place; when one institution controls both the service a person needs and the complaint route for challenging abuse inside it; and when one platform, portal, or intermediary quietly becomes the choke point for payment, records, communication, and identity.

In such settings the separate goods may all nominally exist. The problem is that they are **operationally fused** under one will or one choke point. A good order therefore needs a cleaner rule: bundle the back-office where that helps people, but do not bundle a person's ordinary life under one master key.

## Core claims

### 1. Compound dependency magnifies domination

Dependence becomes morally thicker when one actor can threaten several floors at once. A boss who can only fire is powerful. A boss who can also end housing, health coverage, immigration status, and social standing is more than powerful: they sit at a junction where refusal starts to resemble self-destruction.

### 2. A right is thinner when using one right risks losing several others

Complaint, exit, organizing, refusal, family separation, care choice, or migration regularization can all become formally available but practically unusable when invoking one route endangers rent, treatment, documentation, custody, or lawful stay. The archive should therefore judge not only whether a route exists, but whether activating it detonates other dependencies.

### 3. Decoupling and portability are themselves protective goods

Some goods become more real when they travel with the person rather than remaining captive to a particular employer, landlord, sponsor, school, household head, or vendor. Portable records, independent documents, transferable care registration, public fallback, and benefit continuity are not mere administrative conveniences. They are anti-domination devices.

### 4. Humane bundling for users is different from hostage bundling by gatekeepers

The archive should distinguish two very different ideas. **Humane bundling** means people do not have to re-enter the maze for every adjacent need: life-event design, no-wrong-door entry, once-only proof, and coordinated service delivery can reduce burden. **Hostage bundling** means many essentials depend on staying in one controlled relationship. The first can enlarge freedom; the second shrinks it.

### 5. Where coupling cannot be fully avoided, the remaining power must be bounded

Some domains will retain real linkage: schools affect meal access, hospitals affect records, workplaces affect income, migration systems affect work authorization. The question is whether the linked power is hemmed in by portability, review, independent complaint, notice, transition time, public alternatives, and continuity guarantees, or whether people are simply told to tolerate concentrated dependence.

### 6. Floor goods should not become collateral for discipline, obedience, or gratitude

A good order should hesitate whenever shelter, medicine, papers, devices, family contact, study access, or ordinary mobility are quietly made conditional on tone, obedience, sponsor favor, romantic compliance, unpaid family service, or strategic silence. That structure does not merely allocate goods. It converts floors into leverage.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask who can crash how many floors at once

When evaluating a workplace, household, shelter, tenancy, school, clinic, migration regime, digital platform, or welfare system, ask which actor can trigger losses across several domains with one decision. Multi-front vulnerability is a warning sign even where each separate dependency looks familiar.

### 2. Prefer portable entitlements, independent documents, and public fallback

Where possible, separate identity, legal presence, health access, benefit continuity, schooling continuity, payment rails, and complaint routes from any one private controller. The point is not to eliminate every dependency. It is to make ordinary survival less hostage to one relationship.

### 3. Separate provider power from remedy power

People should not have to ask the same actor both for the service and for permission to challenge how the service is delivered. Independent complaint, transfer, and review routes matter most where the underlying service is hard to leave.

### 4. Build exit with continuity, not exit into free fall

Formal exit is too thin where leaving means sudden loss of housing, treatment, records, lawful stay, income, or children's routines. A humane order should pair exit with bridging rights, transferability, notice, and public fallback rather than celebrating departure while leaving continuity to luck.

### 5. Bundle the path, not the person's whole life

The archive should welcome one-stop access, interoperable records with bounded sharing, and life-event design when these reduce burden on the user. But it should resist designs in which one sponsor, one household head, one employer, one landlord, or one platform becomes the practical owner of many unrelated necessities.

## Failure modes

- **employer citadel**: work, lawful stay, housing, transport, and care are all effectively tied to one boss or labor intermediary.
- **sponsor stack**: one sponsor or authorizer controls renewal, transfer, complaint safety, and the possibility of remaining.
- **partner monopoly**: one intimate authority controls money, documents, devices, movement, and family access.
- **landlord choke point**: address proof, school continuity, benefits usability, utilities, and neighborhood belonging all hinge on one precarious tenure relationship.
- **provider-remedy fusion**: the institution accused of abuse also controls the grievance path, discharge timing, records, or referral onward.
- **one-app life**: one portal, login, or device failure suspends payment, communication, identity proof, service access, and civic presence at once.
- **exit into cascade**: leaving one bad relationship causes simultaneous loss of several basics because no portability or fallback exists.
- **family-only access**: systems quietly assume that food, care coordination, transport, translation, or crisis support can safely be monopolized by family members regardless of abuse, estrangement, or overload.

## Guardrails

- Do not assume all linkage is bad; many goods are genuinely related and some coordination reduces burden.
- Do not confuse humane one-stop service with concentrated private control; the former may reduce ordeal while the latter raises stakes on obedience.
- Do not treat portability as enough on its own; portable rights still need intelligible transfer, continuity, and human fallback.
- Do not pretend total independence is possible or desirable; the aim is not invulnerability but bounded, answerable dependence.
- Do not force every commitment into frictionless exit; chosen ties may still be deep, durable, and demanding, provided essentials are not turned into collateral for staying.
- Do not let anti-hostage language erase the special needs of children, disabled people, or dependent elders, who may need continuity, representation, and support during transitions rather than abstract mobility alone.

## Compression rule for the archive

Bundle the service path where that helps, not the person's life under one master key.
