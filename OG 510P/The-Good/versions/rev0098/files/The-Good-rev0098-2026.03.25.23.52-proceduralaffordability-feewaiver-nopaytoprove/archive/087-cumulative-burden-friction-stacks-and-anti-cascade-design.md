# 087 — Cumulative burden, friction stacks, and anti-cascade design

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must treat cumulative burden, friction stacks, and anti-cascade design as morally central, because lives are often broken not by one spectacular deprivation but by the accumulation of many separately defended demands on time, travel, proof, money, attention, coordination, and emotional steadiness.

## Why this matters

The archive is already strong on administrative burden, humane margin, proportionality, modality pluralism, bounded verification, bounded disclosure, and freedom from compulsory optimization. But it still lacks a direct canon rule for **burden accumulation itself**.

That gap matters because institutions often judge themselves one interface at a time. The clinic asks only for one more appointment. The school asks only for one more meeting. The employer asks only for one more schedule adjustment. The welfare office asks only for one more recertification. The court asks only for one more appearance. The landlord asks only for one more document. Each separate demand can be defended in isolation. Yet for the person living the whole day, these burdens stack. Time conflicts multiply. Travel repeats. Wages are lost. Childcare must be improvised. Private pain must be retold. Phones must stay charged. Records must be moved by hand. One missed step in one system creates new tasks in another.

A salient theory of the good therefore needs a clearer rule: judge not only whether each institutional demand is locally reasonable, but whether the **combined load** remains humanly survivable for ordinary people, especially those already carrying illness, disability, poverty, caregiving, migration precarity, trauma, language barriers, or unstable work.

## Core claims

### 1. Small burdens can become major exclusion by accumulation

A single queue, portal, refill task, hearing date, transport trip, or evidence request may look modest. But many modest demands, timed badly and distributed across different systems, can become equivalent to direct exclusion. The archive should therefore treat cumulative burden as a substantive moral problem rather than as a mere inconvenience multiplier.

### 2. Capacity is relational, variable, and already partly spent elsewhere

People do not meet institutions as blank agents with fresh time and energy. They arrive while sick, grieving, caregiving, translating, commuting, debt-managing, parenting, working irregular shifts, or navigating other offices. A demand that is manageable in the abstract may be impossible in the context of a life already under load.

### 3. Friction stacks are often produced by siloed optimization

Systems frequently improve themselves locally while worsening life globally. A department reduces fraud, another tightens scheduling, another digitizes reminders, another shortens office hours, another centralizes records, another adds a compliance step. Each reform may look rational from inside the silo. Together they offload the work of co-ordination onto users, families, and caregivers.

### 4. Cumulative burden is a distributive and dignity problem

Burden stacks do not land evenly. They fall hardest on people with the least slack, the least paid help, the least schedule control, the least transport access, the most fragile health, the weakest documentation, or the greatest exposure to profiling and disbelief. What looks like a neutral sequence of steps from above can function as classed, disabling, and humiliating sorting from below.

### 5. Good design reduces total workload, not only local friction

The humane aim is not just to make each portal slightly prettier or each office marginally faster. It is to reduce the overall work imposed on people: fewer duplicate proofs, more bundled service points, better timing, interoperable records, quieter handoffs, stable points of contact, and less expectation that households must serve as unpaid systems integrators.

### 6. Burden spikes need pause, triage, and offloading

Lives contain pressure spikes: illness, bereavement, childbirth, eviction risk, disaster, caregiving crisis, detention, migration, relapse, job loss. A good order should not add full ordinary compliance demand on top of such moments. It should build pause rights, grace periods, temporary simplification, delegated help, and coordinated triage when total load is predictably exceeding capacity.

### 7. Measurement should track stack effects and cascade risk

An institution can look efficient by its own indicators while contributing to system-wide overload. The archive should therefore ask about calendar collisions, repeated disclosure, total appointment burden, total form burden, total travel burden, cumulative waiting, cross-agency loops, and how often one missed step produces new obligations elsewhere.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask what else the person is already carrying

When judging a rule, service, reform, or workflow, ask not only whether the immediate step is defensible, but what else it presupposes: battery, transport, literacy, childcare, translation, privacy, schedule control, money, emotional reserve, and the absence of conflicting obligations.

### 2. Count co-ordination labor as real work

Tracking deadlines, moving documents, repeating history, linking systems, staying reachable, reminding institutions, and stitching together fragmented care or administration are not background trivia. They are labor. The archive should count user-side co-ordination work as a real cost in dignity, time, cognition, and exhaustion.

### 3. Prefer bundling, synchronization, and once-only design

Favor no-wrong-door systems, once-only proof where possible, life-event bundling, interoperable records with bounded sharing, predictable calendars, compatible office hours, unified case handling, and plural channels that reduce the chance that people must reassemble one right from many thin institutions. The point is not unlimited pooling; it is to stop using households as unpaid couriers of stable facts, already-submitted documents, and repeated identity proofs when bounded reuse would do.

### 4. Build burden budgets and anti-cascade triggers

Good systems should ask how much total load they are imposing and should have ways to stop escalation when thresholds are crossed: grace periods, deadline extension, one-time telling, synchronized appointments, reduced reporting frequency, human callback, and automatic review when multiple systems are simultaneously demanding action.

### 5. Treat stacked overload as a design failure

If people predictably lose benefits, jobs, housing, health continuity, schooling, or legal standing because several separately ordinary demands arrived together, the archive should read that not as unfortunate personal disorganization but as a design failure in the surrounding order.

## Failure modes

- **death-by-a-thousand-cuts exclusion**: no single step looks outrageous, but the stack becomes impossible.
- **silo optimization**: each institution improves its own metrics while worsening the total life load of the people moving between them.
- **coordination-by-user**: people and families are forced to function as unpaid case managers, record couriers, reminder systems, and interoperability layers.
- **burden dumping**: missing slack is exported downward to caregivers, workers, tenants, patients, and migrants.
- **cascade triggering**: one missed step predictably creates several more obligations or penalties elsewhere.
- **stack blindness**: officials judge each friction locally and never ask what else is hitting the same life at the same time.
- **false individualization**: systemic overload is redescribed as poor compliance, low motivation, bad organization, or personal irresponsibility.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat this note as an argument against all standards, deadlines, or verification.
- Do not let simplification become an excuse for opaque data sharing, surveillance expansion, or loss of contestability.
- Do not assume centralization is always humane; some integration reduces burden while other integration concentrates power dangerously.
- Do not make burden budgeting a covert rationing tool that quietly abandons substantive entitlements.
- Do not mistake low take-up, low complaint volume, or silent dropout for low need; those can be signatures of overload.
- Do not offload coordination to family, employers, landlords, or private apps and then call the service integrated.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order judges the total load it places on ordinary lives and refuses to let many separately defensible demands accumulate into exhaustion, exclusion, or cascade failure.
