# 035 — Attention, cognitive liberty, and anti-capture

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must protect some noncaptured attention, cognitive liberty, and meaningful control over interruption, continuation, and recommender-driven experience, because lives do not go well when schools, workplaces, platforms, and AI systems are built to harvest compulsion, autopilot, or developmental vulnerability.

## Why this matters

The archive already protects preference-formation, privacy, rest, childhood, and truthful world-contact. But it still lacks a direct canon-level rule for the **attentional environment** in which agency is exercised. That gap matters because many contemporary injuries do not arrive first as explicit coercion. They arrive as interruption regimes, infinite continuation, notification pressure, manipulative defaults, and systems that keep people scrolling, responding, or yielding before reflective judgment has a fair chance to form.

A person can remain formally unforced yet still be attentively governed. A child can be told she is learning, socializing, or relaxing while her developmental environment is quietly organized around extraction of time, data, responsiveness, and return visits. A worker can technically clock out while remaining mentally tethered by alerts, rankings, and algorithmic prompts. A theory of the good that notices only outcomes, stated preference, or raw access will miss this earlier layer where agency is softened into habit and attention is redirected before consent has real shape. It now also reads with `archive/075-reputational-mercy-searchability-and-non-permanent-stigma.md`, which adds that ranking systems and search surfaces do not only capture present attention; they can also keep old traces permanently first, turning visibility itself into a reputational gate.

## Core claims

### 1. Attention is part of agency, not merely mental fuel

Agency requires more than options and rights on paper. It also requires some practical ability to notice, dwell, ignore, resist, and redirect one’s own mental life. Where attention is continuously commandeered, agency thins even before a person is outwardly prevented from choosing.

### 2. Cognitive liberty includes protection against engineered autopilot

The relevant threat is not only censorship or surveillance. It is also the deliberate design of environments that encourage compulsive continuation, low-friction surrender, or emotionally loaded re-entry. Infinite scroll, autoplay, variable rewards, night-time notifications, and opaque recommendation loops should be treated as moral design surfaces rather than as neutral convenience features.

### 3. Childhood and adolescence deserve stronger attentional protections

Young people are not merely smaller adult users. Their developmental environment is part of the good they are owed now. Systems that normalize distraction, compulsion, body-image pressure, sleep disruption, or permanent peer comparison should therefore be treated as direct pressure on childhood flourishing, not merely as unfortunate side effects of popular tools.

### 4. Attentional capture distorts education, work, care, and common life at once

When schools import distracting technology without strong pedagogical reasons, when work systems normalize constant alerts, when care systems push families into app-mediated hypervigilance, or when public communication is routed through engagement-maximizing intermediaries, the damage is not confined to one policy silo. The same capture logic can thin learning, rest, intimacy, judgment, and democratic attention together.

### 5. AI-mediated guidance can either widen or narrow mental freedom

AI tutors, assistants, companions, and recommender systems can help people navigate complexity, pace learning, and reduce overload. But they can also become unusually intimate steering systems that pre-sort relevance, sustain dependence, or make one pathway feel frictionless while alternatives recede from view. The archive should therefore evaluate AI help not only by accuracy or usefulness, but also by whether it preserves reviewable judgment and exit from soft dependence.

### 6. Anti-capture is not anti-pleasure, anti-technology, or anti-personalization

The relevant moral aim is not monastic purity. People may choose absorption, entertainment, games, intense study, or demanding creative flow. The problem is asymmetry: when design is optimized to keep people engaged beyond reflection, beyond developmental fit, or beyond what they can realistically interrupt, especially when refusal is made costly and escape requires unusual discipline.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask who controls interruption, continuation, and salience

When evaluating platforms, schools, devices, workplaces, public-service portals, or AI assistants, ask who decides what interrupts, what continues by default, what is ranked as urgent, and how difficult it is to pause, leave, or reorient.

### 2. Treat compulsive design as a serious agency injury when it is systematic

Where environments are intentionally tuned to exploit attentional vulnerability, sleep cycles, social insecurity, or developmental asymmetry, the archive should see more than regrettable inconvenience. It should see a standing pressure on agency, learning, and mental self-direction.

### 3. Prefer humane defaults, meaningful friction, and user-legible control

Favor bounded feeds, stopping cues, quiet defaults, effective break tools, private-by-default protections for minors, controllable notification layers, night protections, transparent recommendation options, and interface designs that make exit, delay, and reconsideration realistically available.

### 4. Read attention across other protected goods

Attentional self-direction should be read through childhood, education, privacy, rest, work, accessibility, and truthful world-contact. A person whose environment is optimized for compulsion is not well served merely because some choice remains technically available.

`archive/027-childhood-development-and-just-guardianship.md` already resists child extraction and overcontrol. This note adds that attentional design itself is now one of the main sites where developmental protection or exploitation occurs. It also gives the archive a clearer way to judge AI tutors, companions, and assistants: not only by what they know, but by whether they preserve reflective uptake, friction for reconsideration, and noncaptive exit.

## Failure modes

- **autopilot governance**: systems guide behavior mainly by habit loops, salience hacks, and continuation defaults rather than by reasons people can inspect.
- **interruption inequality**: some people buy quiet while others live inside alerts, queue checks, unstable schedules, and required responsiveness.
- **child-as-engagement-unit**: developmental life is treated as inventory for retention, ranking, or data extraction.
- **friction asymmetry**: entry is one tap, while pausing, leaving, muting, or switching to a calmer mode is obscure or burdensome.
- **helpfulness dependence**: AI assistants become so convenient that they quietly thin initiative, memory, or judgment rather than supporting them.
- **wellness theater**: platforms advertise screen-time tools or parental controls that are easy to dismiss, hard to configure, or too weak to counter the deeper business logic.

## Guardrails

- Do not equate attentional freedom with one approved style of concentration, seriousness, or productivity.
- Do not treat all personalization, play, or immersive experience as domination by default.
- Do not blame individuals for vulnerabilities that systems are deliberately designed to exploit.
- Do not answer platform capture with unlimited parental or institutional surveillance.
- Do not reduce the issue to youth alone; adults, workers, patients, and citizens are also governed through attentional design.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order should not make compulsion its default interface to the mind; it should preserve some real power to pause, attend, refuse, and turn elsewhere.
