# 003 — No good by single number

## One-line thesis

The archive should presume that **The Good is not faithfully representable by one master scalar**, even when local decisions require rough scoring or ranking.

## Why this matters

Institutions are repeatedly tempted by monism. GDP, utility, life satisfaction, preference aggregation, cost-benefit ratios, engagement, safety scores, carbon numbers, and composite indices all promise clarity. The promise is real: numbers coordinate action. But the moral risk is equally real: numbers erase distinctions between floors, means, side-constraints, and incommensurable goods.

A single scalar can be useful as a local instrument. It becomes dangerous when it claims constitutional authority.

## Why one number is usually too thin

### 1. Final value is contested even before aggregation begins

Disagreement begins at the level of what counts as good in itself: pleasure, desire-satisfaction, knowledge, autonomy, friendship, meaning, dignity, achievement, capability, freedom from domination, beauty, sanctity, ecological integrity, or some subset thereof. Aggregation does not solve that dispute; it hides it.

### 2. Some goods look like thresholds, not tradeable marginals

Bodily integrity, basic care, anti-cruelty floors, minimal agency, and some rights-protections are often better treated as **guardrails or thresholds** than as units in a trade pool. A system that can always compensate for humiliation or domination with enough benefit elsewhere is already morally damaged.

### 3. Goods interact nonlinearly

Education without nutrition, rights without enforcement, freedom without knowledge, care without time, and safety without trust can all fail. A scalar often assumes smooth substitutability where the real structure is conditional and lumpy.

### 4. Who gains matters, not only how much is gained

Distribution, vulnerability, dependency, domination, and historical exclusion affect the moral significance of an increment. Similar numeric gains can matter differently depending on who receives them and under what background conditions.

### 5. Measuring can redirect the system

A metric is never merely descriptive. Once used for reward or punishment, it becomes a target. Then behavior bends toward the legible proxy, often at the expense of the underlying good.

## What to use instead

### 1. Dashboard pluralism

Use a bounded set of dimensions rather than a master number.

### 2. Thresholds and veto zones

Specify where improvement in one dimension does not justify falling below another.

### 3. Narrative judgment

Record why a tradeoff was accepted, by whom, and with what acknowledged remainder.

### 4. Disaggregation

Show who benefits, who pays, and who is left invisible.

### 5. Revision discipline

Treat metrics as tools under governance, not sovereign verdicts.

## Failure modes

- **proxy monarchy**: a convenient measure quietly becomes the constitution.
- **compensation fantasy**: grave harms are treated as offset by unrelated gains.
- **distributional bleaching**: aggregated improvement hides who is carrying the loss.
- **category error scoring**: dignity, truth, love, and ecological viability are treated as if they were interchangeable chips.
- **metric capture**: actors learn to improve the score while degrading the good.

## Guardrails

- Do not reject measurement; govern it.
- Do not let a composite index erase the underlying components.
- Do not report averages without tails and gaps.
- Do not let present measurability determine future ontology.

## Compression rule for the archive

Use numbers to illuminate the good, never to replace the argument about what the good is.
