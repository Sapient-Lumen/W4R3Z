# 001 — Charter and research discipline

## One-line thesis

This archive should seek a theory of **The Good** that is genuinely action-guiding without collapsing plurality, smuggling in sectarian assumptions, or mistaking one convenient metric for value itself.

## Why this matters

Many practical systems already act as if they know what the good is. They optimize for growth, preference satisfaction, votes, stability, comfort, prestige, or technical efficiency. The danger is not only explicit bad theory. It is also **implicit theory by default**, where institutions optimize a proxy and then call the result common sense.

If this archive is worth maintaining, it should make the theory burden visible. It should keep track of what kinds of things are plausibly good in themselves, what kinds of arrangements are good because they protect other goods, which tradeoffs are tragic rather than clean, and which syntheses are stable enough to enter canon.

## Research discipline

### 1. Distinguish levels of value

The archive should keep separate, while allowing interaction among, at least four levels:

- **personal good**: what makes a life go well for a being;
- **social good**: what makes relations among persons better rather than more exploitative, lonely, or cruel;
- **institutional good**: what makes collective systems reliable, contestable, truth-tracking, and non-dominating;
- **temporal good**: what preserves or enlarges the conditions for future flourishing.

A theory that only speaks clearly at one of these levels is probably incomplete.

### 2. Keep canon and quarantine separate

Some views will be strong enough to anchor working judgment. Others will be bold compressions that are promising but under-tested. The archive should preserve both, but it should not let them trade clothes.

Canon is for claims that have survived cross-pressure from multiple theory families, practical cases, and institutional contexts. Quarantine is for claims that may be illuminating but still risk overcompression, false universality, or hidden background assumptions.

### 3. Prefer source-grounded compression

The archive should compress arguments into durable note form, but it should not pretend that compression is neutrality. Each note should stay tethered to a visible source family and avoid long quotation. The point is to keep the archive light while preserving replay paths.

### 4. Refuse monism by convenience

A theory of the good may eventually have a deep unifying structure. But the archive should not assume that unity merely because decision-makers prefer a single number. Apparent commensurability may be a bookkeeping gain purchased by moral loss.

### 5. Track disagreement honestly

Some disagreements are about facts, some about weights, some about concepts, and some about the authority to decide. The archive should mark those differences. It should not file all conflict under “values differ” and move on.

## Working questions

A candidate view of the good should be able to answer at least these questions:

1. What is good **for** a person or sentient being?
2. What is good **between** persons?
3. What is good **about** institutions and common worlds?
4. What is good **across time**, including for future persons?
5. Which goods look like floors, which look like aims of promotion, and which look like spaces for plural self-authorship?
6. Which tradeoffs are legitimate, which are suspect, and which should be treated as tragic residues rather than solved sums?

## Guardrails

- Do not let one theory family seize sovereignty just because it is easy to formalize.
- Do not confuse what is measurable with what is final.
- Do not treat present winners as the natural judges of future good.
- Do not pretend neutrality while importing a hidden picture of the person.
- Do not let archive tidiness erase genuine moral remainder.

## Compression rule for the archive

A usable theory of the good must be plural enough to fit the world, disciplined enough to guide action, and honest enough to say where its own confidence runs out.
