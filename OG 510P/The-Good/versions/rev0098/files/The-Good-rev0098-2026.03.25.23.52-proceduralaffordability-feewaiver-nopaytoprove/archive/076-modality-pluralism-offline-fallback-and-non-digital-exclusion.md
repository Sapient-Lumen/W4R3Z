# 076 — Modality pluralism, offline fallback, and non-digital exclusion

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must treat modality pluralism, offline fallback, and freedom from non-digital exclusion as morally important, because rights, care, schooling, work, movement, and ordinary public life do not genuinely exist for people when participation quietly depends on always-on smartphones, stable connectivity, remembered passwords, app literacy, successful biometric match, or single-channel digital compliance.

## Why this matters

The archive already protects accessibility, administrative dignity, legibility, documentary existence, portable autonomy, privacy, and dependability. But it still lacks a direct canon rule for **channel monoculture** itself: the condition in which everyday life is formally open yet practically routed through one narrow stack of apps, portals, QR codes, digital wallets, push alerts, and self-service flows.

That omission matters because digital systems can widen access and still become exclusionary at the exact point where lives are most fragile. A person can have a valid right or appointment and still lose it because a battery dies, a screen breaks, a signal drops, a password expires, a code is sent to an old phone number, a kiosk times out, a portal rejects a name mismatch, or the only human help available says to go back online. A migrant can be documentarily recognized yet unable to download the credential. A disabled person can be formally included yet pushed through interfaces that assume one sensory, cognitive, and motor profile. An older person can be told that service exists while the actual path requires app stores, biometric capture, or unattended troubleshooting. A good order should therefore ask not only whether digital services exist, but whether non-digital life remains honorably usable.

The opposite mistake is also possible. One can romanticize paper, ignore the access gains of good digital design, or treat every portal as domination. The archive therefore needs a middle rule: prefer plural, interoperable, low-ordeal channels and non-punitive fallback, so digital convenience does not become digital compulsion.

## Core claims

### 1. Digital-by-default becomes domination when digital-only is the practical reality

A system can advertise choice while structuring the real path through apps, portals, QR tickets, SMS codes, or digital identity checks that many people cannot stably meet. When the paper path is disappearing, the phone line never resolves, the office demands online pre-steps, or the in-person route is punitive, channel choice becomes theater.

### 2. Channel monoculture is a distributive and status problem, not only a usability problem

The burden falls hardest on people with low income, unstable charging, homelessness, migration precarity, disability, detention history, language barriers, older age, coercive relationships, shared-device dependence, rural connectivity limits, or institutional churn. What looks like minor friction to the well-buffered becomes disappearance, lateness, sanction, or exclusion for others.

### 3. Human variation requires modality pluralism

A humane order should not presume that everyone can see, hear, type, remember, translate, scan, upload, troubleshoot, self-serve, and respond at the same pace through the same medium. Rights become more real when people can move between channels: in person, by phone, through paper or printable notice, through accessible web design, through trusted delegated help, and through reviewable human contact.

### 4. Offline fallback is moral infrastructure, not nostalgic redundancy

Fallback channels are not leftovers from a pre-digital age. They are resilience and equal-standing infrastructure. Power outages, disasters, detention, hospitalization, domestic control, dead batteries, data caps, broken devices, stolen phones, and account lockouts are ordinary enough that a good order should expect them rather than treating them as edge cases.

### 5. Non-digital access should not become the humiliating slow lane

It is not enough to preserve a nominal fallback if the fallback means longer waits, extra fees, suspicion-first treatment, narrower service scope, or staff trained to push everyone back to self-service tools. A humane system should treat channel variation as an ordinary design requirement rather than as a grudging exception for those who have failed modernity.

### 6. One-token identity dependence is especially dangerous

Where personhood, eligibility, payment, access, and notice all collapse into one app, card, phone number, or biometric credential, daily life becomes too brittle. The archive should therefore prefer systems with secondary credentials, replacement routes, human override, and non-digital proof paths rather than channeling everything through one failure point.

### 7. The aim is channel equivalence under public reasons, not anti-digital retreat

The archive does not require that every process remain unchanged forever or that every channel always do everything equally. It does require that departures from channel equivalence be publicly justified, limited, reviewable, and not structured so that those with least buffer bear the harshest penalty for device, connectivity, or literacy mismatch.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask what happens when the device, login, signal, or biometric match fails

When evaluating schools, clinics, welfare systems, migration regimes, transit, banking, workplaces, courts, housing, and digital public infrastructure, ask whether ordinary participation survives dead batteries, broken screens, lost cards, absent data, forgotten passwords, mismatched records, or ordinary discomfort with self-service systems.

### 2. Prefer plural channels with dignified equivalence

Favor in-person, phone, print, accessible web, low-bandwidth, delegated-help, and human-review routes that remain genuinely usable rather than merely ceremonial. The archive should be especially suspicious when the only reliable path requires always-on smartphone possession or repeated digital self-maintenance.

### 3. Treat mandatory app dependency and portal lockout as direct losses

If people miss medicine, wages, shelter, school, hearings, transport, or family contact because a portal timed out, a code never arrived, a QR ticket could not render, or the only acceptable credential lived inside one device, the archive should count that as a direct degradation of access, dignity, and freedom rather than as ordinary technical inconvenience.

### 4. Build correction, delegation, and replacement into the channel design

A humane order should preserve secondary proofs, printable notice, staffed counters or reachable phone support where stakes are high, recoverable error, revocable and scope-limited trusted-agent help under privacy safeguards, and low-friction replacement when accounts, cards, or devices are lost.

### 5. Keep digital inclusion and channel pluralism together

Expanding affordable connectivity, digital literacy, and accessible public technology can be genuine goods. But those gains should not be purchased by making non-digital life unlivable. The right target is not digital abstinence or digital compulsion, but mode-flexible participation under equal standing.

## Failure modes

- **app-only state**: rights and services technically exist, but the reliable route runs only through apps, QR codes, or portal logins.
- **battery caste**: those with constant power, private devices, and stable connectivity move easily while others live one charge away from exclusion.
- **portal lockout**: password churn, code failures, old phone numbers, or account mismatch silently function as denial.
- **fallback humiliation**: non-digital routes survive only as slower, narrower, more suspicious, or fee-bearing exceptions.
- **digital preclearance trap**: in-person help exists only after online forms, bookings, or uploads have already succeeded.
- **one-token documentary life**: identity, payment, access, and notice collapse into one brittle credential or device.
- **delegation without safeguards**: people must surrender privacy, autonomy, or money to brokers, relatives, or informal fixers because no dignified human channel remains.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat every digital channel as presumptively exclusionary; judge systems by channel plurality, accessibility, repairability, and fallback.
- Do not romanticize paper or face-to-face procedure when those channels are themselves slow, hostile, inaccessible, or corruption-prone.
- Do not preserve nominal fallback while making it punitive, scarce, or socially stigmatized.
- Do not let anti-fraud, efficiency, or modernization rhetoric justify one-token dependence where stakes are high.
- Do not answer digital exclusion only with private coaching, brokers, or family mediation; the aim is ordinary equal access, not premium navigation for a few.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order uses digital tools to widen access without making ordinary life depend on a single device, channel, or mode of compliance.
