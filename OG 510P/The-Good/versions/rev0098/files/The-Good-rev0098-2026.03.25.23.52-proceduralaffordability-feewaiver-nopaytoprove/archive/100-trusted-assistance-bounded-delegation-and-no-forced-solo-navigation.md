# 100 — Trusted assistance, bounded delegation, and no forced solo navigation

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** should treat **trusted assistance** and **bounded delegation** as morally important, because rights, care, remedy, and ordinary administration are thinner than they look when people must either navigate alone beyond their actual capacity or surrender broad control to helpers, brokers, family members, or professionals just to remain governable.

## Why this matters

The archive is now strong on broker dependence, modality pluralism, anti-confessional access, fair hearing, supported dependency, and record contestability. But it still lacks a direct canon rule for a recurring seam between autonomy and help: **how a person may add assistance without losing standing**.

That seam matters because ordinary life often becomes procedurally unmanageable at exactly the moments when stakes are high: illness, disability, aging, bereavement, migration, language difference, trauma, litigation, debt, discharge, family crisis, or digital lockout. A person may need someone to translate, upload, receive notices, gather evidence, speak to an office, track deadlines, or carry a case across institutional handoffs. If the system refuses that help, it quietly demands an idealized solo operator. If it allows help only through broad, sticky, or poorly supervised control, it quietly turns assistance into dispossession.

A good order needs a cleaner rule. Help should usually be addable in **chosen, legible, revocable, scope-limited ways**. People should be able to authorize a trusted person to assist with a matter, a stage, a channel, or a class of decisions without thereby disappearing behind the helper. Where full substitute authority is necessary, the burden should remain on the system to keep that authority bounded, reviewable, and safety-sensitive. Otherwise the archive risks accepting a false choice between lonely self-service and total proxy rule.

## Core claims

### 1. The need for help is ordinary, not evidence of lesser standing

A person can need assistance and still remain the principal bearer of the relevant claim. Illness, pain, stress, low literacy, language difference, sensory overload, cognitive variation, grief, distance, device failure, or institutional complexity do not by themselves convert someone into a lesser civic subject.

### 2. Good delegation is usually chosen, bounded, and revocable

A humane system should usually let people decide who may help, with what matter, through which channel, for how long, and on what informational scope. Assistance should ordinarily be terminable or replaceable without starting the whole case over.

### 3. Assistance should widen agency rather than replace it by default

The point of a helper is not to absorb the person into professional, familial, or custodial control. Good assistance often means the person can still receive notices, inspect the file, appear, speak, limit what may be shared, and resume direct control when able. The archive should therefore distinguish **supported agency** from simple proxy takeover.

### 4. Different scopes of authorization are morally useful

Not every problem requires the same transfer of authority. Sometimes a person needs someone to receive information, sometimes to communicate, sometimes to submit documents, sometimes to argue a case, and sometimes to make decisions during incapacity. A good order should therefore permit narrower and wider forms of authorization rather than forcing one all-or-nothing helper model.

### 5. Qualification, accountability, and fee discipline matter where help becomes specialized

In tax, benefits, disability, health, immigration, veterans’ claims, housing defense, and other high-stakes fields, representation can become a site of expertise, dependency, and extraction at once. Humane systems should therefore preserve ways to verify who is allowed to represent someone, what they may do, what they may charge, and how misconduct can be challenged.

### 6. Delegation should not become the only real lane

Assisted routes are important, but they should not become the hidden operating system of access. A person should not have to find a lawyer, accredited representative, sponsor, or digitally fluent relative before the system becomes usable at all. The solo path should remain real even while help is available.

### 7. Privacy and safety require selective rather than total access

Some helpers need broad file access; others need only a notice, a date, or permission to speak with an office. Systems should prefer role-limited sharing, reviewable permissions, and exceptions for abuse, coercion, or endangerment rather than assuming that any helper must either know everything or nothing.

### 8. Delegable help should survive channel failure and transition

When a person changes phones, leaves a hospital, enters shelter, moves, loses internet, or hands a case from one institution to another, trusted assistance should not have to be painfully rebuilt from zero each time. Portable, inspectable, and replaceable authorization is part of continuity.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask whether people can add help without surrendering themselves

When evaluating courts, clinics, schools, benefit systems, migration pathways, housing systems, debt processes, workplaces, and digital public services, ask whether a person can authorize help in a way that is visible, reviewable, and limited enough to preserve their own standing.

### 2. Prefer scoped permissions over total proxy by default

A humane order should usually support narrower permissions first: receive notices, discuss the case, access specific records, submit material, speak during a hearing, or handle one defined matter. Broader substitute authority may sometimes be necessary, but it should not be the only design available.

### 3. Make assistance easy to start, inspect, change, and revoke

Authorization should not be harder than the underlying claim. People should ordinarily be able to appoint, replace, narrow, or end a helper relationship without opaque delay, humiliating suspicion, or total process restart.

### 4. Keep qualified help available without making paid help mandatory

Where expertise really matters, public or non-extractive representation, legal aid, accredited support, ombuds help, community navigators, and assisted-digital support should reduce dependence on private broker markets rather than merely coexisting with them.

### 5. Build abuse, coercion, and conflict safeguards into representation

Systems should keep room to refuse, narrow, or scrutinize representative access where a helper is unsafe, exploitative, conflicted, or acting beyond their scope. The goal is not helper romanticism but safe, dignity-preserving assistance.

## Failure modes

- **forced solo navigation**: the system assumes ideal self-service even where stakes, distress, or complexity make that unrealistic.
- **all-or-nothing proxy**: people can get help only by granting broad, sticky control rather than matter-limited assistance.
- **representative lock-in**: changing or firing a helper effectively restarts the case or risks losing place, notices, or deadlines.
- **helper-only lane**: the direct path exists on paper but the only actually usable path runs through lawyers, brokers, or insiders.
- **predatory representation**: helpers charge exploitative fees, capture documents, or monetize fear and opacity.
- **unsafe proxy recognition**: abusive relatives, coercive sponsors, or controlling partners are treated as ordinary helpers without meaningful safeguards.
- **scope blur**: a person authorizes one task, but the helper is informally treated as controlling the whole matter.
- **channel-fragile delegation**: authority disappears when the person changes phone, portal account, address, office, or institution.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat every need for help as evidence that autonomy has failed; interdependence is ordinary.
- Do not romanticize representatives, relatives, or professionals; helpers can dominate, exploit, or silence.
- Do not collapse supported agency into full substitute decision-making when narrower authorization would do.
- Do not make appointment, verification, or revocation procedures so hard that people stay trapped with the wrong helper.
- Do not answer complexity by assuming everyone should simply hire expertise.
- Do not let privacy or anti-fraud concerns become blanket reasons to forbid safe, scoped assistance.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order lets people add trusted help without losing their own standing.
