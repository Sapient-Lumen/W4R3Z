# 107 — Alternative evidence, documentary plurality, and no monopoly proof

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must treat **alternative evidence**, **documentary plurality**, and freedom from **monopoly proof** as morally important, because serious claims and ordinary standing are unjustly thinned when institutions act as though only one preferred document can establish a fact that could also be shown through bounded secondary records, agency-held data, corroborating materials, affidavits, or provisional follow-up.

## Why this matters

The archive is now strong on documentary existence, bounded verification, administrative dignity, proof reuse, and non-ordeal access. But a serious seam still remained open: **what happens when the problem is not that a fact is false or unknowable, but that the preferred document is missing, delayed, destroyed, never issued, unreachable, or held by someone else**.

That gap matters because many people do not fail documentary systems through indifference. They fail because births went unregistered, archives burned, records were destroyed in disaster or war, abusive partners hold papers, schools or employers will not cooperate, a home birth never produced the usual certificate, migration scattered the file, or homelessness made safe storage impossible. At that point downstream institutions often overread the absence of one favored document as the absence of the underlying fact. A person can really be who they say they are, really live where they say they live, really belong in a school, really qualify for a benefit, or really hold a family relationship, yet still be told that the case is impossible because one canonical paper is missing.

A good order needs a cleaner rule. The point is not that every claim should be accepted on bare assertion, that fraud controls should disappear, or that all evidence types should count equally in every context. The point is that **institutions should ordinarily ask what fact actually needs to be established and then accept an evidence ladder proportionate to that fact, rather than turning one preferred document into a monopoly gate when bounded alternative proof could still establish the matter on humane terms**.

## Core claims

### 1. Missing the preferred document is not the same thing as missing the underlying fact

A lost certificate, absent lease, inaccessible school record, or unavailable birth registration may leave a person short of one form of proof without making the fact itself false. The archive should therefore resist equating documentary interruption with factual nonexistence.

### 2. Good systems distinguish preferred evidence from exclusive evidence

It is often sensible to prefer certain documents because they are cleaner, earlier, more official, or less manipulable. But a good order should usually distinguish **what it prefers first** from **what it will accept at all**. Preference can be justified without monopoly.

### 3. Documentary plurality is a justice protection, not just an administrative convenience

Where multiple bounded forms of evidence can establish the same fact, allowing that plurality reduces exclusion, broker dependence, travel burden, delay, and humiliating restart. It is part of fair access, not merely better customer service.

### 4. Upstream state failure should not become downstream personal failure

When public registration systems are weak, archives are damaged, offices are far away, wars or disasters destroy records, or agencies cannot share or retrieve what they themselves created, the resulting gap should not simply be pushed onto the governed as if they chose documentary impossibility.

### 5. Alternative evidence should usually come in public, seriousness-sensitive ladders

A humane order should often say what the preferred evidence is, what secondary evidence can substitute if the preferred material is unavailable, what combination of documents may suffice, when affidavits are allowed, and when provisional action may proceed pending later confirmation.

### 6. Monopoly proof redistributes burden toward the already precarious

Single-document absolutism falls hardest on people experiencing poverty, displacement, homelessness, abuse, institutional churn, disability, language barriers, migration, family rupture, and low archival stability. Buffered people can usually buy replacements, hire help, or travel repeatedly; others absorb documentary rigidity as exclusion.

### 7. Anti-fraud aims remain real, but should be pursued through corroboration and bounded escalation rather than impossible-document demands

A good order may require stronger proof for higher-risk or higher-stakes claims. But it should usually prefer cross-checks, layered corroboration, targeted interviews, or temporary provisional status over blanket insistence on one unavailable paper when the underlying fact can still be responsibly assessed.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask what fact actually needs proof

When evaluating school enrollment, benefits, housing, migration, identity systems, health administration, licensing, appeals, and emergency relief, ask whether the institution has clearly identified the fact at issue rather than simply naming the document format it would most like to see.

### 2. Publish an evidence ladder rather than a document monopoly

A humane institution should often make plain what the preferred evidence is, what secondary materials may substitute, when multiple partial records can jointly satisfy the requirement, and when sworn statements or other attestations are allowed because ordinary records are unavailable.

### 3. Prefer bounded alternative evidence when primary material is unavailable

If the person can show that the preferred record does not exist, cannot reasonably be obtained, or is temporarily unreachable, institutions should usually accept relevant secondary material, corroborating records, or other seriousness-appropriate alternatives rather than treat the matter as dead on arrival.

### 4. Use provisional access where delay would itself cause avoidable harm

Where the stakes are schooling, shelter, basic assistance, safety, essential care, or similar floor goods, a good order should often separate immediate access from final archival perfection and allow provisional continuation while documentary completion proceeds.

### 5. Make institutions help close documentary gaps they rely on

If the governing body can retrieve, request, verify, or help obtain the missing record more effectively than the affected person can, it should usually bear more of that labor rather than merely rejecting the case for incompleteness. That retrieval duty should be treated as a design expectation in its own right, not only as an act of discretionary kindness after a documentary gap appears.

### 6. Preserve contestability, privacy, and proportionality

Alternative-evidence design should remain tied to the actual fact, the actual stakes, and the actual risks. It should not become an excuse for unlimited self-attestation, nor should it become a pretext for extracting every possible intimate record when a smaller bounded showing would do.

## Failure modes

- **certificate absolutism**: one preferred document is treated as the only morally or legally imaginable way to establish a fact.
- **upstream-failure-downstream-penalty**: broken registration, destroyed archives, or noncooperative institutions are translated into personal ineligibility.
- **proof monopoly by format**: the system cares more about the favored paper form than about the underlying fact it was supposed to establish.
- **no-record dead-end**: agencies acknowledge that a preferred record does not exist and still provide no usable secondary route.
- **affidavit refusal without substitute**: sworn statements, corroborating records, or bounded attestations are banned even when ordinary records are unavailable.
- **disaster and displacement amnesia**: systems behave as though documentary continuity were always easy despite war, eviction, family violence, homelessness, migration, or flood and fire loss.
- **anti-fraud overread**: every documentary gap is treated as deception rather than as a prompt for proportionate corroboration.
- **provisional-access refusal**: schools, benefits, or other floor goods are withheld entirely while paperwork that could be completed later is pursued.

## Guardrails

- Do not assume every fact can be established by every kind of document; relevance still matters.
- Do not collapse documentary plurality into limitless self-attestation; some claims need multiple pieces of evidence or stronger corroboration.
- Do not treat alternative evidence as a reason to ignore genuine fraud indicators, identity theft, or high-stakes security risks.
- Do not let provisional access become endless unresolved limbo; bounded follow-up still matters.
- Do not use flexibility for the weak while preserving document perfectionism for institutional convenience elsewhere; the point is principled proportionality.
- Do not require people to produce more intimate evidence than the fact at issue reasonably requires.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order asks what fact must be established, not which single paper it prefers, and should usually accept bounded alternative evidence when the preferred document is unavailable.
