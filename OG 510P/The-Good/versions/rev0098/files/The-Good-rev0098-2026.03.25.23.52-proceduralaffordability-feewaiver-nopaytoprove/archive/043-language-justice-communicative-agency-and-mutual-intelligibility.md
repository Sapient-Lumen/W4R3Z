# 043 — Language justice, communicative agency, and mutual intelligibility

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must treat language justice, communicative agency, and mutual intelligibility as morally central rather than as service polish, because rights, care, education, work, remedy, and belonging become half-real when people must guess, nod, defer, or disappear inside communicative worlds not built for them.

## Why this matters

The archive already protects truthful world-contact, accessibility, voice, administrative dignity, fair hearing, childhood, migration, health, and belonging. But it still lacks a direct rule about whether people can **understand and be understood** in the places that organize ordinary life. That gap matters because many people are excluded not only by closed doors or missing income, but by speech-only procedure, untranslated notices, jargon-heavy instruction, missing interpretation, absent sign language, unusable interfaces, literacy assumptions, and the requirement to hand one’s meaning over to someone else in order to get through.

A good order should therefore ask more than whether information was technically published or a service was formally available. It should ask whether ordinary people can receive reasons, express need, describe pain, contest decisions, learn, cooperate, love, teach, parent, testify, and participate without being forced into communicative dependency or pressured to abandon their language, signing practice, or communication mode in exchange for intelligibility.

## Core claims

### 1. Understanding and being understood are constitutive goods, not administrative extras

A life goes worse when crucial parts of it must be lived through guessing, strategic silence, performative nodding, or dependence on unofficial translators. Comprehension and expressive agency matter partly because they unlock other goods, but also because chronic communicative exclusion is itself a degradation of standing and self-direction.

### 2. Language hierarchy can function as domination

Official-language monism, prestige dialect gatekeeping, jargon, and speech-first institutional design can convert one group’s communicative style into everyone else’s admission test. The archive should therefore treat forced linguistic assimilation, routine misrecognition, and communicative dependence as potential forms of domination rather than as culturally neutral coordination costs.

### 3. Translation, interpretation, sign, and alternative communication belong inside justice

Translation and interpretation are not merely convenience features for edge cases. Sign languages, Braille, captioning, augmentative and alternative communication, easy-read formats, and plain-language explanation are part of what it means for law, care, schooling, and administration to be genuinely shareable.

### 4. Literacy is relational and institutional, not just a private trait

People need skills, but institutions also choose whether language is cultivated to be understandable, whether interfaces are navigable, whether trusted help exists, and whether the burden of interpretation is pushed onto families, children, or informal brokers. The archive should resist blaming individuals for communicative breakdowns that were designed upstream.

### 5. A public can only rule itself if it can actually follow what is being said and done

Shared rule degrades when policies, rights, school materials, health instructions, and civic processes are technically public yet practically unintelligible. Communicative agency therefore belongs inside participation, not outside it: people cannot meaningfully authorize, refuse, contest, or revise what they cannot adequately parse.

### 6. AI-mediated communication can help, but it also creates new dependency risks

Machine translation, summarization, transcription, and assistive interfaces can widen access. But they can also silently normalize one language, flatten nuance, misread pain or intent, and make institutional reality legible only through opaque intermediaries. The archive should therefore welcome assistive tools while resisting communicative outsourcing without review, fallback, or accountability.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask whether people can understand and answer what matters

When evaluating schools, hospitals, courts, welfare offices, border systems, workplaces, platforms, or civic processes, ask whether ordinary users can understand the issue, know what is being asked of them, and respond in a mode that preserves their agency.

### 2. Prefer multilingual, multimodal, plain-language design in advance

The archive should favor systems that anticipate linguistic and communicative plurality rather than making each person request, prove, and wait for translation, captioning, sign support, or explanation after exclusion has already occurred.

### 3. Protect communicative plurality without demanding one superior language of life

A good order may need shared languages for coordination, but it should not treat one language, accent, script, or communication tempo as the measure of full personhood, seriousness, or civic worth.

### 4. Treat forced communicative dependence as a warning sign

When a person must rely on a child, partner, boss, guard, caseworker, or opaque machine to understand or be understood in high-stakes settings, the archive should presume that privacy, dignity, and self-direction are at risk.

## Failure modes

- **official-language monism**: one dominant language or register quietly becomes the price of full participation.
- **plain-language refusal**: institutions publish enough to defend themselves but not enough for ordinary people to understand them.
- **translation as afterthought**: interpretation, multilingual provision, or captioning arrives late, only partially, or only after repeated pleading.
- **sign and AAC erasure**: speech and print are treated as the only serious modes of communication.
- **communicative dependency**: people must rely on family members, employers, guards, children, or opaque tools to mediate high-stakes interactions.
- **literacy individualization**: institutions pathologize users for not understanding texts, interfaces, or procedures designed without them in mind.
- **AI linguistic proxy rule**: automated summaries or translations become effectively final even when they distort nuance, consent, testimony, or instruction.
- **accent and register caste**: credibility and civic standing track prestige speech rather than substance.

## Guardrails

- Do not romanticize frictionless communication; translation, interpretation, and explanation cost labor and should be resourced honestly.
- Do not treat every coordination need as illegitimate simply because a shared standard exists somewhere.
- Do not let plain language become an excuse to flatten complexity when complexity is genuinely relevant and can be explained with layered access.
- Do not let family members or children become default interpreters in settings where privacy, safety, or consent are at stake.
- Do not confuse language justice with compulsory cultural preservation or with the elimination of ordinary disagreement and misfire.
- Do not let AI mediation become the silent replacement for accountable human explanation when stakes are serious.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order does not merely speak; it lets people understand and be understood without surrendering dignity, privacy, or equal standing at the point of communication.
