# 040 — Ordinary security, nonviolence, and preventable injury

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must treat ordinary security, nonviolence, and freedom from preventable injury as morally central rather than as a side effect of policing or emergency response, because no life goes well when home, school, work, transport, and public space are organized around assault risk, coercive threat, chronic fear, lethal traffic, or routine exposure to avoidable harm.

## Why this matters

The archive already protects bodily integrity, home, childhood, movement, reachability, and health. But it still lacks a direct rule for **ordinary security itself**. That gap matters because many lives are thinned not only by spectacular catastrophe, but by intimate-partner violence, harassment, coercive control, neighborhood gunfire, unsafe streets, traffic death, intimidation at school or work, and the need to organize every ordinary movement around who might hurt you.

A good order should therefore ask not only whether people remain formally free or can call emergency services after the fact, but whether they can inhabit daily life without being ruled by violence, credible threat, or preventable injury. The relevant issue is not perfect invulnerability. It is whether people can sleep, travel, learn, gather, work, and return home on terms that do not normalize fear, assault, domination, or bodily danger as the ordinary price of participation.

## Core claims

### 1. Security is a lived good, not only a state capacity

People need more than nominal rights plus institutions that react after blood is drawn. They need some reasonably reliable condition of everyday safety in homes, streets, schools, workplaces, transport systems, and public institutions. A theory of the good that notices violence only at the moment of homicide or war has arrived too late.

### 2. Freedom from violence is not exhausted by homicide statistics

Killing is the endpoint of many patterns that begin with control, threats, stalking, beatings, sexual violence, extortion, intimidation, and routine fear. A good order should therefore care about coercive threat, recurrent assault, trauma-generating environments, and the anticipatory shrinking of life that happens when people avoid public space, relationships, routes, jobs, or institutions because danger is expected.

### 3. Preventable injury belongs with security, not only with accident management

Road deaths, dangerous crossings, unsafe vehicles, unprotected work sites, neglected buildings, burns, drowning, and other major injury risks are not morally neutral background events. When people are maimed or killed by design neglect, speed-first planning, absent safeguards, or tolerated hazard, the archive should treat that as a direct security failure rather than as regrettable bad luck.

### 4. Security must not be purchased through domination

A world of checkpoints, arbitrary stops, indiscriminate surveillance, abusive prisons, punitive sweeps, and permanent suspicion can claim to deliver order while deepening fear and civic subordination. The archive should therefore reject the false choice between insecurity and domination. Good security protects people from violence without making them permanently legible, harassed, or disposable to the institutions claiming to keep them safe.

### 5. Safety is unequally distributed and often patterned by status

Women and girls, children, disabled people, migrants, racialized minorities, poor communities, LGBTQI+ people, older people, and workers in dangerous sectors often absorb more violence and more fear with less credible recourse. A serious theory of the good should therefore ask who must plan around danger, who is believed when harm occurs, and whose exposure is quietly normalized.

### 6. Prevention, survivor support, and trustworthy recourse all matter

Ordinary security requires more than deterrent rhetoric. It depends on prevention, safe design, de-escalation capacity, survivor-centered support, accountable justice, and institutions that people can approach without expecting humiliation or retaliation. The relevant aim is not securitized theater but durable conditions under which violence becomes rarer and recourse becomes real.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask whether people can move through ordinary life without credible threat or avoidable danger

When evaluating institutions, ask whether people can get home, go to school, work, use transit, gather publicly, seek help, and inhabit intimate life without assault risk, coercive control, predatory threat, or preventable bodily hazard becoming routine conditions of participation.

### 2. Treat fear-shaped nonuse and danger-shaped withdrawal as direct value losses

If people avoid parks, clinics, schools, transit stops, evening work, political participation, relationships, or public speech because violence or injury is expected, the archive should count that as a direct loss to freedom, dignity, health, and belonging rather than as a merely psychological side effect.

### 3. Prefer civilian, preventive, and accountable security over domination-heavy substitutes

Favor strategies that reduce violence through safe infrastructure, traffic calming, survivor protection, early intervention, conflict mediation, rights-respecting justice, and trustworthy institutions rather than relying mainly on indiscriminate force, mass surveillance, punitive overreach, or collective suspicion.

### 4. Track actual exposure and recourse, not only formal order or enforcement volume

The archive should ask who is assaulted, threatened, killed, injured, displaced indoors by fear, stopped or searched, ignored by institutions, unable to report safely, or pushed into self-confinement. Arrest totals, prison occupancy, checkpoint density, or generalized visibility to the state should not be treated as sufficient evidence that security is going well.

## What this changes in the archive

### 1. It thickens bodily integrity by adding the everyday background conditions of bodily safety

`archive/030-bodily-integrity-consent-and-reproductive-agency.md` already protects embodied authority against invasive control; this note adds that bodily life is also injured when assault risk, coercive threat, or preventable injury structure ordinary movement before any intimate intervention is formally requested.

### 2. It sharpens home, reachability, childhood, and movement

`archive/029-home-dwelling-place-and-non-displacement.md` gains a clearer distinction between merely being indoors and being secure enough to dwell. `archive/037-everyday-reachability-daily-mobility-and-proximate-access.md` gains a sharper account of why dangerous routes, unsafe crossings, and predatory public space are not mere transport inconveniences. `archive/027-childhood-development-and-just-guardianship.md` and `archive/033-movement-migration-membership-and-non-exile.md` gain a cleaner language for child safety, school routes, community violence, persecution, and the difference between movement formally permitted and movement actually safe.

### 3. It keeps the archive from confusing security with punishment theater

This note gives the archive a more disciplined way to judge policing, justice, traffic design, school discipline, platform safety policy, and emergency governance: not by visible force, data extraction, or punitive symbolism alone, but by whether violence and preventable injury actually fall while equal standing and non-domination remain intact. `archive/085-bounded-emergency-exception-drift-and-return-to-ordinary-rule.md` sharpens the emergency-governance side of that judgment by insisting that curfew logic, checkpoint creep, generalized stop powers, and other temporary controls should not become ordinary civic posture once the acute danger has changed.

## Failure modes

- **fear-conditioned freedom**: people are formally free but organize life around assault risk, intimidation, or retaliatory threat.
- **domestic and intimate terror**: homes, partnerships, or family systems become sites of coercive control, violence, stalking, or femicide risk.
- **dangerous public ordinary life**: parks, streets, schools, transit, and workplaces are usable mainly for the protected or the escorted.
- **traffic violence normalization**: death and injury from road design, vehicle speed, and unsafe crossings are treated as background inevitabilities.
- **security through domination**: surveillance, arbitrary stops, collective suspicion, abusive detention, or militarized response are treated as acceptable substitutes for trustworthy safety.
- **survivor abandonment**: reporting channels, shelters, health care, legal aid, and protective recourse are too weak, too dangerous, or too humiliating to use.
- **status-weighted protection**: some groups receive rapid protection and presumption of credibility while others absorb violence plus institutional disbelief.

## Guardrails

- Do not equate security with punitive intensity, prison growth, or permanent police discretion.
- Do not treat violence as solved because homicide falls while fear, coercive control, injury, or institutional abuse remain high.
- Do not let public-order language erase protest rights, sanctuary, due process, privacy, or freedom from arbitrary interference.
- Do not isolate violence from housing, gender order, poverty, weapons availability, transport design, labor conditions, schooling, or migration status.
- Do not romanticize resilience, vigilance, or private self-protection when public institutions have normalized preventable danger.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order lets people inhabit ordinary life without having to trade freedom, dignity, or public presence for protection against violence and avoidable injury.
