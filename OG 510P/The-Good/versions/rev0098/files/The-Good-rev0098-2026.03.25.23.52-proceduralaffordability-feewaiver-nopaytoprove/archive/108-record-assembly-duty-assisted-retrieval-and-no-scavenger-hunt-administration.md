# 108 — Record assembly duty, assisted retrieval, and no scavenger-hunt administration

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must treat **record assembly duty**, **assisted retrieval**, and freedom from **scavenger-hunt administration** as morally important, because governance becomes unjust when institutions that can retrieve, transfer, verify, or request records more effectively instead force ordinary people to act as couriers between offices, schools, providers, and databases.

## Why this matters

The archive is now strong on documentary existence, proof reuse, alternative evidence, submission integrity, and trusted assistance. But a serious seam still remained open: **what happens when the relevant institution does not yet hold the needed record in the right place, but could often fetch, request, transfer, or verify it far more effectively than the person whose life is stalled by its absence**.

That gap matters because many ordeals are produced not by uncertainty about the underlying fact, but by labor allocation inside fragmented systems. A family changes school districts and is told to personally chase the old file. A disability claimant is expected to hunt down treatment records that the adjudicator can directly request. A Medicaid beneficiary is asked for information the state could instead check through available data sources. A patient or student becomes the hand-carried bridge between institutions that already know how to talk to one another, or should. In those moments the system is not merely asking for proof. It is pushing inter-organizational coordination work downward onto the person with the least leverage, least access, and often the most urgent stake.

A good order needs a cleaner rule. The point is not that agencies should fuse all data, ignore consent, or eliminate bounded user responsibility. The point is that **when a governing body can retrieve, transfer, or verify the needed material more effectively than the affected person can, it should usually bear more of that labor rather than turning official fragmentation into a scavenger hunt for the governed**.

## Core claims

### 1. User-as-courier administration is a real moral injury

When people must shuttle records, signatures, verifications, or confirmations between institutions that could more humanely coordinate, they lose time, wages, privacy, stability, and often practical access itself. That burden should count morally, not be dismissed as ordinary bureaucracy.

### 2. The better-positioned actor should usually bear more of the retrieval burden

If one institution can request a record directly, verify a fact through an authoritative source, transfer a file promptly, or use already-available data more easily than the person can, it should usually do so. Justice is not only about what evidence counts, but also about who must assemble it.

### 3. Record assembly duty is distinct from both proof reuse and alternative evidence

`archive/106-proof-reuse-once-only-design-and-no-repeated-demands-for-known-facts.md` addresses cases where the system already knows enough or could safely carry facts forward. `archive/107-alternative-evidence-documentary-plurality-and-no-monopoly-proof.md` addresses cases where the preferred proof is unavailable and another bounded ladder should count. This note covers the adjacent seam: the needed material may exist and may be the preferred record, but the person should not be forced to perform the retrieval choreography when the institution is better placed to do so.

### 4. Prompt record transfer is part of continuity, not optional aftercare

School changes, care transitions, appeal routing, agency transfers, and cross-program renewals all depend on records moving fast enough to preserve the point of continuity. A humane order should treat delayed or ownerless transfer as a substantive failure, not as clerical background noise.

### 5. Retrieval duty should remain bounded by consent, purpose, and relevance

This note does not defend unlimited record fishing. Good retrieval should remain tied to the actual decision, the actual institution, the actual authority to request or receive the material, and the actual stakes at hand. Not every office should be able to pull everything.

### 6. When retrieval fails, the person should not usually bear the whole collapse

If the system cannot obtain the record promptly, a humane order should usually consider provisional access, alternative evidence, targeted follow-up, or preserved position rather than simply converting institutional non-coordination into personal failure.

### 7. Scavenger-hunt administration redistributes burden toward the already precarious

People with money, time flexibility, English fluency, transport, storage, legal help, and stable devices can often chase multiple offices. Others face missing work, dead phones, child care conflicts, domestic control, disability fatigue, or address instability. Record-chasing therefore becomes another inequality multiplier.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask who can get the record with the least human cost

When evaluating benefits, schooling, health administration, licensing, migration, disability adjudication, appeals, and public-service design, ask whether the institution or the person is actually better positioned to obtain the needed record or verification.

### 2. Default toward institution-side retrieval where lawful and proportionate

If the governing body can directly request, transfer, or check the material through an authoritative source with appropriate consent and relevance limits, it should usually do so rather than demand that the user become a courier between fragmented offices.

### 3. Request only what is truly missing

A humane system should usually identify the missing fact or record narrowly, use available data first, and avoid restarting the whole application or demanding a full documentary packet when one bounded retrieval step would resolve the question.

### 4. Make retrieval status legible

If the institution is waiting on another office, provider, school, or agency, the person should ordinarily be able to tell what was requested, when it was requested, what remains outstanding, and whether any action is still actually required from them.

### 5. Preserve a bridge when official coordination is slow

Where the stakes involve floor goods or serious standing, a good order should often preserve provisional access, current placement, or timely challenge rights while record transfer or verification is still pending.

### 6. Keep retrieval duty privacy-bounded and contestable

Institution-side retrieval should remain limited by role, law, necessity, and correction rights. The point is not to maximize data pull, but to stop making people hand-carry official fragmentation on their own bodies and schedules.

## What this changes in the archive

### 1. It sharpens administrative burden by naming scavenger-hunt governance as ordeal design

`archive/036-administrative-burden-procedural-dignity-and-non-ordeal-access.md` already rejects repeated proof, queue domination, and channel fragmentation. This note adds a cleaner rule: when institutions can more humanely retrieve or transfer the needed material themselves, making users chase it across offices is itself part of the ordeal.

### 2. It deepens proof reuse by distinguishing carry-forward from retrieval duty

`archive/106-proof-reuse-once-only-design-and-no-repeated-demands-for-known-facts.md` already rejects ask-again defaults. This note adds that humane administration still fails when the person is forced to fetch records from elsewhere even though the governing institution could have requested or checked them directly.

### 3. It thickens alternative evidence by protecting people while preferred records are being pursued

`archive/107-alternative-evidence-documentary-plurality-and-no-monopoly-proof.md` already resists one-document absolutism. This note adds that record assembly duty and documentary plurality should often work together: the institution should try to retrieve what it can, and where that remains slow or impossible, bounded alternatives should keep the matter live.

### 4. It extends handoff justice into file movement and live ownership

`archive/094-handoff-justice-discharge-discipline-and-no-transfer-into-nothing.md` already protects discharge, referral, release, and transfer from becoming disposal into dead space. This note adds that handoff justice includes actual record movement, prompt response to transfer requests, and clear ownership of the next request for information.

### 5. It strengthens trusted assistance without making helpers the only bridge

`archive/100-trusted-assistance-bounded-delegation-and-no-forced-solo-navigation.md` already protects access to bounded help. This note adds that even with trusted assistance available, institutions should not design the baseline path as a scavenger hunt that practically requires a helper, broker, or fixer to move official paper between systems.

## Failure modes

- **user-as-courier administration**: the person must physically or digitally shuttle records between institutions that could request or transfer them directly.
- **office ping-pong**: each institution says the other must act first, leaving the person to coordinate the deadlock.
- **bring-us-our-own-record**: an agency demands that a user provide information the state or program itself generated or can directly access.
- **narrow-missing-item-overread**: one outstanding verification step is treated as grounds to restart the entire case or deny the whole matter.
- **silent retrieval limbo**: the institution says it is waiting on another record but provides no usable status, timing, or ownership clarity.
- **helper-required baseline**: the path becomes practically navigable only for those with lawyers, social workers, sponsors, or fixers who can do the chasing.
- **privacy-breaking overpull**: the institution responds to burden concerns by grabbing more data than the decision actually requires.
- **transfer-without-bridge**: the record is in motion or requested, but the person loses service, placement, or position before the file arrives.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat record assembly duty as a license for unrestricted data fusion or indiscriminate file sharing.
- Do not ignore consent, confidentiality, or role limits simply because retrieval would be convenient.
- Do not assume institution-side retrieval is always faster; in some cases the person really can supply the material more quickly and with less risk.
- Do not let official requests drift into open-ended waiting without status visibility, escalation, or provisional protection.
- Do not collapse this note into proof reuse; the point here is specifically about who must retrieve records across seams that still exist.
- Do not collapse this note into alternative evidence; retrieval of the preferred record and acceptance of bounded alternatives should often operate together.

## Compression rule for the archive

If institutions can retrieve, transfer, or verify the needed record more easily than the person can, they should usually do so rather than turning official fragmentation into a scavenger hunt for ordinary users.
