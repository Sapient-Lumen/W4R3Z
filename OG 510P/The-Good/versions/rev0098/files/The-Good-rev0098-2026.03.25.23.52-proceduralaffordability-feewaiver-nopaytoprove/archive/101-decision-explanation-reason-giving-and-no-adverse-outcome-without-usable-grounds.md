# 101 — Decision explanation, reason-giving, and no adverse outcome without usable grounds

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must treat **decision explanation**, **reason-giving**, and **usable grounds** as morally important, because rights, care, credit, schooling, migration status, benefits, and other serious interests are thinner than they look when institutions can deny, downgrade, sanction, or refuse without giving reasons specific enough for ordinary people to understand the actual basis, locate what is fixable, and know what kind of review could matter.

## Why this matters

The archive is now strong on fair hearing, notice justice, submission integrity, status visibility, record contestability, and trusted assistance. But it still lacks a direct canon rule for a seam that sits between those gains: **the explanation attached to the outcome itself**.

That gap matters because many serious systems now do something morally evasive. They tell a person that a claim was denied, a benefit was reduced, a file was flagged, a score failed threshold, a waiver was refused, or an application did not qualify, yet they do not say enough about the real basis of that outcome for ordinary correction or review to begin. The message may mention “policy,” “standards,” “insufficient evidence,” “internal criteria,” or a generic category label while leaving unclear which fact mattered, which document failed, which requirement was not met, which issue was dispositive, or what kind of response could realistically change the result.

That failure is not just a drafting flaw. It changes the practical meaning of review. A person who cannot tell why they lost often cannot tell whether to correct the record, provide missing material, challenge the rule, seek accommodation, narrow the dispute, or accept that the case turned on something not easily altered. At that point appeal becomes blind resistance, not reason-tracking challenge.

The archive therefore needs a cleaner rule: serious decisions should ordinarily come with **usable grounds**. The standard is not that every outcome requires a treatise. It is that a person facing a consequential yes-or-no from an institution should usually be able to tell **what was decided, on what actual basis, what issue or deficiency controlled, and where review or correction would have to bite**.

## Core claims

### 1. Reasons are part of the decision, not a courtesy after it

A denial, sanction, closure, downgrade, discharge refusal, or adverse rating is morally incomplete when the institution will act on it while withholding usable grounds. Explanation belongs to fair rule itself, not to a separate customer-service layer.

### 2. Good explanation ties the outcome to actual grounds

In serious contexts, a humane explanation should usually identify the rule, criterion, threshold, fact pattern, or evidentiary deficiency that actually controlled the result. The point is not maximal legal exposition. The point is to avoid reasons so generic that they could have been attached to many different cases without revealing which one this is.

### 3. Reasons should make correction and review more reality-tracking

A good explanation narrows the field of disagreement. It helps the affected person tell whether the problem is factual error, missing proof, wrong rule, failed discretion, timing, identity mismatch, unsupported judgment, or something else. Without that discipline, correction and appeal become guesswork.

### 4. Generic and black-box reasons redistribute harm downward

Buffered people can often decode a bad denial by hiring counsel, asking insiders, or repeatedly pressing for detail. Others absorb the same opacity as finality. Reason-giving is therefore a distributive protection as well as a rule-of-law one.

### 5. Automation does not relax the duty of explanation

If a scoring model, workflow engine, or automated classifier materially shapes an adverse outcome, that is a reason to build better explanation discipline, not an excuse to give less. A system should not be allowed to become too complex or opaque to say why it acted against someone.

### 6. Layered explanation often beats both slogan and document dump

The archive should resist two bad forms at once: the one-line generic denial and the hundred-page administrative mass that never identifies the decisive point. Humane explanation is often layered: visible outcome, issue-by-issue reasons, decisive deficiency, and a route into fuller record or legal detail.

### 7. Some secrecy is compatible with usable grounds, but not with blank adverse action

Privacy, safety, investigative integrity, and third-party protection can justify bounded withholding. But those concerns should not automatically collapse into outcome-without-reason. Good systems usually can still say more than nothing about the operative basis and the available review path.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask whether the person can tell what actually decided the case

When evaluating courts, agencies, schools, hospitals, insurers, lenders, migration systems, platforms, and employers, ask whether the affected person can tell what issue, record item, rule, threshold, or factual deficiency actually controlled the serious outcome.

### 2. Prefer issue-specific reasons over policy labels

A humane order should prefer reasons that identify the real ground of decision rather than labels such as “policy,” “internal standards,” “failed review,” or “did not qualify” standing alone.

### 3. Connect the explanation to the next meaningful path

Good reason-giving should help a person tell whether they should correct the record, supply missing proof, request accommodation, challenge the rule, seek discretionary reconsideration, or pursue a fuller appeal. Where the identified problem is genuinely curable, the archive now also presumes that serious systems should often offer a real cure path before treating that deficiency as final loss.

### 4. Keep reasons durable and retrievable

Serious outcomes should not depend on one phone call, one disappearing portal screen, or one temporary status message. The explanation should ordinarily be stable enough to revisit, share with a helper, compare against the file, and use in later review.

### 5. Treat explanation failure as a justice defect, not only a communications defect

If the result is consequential and the reasons are too generic to guide correction or challenge, the archive should treat that as part of the substantive moral failure of the system rather than as optional letter-quality polish.

## What this changes in the archive

### 1. It thickens fair hearing by disciplining the decision before the appeal

`archive/042-fair-hearing-effective-remedy-and-non-arbitrary-rule.md` already protects hearing, review, and correction. This note adds that those protections remain thinner than they look when the initial adverse decision never says enough about its actual basis for a person to know what to contest.

### 2. It sharpens notice justice by distinguishing warning from grounded outcome explanation

`archive/096-notice-justice-actionable-warning-and-no-rights-lost-by-unusable-notice.md` already protects deadlines, stakes, and next-step clarity. This note adds a downstream discipline: even a timely and readable notice is morally thin if the decision it communicates still lacks specific, usable grounds.

### 3. It deepens record contestability by tying the file to articulated reasons

`archive/099-record-contestability-inspectability-and-no-uncorrectable-governing-file.md` already protects inspectability and correction of the governing record. This note adds that file access is still incomplete when the institution never states which part of that file or which judgment about it actually drove the outcome.

### 4. It extends public reason and rule-of-law discipline into ordinary administration

`archive/006-institutional-good-truth-seeking-rights-and-contestability.md` and `archive/078-public-reason-plural-flourishing-and-non-orthodox-common-life.md` already reject opaque domination. This note adds a practical everyday test: serious administration should usually say enough about the actual basis of its decisions to make power answerable without insider translation.

### 5. It links explanation quality to non-brokered access

`archive/093-broker-dependence-fixer-markets-and-non-insider-rights.md` and `archive/100-trusted-assistance-bounded-delegation-and-no-forced-solo-navigation.md` already resist making help mandatory while preserving optional assistance. This note adds that explanation quality is one of the things that determines whether optional help stays optional or quietly becomes required to decode what happened.

## Failure modes

- **blank adverse outcome**: the institution says no, reduce, terminate, or close without giving reasons usable for ordinary challenge.
- **generic policy incantation**: the explanation names only standards, policy, internal criteria, or score failure without identifying the actual ground.
- **black-box denial**: automation or model complexity is treated as a reason why specific and accurate explanation cannot be given.
- **appeal-without-target**: review exists formally, but the person cannot tell what factual, legal, or evidentiary point the review should address.
- **record-without-rationale**: the person can inspect documents or fields yet still cannot tell which part actually decided the case.
- **issue bundling**: multiple grounds are collapsed into one opaque rejection, preventing focused correction or partial success.
- **document dump masquerading as explanation**: a mass of material is delivered without identifying the decisive deficiency or operative basis.
- **disappearing reasons**: the explanation exists only on a transient screen, unlogged call, or temporary portal state.

## Guardrails

- Do not require every serious decision to come with maximal legal prose; the standard is usable grounds, not essay length.
- Do not ignore legitimate limits tied to privacy, safety, investigative integrity, or third-party protection; some details may need bounded presentation.
- Do not let algorithmic or institutional complexity serve as a blanket excuse for adverse action without specific and accurate reasons.
- Do not confuse explanation with persuasion; a person can understand the grounds and still reasonably reject them.
- Do not treat a list of possible reasons as enough when the system can identify the real decisive reason.
- Do not offload clarity onto appeal bodies, caseworkers, or private helpers after the initial decision should already have said more.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order does not impose serious adverse outcomes without saying enough about the actual basis to make correction, rebuttal, and review more than guesswork.
