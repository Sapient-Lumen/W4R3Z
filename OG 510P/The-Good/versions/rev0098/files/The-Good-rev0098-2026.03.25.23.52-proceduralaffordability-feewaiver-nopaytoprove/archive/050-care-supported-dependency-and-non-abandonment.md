# 050 — Care, supported dependency, and non-abandonment

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must treat care, supported dependency, and freedom from abandonment as morally central rather than as mere private burden or service-sector aftercare, because lives go badly when dependence is met only through exhausted families, coercive institutions, gendered sacrifice, or the quiet withdrawal of help needed to remain alive, included, and self-directing.

## Why this matters

The archive already protects childhood, disability access, health continuity, later life, intimacy, dwelling, economic security, and administrative dignity. But it still lacks a direct rule for what happens when people need sustained help with eating, bathing, communicating, travelling, remembering, regulating distress, caring for children, surviving frailty, or carrying ordinary life through illness or impairment — and when the available answers are **do it all alone, leave women to absorb the load, rely on underpaid workers, or accept confinement and warehousing as the price of support**.

That gap matters because dependency is not a rare exception to otherwise independent adulthood. Human beings begin dependent, often become dependent again through disability, illness, accident, pregnancy, mental distress, caregiving responsibilities, or older age, and remain interdependent throughout life even when they appear self-sufficient. A good order should therefore ask more than whether a family member is present, whether a bed exists somewhere, or whether discharge from hospital was technically completed. It should ask whether care and support are available on terms that preserve dignity, agency, privacy, community presence, and non-ruin for both those who require care and those who provide it.

A humane order should also resist two familiar distortions at once. One is **care romanticism**: pretending love, family duty, or feminine virtue can substitute for public support, respite, wages, or rights. The other is **care as custody**: treating people who need help as passive objects to be supervised, institutionalized, or overruled for administrative convenience. The good requires a middle path: support strong enough to prevent abandonment and bounded enough to avoid domination.

## Core claims

### 1. Dependency is a normal part of human life, not a marginal exception

A theory of the good should not treat dependence as a deviation from the real subject of politics. Children, disabled people, sick people, pregnant and postpartum people, people in mental distress, older people, and many carers all show that vulnerability and interdependence are ordinary features of social life. Care is therefore not morally peripheral infrastructure. It is one of the conditions that makes agency, learning, work, participation, and attachment possible in the first place. `archive/079-meaning-ordinary-worth-and-non-heroic-flourishing.md` adds that care is often not only enabling background labor but also a direct locus of meaning, seriousness, and human worth.

### 2. Care must protect both care receivers and care providers

The archive should reject designs that help one side by consuming the other. Support systems fail when people who require care are abandoned, warehoused, or forced into institutional dependence; they also fail when family members, mostly women, are expected to provide endless unpaid labor at the cost of income, sleep, health, privacy, and public life. A good order should therefore ask simultaneously: **is the needed support available, and is providing it itself made humane and sustainable?**

### 3. Non-abandonment matters, but so does non-domination inside care

People can be badly harmed both by the absence of help and by help delivered as control. Good care is not merely a larger quantity of supervision. It should be organized so that assistance with daily life, communication, movement, treatment, or decision-making enlarges the cared-for person's practical freedom as far as possible rather than replacing it with custodial rule. The archive should therefore prefer support that is person-directed, reviewable, proportionate, and compatible with community life.

### 4. Families and intimacy matter, but family availability is not a policy solution

Family, friendship, and chosen kin often provide irreplaceable forms of care. But treating them as an inexhaustible reserve lets states, employers, and markets offload costs downward. It also reproduces gender asymmetry, isolation, and exitlessness. A good order should therefore support intimate care without presuming that love can safely substitute for respite, income support, leave, training, substitute care, disability support, or accessible public services.

### 5. Community support and home-based care should usually be preferred to segregation and warehousing

Congregate settings are sometimes necessary, especially for short periods or for complex care. But a system that defaults to institutional placement, physical restraint, family separation, or social isolation when support needs rise is usually revealing a design failure elsewhere. The archive should therefore treat community inclusion, home and neighborhood continuity, supported decision-making, and meaningful visitation as primary care goods rather than optional amenities.

### 6. Care work requires decent work, public financing, and temporal protection

High-quality care depends on workers and supporters who are not exhausted, disposable, undertrained, or unable to live on their wages. It also depends on carers having time: leave, predictable schedules, respite, backup, transport, replacement care, and enough income security to refuse impossible combinations of paid work and full-time support. Care systems that rely on burnout, migration precarity, or poverty wages are not merely underperforming; they are degrading the good on both sides at once.

### 7. Self-care and care reciprocity also belong inside the good

The point of a care rule is not to divide the world into providers and dependents once and for all. People need room to care for themselves, recover strength, practice reciprocity where they can, and move in and out of giving and receiving support across time. A humane order should therefore recognize care as a field of mutual vulnerability and mutual maintenance rather than as one class of people permanently carrying another.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask whether needed support can be obtained without abandonment, captivity, or ruin

When evaluating health systems, disability supports, eldercare, childcare, schools, workplaces, housing systems, migration regimes, and public-benefit systems, ask whether people can obtain needed help with daily life and participation without being left alone, pushed into humiliating dependence, or driven into poverty, burnout, or institutional confinement. Also ask whether support can be obtained without repeated traumatic retelling, coerced family disclosure, or sincerity performances that turn care into confessional access.

### 2. Track the care relation on both sides

The archive should evaluate whether those who require care have continuity, choice, privacy, and community presence, and whether those who provide care have decent pay, leave, respite, training, voice, and enough support to avoid exhaustion or coercive obligation.

### 3. Prefer person-directed, community-based, reviewable support

Good systems should make home and community support, respite, substitute care, supported decision-making, and portable benefits ordinary rather than exceptional, while keeping institutional placement, coercive control, and family overrule under strong scrutiny and clear review.

### 4. Treat unpaid care overload and institutional warehousing as high-weight warning signs

A society should not congratulate itself on family values, service provision, or bed capacity when the real system runs on invisible unpaid labor, women’s lost income, migrant worker precarity, social isolation, or mass institutionalization.

## Failure modes

- **care romanticism**: love, family duty, or feminine virtue are used to excuse public withdrawal and hidden overload.
- **abandonment by default**: support exists formally but disappears in practice through waiting lists, unaffordable gaps, or the assumption that someone at home will cope.
- **institutional warehousing**: people are segregated, restrained, or isolated because community support was never built.
- **caregiver burn-through**: the system relies on chronic exhaustion, lost earnings, broken sleep, and disappearing social life among unpaid carers.
- **support as custody**: assistance is delivered through overrule, coercive guardianship, surveillance, or routine denial of privacy and choice.
- **discharge into nothing**: hospitals, schools, prisons, and agencies transfer people out without building the support needed to live afterward.
- **migrant-care extraction**: care demand is met by workers whose own rights, family life, or migration status remain precarious.
- **respite scarcity**: carers can get help only at crisis point, after burnout, or not at all.
- **family-only design**: policy quietly assumes a stable, willing, co-resident family even where none exists or where the family itself is unsafe.

## Guardrails

- Do not romanticize unpaid care or confuse devotion with justice.
- Do not treat institutional placement as a neutral solution merely because a bed or slot exists.
- Do not assume all support enhances freedom; support can dominate when it is not reviewable, chosen, or bounded.
- Do not reduce care to women’s responsibility, migrant labor, or charitable improvisation.
- Do not collapse care into medicine alone; daily support, respite, communication help, accompaniment, and ordinary maintenance matter too.
- Do not presume that family authority or professional expertise automatically outrank the voice of the person receiving support.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order meets dependency without forcing people into abandonment, exhaustion, or custody as the price of being cared for or of caring for others.
