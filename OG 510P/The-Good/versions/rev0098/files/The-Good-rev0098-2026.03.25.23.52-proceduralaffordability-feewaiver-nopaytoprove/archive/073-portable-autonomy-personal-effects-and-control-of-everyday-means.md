# 073 — Portable autonomy, personal effects, and control of everyday means

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must treat secure control over ordinary personal effects, assistive products, documents, keys, devices, medicines, and other everyday means as morally important rather than as minor possessions, because people cannot reliably act, remember, communicate, leave, study, care, or dwell when the small material handles of agency are confiscable, shared only at another's pleasure, or constantly at risk of loss.

## Why this matters

The archive already protects home, privacy, documentary existence, accessibility, economic security, movement, and non-abandonment. But it still lacks a direct canon rule for **the close-at-hand means through which those goods are actually carried from hour to hour**. Without that rule, the theory can denounce domination while overlooking the everyday mechanics by which domination is enacted: no key of one's own, no secure place for medicines, no charger, no safe custody for papers, no wheelchair maintenance, no hearing-aid batteries, no glasses replacement, no phone or offline fallback, no bag, no locker, no way to keep what makes ordinary agency possible.

That omission matters because many forms of deprivation arrive as loss of small means before they arrive as headline catastrophe. A person can be technically housed while having nowhere safe to keep documents or a child's school things. A patient can be nominally discharged while leaving without medicines, charger, glasses, or mobility aid. A disabled person can possess formal rights while the relevant assistive products are unavailable, broken, unaffordable to replace, or treated as optional extras. A migrant, unhoused person, survivor of domestic abuse, foster youth, prisoner leaving detention, or older person moved between institutions can be left with status on paper but without secure custody of the ordinary items through which that status becomes usable.

The opposite mistake is also possible. A theory of personal means can slide into consumerism, gadget maximalism, or private accumulation as the measure of freedom. The archive therefore needs a middle rule: distinguish humane control of essential everyday means from prestige consumption, and protect the former without mistaking endless ownership for the good.

## Core claims

### 1. Small means can be constitutive of freedom

Freedom is not enacted only through abstract rights or large assets. It is often enacted through keys, clothing, bags, phones, chargers, medicine organizers, eyeglasses, hearing devices, wheelchairs, identity papers, bank access, school materials, and other ordinary items that let people move through the day without begging.

### 2. Control often matters more than formal title

What matters is not only whether an item is nominally "owned," but whether the user can actually keep, reach, charge, repair, replace, and carry it without sponsor permission, family veto, custodial gatekeeping, or punitive confiscation. Borrowed access under another person's discretion is often much thinner than secure control.

### 3. Storage, charging, and repair are moral infrastructure

An ID in theory is not enough if there is nowhere safe to keep it. A phone is not a gateway if it cannot be charged. A wheelchair is not real access if maintenance and replacement are absent. The archive should therefore treat lockers, shelves, secure drawers, repair routes, charging points, battery supply, and replacement pathways as part of whether everyday means are genuinely available.

### 4. Assistive products are often body-world bridges rather than optional accessories

For many people, assistive products are not conveniences layered on top of ordinary life. They are part of how ordinary life becomes possible at all. The archive should therefore resist treating glasses, hearing devices, wheelchairs, communication tools, medication organizers, prostheses, and similar supports as discretionary add-ons or luxury upgrades.

### 5. Documentary and digital means need plural fallback

Modern life increasingly routes recognition, payment, movement, schooling, and care through cards, phones, passwords, portals, and app-based notices. A humane order should therefore resist one-token dependence. Losing one phone, card, password, battery charge, or bag should not suspend a person's access to rights, shelter, income, care, or movement.

### 6. Portable autonomy matters most under dependency, displacement, and exit

The stakes are clearest when people are moving between systems or trying to leave controlling ones: hospital discharge, foster transitions, disaster evacuation, migration, detention release, elder relocation, shelter use, intimate-partner escape, or poverty under constant movement. In those settings, the ability to carry and keep one's everyday means becomes part of whether one can act as a person rather than as managed cargo.

### 7. The moral core is humane sufficiency, not possession maximalism

A good order need not promise everyone unlimited private goods. But it should secure enough practical control over the ordinary means of agency that daily life is not continuously routed through dependency, confiscability, and improvisation.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask whether people can keep and reach the close-at-hand means of ordinary life

When evaluating homes, shelters, schools, clinics, care settings, detention, transport, workplaces, border systems, and digital services, ask whether people can safely keep, carry, charge, repair, replace, and use the ordinary items through which they communicate, move, prove identity, manage health, learn, and leave.

### 2. Treat storage, custody, and replacement as part of the good itself

A humane order should not stop at issuing documents, prescribing devices, or permitting possession in theory. It should also protect safe custody, low-friction replacement, repair continuity, and ordinary protection against loss, theft, confiscation, weather damage, or forced abandonment.

### 3. Count confiscability and forced sharing as domination risks

If a person's access to documents, medicines, devices, aids, money, or keys can be casually seized, withheld, inspected, or rationed by parents, partners, employers, landlords, institutions, or platforms, the archive should treat that as a live domination concern rather than as incidental household management or anti-fraud routine.

### 4. Build plural fallback instead of one-token dependence

Prefer systems with offline routes, human help, secondary credentials, secure lockers, charging access, spare-device pathways, repair support, and replacement options so one lost object or dead battery does not collapse a person's access to ordinary life.

### 5. Keep luxury consumption distinct from essential everyday means

The relevant claim is not that all consumer desire must be satisfied. It is that no one should be made practically voiceless, immobile, or dependent because the small means of ordinary agency are unavailable, unsafe to keep, impossible to replace, or always held by somebody else.

## Failure modes

- **issued-but-unusable**: documents, devices, or medicines exist in theory but cannot be safely kept, carried, charged, repaired, or interpreted.
- **sponsor-held life**: employers, partners, camp officials, institutions, or family members keep practical custody over a person's papers, money, keys, devices, or aids.
- **shelter-without-storage**: people are admitted somewhere to sleep but cannot safely keep clothes, documents, school materials, mobility aids, or private items.
- **assistive interruption**: access collapses because batteries, maintenance, consumables, repairs, or replacement paths are missing.
- **digital single-token failure**: one dead phone, lost password, broken screen, or missing network connection shuts off rights, contact, or movement.
- **confiscation as routine discipline**: institutions normalize taking away phones, chargers, aids, medicines, or personal effects as a management shortcut without proportionate justification.
- **consumerist overread**: the importance of everyday means is misread as a demand for endless private accumulation rather than humane control over essential practical tools.

## Guardrails

- Do not collapse this note into gadget worship, status consumption, or private accumulation as the measure of freedom.
- Do not assume formal ownership is the only good arrangement; shared or public provision can be humane if user control, privacy, continuity, and low-friction access are genuinely protected.
- Do not let anti-theft, anti-fraud, or housekeeping rhetoric normalize confiscation, sponsor custody, or humiliating searches.
- Do not treat assistive products as optional accessories when they are often conditions of mobility, communication, and dignified participation.
- Do not ignore the infrastructure of lockers, charging, spare parts, repair, and replacement by focusing only on first issuance or purchase.
- Do not mistake digital convenience for resilience when offline, low-tech, or human fallback is absent.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order gives people secure, ordinary control over the small means by which agency is enacted, so daily life is not hostage to confiscation, breakage, dependence, or nowhere-safe-to-put-what-keeps-a-person going.
