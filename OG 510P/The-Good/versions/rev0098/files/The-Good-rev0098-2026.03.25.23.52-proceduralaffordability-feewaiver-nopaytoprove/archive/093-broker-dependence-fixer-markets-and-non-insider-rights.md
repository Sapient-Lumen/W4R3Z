# 093 — Broker dependence, fixer markets, and non-insider rights

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** should treat **broker dependence** as a first-class design failure: when ordinary people effectively need a fixer, sponsor, recruiter, consultant, lawyer, connected insider, or paid intermediary to reach basic rights, floor goods, public services, or first-step remedies, formal openness has decayed into access-by-mediation.

## Why this matters

The archive is already strong on administrative dignity, legibility, presumptive permission, anti-hostage design, fair allocation under scarcity, and access to remedy. But it still lacks a direct canon rule against a recurring deformation: **ordinary life made broker-dependent**.

That deformation appears when migrants need recruiters, sponsors, or labor intermediaries not merely to find work but to move, complain, or change employers; when rights to documents, hearings, permits, or benefits become practically usable only through paid expediters or inside knowledge; when parents need consultants to navigate schooling systems; when only people with caseworkers can survive public-service mazes; when a fixer market forms around appointments, queue slots, or document repair; and when institutions quietly treat this as normal because help technically exists.

A good order should therefore draw a sharper line. Optional guidance, advocacy, translation, legal aid, community accompaniment, and specialist support can be good. But they are good **as support**, not as the hidden operating system of ordinary access. Rights that require a broker to become usable are thinner than they look.

## Core claims

### 1. Optional help is different from compulsory mediation

People often benefit from guides, advocates, interpreters, social workers, organizers, navigators, and lawyers. The problem is not the existence of help. The problem is when help stops being supportive and becomes the practical precondition for crossing the threshold at all.

### 2. Broker dependence redistributes power upward

When access depends on fixers, insiders, or sponsors, power shifts toward those who already possess money, networks, language fluency, professional literacy, or institutional familiarity. Systems then reward connection and managed navigation rather than equal standing.

### 3. A direct path should exist for ordinary first-step access

For basic rights, floor-adjacent goods, and first-step public processes, an ordinary newcomer should usually be able to find the entry point, understand the first decision, submit the basic claim, receive the basic answer, and know how to correct an error without needing patronage, paid brokerage, or institutional kinship.

### 4. Intermediary markets are diagnostic signals

When recruiters, expediters, visa agents, admissions consultants, queue watchers, document fixers, or politically connected middlemen become normal rather than exceptional, that is usually evidence that the underlying public path is too opaque, slow, brittle, exclusionary, or discretionary.

### 5. Genuine complexity does not license insider-only design

Some domains really are complex: migration law, disability adjudication, serious illness, tax, environmental regulation, and high-stakes litigation may require expertise. But even there, the archive should ask whether the **first usable layer** is direct, plain, and navigable, and whether expert assistance is publicly supported rather than privately mandatory.

### 6. Public support should reduce mediation burdens rather than entrench them

If interpreters, legal aid, worker centers, trusted navigators, ombuds services, public defenders, or community health workers are needed, they should function as equalizing supports that lower dependence on private brokers, not as thin patches over systems designed to remain hostile to the unconnected.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask whether an ordinary person can start without a fixer

When judging a school system, migration regime, housing office, court, clinic, benefits platform, licensing path, or public archive, ask whether a first-time user with ordinary stress and no insider network can begin, understand the first requirements, and receive a first response without private brokerage.

### 2. Preserve a direct path and make expert help supplemental

A humane order should build a plain, visible, low-threshold route for ordinary access while also offering optional guidance, advocacy, translation, trusted assistance, and specialist help for those who want or need it. `archive/100-trusted-assistance-bounded-delegation-and-no-forced-solo-navigation.md` now sharpens the positive side of this rule: support should be addable in chosen, bounded, and revocable ways without becoming the hidden operating system of access.

### 3. Treat broker markets as evidence against the system, not merely as user choice

When paid intermediaries become routine for securing appointments, permits, records, remedies, or legal status, institutions should read that as a signal of design failure, not as proof that a healthy service ecosystem has emerged.

### 4. Separate assistance from capture

Recruiters, sponsors, consultants, and community intermediaries should not control whether people may complain, transfer, withdraw, compare options, or independently verify what the real path is. Assistance should widen freedom, not become the door-owner.

### 5. Prefer public, reviewable equalizers over private tollbooths

Where specialized help is genuinely needed, public or non-extractive support structures such as legal aid, ombuds services, worker centers, interpreters, community navigators, and trusted in-person assistance are generally preferable to opaque broker markets that monetize confusion or dependency.

## Failure modes

- **fixer tollbooth**: basic documents, appointments, permits, or corrections are formally public but practically routed through paid intermediaries.
- **sponsor translation monopoly**: one recruiter, employer, landlord, or family authority becomes the only credible interpreter of what the system will allow.
- **lawyer-or-dropout design**: first-step access is so obscure or technical that unrepresented users predictably abandon valid claims.
- **consultant capture**: schooling, housing, migration, licensing, or procurement systems quietly assume families or firms will hire experts to make the official path usable.
- **brokered scarcity**: access to scarce public goods turns on who can pay for slot monitoring, expediter services, or insider timing information.
- **community-guide substitution**: underinvestment in humane public design is excused because nonprofits, volunteers, or social ties keep rescuing people from the maze.
- **intermediary retaliation risk**: people cannot safely complain, switch providers, or verify information because the intermediary also controls sponsorship, translation, or future access.

## Guardrails

- Do not confuse all intermediaries with corruption; advocacy, accompaniment, and specialist support can be essential and humane.
- Do not require every serious domain to become fully self-service; some cases properly need expert help.
- Do not attack legal aid, worker centers, ombuds services, interpreters, or community navigators for existing; the problem is systems that make them mandatory for ordinary access.
- Do not assume digital self-service solves broker dependence; portal opacity often just moves the fixer market online.
- Do not ignore trust and proximity; some people will reasonably prefer mediated help even when a direct path exists.
- Do not treat private workarounds as proof that the public route is good enough.

## Compression rule for the archive

Help should be available, but rights should not require a fixer.
