# 106 — Proof reuse, once-only design, and no repeated demands for known facts

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must treat **proof reuse**, **once-only design**, and freedom from **repeated demands for known facts** as morally important, because rights and services go thin when people are forced to act as couriers of stable information, already-submitted evidence, or already-verified identity across offices that could instead reuse, carry, or safely re-check what they already hold.

## Why this matters

The archive is now strong on administrative burden, non-confessional access, submission integrity, status visibility, record contestability, trusted assistance, and clock fairness. But a serious seam still remained open: **what institutions may ask again when they already know, already hold, or could responsibly verify the answer without making the user start the proof ritual over**.

That seam matters because repeated proof is not only redundant. It shifts labor, travel, memory, printing, disclosure, and blame onto the governed. A person may submit the same pay stub to three adjacent programs, retype the same household facts into multiple portals, carry a discharge summary from one clinic to another, resupply identity data after it was already verified, or re-upload documents that the system itself previously accepted. Sometimes the user is even asked to prove facts the agency itself generated. The result is not just inefficiency. It is a quiet transfer of systems-integration work onto people least able to absorb it.

A good order needs a cleaner rule. The point is not that every file should flow everywhere, that all data should be merged into one giant record, or that users should lose the ability to correct, limit, or contest reused information. The point is that **institutions should ordinarily bear more of the burden of carrying forward stable facts, already-submitted materials, and previously-verified information when safe, lawful, and role-appropriate reuse is possible**. Otherwise ordinary access becomes an endurance test in clerical repetition.

## Core claims

### 1. Repeated proof is a substantive burden, not harmless duplication

Every repeated request for the same fact consumes time, attention, bandwidth, printer access, translation effort, emotional steadiness, or disclosure tolerance. The archive should therefore treat re-documentation as a real moral cost rather than as neutral paperwork.

### 2. Users should not serve as the default interoperability layer

When agencies, schools, hospitals, courts, insurers, universities, employers, or platforms refuse to carry forward stable information they already hold, they push co-ordination work onto ordinary people. A humane order should resist making the claimant, patient, student, or tenant the default courier between systems. Even where the next system does not yet hold the needed material, a humane order should still ask whether the institution can retrieve or request it more effectively before pushing that chore onto the user.

### 3. Stable, already-verified facts deserve a presumption of bounded reuse

Some information changes quickly and some is contestable. But much information is stable, recently verified, or already attached to the live matter. Where facts are sufficiently reliable and the use is relevant, institutions should often reuse them rather than demand re-entry or re-proof from zero. And when a genuinely new showing is still required, they should not default to one monopoly document if bounded alternative evidence could establish the same fact.

### 4. Once-only design must stay bounded by purpose, privacy, and contestability

This note does not defend limitless pooling. Good reuse should remain role-limited, purpose-specific, intelligible to the user, and open to correction where stakes are serious. The archive should reject both extremes: needless duplication on one side, surveillance-by-integration on the other.

### 5. Repeated proof often becomes repeated exposure

When the same evidence must be resubmitted to multiple offices, users are not only repeating facts. They are often reopening intimate finances, health conditions, trauma histories, disability documentation, family disruption, migration papers, or other sensitive material. Proof reuse therefore also protects privacy and dignity.

### 6. Carry-forward design reduces cliff-edge loss and serial restart

If institutions cannot reuse what they already know, every transfer, renewal, appeal, correction, or adjacent application becomes another chance to lose place through omission, mismatch, dead upload, or expired patience. Carry-forward design is therefore also a continuity protection.

### 7. Good reuse should usually be visible, reviewable, and fixable

A person should ordinarily be able to tell when information is being prefilled, reused, or carried over, what source it came from, and how to correct it if it is wrong or outdated. Reuse without legibility can become a hidden-file problem by another route.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Ask whether the institution already knows enough

When evaluating applications, renewals, handoffs, school enrollment, care transitions, licensing, benefits, casework, and complaints, ask which facts are genuinely new and which are already verified, stable, or available within the relevant system or its bounded partner systems.

### 2. Prefer once-only proof where safe and relevant

A humane order should usually avoid asking users to restate or resubmit information that the institution already holds, can lawfully reuse, or can verify through existing records without degrading accuracy or due process.

### 3. Make reuse legible to the user

If information is prefilled, carried forward, or reused, the system should ordinarily say so, identify the source in understandable terms, and explain what can and cannot be updated through that channel.

### 4. Preserve correction and disagreement paths

Reuse should not hard-freeze error. If carried-forward information is wrong, stale, or context-misfitted, the archive should ask whether the person can correct it, annotate disagreement, or stop the mistake from propagating across related decisions.

### 5. Distinguish humane reuse from coercive pooling

Bounded reuse can reduce burden. Unlimited internal circulation can expand surveillance, function creep, and hidden scoring. Good design therefore needs purpose limits, access limits, and relevance limits rather than treating all administrative convenience as a justification for unrestricted data flow.

### 6. Treat repeated resubmission after receipt as a warning sign

If a person already received a receipt, confirmation, or status record for a submission, institutions should need a reason before demanding the same material again. The archive should ask whether the problem is genuinely missing evidence or merely poor internal carrying-forward.

## Failure modes

- **ask-again default**: institutions routinely demand the same facts from the same person because internal carrying-forward is weak or ignored.
- **user-as-courier**: people must move records, proofs, and confirmations between systems that could have coordinated more humanely.
- **receipt-without-carry-forward**: a submission is acknowledged and later treated as though it were never available to the next step.
- **prefill opacity**: information is reused without telling the person what source it came from or how to fix it.
- **reverification theater**: stable facts are repeatedly checked or re-proven without a grounded reason.
- **privacy-by-duplication**: institutions avoid bounded sharing by forcing users to keep re-exposing sensitive information themselves.
- **integration-by-surveillance**: a worthy desire to reduce repetition mutates into excessive pooling, hidden cross-use, or purpose drift.
- **stale-data propagation**: reused information keeps moving even after the person has reason to dispute or update it.

## Guardrails

- Do not assume once-only design means all data should automatically flow everywhere.
- Do not treat convenience as sufficient reason to override purpose limits, confidentiality, or legal boundaries.
- Do not mistake stable facts for all facts; some information genuinely changes quickly or requires fresh confirmation.
- Do not let prefill or carry-forward hide the source of information or erase the user’s correction path.
- Do not use failed interoperability as a standing excuse for making households perform clerical relay forever.
- Do not let anti-fraud rhetoric justify repetitive proof demands when the same goal can be met through narrower verification or bounded reuse.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order should not keep asking ordinary people to carry facts, proofs, and documents that the relevant system already knows, already has, or could responsibly verify without making them start over.
