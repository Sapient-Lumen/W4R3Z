# 026 — Accessibility, accommodation, and world-fit

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** must treat accessibility, accommodation, and inclusive world-fit as constitutive requirements rather than optional patches, because lives do not go well when institutions, environments, interfaces, and social norms silently assume one narrow “normal” bodymind and then charge others extra time, pain, dependence, or segregation for entry.

## Why this matters

The archive already protects dignity, participation, privacy, time sovereignty, and anti-humiliation. But it still lacks a direct rule about **normate design**. Without such a rule, the theory can say generous things about equal standing while quietly treating access barriers as regrettable side costs borne by whoever fails to match the default body, mind, tempo, or sensory profile for which the world was built.

A good order should not start from the premise that a humane life belongs chiefly to those who can see standard print, hear standard speech, climb standard stairs, tolerate standard noise, sustain standard schedules, process standard forms, or live without assistance. Accessibility is not only a technical compliance issue. It is part of whether a world is actually shareable.

## Core claims

### 1. Disability is often a relation between persons and worlds, not only a property inside a person

Some difficulties arise from impairments themselves. But many are intensified, produced, or stabilized by environments, institutions, expectations, and infrastructures built for a narrow norm. The archive should therefore resist theories of the good that read exclusion as personal shortfall when much of the harm is world-made.

### 2. Accessibility is a precondition for agency, voice, privacy, and belonging

Rights, participation, culture, work, and sanctuary are nominal when doors, transit, websites, classrooms, clinics, forms, communications, and public meetings are inaccessible. A good order should therefore treat access as a constitutive enabling condition for many other goods, not as a charitable add-on.

### 3. Accommodation is not special pleading

Reasonable accommodation is not an unfair privilege granted to a few after the real system has already been designed. It is one way of honoring equal standing under human variation. The archive should treat delays, denials, and burdensome proof rituals around accommodation as moral injuries rather than as trivial administrative friction.

### 4. Inclusive design and individualized support both matter

Universal or inclusive design can reduce many predictable barriers in advance. But no generic design will fit everyone. A good order therefore needs both broad accessibility and individualized supports such as interpretation, assistive technology, personal assistance, flexible pacing, or alternative communication channels. The choice should not be between mass standardization and endless case-by-case pleading.

### 5. Segregated access is often a warning sign

Separate entrances, separate transport, separate classrooms, separate communications, or institution-like default placements can sometimes be temporarily necessary, but they should usually trigger scrutiny. The archive should ask whether separation protects agency and comfort or instead hides exclusion behind a service veneer.

### 6. The good should not be indexed to forced normalization

Medicine, rehabilitation, and assistive technologies can be genuine goods. But the archive should resist the idea that a person must approximate a dominant bodymind, communication style, productivity rhythm, or sensory profile in order to count as flourishing. Support should enlarge livable possibility, not enforce one ideal form of humanity.

## A working rule for the archive

### 1. Treat access barriers as design failures unless clearly unavoidable

When people are blocked from movement, communication, knowledge, work, care, culture, or civic life, the first question should usually be what in the world failed to fit them, not what about them failed the world.

### 2. Prefer anticipatory inclusion to petition-based exception

The archive should favor environments and systems designed for plural embodiment in advance rather than ones that make each person ask, prove, wait, and disclose in order to enter.

### 3. Keep support tied to agency and community inclusion

Assistance should ordinarily expand choice, privacy, and community presence, not trap people in dependency, surveillance, or segregated care pathways.

### 4. Read accessibility across material, sensory, cognitive, linguistic, and temporal dimensions

Access is not only ramps and elevators. It also includes captioning, interpretation, plain language, alternative formats, flexible pacing, predictable scheduling, sensory tolerability, quiet space, navigable bureaucracy, digital accessibility, and transport fit.

## What this changes in the archive

### 1. It thickens dignity beyond anti-humiliation alone

`archive/020-dignity-recognition-humiliation-and-belonging.md` protects equal standing against stigma and civic invisibility. This note adds that equal standing is also broken when public worlds are routinely built for some bodies and minds while others must beg for translation, access, or admissibility.

### 2. It sharpens voice and privacy by making entry conditions visible

`archive/022-voice-participation-and-co-authorship.md` and `archive/023-privacy-sanctuary-and-protected-opacity.md` protect meaningful say and protected opacity. This note adds that inaccessible meetings, unreadable forms, speech-only channels, forced caregiver mediation, or accommodation-by-exposure can dissolve both voice and sanctuary before they begin.

### 3. It links time sovereignty to barrier burden

`archive/025-rest-rhythm-and-time-sovereignty.md` protects usable time. This note adds that inaccessible worlds steal time through detours, waiting, repetition, conversion work, administrative proof, and fragile coordination with caregivers or support systems.

## Failure modes

- **normate default**: institutions quietly assume one standard bodymind and read mismatch as personal deficiency.
- **petitionary access**: people must repeatedly ask, prove, and wait for entry into ordinary social life.
- **segregation by service design**: separate channels or placements become the default answer to ordinary inclusion.
- **cure coercion**: support is offered mainly on the condition that people move closer to a dominant norm.
- **communication exclusion**: information is formally public but unavailable in accessible, interpretable, or cognitively usable forms.
- **dependency penalty**: needing assistance triggers surveillance, reduced privacy, lower credibility, or diminished say.
- **emergency abandonment**: crises reveal that access and support were treated as optional only until stress arrived.

## Guardrails

- Do not romanticize impairment or deny that some conditions involve real pain, fatigue, or loss that justice alone cannot erase.
- Do not assume universal design removes the need for individualized accommodation or support.
- Do not let support turn into paternal custody or forced disclosure.
- Do not treat accessibility as a niche issue affecting only a stable minority; human variation, illness, injury, ageing, and situational impairment are ordinary features of life.
- Do not confuse “technically accessible” with actually welcoming, usable, and dignity-preserving.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good order is built for human variation in advance, so people do not have to earn entry into ordinary life by approximating a narrow norm.
