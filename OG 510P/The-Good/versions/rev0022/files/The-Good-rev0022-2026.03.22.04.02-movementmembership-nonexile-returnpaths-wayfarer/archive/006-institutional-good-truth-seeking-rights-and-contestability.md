# 006 — Institutional good: truth-seeking, rights, and contestability

## One-line thesis

A good institutional order must do more than distribute benefits; it must help a community **stay in touch with reality**, protect rights, expose error, and remain contestable by those it governs.

## Why this matters

Many theories of the good focus on outcomes or character and underplay institutions as epistemic and moral machinery. But institutions shape whether a society can even notice harm in time to correct it. An order that cannot hear complaints, test claims, retain records, correct mistakes, or restrain concentrated power can produce local gains while drifting into durable wrong.

## Working components of institutional good

### 1. Truth-seeking and world-contact

Institutions should improve collective orientation to reality: public statistics, scientific inquiry, adversarial testing, investigative journalism, transparent records, and feedback channels all matter because good action requires some contact with what is true.

### 2. Rights and status protections

Rights are not merely liberal ornament. They are part of how institutions refuse to cash out persons only as means or data points. The archive should treat enforceable rights and remedies as part of the good institutional ecology.

### 3. Contestability and participatory say

A good institution can be challenged. It leaves evidence trails, reasons, review routes, and correction mechanisms. But contestability after the fact is still too thin if affected people have no meaningful standing before design, priority-setting, or implementation. `archive/022-voice-participation-and-co-authorship.md` makes this co-authorship requirement explicit.

### 4. Epistemic pluralism under discipline

A society benefits when institutions can draw on diverse standpoints and forms of knowledge. But diversity must be linked to procedures that can sort signal from noise. Otherwise pluralism decays into fragmentation.

### 5. Capacity without domination

Institutions must be able to coordinate action. Weak states and hollow administrations are not inherently good. The task is to build capacity that is bounded, reason-giving, and answerable rather than arbitrary.

## Structural claims

### 1. Institutional goods are partly constitutive, not only instrumental

Living under institutions that can be questioned, corrected, and justified is itself part of a better common life. It is not merely a route to later welfare gains.

### 2. Error correction is a good-making feature

Because agents and systems are fallible, the ability to detect and repair error is not optional hygiene. It is a central criterion of institutional goodness.

### 3. Information injustice is moral harm

When institutions systematically blind some people, bury relevant facts, or privilege the perspectives of the already powerful, the harm is not merely epistemic; it alters standing and exposure to domination.

## Failure modes

- **output fetishism**: an institution is judged only by short-run deliverables.
- **benevolent opacity**: secrecy is justified by allegedly good outcomes.
- **rights without remedies**: protections exist in principle but not in reachable practice.
- **participation theater**: voice exists without uptake.
- **epistemic monoculture**: one channel of expertise becomes uncorrectable.

## Guardrails

- Do not identify institutional good with administrative smoothness alone.
- Do not let efficiency displace traceability and reasons.
- Do not confuse visibility with contestability.
- Do not treat knowledge infrastructure as a luxury add-on.

## Compression rule for the archive

A good institution is one that can act, explain, be challenged, and learn—without demanding that the governed trust what they are forbidden to inspect.
