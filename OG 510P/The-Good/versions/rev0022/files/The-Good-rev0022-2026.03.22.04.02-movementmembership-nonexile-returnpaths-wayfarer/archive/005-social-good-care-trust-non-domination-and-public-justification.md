# 005 — Social good: care, trust, non-domination, and public justification

## One-line thesis

The social dimension of the good is not just many private goods added together; it includes **relations and norms** such as care, trustworthiness, fair standing, non-domination, and justifiability to one another.

## Why this matters

A society can raise average welfare while still becoming more humiliating, extractive, lonely, or arbitrary. A theory of the good that only counts individual outcomes but not the quality of relation among persons will miss domination, abandonment, and moral injury that are social in structure.

## Working components of social good

### 1. Care as civic substrate

Every society relies on dependency work: raising children, tending illness, supporting disability, accompanying old age, repairing stress, and sustaining everyday life. A good society therefore cannot treat care as private residue left behind by “real” production.

### 2. Trust and trustworthy practice

Trust matters because social life requires reliance. But the archive should prefer the language of **trustworthiness** where possible. The aim is not warm feeling detached from institutions, but environments where reliance is warranted.

### 3. Non-domination

People fare better in social orders where they are not subject to arbitrary power in households, workplaces, states, platforms, or markets. Even benevolent masters distort the good because dependence on unchecked will injures standing.

### 4. Recognition and equal moral standing

Good social life requires not only material provision but treatment that registers persons as beings whose claims count. Humiliation, stigma, caste ordering, and civic invisibility are not small side harms. `archive/020-dignity-recognition-humiliation-and-belonging.md` gives the archive a more explicit rule for this status dimension.

### 5. Public justification

Where common rules bind us, the social good includes institutions and norms that can justify coercion in terms others can in principle engage with. This disciplines perfectionist overreach and reduces the chance that one group’s favored life form simply rules by cultural confidence.

### 6. Participatory standing

Being protected by good rules is not the same as helping shape them. A socially good order should therefore give people meaningful opportunities to participate in public affairs and in other rule-laden domains that structure everyday life. `archive/022-voice-participation-and-co-authorship.md` sharpens this claim so non-domination is not reduced to benevolent administration.

## Tensions to manage

### 1. Care and freedom

Care can be nurturing or paternalistic. The archive should therefore ask whether support enlarges a person’s real freedom or replaces their agency without necessity.

### 2. Trust and contestation

A good society needs enough trust to coordinate, but not so much deference that institutions become immune to scrutiny.

### 3. Public reason and moral depth

Public justification can prevent domination by sectarian doctrine, but it should not require a morally empty public sphere. The challenge is to preserve thick commitments in civil society while keeping coercive authority justifiable.

## Failure modes

- **care dumping**: a society praises care while offloading it onto exhausted households.
- **friendly domination**: dependence is normalized because power presents itself as protective.
- **prestige equality with material neglect**: formal respect hides abandonment.
- **public reason as moral anesthesia**: only the thinnest vocabulary is allowed to shape common life.
- **trust without reasons**: institutions ask for confidence they have not earned.

## Guardrails

- Do not treat loneliness, humiliation, or dependency exploitation as secondary concerns.
- Do not treat arbitrary power as acceptable when it delivers convenience.
- Do not confuse harmony with justice.
- Do not confuse public justification with value-neutral emptiness.

## Compression rule for the archive

A socially good order is one in which people can rely on one another and their institutions without having to bow before arbitrary power, social abandonment, or civic invisibility.
