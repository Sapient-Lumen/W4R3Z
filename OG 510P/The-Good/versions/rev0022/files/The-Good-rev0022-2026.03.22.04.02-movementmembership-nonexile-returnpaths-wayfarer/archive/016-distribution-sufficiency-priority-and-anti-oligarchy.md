# 016 — Distribution, sufficiency, priority, and anti-oligarchy

## One-line thesis

A salient theory of **The Good** needs a **distribution rule** in addition to a list of goods: protect sufficiency floors first, give extra moral weight to equivalent gains and losses affecting the worse-off, treat equal civic standing as a non-optional relational constraint, and treat extreme concentrations of wealth, status, or authority as presumptively suspect when they convert advantage into arbitrary power over others.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a working synthesis of protected kernels, a triage protocol for tragic collisions, an admission rule for new candidate goods, and a scope rule for who counts directly. What it still lacks is a disciplined answer to a basic question: **once some good is being allocated, how should its benefits and burdens be distributed?**

Without a distribution rule, four failures recur.

First, floor language can be honored in rhetoric while some groups remain permanently below it.
Second, average improvement can hide the fact that marginal gains are going to those already secure.
Third, equality can be flattened into envy talk, as if the only objection to hierarchy were that some have more.
Fourth, distributive arguments can forget that some inequalities are dangerous not because they offend a pattern, but because they install durable dependence, humiliation, and capture.

The archive needs a cross-cutting rule that can govern all kernels under scarcity.

## Three distinct distributional questions

A serious theory of the good should not ask only one distributive question.

### 1. Who is still below an acceptable floor?

This is the **sufficiency** question. Before chasing refined improvements, identify who still lacks enough security, care, agency, standing, or material basis to live without systematic maiming.

### 2. Whose next gain matters more?

This is the **priority** question. When two gains are alike in kind and size, there is normally stronger moral reason to deliver the gain where deprivation is deeper.

### 3. What inequalities deform social relations or political power?

This is the **relational equality** and **anti-domination** question. Some distributions are objectionable not merely because one person has less, but because the resulting hierarchy lets some command, silence, buy access, or force deference from others.

These questions overlap, but none can fully replace the others.

## Working distribution rule for the archive

### 1. Repair floor breaches first

Where people or other direct moral patients remain under kernel-relevant floors, bringing them above those floors usually outranks polishing already-good positions.

### 2. Give diminishing moral urgency to gains as security rises

Above a protected floor, additional improvements still matter, but the archive should generally treat equivalent improvements as more urgent when they go to those who are worse off in the relevant dimension. The point is not that equality is the only value. The point is that deprivation changes the moral importance of a marginal gain.

### 3. Refuse domination-producing concentration

Distributions should be treated as morally defective when they predictably allow some actors to convert money, office, platform control, landlord power, gatekeeping authority, or inherited status into unchecked power over the basic options of others.

### 4. Track the relevant dimension, not just income

A distribution can improve in money terms while worsening in time, care burdens, exposure to coercion, disability access, environmental risk, or informational vulnerability. The archive should therefore ask: **equal or unequal in what respect?**

### 5. Preserve room for justified asymmetry

Need, responsibility, role obligations, urgency, and rescue conditions can justify departures from equality. But such departures must answer two tests: they must not push anyone beneath protected floors, and they must not harden into civic inferiority or oligarchic control.

## Anti-oligarchy as a derived but high-weight rule

The archive does not need to treat strict material equality as a kernel in order to treat oligarchic concentration as a major threat. The reason is structural.

When wealth or authority becomes sufficiently concentrated, it tends to purchase agenda control, legal insulation, extraction from dependent labor, unequal exposure to risk, and differential ability to shape the information environment. At that point the problem is no longer only distributive pattern. It is a breach of non-domination, contestability, and fair standing.

For this reason, ceilings on concentration, anti-capture institutions, inheritance scrutiny, labor counter-power, public options, and basic goods provision can be defended inside this archive not as levelling for its own sake, but as protections against private mastery.

## What this note is not saying

### 1. Not strict levelling

The archive is not committed to making every dimension equal at all times.

### 2. Not a secret scalar score

The archive is not replacing one-number monism with a hidden weighted distribution formula. Floors, priority, and anti-domination remain partly judgment-guiding rather than algorithmically decisive.

### 3. Not income fetishism

Material resources matter, but they are one among several distributional media.

## Pressure cases

### 1. Housing

A city may raise average housing quality while leaving low-income tenants exposed to eviction, long commutes, landlord dependency, and civic displacement.

### 2. Health triage and waitlists

Equivalent medical gains may matter more for those facing steeper baseline suffering or shorter healthy life prospects.

### 3. Education and elite pathways

Open access rhetoric can coexist with inherited advantage, tutoring markets, prestige sorting, and institutional capture.

### 4. Climate transition

A green transition can be morally disfigured if its costs are shifted onto workers, poorer households, or regions already carrying extractive damage.

### 5. Information infrastructure

Even where speech is formally open, concentrated platform or media power can create epistemic dependence that undermines equal standing.

## Implications for the existing synthesis

- `archive/010-priority-kernels-a-pluralist-working-synthesis.md` should treat distribution as a **transversal governance rule**, not as a late-stage optional concern after the “real” goods have been named.
- `archive/008-measurement-dashboard-judgment-thresholds-and-policy-translation.md` should require distributional reporting that keeps floors, gaps, and concentration visible together.
- `archive/013-kernel-collisions-triage-tragic-remainder-and-repair.md` should be read with a strong presumption that avoidable remainder should not be offloaded onto the already burdened.
- `archive/012-open-questions-and-test-cases.md` should keep oligarchy, housing, inheritance, and transition-burden cases in the recurring stress set.

## Guardrails

- Do not let “efficiency” wash out floor breaches.
- Do not treat every inequality as equally objectionable.
- Do not talk as if raising the worst off eliminates all concern with hierarchy.
- Do not talk as if relational equality eliminates all concern with material deprivation.
- Do not let anti-oligarchy language become a pretext for suppressing plural civil society.

## Compression rule for the archive

Secure enough for all, weight the next gain toward the worse-off, and block concentrations of advantage that turn plural flourishing into organized dependency.
