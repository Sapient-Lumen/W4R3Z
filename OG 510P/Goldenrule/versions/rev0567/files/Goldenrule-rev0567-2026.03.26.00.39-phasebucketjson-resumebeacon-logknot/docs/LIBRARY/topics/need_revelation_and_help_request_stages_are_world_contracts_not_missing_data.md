# Need revelation and help-request stages are world contracts, not missing data

Recent work adds a missing layer to Concord's future helping, mutual-aid, and solidarity lanes: **a world where need is private and help requires an explicit ask is not the same institution as a world where need is visible or helpers can safely offer without waiting**.

- `RS-GR-199` shows that asking can be painful because it removes the excuse that the helper did not know about the need; making need common knowledge raises the informational stakes of refusal.
- The same paper shows a paradoxical result: **greater need can reduce the propensity to ask**, because rejection hurts more when the need is larger.
- `RS-GR-199` also shows that spontaneous offers are double-edged. They can solve ask-shyness, but the absence of an unsolicited offer can itself discourage asking by signaling weak concern.
- `RS-GR-202` adds that visible organizational norms around help-seeking can change whether asking feels psychologically safe or inappropriate.
- Together, these results warn that a benchmark can look uncaring, stoic, or impressively self-reliant because the world quietly made **need revelation costly** or made **asking the only route to help**, not because the underlying reciprocity policy became better or worse.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future benchmark should not treat unmet need as a pure action-policy failure when the world hides need by default.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world where need is automatically visible;
2. a world where need is private unless explicitly requested;
3. a world where helpers may proactively offer before an ask;
4. a world where asking changes the social meaning of later refusal.

Those choices do not merely change data availability.
They change whether non-help means indifference, ignorance, fear of rejection, or failure to make an unsolicited offer.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds helping, mutual-aid, or partner-support lanes, publish at least:

1. whether need is public, private, partially inferable, or revealed only through an ask;
2. whether agents have an explicit right to ask for help, and whether asking is costly, stigmatized, or score-relevant;
3. whether unsolicited offers are allowed before any ask;
4. whether a refusal after an ask is scored differently from non-help when no ask occurred.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake hidden need and ask-shyness for Golden-Rule success or failure.
