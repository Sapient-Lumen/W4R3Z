# Inequality source and capability alignment are world contracts, not just initial conditions

Recent work adds a missing institutional layer for Concord's future helping, redistribution, and public-goods lanes: **inequality does not merely set the starting numbers; it changes what participants experience as fair cooperation**.

- `RS-GR-161` reports that the effect of inequality on cooperation depends on the interaction between endowments, productivities, and the return structure of the public-good world.
- The paper further reports that so-called aligned inequality can improve group payoff under linear returns but reduce payoff under threshold returns, because agents no longer agree on which equilibrium looks fair.
- `RS-GR-162` adds causal evidence that the *source* of inequality matters too: merit-based inequality leads groups to contribute less than luck-based inequality in a public-goods setting.
- The paper further reports that this contribution drop is substantially mediated by fairness perceptions and is driven mainly by richer participants in the merit-framed world.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future benchmark should not treat unequal starting conditions as inert background setup.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. luck-based endowment inequality;
2. merit- or performance-based inequality;
3. productivity inequality with equal resources;
4. aligned versus misaligned endowment/productivity inequality;
5. linear-return versus threshold-return worlds under the same visible inequality.

Those choices do not merely perturb payoffs.
They change what agents think they owe each other, whether the same contribution looks fair, and whether high-capability agents read cooperation as obligation or optional generosity.

## Minimal implementor handoff

When Concord adds richer helping, redistribution, or public-goods lanes, the world contract should declare:

1. whether inequality comes from luck, merit, past success, exogenous assignment, or endogenous world history;
2. whether differences are in endowment, productivity, need, vulnerability, or some aligned combination of these;
3. whether return technology is linear, thresholded, congested, or otherwise nonlinear;
4. whether high-resource and high-productivity positions coincide or are deliberately separated.

Without that publication layer, future results can mistake merit framing, capability alignment, or payoff-function artifacts for genuine Golden-Rule-like robustness.
