# Precautionary defaults and burden of proof under irreversible risk are world contracts, not generic future care

Recent work adds another missing layer to Concord's future-facing worlds: **it matters who must prove safety, what counts as enough science to act, whether uncertainty licenses delay, and whether institutions default toward precaution when harms are serious, cumulative, or potentially irreversible**.

- `RS-GR-270` shows that the ICJ's 2025 climate advisory opinion tied due diligence to best available science, preventive action, risk and impact assessment, and non-delay even under uncertainty.
- `RS-GR-271` shows that recent adaptation guidance reading the ICJ opinion treats best-available-science planning, precautionary and forward-looking measures, and timely due diligence against foreseeable harm as binding rather than optional.
- `RS-GR-272` shows that UN experts called for a precautionary pause on deep-sea mining precisely because serious and irreversible harm can coexist with uncertainty about prevention, including harm transmitted to future generations.
- `RS-GR-273` shows that governance can shift the burden of proof itself: under REACH, chemical safety must be demonstrated by manufacturers and importers rather than inferred by regulators after damage appears; the same paper also stresses proactive prevention because low-dose harms often emerge only after long latency.
- Together, these sources warn that a benchmark can look more future-protective because it moved from "act only after conclusive proof" to "act under best-available science and precaution", or because it shifted the burden of proving safety away from later victims and onto present decision-makers or producers.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future-facing benchmark should not report that agents or institutions "cared more about posterity" when the world really changed its default under uncertainty.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world where uncertainty permits delay until near-conclusive proof arrives;
2. a world where precaution is allowed but not required;
3. a world where serious or irreversible risk triggers heightened care, earlier intervention, or special review;
4. a world where best available science and active risk assessment are mandatory;
5. a world where the burden of demonstrating safety, controllability, or non-harm shifts onto present actors creating the risk.

Those are not epistemic footnotes.
They change when action starts, who bears delay costs, and whether future harms are treated as regrettable surprises or as risks that must be actively prevented.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds long-horizon risk, stewardship, contamination, climate, or resilience lanes, publish at least:

1. the default action rule under uncertainty: wait-for-proof, permissive precaution, mandatory precaution, or another stance;
2. which harms trigger heightened care: significant, cumulative, widespread, irreversible, rights-threatening, or another threshold;
3. what counts as sufficient scientific basis for action, and whether best available science or local / traditional knowledge is part of that standard;
4. who bears the burden of proving safety, controllability, or non-harm when risky activity is proposed;
5. whether headline results survive one same-behavior comparison where only the precaution default or proof burden changes.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake a stricter uncertainty rule or reversed proof burden for the same thing as deeper reciprocity or stronger care for future generations.
