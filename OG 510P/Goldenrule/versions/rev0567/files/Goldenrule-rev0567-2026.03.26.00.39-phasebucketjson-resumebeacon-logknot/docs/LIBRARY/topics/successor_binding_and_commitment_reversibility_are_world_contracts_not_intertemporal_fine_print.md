# Successor binding and commitment reversibility are world contracts, not intertemporal fine print

Recent work adds a missing layer to Concord's future stewardship, climate, and successor-world lanes: **a world where the present cohort can bind the next cohort is not the same institution as a world where every cohort can freely reset the rule**.

- `RS-GR-207` shows that introducing a commitment mechanism — one that limits the next generation's choice horizon and obliges it to preserve the common pool for later generations — increased replenishment and overall sustainability across generations.
- The same source also finds a path-dependent effect: once one generation invests in a commitment mechanism, the next generation becomes more likely to reinvest in one, suggesting that successor-binding can create its own convention rather than acting as a one-shot patch.
- `RS-GR-207` adds an important caution: binding successors is not free. It creates rigidity, can conflict with democratic autonomy, and may require a limited lock duration or explicit escape procedure.
- Together, these results warn that an intergenerational benchmark can look farsighted because the world quietly allowed one cohort to freeze later cohorts' action set, not because agents discovered a stronger Golden-Rule norm.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future benchmark should not collapse all intergenerational worlds into one generic “future affected” setting.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world where each cohort can fully revise inherited policy;
2. a world where the current cohort can bind the next cohort for one period only;
3. a world where commitment persists until later cohorts pay to escape it;
4. a world where successors are bound but still retain a formal override or emergency exit.

Those are not timing details.
They change autonomy, strategic trust in successors, and what observed sustainability is actually evidence of.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds intergenerational, stewardship, or successor-world lanes, publish at least:

1. whether current agents may bind successors at all;
2. which action dimensions are frozen or constrained by the commitment;
3. the binding duration and whether commitment is one-step, multi-step, or indefinite;
4. whether successors have an escape, override, appeal, or buyout procedure, and at what cost.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake lock-in design for Golden-Rule progress.
