# Outside options should publish loner externality and group-formation flexibility

Recent work adds a second missing layer to Concord's future exit and self-reliance lanes: **an outside option is not one stable institution**.

- `RS-GR-197` shows that an outside individual option can *increase* collaborative success when groups form flexibly and loners impose only limited negative externalities on those who remain.
- The same paper argues that the effect of an outside option depends on the **externality of loners**: whether those who leave still materially damage the group's chances.
- `RS-GR-198` provides the contrasting case: when self-reliance solves the shared problem individually rather than merely filtering pessimists, cooperation falls and inequality rises.
- `RS-GR-195` adds that once private solutions exist, conditional cooperation and early public commitment become especially important because a population can otherwise slide into the private-solution trap.
- Together, these results warn that “outside option present” is not yet a world description. The same formal right to opt out can act like useful voluntariness, harmful self-reliance, or unequal escape depending on what leaving does to the remaining group.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future benchmark should not collapse all outside-option worlds into one generic opt-out lane.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. leaving a group while leaving the shared project mostly intact;
2. leaving a group while imposing large external harm on those who stay;
3. fixed groups that cannot reform after pessimists depart;
4. flexible groups that can re-form after exit and thereby benefit from filtering.

Those choices do not merely change how free agents feel.
They change whether exit is an act of abandonment, a screening device, a private-insurance move, or a coordination aid.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds outside options, self-reliance, or voluntary-collaboration lanes, publish at least:

1. whether groups are fixed or may form and reform flexibly after exit;
2. what externality a loner imposes on the remaining group, if any;
3. whether the outside option solves the problem privately, merely delays it, or only lets pessimists step aside;
4. whether headline results survive both a no-outside-option baseline and a matched fixed-group versus flexible-group comparison.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake group-filtering or low loner externality for Golden-Rule progress.
