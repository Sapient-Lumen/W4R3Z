# Helping worlds should separate unwillingness, inability, and need burden

Recent work adds a missing evaluation layer for Golden-Rule-style benchmarks: **help failures and help requests do not mean one thing**.

- `RS-GR-155` reports that partner choice depends on both warmth (willingness to cooperate) and competence (ability to contribute), and that task affordances change which dimension dominates selection.
- The paper shows that a partner who looks warm can still be a poor fit for competence-heavy tasks, so judging all help failures as unwillingness is too coarse.
- `RS-GR-156` reports that people can devalue or blame victims of misfortune as a way to avoid the costs of helping them while protecting their own generous self-image.
- The paper further reports that help-seeking victims can be seen as poor cooperative prospects or even implicit exploiters when their need is framed as costly.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future benchmark could easily hide two important institutional choices:

> whether non-help is read as refusal versus inability,
> and whether needing help lowers a recipient's standing by making them look costly.

That would be a mistake.
A Golden-Rule-like policy should not be scored inside a world that silently collapses skill limits, overload, and bad intent into one negative action class, or that quietly penalizes needy recipients for imposing burden.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds richer helping, partner-choice, or support-allocation lanes, publish at least:

1. whether evaluations track willingness and ability separately or collapse them into one reputation dimension;
2. whether tasks differ in the relative importance of warmth / trustworthiness versus competence / capacity;
3. whether recipients carry explicit need or burden signals, and whether those signals reduce their attractiveness as future partners;
4. whether a failed help attempt due to inability, overload, or bad luck is judged differently from deliberate refusal.

Without that split, future results can reward cruelty masked as "high standards" or punish vulnerable agents for being expensive to help.
