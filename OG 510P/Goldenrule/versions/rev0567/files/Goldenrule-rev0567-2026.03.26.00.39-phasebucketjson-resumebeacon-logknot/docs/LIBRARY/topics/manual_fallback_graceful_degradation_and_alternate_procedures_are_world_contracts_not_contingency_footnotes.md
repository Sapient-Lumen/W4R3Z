# Manual fallback, graceful degradation, and alternate procedures are world contracts, not contingency footnotes

Recent work adds another missing layer to Concord's long-horizon resilience and successor-protection worlds: **it matters whether a system can continue through manual processing, alternate facilities, alternate information flows, and degraded-but-governable service modes — rather than assuming that future stewards either enjoy full automation or suffer total failure**.

- `RS-GR-322` shows that FEMA's current emergency-planning guidance asks institutions to specify continuity / alternate facilities, continuity communications, essential records, and human-capital continuity.
- `RS-GR-323` shows that NIST now treats documented fallbacks, data redundancy, tested rollover technologies, manual processing, incident-response rehearsal, and anti-capricious vendor termination clauses as distinct contingency controls for high-risk third-party systems.
- `RS-GR-324` shows that NIST's contingency-control baseline explicitly includes orderly degradation, shutdown, fallback to manual mode, alternate information flows, and alternate processing sites.
- `RS-GR-325` shows that federal response-and-recovery planning expects resilient communications capabilities and cross-jurisdictional backup capabilities rather than assuming primary paths stay intact.
- Together, these sources warn that a benchmark can look more resilient, more successor-safe, or more future-protective because it changed **manual-fallback capacity, graceful-degradation design, alternate-processing paths, or backup communications / interface architecture** — not because the underlying Golden-Rule disposition improved.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future-facing benchmark should not report that an institution “protects successors under disruption” when the world mainly changed its **degraded-operations architecture**.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world where failure means hard outage;
2. a world with nominal backups but no usable manual or alternate workflow;
3. a world with alternate facilities or communications but weak data / record continuity;
4. a world with rehearsed manual processing, alternate information flows, and clear degraded-service promises;
5. a world that looks robust only because it preserved governable fallback modes rather than assuming perfect automation.

Those are not contingency-footnote details.
They change whether successors inherit a brittle dependency chain or an operating system that bends without breaking.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds long-horizon resilience, archive, service, or stewardship lanes, publish at least:

1. whether failures degrade gracefully, partially, or catastrophically;
2. whether manual processing, alternate information flows, and alternate processing sites exist and are useable under pressure;
3. whether essential records, communications, and staffing assumptions survive fallback mode;
4. whether third-party or vendor failure changes only performance or also eliminates legal / practical access to critical support;
5. whether headline results survive one same-behavior comparison where only fallback / degraded-operations semantics change.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake backup paths, manual workarounds, or graceful degradation for the same thing as deeper reciprocity toward successors.
