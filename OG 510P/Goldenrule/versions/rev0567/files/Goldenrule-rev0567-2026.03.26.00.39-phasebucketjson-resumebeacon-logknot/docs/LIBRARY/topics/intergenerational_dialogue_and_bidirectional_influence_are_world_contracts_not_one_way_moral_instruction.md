# Intergenerational dialogue and bidirectional influence are world contracts, not one-way moral instruction

Recent work adds another missing layer to Concord's intergenerational worlds: **cross-age cooperation depends not only on who benefits, but also on who gets to speak, who influences whom, and whether contact reduces age-coded blame or hardens it**.

- `RS-GR-231` shows that youth-led dialogue with older generations can surface previously unspoken climate fears, responsibility differences, and empathy, enabling support and collaboration rather than leaving conflict implicit.
- `RS-GR-232` shows that sustainable practices can transmit in reverse from younger to older family members, and that communication quality and receptivity amplify that effect.
- `RS-GR-233` shows that intergenerational programs often improve mental health, social inclusion, community cohesion, and ageism reduction for both younger and older participants.
- Together, these results warn that a benchmark can look more cooperative because it changed voice symmetry, dialogue leadership, or anti-ageism contact structure rather than because the underlying reciprocity norm improved.

## Why this matters for Concord

An intergenerational benchmark should not report "cooperation improved across generations" without publishing whether interaction was one-way instruction, youth-led dialogue, reciprocal exchange, or sustained contact that changes age stereotypes.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world where one generation only receives moral instruction from the other;
2. a world where younger agents can influence older agents, but not vice versa;
3. a world with reciprocal dialogue and explicit voice on both sides;
4. a world where repeated intergenerational contact also reduces ageism and strengthens cohesion.

Those are not one communication knob.
They change whether the benchmark is measuring obedience, reciprocal learning, contact-mediated stereotype repair, or genuinely shared stewardship.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds intergenerational or future-facing dialogue lanes, publish at least:

1. who may initiate, lead, and steer the dialogue or contact process;
2. whether influence can run older-to-younger, younger-to-older, or in both directions;
3. whether contact is one-shot, repeated, family-based, community-based, or programmatic;
4. whether headline results survive one comparison that holds payoffs fixed while changing only voice symmetry or dialogue leadership.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake contact structure or stereotype repair for Golden-Rule progress.
