# Avoidance-first, substitutability, and restoration order are world contracts, not generic future protection

Recent work adds another missing layer to Concord's future-facing worlds: **it matters whether harms must be avoided upstream, can be minimized only after key choices are locked in, are expected to be restored later, or are treated as compensable through substitution or offsets — and those steps are not interchangeable on successor-relevant timescales**.

- `RS-GR-274` shows that irreversibility and reversibility are often used without explicit definition, even though their meaning depends on temporal and spatial scales and has direct implications for ecosystem management and policy decisions.
- `RS-GR-275` shows that recent SEA / EIA work argues for strengthening the avoidance step and treating enhancement proactively rather than relying on reactive mitigation after damage pathways are mostly set.
- `RS-GR-276` shows that real-world EIA practice can score as procedurally compliant while still showing limited avoidance, weak remediation, semantic ambiguity across mitigation steps, and poor ecological follow-through.
- `RS-GR-276` also proposes substitutability principles that explicitly prioritize avoidance and recognize limits to biodiversity substitution.
- `RS-GR-277` shows that restoration hierarchy work distinguishes full restoration at sufficient ecological scale from fragmented mitigation or partial restoration and argues that legal structures often bias choices toward the weaker path.
- Together, these sources warn that a benchmark can look more future-protective because it changed the harm-handling order — avoid first, minimize later, restore onsite, compensate elsewhere, or attempt full restoration at scale — not because underlying reciprocity improved.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future-facing benchmark should not report that it "protected future generations" when the world really changed the **repairability and substitution contract**.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world where risky activity proceeds unless later mitigation is added;
2. a world where avoidance is weak and mostly procedural;
3. a world where restoration is promised but only on successor-irrelevant timescales or weak ecological equivalence;
4. a world where compensation / offsetting is allowed despite low substitutability;
5. a world where avoidance is upstream, restoration order is explicit, and some harms are treated as non-substitutable on the timescale that matters to successors.

Those are not implementation footnotes.
They change whether future beneficiaries inherit intact option space, delayed repair burdens, or permanent losses disguised as "managed" transitions.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds long-horizon stewardship, restoration, compensation, or damage-management lanes, publish at least:

1. the harm-handling order: avoid, minimize, restore, compensate / offset, full restoration, or another sequence;
2. the spatial and temporal scale used to judge reversibility or irreversibility;
3. what counts as ecologically or morally substitutable, and which losses are treated as non-substitutable;
4. whether restoration is upstream, onsite, delayed, offsite, monitored, and enforceably followed through;
5. whether headline results survive one same-choice comparison where only avoidance priority or substitutability assumptions change.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake earlier avoidance, stronger non-substitution rules, or larger-scale restoration for the same thing as deeper Golden-Rule behavior.
