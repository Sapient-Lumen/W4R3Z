# Prefunding and financial assurance for long-horizon liabilities are world contracts, not budget aftercare

Recent work adds another missing layer to Concord's future-facing worlds: **it matters whether long-horizon liabilities are left largely pay-as-you-go, partially prefunded, fully prefunded, bonded or insured, or backed by ring-fenced reserves with real independence and conservative cost estimation**.

- `RS-GR-288` shows that public-segregated decommissioning funds can suffer from moral hazard, conflicts of interest, time inconsistency, concentration risk, and taxpayer spillover when governance is weak or funding falls short.
- `RS-GR-288` therefore warns that “there is a fund” is not yet enough; reserve independence, investment policy, and shortfall assignment still shape who pays later.
- `RS-GR-289` shows that long-horizon adequacy depends on contingency method, variance assumptions, confidence levels, and cost correlations, meaning reserve sufficiency is partly a modeling rule rather than a single self-evident number.
- `RS-GR-290` shows that offshore financial assurance turns on explicit architecture choices such as credit thresholds, predecessor liability, and P50 versus P70 cost coverage.
- `RS-GR-291` shows that Australia is explicitly reforming decommissioning around financial planning, assurance, risk assessment, and continued industry responsibility for liabilities expected over 30 to 50 years.
- Together, these sources warn that a benchmark can look more successor-friendly because liabilities were prefunded, conservatively estimated, or ring-fenced away from future taxpayers — not because the underlying Golden-Rule disposition improved.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future-facing benchmark should not report that an institution “cares more about successors” when the world mainly changed its **liability-funding and assurance architecture**.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world that leaves cleanup or upkeep largely to future budgets;
2. a world that partially prefunds expected liabilities but can still socialize shortfalls;
3. a world that uses bonded, insured, or otherwise secured obligations with explicit draw rules;
4. a world that ring-fences reserves but lets optimistic cost estimates or weak governance quietly undercut adequacy;
5. a world that looks prudent only because the accounting, confidence level, or backup guarantor changed.

Those are not finance footnotes.
They change whether successors inherit bounded obligations, contingent liabilities, or a polite promise with no durable funding spine.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds stewardship, infrastructure, decommissioning, or successor-burden lanes, publish at least:

1. whether liabilities are pay-as-you-go, partially prefunded, fully prefunded, bonded / insured, or backed by a segregated reserve;
2. who controls the reserve, who can raid or reclassify it, and who absorbs shortfalls if costs outrun assets;
3. what cost-estimation confidence level, contingency rule, and update cadence are used;
4. whether predecessor, parent, or guarantor liability remains live after ownership transfer;
5. whether headline results survive one same-behavior comparison where only prefunding / assurance architecture changes.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake prefunding discipline, stronger backup liability, or conservative estimation for the same thing as deeper reciprocity toward successors.
