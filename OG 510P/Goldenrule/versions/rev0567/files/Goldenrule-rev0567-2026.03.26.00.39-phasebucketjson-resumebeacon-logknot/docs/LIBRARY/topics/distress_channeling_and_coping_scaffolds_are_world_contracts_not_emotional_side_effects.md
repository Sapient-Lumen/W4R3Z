# Distress channeling and coping scaffolds are world contracts, not emotional side effects

Recent work adds another missing layer to Concord's future-facing worlds: **future-oriented urgency can either mobilize or suppress action depending on how distress is paired with coping, agency, and hope, not just on how much worry the world induces**.

- `RS-GR-223` shows in a three-wave longitudinal study of adolescents that higher-than-expected climate distress predicted lower-than-expected pro-environmental behavior one year later, unless meaning-focused coping was high; with stronger meaning-focused coping, distress instead predicted increases in later behavior.
- `RS-GR-224` shows in a large adult sample that climate worry was associated with worse mental-health symptoms, and that hope did not simply cancel worry; rather, behavioral engagement combined with higher individual efficacy-based hope was linked to better mental health than engagement without hope.
- Together, these results warn that a benchmark can look more action-oriented or more demoralized because it changed coping scaffolds, agency, and action channels around distress, not because the underlying reciprocity norm changed.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future-facing benchmark should not report "urgency increased cooperation" or "worry reduced engagement" without publishing how the world channels distress.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world that heightens future burden or worry but offers no meaningful action path;
2. a world that pairs urgency with meaning-focused coping or agency-building cues;
3. a world where pro-future action is available but not plausibly effective;
4. a world where action is paired with efficacy-based hope and therefore experienced differently by participants.

Those are not background emotional details.
They change whether the benchmark is measuring moral commitment, hopeless overload, or agency-supported engagement.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds urgent future-risk prompts, climate-burden worlds, or emotionally heavy stewardship lanes, publish at least:

1. how much future burden, worry, or distress the world induces or assumes;
2. whether participants receive meaning-focused coping, efficacy-based hope, or other agency-support scaffolds;
3. whether future-facing action channels are actually available and plausibly effective inside the world;
4. whether headline results include at least one low-scaffold baseline and one delayed follow-up on behavior or distress rather than only immediate post-prompt action.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake agency scaffolding or unmanaged distress for Golden-Rule progress or decline.
