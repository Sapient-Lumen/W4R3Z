# Concurrent relationship portfolios are world contracts, not background complexity

Recent work adds a missing layer to Concord's future reciprocity lanes: **a world where agents handle one repeated relationship at a time is not the same institution as a world where they juggle several obligations at once**.

- `RS-GR-180` shows that in human concurrent games, cooperation fell relative to the single-game control in both same-partner and different-partner treatments.
- The paper further shows that the strategic meaning of concurrency depends on partner topology: keeping two games with the same counterparty is not equivalent to splitting them across different counterparties.
- `RS-GR-181` adds that, in evolutionary multichannel models, linking games can sustain cooperation in a lower-benefit lane by borrowing leverage from a higher-benefit lane.
- Together, these results warn that a benchmark can look more or less cooperative simply because agents moved from one focal relationship to a concurrent obligation portfolio.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future benchmark should not treat concurrent obligations as harmless realism garnish.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a single repeated interaction with one active counterpart;
2. two or more concurrent lanes with the same counterpart;
3. two or more concurrent lanes with different counterparts;
4. worlds where low-stakes and high-stakes lanes run in parallel and can influence each other.

Those choices do not merely change sample volume.
They change whether reciprocity is dyadic, portfolio-based, or partly enforced by leverage from another lane.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds multi-relationship, multi-project, or multi-claimant reciprocity lanes, publish at least:

1. how many concurrent relationships or games each agent manages;
2. whether concurrent lanes share the same counterpart or different counterparts;
3. whether payoffs differ across lanes and which lane is more valuable;
4. whether headline cooperation is reported per lane and per portfolio rather than as one pooled rate.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake concurrency structure or partner topology for Golden-Rule progress.
