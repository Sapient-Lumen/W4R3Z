# Future-generation depth and temporal horizon are world contracts, not story scale

Recent work adds a missing layer to Concord's intergenerational and stewardship lanes: **a world that helps the next generation is not the same institution as a world that helps the seventh, thirtieth, or fiftieth generation**.

- `RS-GR-211` shows that ordinary participants do not treat “future generations” as one flat moral target. Reported concern, obligation, and collective-decision scope all decline across generational distance, with relatively strong concern concentrated much nearer to the present than a generic future-facing label suggests.
- The same source also shows an asymmetry worth preserving in benchmark design: responsibility can extend farther than felt concern, so worlds that ask for distant stewardship may still be judged under a thinner motivational regime than near-future helping even when agents endorse a duty in principle.
- `RS-GR-211` therefore warns against a common compression error: reporting one headline for “future beneficiaries” while quietly moving the benefit horizon from near-future descendants to remote generations many cohort-steps away.
- Together, these results mean that benchmark outcomes can look more or less caring because the world shortened or lengthened beneficiary distance, not because the underlying Golden-Rule policy improved.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future benchmark should not collapse all future-facing worlds into one generic “benefits future generations” label.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world where payoffs land mainly on the next cohort;
2. a world where consequences accumulate over several successor cohorts;
3. a world where the main beneficiaries are remote generations that current agents can barely imagine;
4. a world where a single reported score averages near- and far-future welfare together.

Those are not narrative scale choices.
They change moral discounting pressure, patience requirements, and what an apparent sustainability gain is actually evidence about.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds intergenerational, climate, or stewardship lanes, publish at least:

1. which cohorts receive the main benefits and harms, using explicit generation steps or time delays rather than a generic “future” label;
2. whether the benchmark headline is next-generation, medium-horizon, far-horizon, or a weighted mixture across horizons;
3. how one generation is operationalized (for example, cohort step, round block, or calendar proxy);
4. whether the main claim survives at least one nearer-horizon and one farther-horizon comparison beside the default lane.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake horizon-shortening for Golden-Rule progress.
