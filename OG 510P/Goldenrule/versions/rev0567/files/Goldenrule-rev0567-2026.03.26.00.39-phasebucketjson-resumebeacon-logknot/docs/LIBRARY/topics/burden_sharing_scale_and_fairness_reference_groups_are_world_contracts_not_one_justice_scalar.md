# Burden-sharing scale and fairness reference groups are world contracts, not one justice scalar

Recent work adds another missing layer to Concord's future-facing and climate-adjacent worlds: **policy support and cooperative uptake can turn on which fairness reference group is made salient, not just on whether the intervention is called fair in the abstract**.

- `RS-GR-227` shows that support for net-zero policy instruments is strongly predicted by distributive-fairness perceptions, especially expected impacts on future generations, low-income households, and rural communities.
- `RS-GR-228` shows that in Nordic survey data, support for climate mitigation policies tracks perceived household and local-community impacts more strongly than abstract societal distributive justice.
- Together, these results warn that a benchmark can look more acceptable, more just, or more cooperative because it shifted the fairness reference group from broad society to household, local community, future generations, or a vulnerable subgroup.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future-facing benchmark should not report "fair institutions improved cooperation" without publishing who fairness is being computed for and at what scale burdens are felt.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world where fairness is scored only at the aggregate societal level;
2. a world where fairness is scored for households or local communities;
3. a world where fairness explicitly foregrounds low-income, rural, or otherwise burdened present-day groups;
4. a world where future generations are one fairness reference group among several rather than the only target.

Those are not one justice knob.
They change whether cooperation is tracking impartial fairness, local burden aversion, subgroup protection, or intergenerational obligation.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds burden-sharing, decarbonisation, stewardship, or future-facing policy lanes, publish at least:

1. which fairness reference groups are explicit in the world (household, local community, broad society, low-income groups, rural groups, future generations, or another set);
2. whether burdens are experienced personally, locally, societally, or intergenerationally;
3. whether redistributive protections or subgroup shields exist and who receives them;
4. whether headline results survive one same-payoff comparison where the fairness reference group changes but aggregate efficiency does not.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake fairness-target selection for Golden-Rule progress.
