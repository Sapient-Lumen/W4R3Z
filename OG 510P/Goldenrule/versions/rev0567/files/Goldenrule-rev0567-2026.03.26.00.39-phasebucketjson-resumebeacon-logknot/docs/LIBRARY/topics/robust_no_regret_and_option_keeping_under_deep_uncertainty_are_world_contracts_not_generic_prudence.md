# Robust no-regret policy and option-keeping under deep uncertainty are world contracts, not generic prudence

Recent work adds another missing layer to Concord's future-facing worlds: **it matters whether an action is optimized for one reference future, built to remain acceptable across many futures, designed as a low- or no-regret move, or embedded in an adaptive pathway that deliberately keeps options open for later correction**.

- `RS-GR-278` shows that the OECD's 2025 foresight toolkit is explicitly about designing and preparing robust and adaptable public policies by challenging assumptions, creating scenarios, and stress-testing strategies across a range of possible futures.
- `RS-GR-279` shows that robust decision work under deep uncertainty can surface trade-offs and vulnerabilities that do not appear in reference-scenario optimization, and that robust policies may still depend critically on a few uncertainty dimensions.
- `RS-GR-280` shows that the current DMDU tool landscape is broad enough to support all core components of robust, adaptive strategy design, but practical uptake is still limited and implementation resources remain fragmented.
- `RS-GR-281` shows that OECD adaptation guidance explicitly separates no-regret, low-regret, win-win, and high-regret actions, warns against rigid pathway commitment under uncertainty, and treats flexibility as a first-class design property.
- `RS-GR-278` and `RS-GR-281` together imply that "future-ready" does not mean one thing: robustness, adaptability, flexibility, and immediate co-benefits can be mixed in different ways.
- Together, these sources warn that a benchmark can look more future-regarding because it became no-regret, low-regret, stress-tested, or adaptive-pathway-compatible — not because the agents developed deeper moral concern for posterity.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future-facing benchmark should not report that an institution "cares more about future generations" when the world mainly changed its **uncertainty-management architecture**.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world optimized for one guessed future;
2. a world that performs well across many plausible futures;
3. a world that privileges no-regret or low-regret moves with immediate or cross-scenario benefits;
4. a world that keeps options open through staged commitments, adaptive pathways, or explicit re-planning points;
5. a world that locks in high-regret irreversible commitments early and only later discovers its vulnerabilities.

Those are not optimization details.
They change who bears forecast error, who inherits stranded commitments, and whether successor governance retains room to learn.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds long-horizon planning, adaptation, resilience, or institutional-design lanes, publish at least:

1. whether the policy is reference-scenario optimal, robust across scenarios, no-regret / low-regret, adaptive-pathway based, or another type;
2. which uncertainties were stress-tested and which were held fixed;
3. what explicit vulnerabilities, regret conditions, or failure triggers remain;
4. whether the design preserves option value through staging, reversibility, contingency plans, or delayed irreversible commitment;
5. whether headline results survive one same-behavior comparison where only robustness / regret architecture changes.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake robust option-keeping or no-regret design for the same thing as stronger reciprocity or deeper care for future beneficiaries.
