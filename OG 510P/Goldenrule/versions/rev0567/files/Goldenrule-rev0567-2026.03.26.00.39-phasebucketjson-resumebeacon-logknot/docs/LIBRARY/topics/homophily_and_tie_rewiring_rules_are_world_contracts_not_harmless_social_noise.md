# Homophily and tie rewiring rules are world contracts, not harmless social noise

Recent work adds another missing layer to Concord's future networked worlds: **stable cooperation can emerge because the world segregates or separates people, not because agents became more reciprocal**.

- `RS-GR-185` shows that, in student social networks, prosocial behavior was not rewarded with more friendship nominations; instead, generous and selfish students became separated through negative tie dynamics.
- `RS-GR-186` adds that in student relationship formation, homophily and prior acquaintanceship can shape who connects with whom more strongly than experimentally measured reciprocal trust.
- Together, these results warn that a dynamic network can look morally improved when the real change is selective separation, demographic sorting, or chance-driven tie formation.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future benchmark should not collapse homophily, rewiring, and forced mixing into one generic “dynamic network” option.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. forced mixing with no endogenous tie choice;
2. rewiring that reacts to observed behavior only;
3. rewiring that also reacts to similarity, shared labels, or prior acquaintance;
4. worlds where defectors are punished by exclusion versus worlds where unlike agents simply stop meeting each other.

Those choices do not merely change realism.
They change whether cooperation is sustained by moral improvement, by selective avoidance, or by segregation.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds endogenous relationships or network rewiring, publish at least:

1. how ties are formed, broken, and replaced;
2. whether homophily, labels, shared background, or prior acquaintance influence tie choice;
3. whether separation is driven by positive attraction, negative repulsion, or both;
4. whether a forced-mixing or no-homophily baseline is kept beside any selective-separation world.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake sorting or segregation effects for Golden-Rule progress.
