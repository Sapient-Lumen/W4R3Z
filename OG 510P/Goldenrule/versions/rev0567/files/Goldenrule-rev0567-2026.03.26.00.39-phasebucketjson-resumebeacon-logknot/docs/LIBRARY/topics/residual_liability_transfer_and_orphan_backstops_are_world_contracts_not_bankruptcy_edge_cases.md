# Residual liability transfer and orphan backstops are world contracts, not bankruptcy edge cases

Recent work adds another missing layer to Concord's long-horizon liability worlds: **it matters whether residual risk stays with the operator, can be shared across multiple agents, transfers to the state under explicit conditions, is cushioned by pooled or trust-based mechanisms, or quietly defaults to taxpayers when firms fail or disappear**.

- `RS-GR-300` shows that governments actively consider parent guarantees, letters of credit, trust-style security arrangements, clarified liability, and bankruptcy-priority rules specifically to stop decommissioning costs from falling onto taxpayers.
- `RS-GR-301` shows that post-closure outcomes depend on who can share or accept residual risk and that relinquishment is itself a governance design problem, not a naturally settled end state.
- `RS-GR-302` shows that some regimes keep operators liable in perpetuity while others allow maintenance, monitoring, and residual liability to transfer to the state under an agreed financial regime.
- `RS-GR-303` shows that uncertainty over closure acceptance criteria, residual liabilities, and liability apportionment can itself block beneficial post-mining reuse.
- `RS-GR-297` shows that, absent durable perpetual-care mechanisms, long-term liability often defaults to the state.
- Together, these sources warn that a benchmark can look more successor-friendly because orphan-risk assignment, transfer conditions, or public-backstop architecture changed — not because the underlying Golden-Rule disposition improved.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future-facing benchmark should not report that an institution “protects successors better” when the world mainly changed its **residual-risk custody and orphan-backstop architecture**.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world where operators remain liable in perpetuity;
2. a world where liability can transfer only after explicit payment and verification;
3. a world where multiple agents, parents, or pooled vehicles share the burden;
4. a world where state assumption exists as a priced and tightly conditioned last resort;
5. a world that looks safe only because orphan risk was quietly socialized or hidden in a public backstop.

Those are not bankruptcy edge cases.
They change whether successors inherit a bounded and funded liability chain, a contest over apportionment, or a future taxpayer rescue.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds long-horizon liability or stewardship lanes, publish at least:

1. who holds residual liability after nominal closure and under what transfer conditions that can change;
2. whether parents, partners, pooled funds, trust structures, or public entities share or backstop residual risk;
3. what happens under insolvency, abandonment, or owner disappearance;
4. whether public assumption is absent, discretionary, rule-based, or automatic after a threshold event;
5. whether headline results survive one same-behavior comparison where only orphan-risk or transfer semantics change.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake public backstops, pooled residual-risk sharing, or easier relinquishment for the same thing as deeper reciprocity toward successors.
