# Representative selection route and cohort composition are world contracts, not generic future voice

Recent work adds another missing layer to Concord's future-facing worlds: **it matters who is allowed to speak for future beneficiaries, how those proxy speakers are selected, and which present-day cohorts are systematically over- or under-represented before any future-facing institution even begins to act**.

- `RS-GR-250` argues that democratic legitimacy for future-oriented representation does not require ordinary electoral authorization in every case; self-proclaimed, surrogate, and non-electoral representative institutions can be legitimate when those represented could not have authorized the claim themselves.
- The same source also treats citizen panels, deliberative forums, and carefully selected samples as meaningfully different representative devices rather than as cosmetic alternatives to elections.
- `RS-GR-251` argues that not all future-generation representatives are interchangeable: a plausible representative should satisfy a hypothetical-acceptance criterion, with epistemic / experiential similarity and motivation to act on behalf of future generations as key derivative tests.
- `RS-GR-252` shows that cohort composition is already institutionally skewed in many democracies: across OECD countries, people aged 20-39 were 34% of the voting-age population on average but only 22% of MPs in 2024.
- `RS-GR-253` shows that youth in intergenerational-governance work see themselves as current stakeholders and possible proxies for future generations only when participation is meaningful, diverse, supported, and tied to transparent feedback and accountability.
- Together, these results warn that a benchmark can look more future-regarding because proxy speakers changed from incumbents to youth, because the institution recruited directly affected communities, because the sample became more demographically balanced, or because claims gained contestable legitimacy — not because the base reciprocity norm improved.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future-facing benchmark should not report "future generations were represented" without publishing who was allowed to carry that voice, how they entered the institution, and what present-day cohort balance they embodied.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world where future interests are voiced only by incumbent officeholders;
2. a world where voice comes from appointed experts or ombuds figures;
3. a world where voice comes from youth quotas, descendant-linked actors, or climate-affected communities;
4. a world where voice comes from randomly selected citizens or deliberative mini-publics;
5. a world where the same formal powers are held fixed but representative source, diversity, and contestability differ.

Those are not staffing details.
They change whether apparent future-regard reflects a different constituency, a different legitimacy theory, or a different proxy-selection route.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds future-beneficiary, stewardship, or intergenerational-governance lanes, publish at least:

1. who may speak for future beneficiaries: incumbents, youth, descendants, experts, affected communities, sortition samples, ombuds / commissioners, role-played future selves, or another source;
2. how those representatives are selected or authorized: election, appointment, quota, random selection, targeted recruitment, role-play, or hybrid route;
3. what cohort-balance and diversity constraints apply, including age composition and inclusion of marginalized or directly affected groups;
4. how representative claims are challenged, reviewed, or replaced if their legitimacy is disputed;
5. whether headline results survive one same-power comparison where only representative source or selection route changes.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake a new proxy class or a younger / more affected representative pool for a generic improvement in Golden-Rule reciprocity.
