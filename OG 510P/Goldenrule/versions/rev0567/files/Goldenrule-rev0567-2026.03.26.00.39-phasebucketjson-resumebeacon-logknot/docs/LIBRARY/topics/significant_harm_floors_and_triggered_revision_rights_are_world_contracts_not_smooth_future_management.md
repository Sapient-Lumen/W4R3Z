# Significant-harm floors and triggered revision rights are world contracts, not smooth future management

Recent work adds another missing layer to Concord's future-facing worlds: **long-horizon governance can be organized around hard harm floors and explicit pathway-switch triggers rather than around smooth average-welfare optimization alone**.

- `RS-GR-246` shows that recent Earth-system governance work distinguishes safe boundaries from *just* boundaries and argues that long-horizon governance may need explicit upper limits on significant harm rather than only aggregate optimization.
- `RS-GR-247` shows that adaptation tipping points are not one thing: policy-based, acceptance-based, transition-based, and opportunity-based thresholds can each trigger different interventions and trajectories.
- `RS-GR-248` shows that resilience-pathway planning treats thresholds and tipping points as decision points; once a relevant threshold is exceeded, a decision point can trigger additional or alternative measures rather than leaving the pathway fixed.
- `RS-GR-249` extends dynamic pathways planning by adding target points for mitigation and sustainable-development goals and identifying critical decisions over time, so review and switching rights are part of the design rather than aftercare.
- Together, these results warn that a benchmark can look more future-protective because the world adopted hard no-go floors, because it defined explicit trigger conditions for review or switching, or because it gave actors authority to reopen pathways when thresholds were approached.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future-facing benchmark should not report "the policy protected future generations" without publishing whether the world uses smooth average-welfare tradeoffs or threshold-style protection with explicit switch / review rights.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world where harms are traded smoothly against gains with no hard floor;
2. a world where certain harms are treated as threshold breaches or no-go regions;
3. a world where thresholds exist but no actor has authority to reopen the path when they are approached;
4. a world where monitored indicators trigger mandatory review, measure substitution, or pathway switching;
5. a world where review rights, trigger ownership, and switching authority differ across present and successor cohorts.

Those are not monitoring details.
They change whether apparent protection reflects stronger reciprocity, different loss floors, earlier intervention rights, or a formally adaptive institution.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds stewardship, resilience, climate, or long-horizon risk lanes, publish at least:

1. whether the world is smooth-tradeoff, floor-constrained, or threshold-triggered;
2. what counts as a significant-harm floor or adaptation tipping point, and whether it is quantitative, qualitative, or mixed;
3. which indicators are monitored and who owns the trigger call;
4. what happens when a threshold is approached or crossed: warning only, mandatory review, measure substitution, pathway switch, or shutdown;
5. whether present and successor cohorts have the same or different rights to invoke review and change course.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake hard floor protection and adaptive revision rights for the same thing as generic future-regarding virtue.
