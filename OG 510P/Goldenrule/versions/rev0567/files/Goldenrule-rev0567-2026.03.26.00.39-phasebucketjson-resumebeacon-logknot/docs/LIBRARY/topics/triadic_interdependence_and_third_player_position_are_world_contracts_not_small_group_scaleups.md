# Triadic interdependence and third-player position are world contracts, not small-group scaleups

Recent work adds a missing layer to Concord's future reciprocity lanes: **a three-person or small-group world is not just a dyad with one extra body**.

- `RS-GR-191` shows that in triadic Prisoner’s Dilemmas, structural incentives and information scope materially change cooperation, and that selective cooperation by a third player can stabilize cooperative dyads while destabilizing defective dyads.
- The same paper further shows that experiential information about actions and outcomes can raise cooperation while full descriptive payoff information can reduce cooperation, so “more information” is not one monotone dial in small groups.
- Together, these results warn that a benchmark can look more or less reciprocal simply because the third player's position changes the local leverage structure.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future benchmark should not treat triads or small groups as harmless scaleups from pairwise reciprocity.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. one dyadic repeated interaction;
2. a triad where one agent simultaneously sits in two linked dyads;
3. a small group where information about mutual interdependence is partial versus fully described;
4. a world where a third party can selectively stabilize one relationship while weakening another.

Those choices do not merely change group size.
They change whether “direct reciprocity” is still local, whether third-party position creates bridge leverage, and whether the observed gain comes from a small-group architecture rather than from a better moral rule.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds triadic or other small-group reciprocity lanes, publish at least:

1. the local interdependence pattern (who affects whom, and whether each agent sits in one or multiple linked dyads);
2. whether third-party action can differentially stabilize or destabilize pairwise relationships;
3. whether information is experiential, descriptive, or both, and which parts of the small-group dependency structure are actually revealed;
4. whether headline results survive a dyadic companion baseline with matched payoff incentives.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake small-group architecture for Golden-Rule progress.
