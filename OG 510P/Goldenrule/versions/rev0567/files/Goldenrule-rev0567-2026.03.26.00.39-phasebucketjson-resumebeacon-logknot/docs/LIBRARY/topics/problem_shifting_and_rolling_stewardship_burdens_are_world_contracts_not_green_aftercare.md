# Problem-shifting and rolling stewardship burdens are world contracts, not green aftercare

Recent work adds another missing layer to Concord's future-facing worlds: **a policy can look sustainable while quietly shifting burdens across sectors, geographies, time, or successor maintenance obligations, so a green headline is not yet an intergenerational victory**.

- `RS-GR-238` shows that climate policies and measures can generate sectoral, geographical, and temporal problem shifts, including temporal shifts that intensify burdens for future generations rather than resolving them.
- The same source warns that policy packages can reduce one visible problem while shrinking future solution space or exporting costs elsewhere, so "mitigation" is not automatically net future-protective.
- `RS-GR-239` shows that long-term transgenerational projects differ sharply in whether they impose rolling stewardship and dependence on successor agency or instead approach passive safety that lowers future maintenance dependence.
- The same source also sharpens a distinction that Concord should preserve: reversibility and retrievability can increase future choice in one sense while simultaneously increasing successor exposure to contingency, upkeep, and dependence on continued institutional competence.
- Together, these results warn that a benchmark can look more future-regarding because burdens were exported to other sectors or regions, because the main costs were deferred to later cohorts, or because a solution quietly assumes indefinite competent stewardship by successors.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future-facing benchmark should not report "the policy helped future generations" without publishing both problem-shifting and maintenance-dependence semantics.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world where harms are reduced locally but displaced to another sector, region, or vulnerable population;
2. a world where immediate gains are purchased by shrinking future solution space;
3. a world where protection requires rolling monitoring, upkeep, repair, and institutional continuity across generations;
4. a world where the solution approaches passive safety and does not rely heavily on successor maintenance;
5. a world where reversibility is high but only because successor cohorts inherit more ongoing burden.

Those are not aftercare details.
They change whether a benchmark is measuring genuine burden reduction, burden export, future lock-in, or dependence on uninterrupted stewardship competence.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds sustainability, stewardship, transition, or long-horizon infrastructure lanes, publish at least:

1. whether burdens can shift across sectors, regions, populations, or time even when the headline problem improves;
2. whether the world shrinks or preserves future solution space;
3. whether the solution requires rolling stewardship (inspection, upkeep, repair, competent institutions) or approaches passive safety with low successor dependency;
4. whether reversibility increases future agency, future burden, or both;
5. whether headline results survive one low-problem-shift baseline and one low-maintenance / passive-safety baseline beside any open-ended stewardship lane.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake burden export or successor maintenance dependency for Golden-Rule progress.
