# Asset-condition ledgers and deferred-maintenance liabilities are world contracts, not wear and tear

Recent work adds another missing layer to Concord's future-facing worlds: **it matters whether deterioration is explicitly measured through inventories, condition ratings, depreciation logic, lifecycle costing, and funding-adequacy metrics, because hidden maintenance backlogs can quietly transfer large burdens to successors even when nobody changed the nominal policy goal**.

- `RS-GR-292` shows that deferred maintenance is an accumulated liability created when preservation spending falls below depreciation, with a large backlog emerging even while total infrastructure spending continues.
- `RS-GR-293` shows that many governments still lack complete asset inventories, condition data, repair histories, and lifecycle-cost estimates, which makes prioritization and long-term planning structurally weak.
- `RS-GR-294` shows that trend stories can mislead: some states improve but still remain materially below full funding adequacy when measured with the Asset Sustainability Index.
- `RS-GR-295` shows that infrastructure-fund design can distort successor burden if maintenance and replacement are excluded or if contingent liabilities stay off to the side of transparent fiscal accounts.
- `RS-GR-296` shows that deferred-maintenance backlogs can become a first-class governance risk rather than a facilities nuisance once they scale high enough.
- Together, these sources warn that a benchmark can look more future-protective because deterioration became visible, measurable, and governable — not because the underlying Golden-Rule disposition improved.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future-facing benchmark should not report that an institution “acts more responsibly toward the future” when the world mainly changed its **condition-ledger and backlog-visibility architecture**.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world with incomplete inventories and informal condition knowledge;
2. a world that measures asset condition but not lifecycle cost or depreciation;
3. a world that publishes backlog and funding-adequacy ratios tied to condition targets;
4. a world that allows new construction to crowd out preservation while still reporting high total investment;
5. a world that looks prudent only because it finally surfaced a maintenance liability that had long been hiding.

Those are not reporting niceties.
They change whether successor burdens are seen early enough to manage, or inherited as surprise deterioration plus emergency cost.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds infrastructure, stewardship, or public-asset lanes, publish at least:

1. whether a complete asset inventory exists and how condition is measured;
2. what depreciation, backlog, or deferred-maintenance logic is used and at what cadence it is updated;
3. the split between preservation / rehabilitation spending and new-build spending;
4. whether lifecycle costs, condition targets, and any funding-adequacy metric such as ASI are published;
5. whether headline results survive one same-behavior comparison where only asset-ledger richness or backlog visibility changes.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake condition visibility, backlog disclosure, or better maintenance bookkeeping for the same thing as deeper reciprocity toward successors.
