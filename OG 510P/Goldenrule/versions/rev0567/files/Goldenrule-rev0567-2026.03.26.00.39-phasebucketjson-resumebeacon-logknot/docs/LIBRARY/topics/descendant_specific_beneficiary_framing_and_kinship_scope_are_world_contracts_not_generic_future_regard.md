# Descendant-specific beneficiary framing and kinship scope are world contracts, not generic future regard

Recent work adds another missing layer to Concord's future-facing worlds: **acting for 'future generations' is not the same as acting for one's own children, grandchildren, or descendant line, and those kin-linked frames can change both motivation and cost structure**.

- `RS-GR-229` shows that respondents asked to choose policies from the standpoint of their children and grandchildren were more likely to avoid burdening future generations, even while respondents in general still avoided direct present-day burdens.
- `RS-GR-230` shows that parenthood can heighten climate concern and willingness to act for children's futures while also increasing energy use, transport needs, plastic waste, and dependence on supportive structures.
- Together, these results warn that a benchmark can look more future-regarding because it narrowed the beneficiary from abstract future people to family-linked descendants or activated a caregiver identity that changes both moral salience and household constraints.

## Why this matters for Concord

A future-facing benchmark should not report "agents cared about future generations" without publishing whether those beneficiaries were anonymous future people, own descendants, same-community successors, or a caregiving role made psychologically salient.

There is a real institutional difference between:
1. a world where beneficiaries are abstract unknown future people;
2. a world where beneficiaries are framed as one's own children or grandchildren;
3. a world where caregiver or parent identity is explicitly activated;
4. a world where the same caregiver framing also changes present household burdens or practical constraints.

Those are not interchangeable moral readouts.
They change whether the benchmark is measuring universal future regard, kinship-linked stewardship, caregiving-role activation, or willingness to act under family-life friction.

## Minimal implementor handoff

If Concord adds future-facing, climate, or stewardship lanes, publish at least:

1. whether future beneficiaries are abstract future people, own descendants, local successors, or another socially narrowed group;
2. whether parent / caregiver / family-role identity is explicitly activated or merely latent;
3. whether that role activation also changes current costs, workload, mobility, or household-consumption constraints;
4. whether headline results survive one matched-cost comparison where descendant-specific framing is replaced by universal future-beneficiary framing.

Without that compact contract, future inheritors can mistake kinship scope or caregiving activation for Golden-Rule progress.
