# Vision dialogue — what PoliceMisconduct could and should become

This surface records the rev0002 conversation-level interpretation of the seed. It is not a product spec, a legal memo, or a decision to admit live records.

## Working thesis

PoliceMisconduct should become a **public-record accountability substrate**: a shared, source-bound, correction-capable record layer that lets many downstream civic actors reason about policing without redoing the same brittle acquisition, normalization, and identity work.

The project should not begin as a public shaming site or as a broad accusation index. It should begin as infrastructure that can say, with exact humility:

- what record exists;
- who produced it;
- what the record actually supports;
- what it does not support;
- what uncertainty remains;
- what privacy/display limits apply;
- what would cause the claim to roll back;
- and who can contest or amend the entry.

## Four nested things it could become

### 1. The source graph

A custody-preserving map of public records: court filings, dockets, orders, settlements, POST/decertification documents, consent decrees, monitor reports, policies, union contracts, FOIA productions, official statements, public databases, news records, civil-society datasets, and archived video/audio references.

The source graph is the safe first layer because it can often be built before person-level display is settled. It answers: **what are the public carriers, where are they, how stale are they, and what fields can be extracted?**

### 2. The claim graph

Every extracted statement becomes a bounded claim with a state: observed-in-source, asserted-by-party, administratively found, judicially adjudicated, settled-without-admission, reversed, sealed, corrected, disputed, or withdrawn. The claim graph should refuse the dangerous shortcut from “mentioned in a record” to “true.”

This is where the archive becomes unusually valuable: it preserves distinctions that normal databases flatten.

### 3. The entity graph

Officer, department, incident, lawsuit, settlement, policy, and civilian-protected entities are linked conservatively. The identity graph should be powerful internally and cautious publicly. Name-only merges must remain prohibited; unmerge receipts should be as important as merge receipts.

The central public-interest target is not voyeurism. It is the pattern the seed names: officers moving across jurisdictions after serious misconduct and becoming hard to trace. The mature entity graph should make that kind of disappearance structurally difficult.

### 4. The civic interface layer

Different users need different views:

- public defenders need Brady/Giglio-relevant credibility surfaces and testimony-history context;
- journalists need timelines, repeat-defendant patterns, and source packs;
- families need one dignified place to locate official, court, and news records about a death or injury;
- researchers need normalized fields and denominator warnings;
- oversight bodies need department-level policy, discipline, settlement, and monitor histories;
- community members need readable department-level risk/cost/policy context without being forced through PACER, FOIA portals, and news archives.

This interface layer should be role-sensitive and harm-aware, not a single maximal public dump.

## What it should become at maturity

At 10,000+ revisions, the cube should feel less like a website and more like civic plumbing. A person should be able to ask:

- What public records exist for this department?
- What did this settlement actually resolve, and what did it not admit?
- Which officers are repeat civil-rights defendants, and under what adjudication states?
- Did a policy change occur before or after the incident?
- Was an officer later hired elsewhere after termination, resignation under investigation, decertification, arbitration, or adverse findings?
- Is this officer linked by strong identifiers or only weak name similarity?
- What records are missing, sealed, destroyed, denied, or still pending?
- What would change this entry if a new appeal, correction, or public record appears?

The best version of the project makes difficult facts discoverable while making careless conclusions harder.

## What it should not become

- Not a revenge tool.
- Not a list of “bad cops.”
- Not a place where settlement equals guilt.
- Not a place where allegation equals finding.
- Not a place where public-record legality substitutes for ethical display.
- Not a police intelligence tool against civilians, witnesses, families, organizers, or vulnerable communities.
- Not an extractive tragedy index that ignores family dignity.
- Not a single-score risk product whose simplicity hides uncertainty.
- Not a commercial surveillance product with accountability branding.

## Product posture

The first durable public view should probably be **department-level and source-level**, not officer-level. Department settlement ledgers, policy/union-contract histories, consent-decree monitor archives, and source inventories are safer early products because they test normalization while reducing immediate person-level harm.

Officer-level and incident-level aggregation can come later, after the archive has mature identity resolution, privacy classes, correction routes, and legal review.

## Pilot instinct

Rev0002 does not choose a pilot, but it records a preference: begin with a low-harm, high-structure pilot such as:

1. consent-decree / monitor-report departments;
2. public settlement ledgers for a small set of cities;
3. policy and union-contract archives for those same departments;
4. then linked lawsuits and incident records only where source custody and display rules are strong.

This staged pilot lets the cube prove it can normalize records before it names living people in a public-facing aggregate.

## Design maxim

The corpus is for accountability, not certainty theater. Its job is to let source-bound, dignity-preserving records accumulate until patterns that were previously anecdotal become visible, contestable, correctable, and harder to ignore.
