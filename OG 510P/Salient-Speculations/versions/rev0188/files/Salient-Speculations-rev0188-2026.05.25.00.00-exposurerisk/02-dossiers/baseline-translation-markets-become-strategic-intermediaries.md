# Dossier: Baseline Translation Markets Become Strategic Intermediaries

## Core claim

Once shared scenario baselines spread, access to counterfactual systems remains uneven, and revalidation becomes a standing burden, a further bottleneck appears.
The important question is no longer only **which reference future is official** or **who can access the model environment**.
It becomes **who can translate a common baseline world into sector-ready indicators, local decisions, regulator-ready evidence, and repeatable institutional action**.

The stronger version of the thesis is that **baseline translation markets become strategic intermediaries**.
A growing share of consequential institutions may not rely primarily on raw model outputs.
They may rely on organizations that sit between generic counterfactual infrastructure and operational choice: climate-service providers, sector-model consultancies, evidence-pack vendors, validation bureaus, public support centres, and other specialists who convert shared baseline futures into decision-ready packages.

In that world, the practical question is no longer only *which model is credible?*
It becomes *who can turn that credible model and shared baseline into something my sector can actually use, defend, update, and pay for?*

## Why this belongs in the archive

The archive now has dossiers on **simulation capacity becomes governance infrastructure**, **model credibility standards become a new operating choke point**, **shared scenario baselines become a contested political object**, **counterfactual access becomes an institutional inequality multiplier**, and **revalidation windows become a standing operational burden** [S692–S729].
Those dossiers establish that institutions are building maintained counterfactual systems, converging around common baseline worlds, distributing access unevenly, and discovering that admissibility must be maintained over time.
But they still leave one intermediary layer underdescribed: **who translates shared baselines into sector-specific, locally admissible, decision-ready outputs?**

That translation layer is already becoming explicit.
NGFS says its climate scenarios are designed to provide a **common reference framework** for analysing the impacts of climate change on the economy and financial sector [S712].
That is an important first step, but a common framework is not the same thing as a decision.
Someone still has to adapt the shared reference world to a bank’s portfolio, a city’s drainage plan, an insurer’s underwriting model, or an infrastructure operator’s investment sequence.

The climate-services world makes this intermediary role overt.
WMO’s Climate Services Information System says it guides the development and application of science-based climate information and services in support of decision-making across climate-sensitive sectors such as agriculture, water, energy, disaster risk reduction, and health [S730].
Copernicus says it provides climate data and impact information across sectoral areas through the Climate Data Store, and that the CDS is designed to enable users to **tailor services** to more specific public or commercial needs [S731].
This is strong official evidence that the governing problem is no longer only making baseline data available.
It is making that baseline usable inside sector-specific decisions.

Destination Earth pushes the same structure further.
Its Climate Change Adaptation Digital Twin now describes methods for translating raw climate data into impact-sector information while simulations are running.
It says one-pass algorithms condense and translate complex outputs into sector-specific indicators and statistics, making them technically manageable and directly relevant for climate services and decision-making [S732].
That matters because it shows the translation layer moving inside the workflow itself.
The valuable output is no longer simply the baseline world; it is the transformed indicator package that a sector can actually act on.

WMO’s 2025 climate-services dashboard shows why this translation layer may become a real inequality and market problem.
WMO says climate services require engagement between provider and recipient to support specific decision-making processes and notes that only 14% of WMO Members were equipped to provide advanced climate services in 2024; it also says many members still do not provide tailored climate projections and sector-specific products [S733].
That implies that translation capacity is not a trivial final step.
It is scarce, uneven, and institutionally consequential.

The same architecture appears outside climate.
FDA’s Model-Informed Drug Development Paired Meeting Program requires applicants to specify the **question of interest** and **context of use** for model-based evidence [S700].
EMA’s ICH M15 guideline likewise treats question of interest, context of use, model influence, consequence of wrong decision, verification, validation, and applicability assessment as core elements of model-informed evidence [S701].
FDA’s 2025 AI guidance then adds a lifecycle-maintenance layer, treating ongoing monitoring and re-execution of credibility steps as necessary after relevant changes [S725].
Together these sources imply that shared or credible models do not directly govern action by themselves.
They require a specialized layer that translates model outputs into the precise evidentiary and operational form needed for a particular domain.

Taken together, these signals support a broader speculation: **once common baseline worlds and formal model-admissibility rules spread, the organizations that translate those worlds into sector-ready, locally defensible choices may become some of the most important quiet intermediaries in governance**.
The bottleneck moves from model possession toward translation capability.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Translation firms become silent governance centres

The organizations that convert shared baseline worlds into sector-specific indicators, filings, stress tests, or planning packages may start shaping what counts as prudent action even when they do not own the underlying models.

### 2. Decision-ready indicator bundles become quasi-standards

Repeatedly used translation outputs — heat metrics for schools, coastal exposure scores for lenders, technology-cost packages for planners, model-analysis templates for regulators — may become the de facto units through which institutions perceive risk.

### 3. Smaller institutions buy subscriptions to interpretation rather than capacity to model

Many ministries, municipalities, hospitals, utilities, or mid-sized firms may not build internal counterfactual teams.
They may instead buy retained access to translators who update assumptions, rerun sector metrics, maintain evidence packs, and explain what changed.

### 4. Baseline updates create recurrent translation churn

A change in an official scenario family, benchmark, or guidance document may trigger cascades of downstream work not because institutions rebuild models from scratch, but because translators must regenerate sector-specific packages and defend them again.

### 5. Challenge rights concentrate in the hands of intermediaries

The actors most capable of contesting baseline updates, revalidation demands, or scenario parameter choices may be the specialists who know how the common world gets turned into actionable local outputs.

### 6. Public translation utilities become a live policy option

If translation capacity proves too concentrated or too expensive, governments may create shared climate-service centres, sector-extension services, or public evidence-pack utilities so weaker institutions are not forced into permanent dependence on private intermediaries.

### 7. Liability and assurance markets grow around translation quality

As more decisions depend on translated baseline outputs, institutions may increasingly ask who is responsible when sector-specific indicators are poorly chosen, stale, biased, or insufficiently documented for the actual context of use.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Shared baselines become so easy to use that most institutions can parameterize and interpret them directly with little specialist help.
- Official bodies publish sector-ready packages comprehensive enough that intermediary translation markets remain thin or low leverage.
- AI tooling automates baseline translation so effectively that sector-specific adaptation becomes cheap, routine, and weakly differentiated.
- Most institutions continue treating shared scenarios as loose background context rather than decision-bearing inputs that require careful contextualization.
- Legal authority, politics, or data scarcity remain much more constraining than translation quality in most sectors.

## Research queue

- Which sectors first depend most heavily on translation intermediaries: climate adaptation, finance, energy planning, insurance, drug regulation, or infrastructure procurement?
- What becomes the scarce unit in practice: domain knowledge, workflow integration, evidentiary documentation, regulatory dialogue, or update management?
- Where do public alternatives emerge first: climate-service centres, regulatory help desks, sector model cooperatives, or shared evidence-pack utilities?
- Which translation outputs become quasi-standards first: indicator bundles, stress-test templates, model-analysis plans, or procurement-ready scenario packages?
- When do buyers start negotiating not only for model access, but for translation rights, update cadence, rerun support, and obligations to regenerate sector-specific outputs after baseline changes?
