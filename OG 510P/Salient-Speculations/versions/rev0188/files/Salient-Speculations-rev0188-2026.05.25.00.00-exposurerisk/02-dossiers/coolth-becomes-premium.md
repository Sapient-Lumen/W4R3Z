# Dossier: Coolth Becomes a Premium

## Core claim

As warming, electrification, and compute intensity rise together, **coolth** — reliable access to tolerable thermal conditions for people, machines, and food systems — is starting to behave like a premium good. The speculative claim is not merely that heat gets worse. It is that cool ambient conditions, efficient cooling systems, shade, thermal design, and low-water cooling options begin to sort places, occupations, and investment flows.

This is broader than a climate-adaptation note and broader than a data-centre siting note. The stronger pattern is that **thermal geography** starts to reorganize competitiveness. Some cities, corridors, and facilities become more attractive not just because they are cheaper or greener, but because they can keep humans and equipment functioning without extreme cooling costs, water draw, or heat-related productivity loss.

## Why this belongs in the archive

This archive already has dossiers on compute-energy sovereignty, climate-water-relocation, second-tier cities, embodied reliability, and hidden chokepoints. The missing layer was the thermal constraint that increasingly cuts across all of them. Once heat rises far enough, more systems are disciplined not only by energy and water in general, but by the ability to maintain **usable cool conditions**.

ECMWF reported that 2025 was the third-warmest year on record and that **half of global land area experienced more days than average with at least strong heat stress** [S180]. That matters because the question is no longer whether heat remains episodic. The relevant shift is that heat stress is becoming a more normal background condition across larger shares of inhabited and economically active territory.

WHO and WMO now treat worker heat stress as both a health and economic problem. Their 2025 report and guidance say the frequency and intensity of extreme heat events have risen sharply and that **worker productivity drops by 2–3% for every degree above 20°C** [S181]. Once that becomes institutionally legible, coolth stops being a comfort variable and starts becoming a labor-capacity variable.

The World Bank is already framing the urban version of the problem in macroeconomic terms. Its 2025 Europe and Central Asia heat report says hot days in the region’s major cities could more than triple by 2050 and that extreme heat could reduce annual GDP by an estimated **2.5% by 2050**, while also straining grids, transport systems, health systems, and building stock [S182]. This pushes thermal resilience toward the centre of urban competitiveness and municipal planning.

The compute layer sharpens the same pattern. The IEA says data-centre operators should locate new facilities in areas with **suitable climates and low water stress** and notes that data-centre sustainability reporting in the EU now includes water use [S183]. DOE’s federal guidance is even blunter: many data-centre cooling systems are **water-intensive applications**, and water use scales with heat load and cooling efficiency [S184]. As compute demand rises, cool climates and low-water thermal management may become strategic location advantages rather than just technical preferences.

Agriculture adds a second material layer. FAO and WMO said in late 2025 that a **single heatwave can cut agricultural productivity by up to 50%**, while agricultural workers are 35 times more likely to die from occupational heat exposure than workers in other sectors [S185]. If heat increasingly degrades both crop yields and safe labor capacity, then coolth becomes a constraint on food systems too.

The speculative consequence is that regions able to provide shade, passive cooling, district cooling, heat-safe working hours, water-efficient cooling, and lower ambient temperatures may gain a compounding advantage. They can preserve human productivity, reduce cooling costs, protect infrastructure life, and host compute-intensive facilities with less thermal stress. By contrast, places that cannot reliably buy, design, or govern access to coolth may experience a slow competitiveness tax.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Thermal comfort becomes a location premium

Places with naturally cooler climates, lower wet-bulb risk, better building stock, more shade, or access to low-water cooling may attract disproportionate shares of compute, logistics, advanced manufacturing, and climate-sensitive migration.

### 2. Heat-safe workability becomes a labor-market variable

Governments and firms may increasingly redesign hours, staffing, and occupational standards around thermal exposure. Some work may move earlier, later, indoors, or into cooler regions.

### 3. Cooling systems become strategic infrastructure

High-efficiency cooling, district cooling, passive-design retrofits, heat-resilient building envelopes, and water-smart thermal management may become infrastructure classes in their own right rather than secondary engineering details.

### 4. Urban desirability shifts toward thermal governance

A city may become attractive not only because of wages or amenities, but because it can keep streets, transit, housing, schools, and hospitals usable during long hot periods.

### 5. Compute geography partially follows coolth

As AI raises data-centre demand, siting may increasingly reward cool regions, places with lower water stress, and jurisdictions that can integrate thermal disclosure, reuse of waste heat, and resilient grid planning.

### 6. Thermal inequality becomes more visible

Households, schools, hospitals, and small firms with weaker access to cooling, shade, or retrofits may face rising penalties in health, learning, and productivity, even within the same city.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Cooling technologies become dramatically cheaper and less water-intensive everywhere, flattening the location premium.
- Urban design and building retrofits adapt quickly enough that thermal conditions stop sorting places in economically meaningful ways.
- Remote work, automation, and nocturnal scheduling absorb much of the labor-productivity damage from daytime heat.
- Water scarcity, insurance retreat, or other climate stresses prove more decisive than thermal conditions themselves.
- Compute demand shifts toward architectures or operating practices that sharply reduce cooling loads.

## Research queue

- Which sectors re-sort first around coolth: data centres, advanced manufacturing, warehousing, tourism, agriculture, or household migration?
- Which measures most effectively convert heat risk into governable variables: worker-protection rules, building codes, district cooling, tree-canopy targets, or data-centre water disclosures?
- Which places gain most from naturally cooler climates versus from competent thermal governance?
- How often do thermal constraints actually show up as electricity constraints, water constraints, or labor-scheduling constraints rather than as direct temperature constraints?
- When does coolth become visible in financing, insurance, siting, and employer recruitment decisions rather than only in climate-adaptation plans?
