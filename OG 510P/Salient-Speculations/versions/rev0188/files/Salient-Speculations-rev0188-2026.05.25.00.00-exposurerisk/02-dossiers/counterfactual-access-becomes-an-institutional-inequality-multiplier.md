# Dossier: Counterfactual Access Becomes an Institutional Inequality Multiplier

## Core claim

Once simulation capacity spreads, credibility standards harden, and shared scenario baselines start coordinating action, a further bottleneck appears.
The important question is no longer only **who has a model** or **which baseline future is official**.
It becomes **who can repeatedly access maintained counterfactual systems strongly enough to test interventions, challenge assumptions, translate shared baselines into local decisions, and keep that capability live over time**.

The stronger version of the thesis is that **counterfactual access becomes an institutional inequality multiplier**.
Actors that can routinely enter simulation environments, use trusted benchmark worlds, obtain compute and expert support, and translate those outputs into operational decisions may accumulate a durable form of administrative and strategic advantage.
Actors without that access may become dependent on vendors, stale baseline assumptions, delayed external reports, or policy choices made by better-instrumented institutions.

In that world, the practical question is no longer only *can this sector simulate?*
It becomes *who can actually get inside the counterfactual layer often enough, cheaply enough, and credibly enough to govern with it rather than merely receive its outputs second-hand?*

## Why this belongs in the archive

The archive now has dossiers on **simulation capacity becomes governance infrastructure**, **model credibility standards become a new operating choke point**, and **shared scenario baselines become a contested political object** [S692–S718].
Those dossiers establish that institutions are building counterfactual systems, formalizing admissibility, and converging around shared reference worlds.
But they still leave open the distribution question: **who gets practical access to those systems, on what terms, and with what supporting institutions?**

That question is becoming increasingly explicit in official access architecture.
The European Commission’s Apply AI Strategy says the European Digital Innovation Hubs have been transformed into **Experience Centres for AI** that serve as **access points** to the EU AI innovation ecosystem, including AI Factories, testing and experimentation facilities, and regulatory sandboxes [S719].
The Commission’s AI Factories programme says these factories provide access to computing time and support services, are open to users including **public authorities**, and will prioritize access for AI startups and SMEs [S720].
This is a strong signal that advanced model and compute capacity is being treated not as something every organization simply has, but as something many actors must reach through shared gateways.

The infrastructure itself is plainly not trivial.
EuroHPC’s supercomputing estate includes systems explicitly used for **AI** and **digital twins**, while some hosting sites advertise pay-per-use conditions for access [S721].
Destination Earth’s digital twins provide interactive access to models, data, and workflows, and allow impact-sector applications to run **within the digital twin workflow** rather than only on exported outputs [S722].
These are maintained, high-value environments. The organizations that can use them directly are not in the same position as organizations that can only consume translated summaries downstream.

The territorial side of the gap is also visible.
The European Commission’s 2025 Cities Agenda says large urban centres often act as early adopters of digital tools, while less connected territories need support, connectivity, skills, and access to interoperable data ecosystems; it also says digital twins can enhance resilience and planning capacity [S723].
The OECD’s 2025 paper on emerging divides in AI says adoption is accelerating in a **multi-speed** pattern, with leaders pulling away and stronger uptake among capital regions, large firms, and knowledge-intensive services [S724].
Even before we isolate digital twins or scenario infrastructures specifically, the wider capability pattern is already clear: access to advanced computational systems is not diffusing evenly.

Regulatory science shows the same logic in a more specialized form.
FDA’s Model-Informed Drug Development Paired Meeting Program offers selected sponsors additional interaction with agency staff on MIDD approaches [S700].
That matters here because it suggests that counterfactual advantage is not only about raw compute. It is also about access to the expert pathways, review channels, and interpretive support that make model use actionable.

Taken together, these signals support a broader speculation: **as maintained counterfactual systems become ordinary inputs to planning, supervision, and operations, unequal access to those systems may behave like a compounding institutional inequality — not merely a technology gap, but a governance gap**.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Shared access points become strategic policy objects

AI factories, testing facilities, public modelling portals, sector-specific digital-twin platforms, and expert-access programmes may become as politically important as the models themselves because they decide which actors can actually participate in the counterfactual layer.

### 2. Translation dependence deepens

Many ministries, municipalities, hospitals, utilities, and mid-sized firms may not use raw scenario worlds or twin environments directly.
They may depend on vendors, labs, consultants, or shared centres that translate official baselines into local operating choices.

### 3. Baseline politics becomes asymmetric

Institutions that can rerun or challenge official models may be able to contest scenario updates, benchmark choices, and stress-test assumptions.
Institutions that cannot may only react after baseline changes have already altered compliance burdens, investment screens, or planning defaults.

### 4. Counterfactual-rich organizations reorganize faster

Organizations with standing access to credible model environments may iterate policies, siting choices, resilience plans, or operational rules more quickly than peers who still depend on lagging statistics or outsourced one-off reports.

### 5. Public support layers become a live design problem

If unequal access matters enough, some sectors may need public-service counterparts: shared simulation centres, municipal model cooperatives, subsidized compute access, model-extension services, or baseline-translation bureaus.

### 6. Vendor dependence becomes a governance variable

The most important asymmetry may not be simple exclusion from compute.
It may be being forced to govern through proprietary intermediaries that control the workflow, assumptions, update cadence, and cost of re-running counterfactual analysis.

### 7. Counterfactual literacy stratifies institutions internally

Inside organizations, influence may shift toward teams that can formulate interventions in simulation-ready ways, interpret uncertainty correctly, and negotiate with model builders or validators.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Shared public tools, open benchmark worlds, and cheap inference or simulation services diffuse fast enough that access gaps narrow rather than compound.
- The main constraint remains political bargaining, legal authority, or data quality, such that uneven access to counterfactual tools has limited practical effect.
- Sector-specific templates and regulatory standardization make model use routine enough that specialist access intermediaries lose their leverage.
- Institutions mostly consume official scenario outputs as fixed defaults, with little value from rerunning, translating, or challenging the underlying models.
- Human judgment, field observation, and ordinary administrative practice remain far more decisive than maintained counterfactual tooling in most domains.

## Research queue

- Which domains first show the clearest split between **counterfactual-rich** and **counterfactual-poor** institutions: cities, utilities, climate adaptation, health regulation, insurers, or infrastructure planners?
- What is the minimum viable access stack in practice: compute, data, workflows, validators, translators, or regulatory dialogue?
- Which public intermediaries emerge first: AI-factory access programmes, municipal digital-twin hubs, sector-wide modelling cooperatives, or standards-backed translation bureaus?
- Where does the sharpest dependence on outside translators appear: smaller cities, regional banks, public hospitals, infrastructure operators, or lower-capacity regulators?
- When do institutions begin demanding rights not only to scenario outputs, but to reruns, local parameterization, challenge procedures, or shared access to the underlying workflow?
