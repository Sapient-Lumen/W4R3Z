# Human-review capacity becomes a critical compliance bottleneck

## Core claim

Many regimes now gesture toward human review, complaint handling, reasoned decisions, explanation, or oversight. But a right to human review is not operational unless someone with enough training, language access, context, authority, and time can actually reverse, restrict, correct, explain, or escalate the decision.

The speculative claim: **human-review capacity becomes a critical compliance bottleneck**. Reviewer rosters, training records, queue capacity, reviewer independence, override authority, language coverage, escalation liveness, and review-quality telemetry become compliance artifacts. Human review becomes a supply chain.

## Why this belongs in the cube

The archive has already promoted human fallback, appeal workflows, AI authority logs, complaint telemetry, and remedy budgets. But these can still sound like interface features. This dossier reframes them as capacity infrastructure.

The DSA requires platforms to offer contestation routes and promptly review certain decisions [S1541, S1542]. The AI Act timeline and high-risk system obligations elevate human oversight, explanation, monitoring, and suspension duties in regulated contexts [S1548, S1551]. These rules will not be satisfied by a button if the review function is underpowered.

The bottleneck is not “human in the loop.” It is **qualified human with authority in the loop**.

## Capacity dimensions

A credible human-review system needs:

- queue capacity by case type and language;
- reviewer training and domain expertise;
- independence from the original automated or moderation decision;
- authority to reverse, pause, restrict, escalate, or request evidence;
- access to the explanation packet and source records;
- conflict-of-interest rules;
- escalation paths to legal, safety, engineering, vendor, or regulator teams;
- quality assurance and sampled second review;
- metrics on overturned decisions, unresolved cases, and repeated error classes.

## Speculative consequences

### 1. Review capacity becomes a procurement question

Buyers will ask vendors not merely whether appeals exist, but what review capacity supports them: staffing ratios, SLA compliance, reviewer authority, and language coverage.

### 2. Outsourced review becomes an assurance market

Specialized review providers will handle AI explanations, content moderation disputes, product-passport conflicts, supplier score appeals, and identity-match challenges. Their independence and incentives will become contested.

### 3. Human review generates its own evidence trail

Every reviewer action will create logs: what was seen, what was not available, what rules were applied, whether automation was overridden, and why escalation was or was not triggered.

### 4. Rubber-stamp review becomes a named failure mode

Institutions may meet formal review requirements while reviewers lack authority, time, context, or incentives to disagree with the automated system. That will become a supervisory finding.

## Abuse and capture

Human review can become theatre. Firms can underfund it, route hard cases to contractors, hide behind queues, use reviewers as liability shields, or measure productivity in ways that punish reversals. Conversely, human reviewers can be captured, inconsistent, biased, threatened, or overwhelmed by coordinated campaigns.

The remedy lifecycle should therefore track not only outcomes but review quality and capacity.

## Falsifiers

The thesis weakens if regulators accept minimal human-contact workflows. It strengthens if audits, enforcement actions, procurement clauses, or civil-society reports begin focusing on reviewer staffing, expertise, reversal authority, or queue backlogs.
