# Informal-market refuges form around over-legible infrastructure

## Core claim

When proof infrastructure becomes too expensive, invasive, brittle, or hard to appeal, some actors will not simply become compliant. They will move to less formal channels.

That is the speculative claim: **informal-market refuges form around over-legible infrastructure**. Product passports, EUDR due-diligence statements, digital identity checks, AI documentation packets, repair authorization records, procurement validation reports, and eligibility credentials may all be defensible in substance. But if the evidence burden is too high for small suppliers, informal workers, low-connectivity regions, older people, migrants, low-capacity agencies, or small model providers, market activity will bifurcate into proof-rich formal channels and proof-poor informal refuges.

The archive's legibility thesis therefore needs a distributive correction: more legibility can increase trust in the formal system while pushing excluded actors into less visible systems.

## Why this belongs in the archive

World Bank ID4D data continues to show large gaps in official and digitally verifiable identity [S1510]. The ID4D grievance-redress guide lists ordinary failure modes such as inability to enroll, biographic errors, and authentication failures [S1511]. Those are not edge cases if proof-based access becomes routine.

Supply-chain proof regimes create a parallel issue. FAO's transparent supply-chain work emphasizes the importance of geospatial data and public tools for deforestation-free supply chains [S1512]. Public tools aimed at keeping small farmers in compliant supply chains show that evidence capacity is itself a market-access problem, not a mere implementation detail [S1513]. The EU deforestation regulation and its information system make due-diligence statements and geolocation-based proof central to market access [S1485][S1486].

The archive already promoted small-supplier evidence brokers. This dossier adds the darker mirror: when evidence brokers, public tools, and appeal paths are insufficient, informal refuges become rational.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Formal compliance may shrink supplier diversity

Large buyers may reduce sourcing complexity by working only with proof-ready suppliers, even if smaller excluded suppliers are substantively compliant. The informal refuge may be created not by illegality but by simplified risk management.

### 2. Proof brokers become inclusion infrastructure or toll collectors

An evidence broker can keep small actors in formal channels. It can also become a rent-extracting gatekeeper. The difference depends on public profiles, shared validators, transparent fees, appeal rights, and cooperative ownership models.

### 3. Informal channels may be safer for privacy but worse for safety

A consumer, farmer, repairer, or supplier may avoid over-disclosure by staying outside formal platforms. But that also means weaker recall propagation, less warranty protection, fewer enforceable records, and lower access to formal finance or insurance.

### 4. Enforcement can create proof laundering

If formal proof is required but hard to produce, intermediaries will offer to “solve” the paperwork. Some will genuinely package evidence. Others will launder weak, copied, borrowed, or forged proof objects.

### 5. Fallback channels become political

Governments may need low-tech, offline, community-attested, paper-backed, or cooperative proof routes. Those routes will be criticized as loopholes by incumbents and defended as inclusion infrastructure by excluded groups.

## How this gets abused

- Bad actors hide in informal channels to avoid safety, labor, tax, environmental, or recall rules.
- Formal buyers claim small actors are “too risky” when the real barrier is evidence convenience.
- Brokers charge monopoly rents for proof packaging.
- Platforms use compliance rhetoric to exclude competitors.
- Public agencies over-automate eligibility and underfund grievance redress.
- Privacy objections are used as cover for traceability evasion.

## Who pays, who saves, who captures

Small actors pay in time, fees, disclosure, and dependency. Large buyers save by standardizing intake. Governments pay if they want public tools, cooperative evidence utilities, and grievance systems. Private evidence brokers capture value when compliance is mandatory but tooling is fragmented. Informal networks capture value when formal proof becomes too burdensome.

## Near misses

- Informality is not always evasion. It can be a fallback when formal proof systems are inaccessible.
- Compliance burden is not always bad. The question is whether the burden tracks risk and whether compliant small actors can meet it.
- Public tooling is not enough if buyers still demand proprietary extra proof.
- Digital ID expansion is not inclusion if redress and offline alternatives are weak.

## Falsifiers

The thesis weakens if public proof tools become cheap and widely trusted; if buyers accept cooperative or government-backed profiles; if grievance systems handle errors quickly; if proof requirements scale by risk and actor size; or if formal markets absorb small suppliers without significant concentration.

## Signals to watch

- Supplier consolidation after new traceability or product-passport mandates.
- Growth of informal repair, resale, commodity, or service channels outside proof-heavy platforms.
- Public funding for evidence utilities and shared validators.
- Buyer complaints about “insufficient documentation” despite substantive compliance.
- Civil-society campaigns around proof-based exclusion.
