# Dossier: Conformity Assessment Becomes Strategic Infrastructure

## Core claim

The important shift is not just that more products face rules, that standards proliferate, or that regulators ask for more paperwork. It is that **the institutions that prove things meet rules are themselves becoming strategic infrastructure**.

The stronger version of the thesis is that **many markets stop turning mainly on invention, manufacturing, or distribution and start turning on whether a recognized body can test, inspect, certify, or prequalify a product fast enough and in the right jurisdictional form**. In that world, the relevant question is no longer only *does this product meet the rule?* It becomes *which recognised pathway proves that, who is allowed to run it, how long it takes, whether the result is portable across borders, and what happens when assessment capacity becomes scarce?*

That sounds bureaucratic until the layer thickens. Once conformity assessment becomes infrastructural, test labs, notified bodies, certification schemes, accreditation systems, quality-management evidence packs, and mutual-recognition arrangements begin to shape market access, public procurement, product roadmaps, shortage risk, and industrial geography.

## Why this belongs in the archive

The archive already has dossiers on governed object identity and proof of place. The missing layer was **proof of rule-satisfaction**: the operational fact that more products, systems, and supply chains now need an accepted assessment path before they can be marketed, procured, imported, or trusted.

The WTO treats this layer as foundational. Its Technical Barriers to Trade framework says technical regulations, standards, and conformity assessment procedures are part of the basic architecture of world trade, and that these procedures matter enough to be governed multilaterally so they do not become unnecessary obstacles [S592]. The WTO’s 2024 TBT work then underlined that conformity assessment procedures are a key area of committee work, are used to determine whether products fulfil technical requirements, and are “vital for trust in trade” [S593]. That language matters because it frames testing, inspection, certification, and verification not as back-office formalities but as trust infrastructure.

The European Union is making the same layer explicit sector by sector. The Commission’s medical-devices page says notified bodies are organisations designated by Member States to assess the conformity of certain products before they are placed on the market, and that they perform those tasks whenever third-party intervention is required [S594]. The Commission’s 2025 medical-devices simplification package then says it is trying to make the framework easier, faster, and more effective while digitalising procedures [S595]. That is a revealing pattern: once conformity assessment throughput becomes consequential enough, governments start redesigning the rules around the proving apparatus itself rather than only the product rules.

AI extends the same logic into software and decision systems. The Commission’s 2026 AI Act FAQ says providers of high-risk AI systems must undergo a conformity assessment before placing a system on the EU market or putting it into service, and that in some cases the assessment must be carried out by an independent conformity assessment body [S596]. It also says providers who follow harmonised standards benefit from a presumption of conformity [S596]. This is an important threshold crossing. Compliance in AI is not only about model behavior or documentation; it increasingly depends on recognised standards pathways and, in some cases, third-party assurance capacity.

Cybersecurity is building the same service layer. The Commission’s Cyber Resilience Act conformity-assessment guidance says some important products require notified-body participation when harmonised standards are not used, and some critical products require mandatory third-party conformity assessments in any case [S597]. ENISA’s certification infrastructure then makes the coordination problem concrete: it maintains information on EU cybersecurity certification conformity-assessment bodies notified by national authorities, and its EUCC scheme provides an EU-wide assessment process for certifying ICT products [S598][S599]. This shows certification capacity becoming a live operating layer for software and hardware market access rather than a niche specialist concern.

Global health procurement has long run on a similar logic, and that makes the pattern easier to see. WHO says prequalification exists to ensure key health products meet global standards of quality, safety, and efficacy [S600]. Its procurement guidance for medicines says that after WHO approval, a finished pharmaceutical product is deemed prequalified and becomes eligible to participate in UN procurement tenders [S601]. That is the core infrastructural move in plain form: assessment status is not just a quality signal but an eligibility switch for large purchasing systems.

The United States is applying the same logic at the border. FDA says certifications under its Accredited Third-Party Certification Program can establish eligibility for the Voluntary Qualified Importer Program and, in certain circumstances, can be required to prevent potentially harmful food from entering the country [S602]. FDA’s import-certification guidance adds that import certification can become a condition of admission and is designed to maintain market access for compliant products while responding to recurring or systemic risks [S603]. Once that logic spreads, products increasingly move not only with invoices and shipping documents, but with accepted assurance pathways.

Taken together, these sources suggest a real shift: **societies are beginning to discover that the bottleneck is often not whether a thing can be built, but whether it can be proved compliant by an institution that other institutions accept**.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Certification capacity becomes a hidden industrial bottleneck

In more sectors, delays at notified bodies, accredited labs, or scheme operators may start behaving like factory bottlenecks, even when design and manufacturing capacity exist.

### 2. Standards interpretation becomes competitive infrastructure

Firms with strong internal regulatory engineering, better test evidence, or earlier access to recognised schemes may gain durable advantage even without better products in any obvious consumer sense.

### 3. Mutual recognition becomes industrial policy

Governments may increasingly compete not only through subsidies and tariffs, but through whose certificates, test reports, and accredited labs are accepted where.

### 4. Evidence packs become reusable capital

Technical documentation, quality-management systems, traceability records, audit trails, and test artifacts may start behaving like reusable compliance assets that can be re-applied across jurisdictions, procurement systems, and product generations.

### 5. Product roadmaps become recertification roadmaps

For regulated software, devices, industrial components, and food systems, release cycles may increasingly be shaped by assessment windows, surveillance obligations, and the cost of proving that a modification still fits the approved path.

### 6. Public procurement and market access fuse

Prequalification lists, approved-vendor schemes, and recognised certificates may increasingly blur the line between “safe enough to buy,” “legal to import,” and “eligible for public systems.”

### 7. Small firms get squeezed by assurance overhead

Where conformity regimes thicken, smaller firms may struggle less with invention than with dossier assembly, testing costs, surveillance audits, and the need to navigate multiple overlapping schemes.

### 8. Certifiers and labs become geopolitical actors

Accreditation bodies, notified bodies, and scheme owners may start acting as alliance infrastructure, especially where trusted supply, cyber assurance, medical access, or strategic procurement depend on recognised proofs rather than local claims alone.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Self-attestation remains dominant in most important sectors, with third-party pathways staying marginal.
- Conformity assessment capacity expands fast enough that it rarely constrains time to market, procurement access, or cross-border trade.
- Mutual recognition becomes broad and low-friction enough that assessment location stops mattering strategically.
- Major new regimes in AI, cyber, food, or health retreat from recognised assessment pathways because they prove too slow, expensive, or politically brittle.

## What to watch next

- Whether governments start subsidising or fast-tracking testing labs, notified bodies, accreditation, or conformity-assessment digital infrastructure.
- Whether medical-device, AI, cyber, food, or energy regimes publish more explicit metrics on certification backlogs and assessment capacity.
- Whether firms begin marketing “pre-certified” modules, reusable evidence packs, or certification-as-a-service as a mainstream advantage.
- Whether mutual-recognition negotiations around labs, schemes, and certificates become more visible in trade diplomacy and industrial strategy.
- Whether product updates, software patches, or supply substitutions increasingly trigger questions about recertification burden rather than engineering feasibility alone.
