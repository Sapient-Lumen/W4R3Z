# Dossier: Queue Governance Becomes Infrastructure

## Core claim

The important shift is not merely that systems face congestion, scarcity, or excess demand.  
It is that **more sectors are beginning to formalize how scarce access is sequenced over time**.

The stronger version of the thesis is that **queue governance becomes infrastructure**: not just a temporary administrative headache, but a standing operating layer spanning grid interconnection, airport slots, rail capacity, hospital treatment pathways, and immigrant-visa scheduling.

In that world, the practical question is no longer only *is there enough capacity?*  
It becomes *what list am I on, what timestamp or readiness test governs my place, who can see or challenge it, what causes forfeiture, and which institution is allowed to reorder the line?*

## Why this belongs in the archive

The archive already had dossiers on permitting, priority ladders, portability, reachability, and reconstructability.  
What it still lacked was the layer that sits across many of them: **formalized sequencing under ordinary scarcity**.

Electricity regulation makes that layer unusually explicit. FERC says interconnection queues have produced severe delays and backlogs across the country and that Order No. 2023 reforms existing processes to reduce those backlogs, improve certainty, and ensure access for new technologies [S675]. The same explainer notes that the traditional serial study process was a major source of delay and that the rule shifts transmission providers toward a first-ready, first-served cluster-study approach [S675]. That is a strong signal that access to the grid is no longer governed only by engineering feasibility or capital. It is also governed by queue design.

Transport shows the same pattern in more visibly slot-based form. The FAA says it uses runway slots to limit scheduled air traffic at capacity-constrained airports and maintains formal schedule review and approval processes at several others [S676]. In Europe, the Commission says allocation of rail capacity — train paths needed to operate on the network — is an essential function that must be performed neutrally to ensure fair access [S677]. In November 2025, the Commission then welcomed political agreement on new rail-capacity rules, saying EU rail tracks are increasingly congested and that current capacity management is annual, national, and manual, which does not favor cross-border traffic [S678]. This matters because it shows that mobility systems increasingly depend on administered sequencing layers rather than on unconstrained access.

Healthcare extends the thesis beyond transport and infrastructure. NHS England says the NHS Constitution gives patients a right not to wait longer than 18 weeks from GP referral to treatment [S679]. Its January 2026 RTT publication says the median waiting time for incomplete pathways was 13.6 weeks and the 92nd percentile was 40.3 weeks [S680]. That means waiting is not just a byproduct of capacity pressure. It is a measured and governed surface with rights, clocks, standards, and accountability.

Migration and border systems show the same structure in legal-administrative form. The State Department’s April 2026 Visa Bulletin says immigrant-visa numbers are allocated, to the extent possible, in chronological order of reported priority dates within numerical limits, and that oversubscribed categories are controlled by final action dates [S681]. The Department’s IV Scheduling Status Tool adds a second queue layer: interviews at a particular embassy or consulate are scheduled based on when the case became documentarily complete, but only if a visa is available [S682]. In other words, queue governance is increasingly multi-stage. One institution controls category-level numerical access while another governs post-level appointment flow.

Taken together, these signals support a broader speculation: **as scarce capacity becomes more instrumented, institutions will increasingly compete and conflict over queue architecture itself**. The hidden chokepoints are likely to be readiness tests, timestamp rules, batching logic, slot-return requirements, anti-hoarding rules, appeal routes, transparency dashboards, and the legal status of place in line rather than capacity expansion alone.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Queue position becomes a politically visible asset

Access fights may increasingly turn on where someone sits in a sequence, not only on whether the underlying resource exists. Queue position may start to feel like a quasi-property interest even when law does not treat it that way.

### 2. Readiness filters replace simple first-come logic

More sectors may shift from raw chronological order toward “first-ready, first-served,” completeness thresholds, verified need, or usage-it-or-lose-it rules to suppress placeholders and speculative hoarding.

### 3. Transparency rights become a legitimacy surface

Public conflict may increasingly focus on whether people can see the queue, understand their status, know the governing rules, and challenge reordering decisions.

### 4. Scheduling intermediaries gain quiet leverage

Slot coordinators, queue software vendors, market operators, referral hubs, and booking platforms may become more important because they practically determine how scarcity is administered.

### 5. Multi-stage queues proliferate

More systems may split into eligibility queues, readiness queues, appointment queues, and final-allocation queues, making access depend on surviving several sequencing layers rather than one list.

### 6. Anti-hoarding and expiration rules become economically important

Institutions may increasingly use deposit rules, milestone deadlines, slot-return obligations, inactivity expiry, and evidence-of-readiness checks to prevent capacity from being frozen by speculative claims.

### 7. Queue reform becomes a substitute for capacity growth

Where building more capacity is slow or expensive, governments may increasingly promise better queue design, better sequencing data, and fairer slot allocation rather than only more infrastructure.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Capacity expansions remove queue pressure in the sectors that matter most, making sequencing design secondary.
- Dynamic pricing, auctions, or bilateral contracting displace queues in many major domains.
- Queue data remains too local, inconsistent, or politically invisible to become a durable policy surface.
- Most sectors continue treating waitlists as temporary backlog artifacts rather than as governed operating systems.
- AI-assisted scheduling reduces delay without making queue rules, appeals, or transparency much more salient.

## Research queue

- Which sectors move fastest from first-come sequencing to readiness-scored or usage-conditioned sequencing?
- Where does queue visibility become a legal or consumer-rights demand?
- Which queue attributes matter most in practice: timestamp, completeness, priority class, milestone progress, or local-capacity match?
- When do multi-stage queues create more friction than they solve?
- Which institutions gain power by operating the sequencing layer rather than the underlying service or capacity?
