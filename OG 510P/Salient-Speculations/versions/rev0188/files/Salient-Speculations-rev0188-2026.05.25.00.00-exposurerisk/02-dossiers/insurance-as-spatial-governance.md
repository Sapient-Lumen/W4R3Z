# Dossier: Insurance and Underwriting Become a Hidden System of Spatial Governance

## Core claim

As climate losses rise and risk pricing hardens, insurance will increasingly stop behaving like a downstream financial detail and start functioning like a **hidden planning system**. In many places, what gets built, financed, repaired, rented, or abandoned will be shaped less by formal prohibition than by whether a property, project, or jurisdiction remains **underwritable**.

## Why this belongs in the archive

The ordinary framing treats insurance as something that adjusts after risk has already been created. The more salient speculative frame is that insurance is becoming an **upstream allocator** of where people can live, what lenders will fund, and which adaptation pathways remain politically possible.

Swiss Re reports that global insured losses from natural catastrophes reached USD 137 billion in 2024, with 57% of economic losses uninsured, and projects insured losses to remain on a rising trend toward roughly USD 145 billion in 2025 [S24]. Munich Re likewise reports USD 108 billion in insured natural-disaster losses in 2025 and notes that 2025 joined a growing run of years with insured losses above USD 100 billion [S25]. NAIC’s 2026 Natural Catastrophe Risk Dashboard public report says most risk and insurance indicators show multi-year increases in metrics of concern including policyholder rate increases, non-renewals, residual markets, and insolvencies related to natural catastrophe causes [S30].

That pressure is no longer confined to insurance markets themselves. FHFA has elevated natural-disaster risk into housing-finance governance, including dedicated insurance symposia and a mortgage-loan natural-disaster dashboard [S26]. FEMA also notes that federally regulated or insured lenders require flood insurance for buildings in Special Flood Hazard Areas, showing how hazard maps and insurance obligations already operate as mortgage-gating mechanisms in part of the housing market [S31]. Freddie Mac finds that even modest homeowners-insurance cost increases matter at the margin, with materially higher burdens for lower-income borrowers and especially high burdens in several central U.S. states [S27]. The Minneapolis Fed reports multifamily owners in its 2025 survey saw premiums double from 2021 to 2024 on average, with three in five respondents raising rents to mitigate costs and some owners delaying or reconsidering affordable-housing development [S28]. In a 2025 speech, Federal Reserve Vice Chair for Supervision Michael Barr argued that when insurance markets cannot price risk appropriately, market signals cannot effectively influence development and adaptation in high-risk areas, contributing to a buildup of risk [S29].

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Underwriting becomes de facto zoning

Many places will remain legally buildable long after they cease to be comfortably insurable. The practical veto will often arrive through premium spikes, exclusions, deductibles, non-renewal, or lender requirements rather than through a dramatic statutory ban.

### 2. Residual markets become adaptation politics in disguise

As private coverage retracts or becomes costlier, FAIR plans, residual pools, public reinsurance, catastrophe funds, and other backstops may become central political battlegrounds. The real question will often be whether a society chooses to socialize settlement risk rather than whether it admits it is doing so.

### 3. Building-code compliance and hardening become passports to capital

Retrofitting, roof standards, defensible-space rules, drainage upgrades, and parcel-level mitigation may increasingly determine access not just to coverage but to credit and acceptable carrying costs. This would make resilience less a moral preference than an eligibility regime.

### 4. Retreat is laundered through portfolio language before it is named openly

In many jurisdictions, explicit “managed retreat” will remain politically toxic. But portfolios may still be rebalanced through underwriting decisions, loan terms, reserve requirements, infrastructure triage, and selective public backstops. Retreat may therefore first appear in the language of exposure management, not in the language of migration.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Public subsidies, residual markets, or federal backstops keep coverage broadly available for much longer than expected.
- Adaptation and hardening technologies reduce loss severity fast enough to stabilize underwriting.
- Reinsurance capacity expands and loss experience cools enough to mute pressure on primary markets.
- Housing demand in exposed areas remains so strong that premium increases fail to meaningfully change settlement behavior.
- Political intervention caps or delays risk-based pricing so thoroughly that insurance remains a lagging rather than leading signal.

## Research queue

- Which thresholds matter most in practice: non-renewal, deductibles, exclusions, lender overlays, or premium-to-income ratios?
- Which public backstops are acting as temporary bridges versus long-run settlement subsidies?
- How quickly do mitigation investments translate into better underwriting outcomes at parcel, neighborhood, and municipal scales?
- In which markets does underwriting begin to move before home prices and migration do?
- When does “insurability” become an explicit criterion in infrastructure, housing, and bond-market decisions?
