# Dossier: Shared Counterfactual Utilities Become a New Public-Sector Support Layer

## Core claim

Once shared scenario baselines spread, access to maintained model environments remains uneven, translation becomes specialized, and revalidation becomes recurrent, many institutions still cannot sustain their own counterfactual stack.
The stronger thesis is that **shared counterfactual utilities become a new public-sector support layer**.

A growing number of states, consortia, and quasi-public operators may respond by building or underwriting shared facilities that provide some combination of model access, scenario reruns, user support, translation help, validation guidance, co-design, training, and update services.
These need not look identical. They may take the form of climate-service systems, scientific user facilities, AI-factory ecosystems, digital-twin platforms, model-help desks, sector-extension services, or publicly backed simulation centres.

In that world, the practical question is no longer only *who owns the best model?*
It becomes *which institutions can claim supported, admissible access to maintained counterfactual capacity without having to build the whole stack alone?*

## Why this belongs in the archive

The archive already has dossiers on **simulation capacity becomes governance infrastructure**, **model credibility standards become a new operating choke point**, **shared scenario baselines become a contested political object**, **counterfactual access becomes an institutional inequality multiplier**, **revalidation windows become a standing operational burden**, and **baseline translation markets become strategic intermediaries** [S692–S733].
Those entries establish that consequential institutions are building counterfactual systems, converging around common baseline worlds, distributing access unevenly, and discovering that admissibility must be maintained and translated over time.
But they still leave one important institutional response underdescribed: **instead of accepting permanent inequality or pure vendor dependence, public and quasi-public bodies may build shared support layers that let weaker institutions borrow counterfactual capacity as a service**.

Climate services already show the structure clearly.
WMO’s Climate Services Information System says climate services are organized as a three-tier network of global, regional, and national providers, and that these entities generate, exchange, disseminate, and add value to climate information from global to local scales while facilitating two-way interaction with users [S730].
WMO’s 2025 dashboard then shows why such a support layer matters: only 14% of WMO Members were equipped to provide advanced climate services in 2024, and many still do not provide tailored climate projections and sector-specific products [S733].
This is strong evidence that decision-relevant counterfactual capacity is not naturally universal; it often has to be distributed through organized support systems.

Destination Earth makes that support-layer logic explicit at a higher-compute frontier.
The European Commission says DestinE’s digital twins will provide users with **tailored access** to high-quality knowledge for user-specific scenario development and evidence-based decision-making [S734].
The Climate Change Adaptation Digital Twin says first prototype data are already available for users with upgraded access, that the system will support bespoke on-demand “what-if” simulations for selected key stakeholders, and that user needs are being built into simulation design and outputs [S735].
ECMWF’s 2026 phase-three announcement then says the next phase will focus on **operating and further evolving** the digital twins and digital-twin engine [S736].
Taken together, these sources show a public-backed move from one-off modelling toward maintained, shared, supported counterfactual service layers.

The EU’s AI-access layer points in the same direction.
The Commission says AI Factories provide access to computing time and support services, and that they are open to European users from industry, research, academia, and public authorities [S720].
EuroHPC adds that public-sector and industry users can benefit from EuroHPC supercomputers through access calls no matter where in Europe they are located, while AI Factories and AI Factory Antennas offer free, customised support to SMEs and startups [S737].
That matters because it shows advanced model capacity being distributed not only by private markets, but through structured, publicly organized access and support channels.

The United States has parallel structures.
DOE’s Office of Science says its national scientific user facilities provide researchers with advanced tools including supercomputers [S738].
DOE’s supercomputing page says national-lab supercomputing facilities are available to researchers from industry and academia and that widespread access allows public investments in state-of-the-art technology to deliver maximum public benefit [S739].
DOE’s 2026 announcement on the Teton supercomputer adds that it can be accessed through the Nuclear Science User Facilities and that NSUF provides researchers access at no cost to a wide variety of resources [S740].
NSF’s NCAR infrastructure programme makes the same support-layer logic explicit by describing sophisticated computer models, supercomputing, extensive data sets, and research aircraft as facilities and services provided to the university community that are beyond the reach of any individual university [S741].

Taken together, these signals support a broader speculation: **as maintained counterfactual capacity becomes more important and more expensive to keep admissible, governments and consortia may increasingly treat shared access-and-support layers as a legitimate part of public capability, much as they already do with laboratories, weather services, metrology, and other scientific infrastructures**.
The bottleneck shifts again — from model ownership alone toward the design and governance of shared counterfactual utilities.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Simulation support starts to look like extension infrastructure

Some ministries, municipalities, hospitals, utilities, and mid-sized firms may increasingly rely on publicly backed centres that help them formulate questions, access model runs, interpret outputs, and maintain evidentiary packages instead of building elite in-house teams.

### 2. Access-call design becomes distributive politics

Once shared utilities matter, queue design, review criteria, sector prioritization, and appeal rights around access calls may quietly shape who gets to plan with credible counterfactuals in time to matter.

### 3. Support staffing becomes as important as raw compute

The scarce unit may often be co-design time, analyst attention, help-desk depth, or validation support rather than hardware alone.
Institutions may discover that a shared utility with weak support is much less equalizing than a smaller utility with strong human assistance.

### 4. Public and private layers stratify rather than substitute cleanly

Public utilities may provide baseline access and first-line support, while private intermediaries sell faster turnaround, domain specialization, litigation support, or bespoke evidence packs.
That could create a two-tier counterfactual economy even where public access exists.

### 5. Public-interest challenge capacity improves

Civil-society groups, smaller regulators, and under-resourced local governments may gain more ability to challenge official baselines or vendor claims if shared utilities give them enough access to rerun or inspect relevant scenarios.

### 6. Shared utilities become policy objects in their own right

Questions about who funds them, who is eligible, which sectors receive priority, how support is triaged, what gets published, and how disputes are handled may become live administrative politics rather than quiet technical operations.

### 7. Public capability is judged by counterfactual service quality

Over time, institutional competence may be judged less by whether every agency owns its own model stack and more by whether the surrounding public support layer can deliver timely, credible, domain-relevant counterfactual assistance when decisions are contested.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Most consequential institutions build and maintain their own model environments, leaving shared support layers peripheral.
- Private vendors become cheap and reliable enough that public or quasi-public support layers remain thin.
- AI tools compress the expertise burden so dramatically that co-design, translation, and validation support no longer stay scarce.
- Shared facilities remain research showcases with little effect on live administrative or regulatory decisions.
- Politics, data access, or legal authority remain far more constraining than access to supported counterfactual capacity.

## Research queue

- Which sectors first depend on shared utilities most strongly: climate adaptation, infrastructure planning, drug regulation, energy systems, water management, or insurance?
- What becomes the scarce unit in practice: compute allocation, user support, domain translation, validation help, or update management?
- How are access priorities set, challenged, or appealed when demand exceeds available support?
- Which parts of the support layer stay public, and which are rapidly privatized into higher-tier advisory markets?
- When do institutions start treating access to shared counterfactual utilities as a baseline administrative entitlement rather than an innovation programme?
