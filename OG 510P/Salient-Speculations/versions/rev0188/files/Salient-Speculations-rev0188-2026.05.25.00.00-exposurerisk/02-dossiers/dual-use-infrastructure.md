# Dossier: Dual-Use Infrastructure Becomes the Baseline

## Core claim

A growing share of the systems that look merely civilian or commercial in peacetime will increasingly be treated as **dual-use infrastructure by default**. Cables, cloud regions, launch capacity, satellite constellations, ports, energy interconnectors, logistics hubs, and certain data or sensing platforms will be financed, protected, regulated, and sometimes screened for ownership not only as economic assets, but as **security-relevant operating layers**.

The important shift is not just that militaries use civilian systems in emergencies. It is that governments and alliances are beginning to assume that core infrastructure must remain usable across **commerce, crisis, coercion, and conflict**. That assumption changes procurement, resilience standards, ownership scrutiny, and public-private integration.

## Why this belongs in the archive

The ordinary framing still separates “civil infrastructure,” “commercial technology,” and “national security.” The more salient speculative frame is that the separation is weakening across multiple sectors at once.

At the alliance level, NATO’s 2025 defence-investment plan explicitly includes up to 1.5% of GDP for defence- and security-related spending such as **critical infrastructure and network protection**, civil preparedness, and resilience [S60]. That is a direct signal that infrastructure protection is no longer peripheral to defence accounting. NATO’s growing treatment of undersea systems makes the point even more clearly: its Critical Undersea Infrastructure Network now convenes civilian authorities, military authorities, industry, and technical experts to protect cables and pipelines that underpin connectivity and energy security [S63].

At the regulatory level, the EU’s Critical Entities Resilience framework requires Member States to identify and support entities providing essential services across eleven sectors including energy, transport, banking, financial market infrastructure, health, drinking water, wastewater, digital infrastructure, public administration, space, and food [S61]. This is a broad civil infrastructure map being re-described in resilience and security language. The EU’s 2025 cable-security action plan then sharpens the pattern by treating both **submarine communication cables and submarine electricity cables** as strategic assets whose disruption can directly affect essential services, daily life, and energy security [S62].

At the operational level, commercial systems are being integrated into defence architectures more explicitly. NATO’s Commercial Space Strategy states that the Alliance needs better means to integrate commercial space solutions into training, exercises, and operations, and to build standards for military applications of commercial space solutions [S64]. The U.S. Space Force goes further still: its Commercial Space Strategy says it will better integrate commercial space solutions into the national security space architecture, prioritize hybrid architectures, and seek commercial support across mission areas including satellite communications, launch, space domain awareness, positioning, navigation, and timing [S65].

At the ownership and control level, foreign investment screening is increasingly tied to infrastructure security. CFIUS is expressly empowered to review certain foreign investments and real-estate transactions for national-security effects, and recent rulemaking has expanded coverage around military installations and mitigation procedures [S66]. The EU likewise frames its FDI screening system as a way to identify, assess, and mitigate risks to security or public order, with an upgraded regime now pushing toward wider harmonization and broader national screening capacity [S67]. Meanwhile, U.S. critical-infrastructure doctrine now spans sixteen sectors, and CISA explicitly treats even space-system resilience as part of infrastructure risk management [S68].

Taken together, these are not isolated hardening measures. They suggest a deeper shift: **the commercial backbone of modern society is being reclassified as strategic terrain**.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Infrastructure procurement becomes security procurement

Tenders for cables, cloud services, launch, ports, sensing, and grid equipment will increasingly be evaluated not only on price and performance, but on resilience, jurisdiction, trusted-vendor status, recovery capability, and wartime or crisis usability.

### 2. Ownership becomes an operational variable

Who owns, finances, hosts, or can remotely service an asset will matter more. Investment screening, beneficial ownership scrutiny, and vendor-origin politics will increasingly shape what gets built and where.

### 3. Commercial redundancy starts counting as deterrence

Proliferated commercial constellations, spare cable routes, reserve logistics paths, multi-cloud architectures, and distributed energy interconnections may increasingly be valued as strategic depth rather than as merely business continuity tools.

### 4. Civil operators become quasi-security actors

Cable operators, launch providers, cloud firms, ports, utilities, and sensor-network operators will increasingly be pulled into joint exercises, incident reporting regimes, threat information sharing, and resilience planning once reserved mainly for the state.

### 5. Hidden chokepoints get politically priced

Insurance, financing, export controls, allied procurement, and infrastructure subsidies may increasingly favor assets that can be defended, repaired, monitored, and politically trusted. This could create a premium for “allied-operable” infrastructure analogous to earlier premiums for compliance, quality, or legibility.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Geopolitical tensions ease enough that most infrastructure returns to a mainly commercial treatment.
- Public-private integration proves too slow, brittle, or legally constrained to become operationally meaningful.
- Governments choose mostly sovereign government-owned systems rather than hybrid civil-commercial architectures.
- Vendor origin and ownership remain secondary to cost in most procurement and financing decisions.
- Strategic hardening stays confined to a few exceptional domains such as satellites or undersea cables rather than spreading across the broader infrastructure stack.

## Research queue

- Which domains move first from “resilience language” to genuinely security-shaped procurement: cables, cloud, ports, energy interconnectors, or logistics hubs?
- Where does foreign-ownership scrutiny bite hardest in practice: land near bases, data infrastructure, energy assets, or transport nodes?
- Which forms of public-private integration actually work under pressure: exercises, information-sharing, pre-negotiated surge contracts, or shared standards?
- Does dual-use status produce more redundancy, or does it concentrate trusted infrastructure into fewer allied vendors?
- Which jurisdictions become preferred hosts because they can offer both commercial utility and credible strategic protection?
