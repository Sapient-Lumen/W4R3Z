# Dossier: Reachability Becomes Civic Infrastructure

## Core claim

The important shift is not simply that more of life runs through telecom networks, or that outages are annoying. It is that **reachability is starting to be treated as civic infrastructure**.

The stronger version of the thesis is that **the ability to warn people, route them to help, locate them when they call for help, and keep essential communications channels usable during disruption becomes an explicit governance responsibility rather than background telecom performance**. In that world, the relevant question is no longer just whether a country has internet access or mobile coverage on paper. It is whether people can actually be reached in time, in a usable format, through resilient channels, and whether they can reliably reach emergency and crisis services back.

That sounds narrower than it is. Once reachability becomes infrastructural, it runs through disaster warnings, 911 / 112 / 999 access, crisis lines, public-service callbacks, telecare, location routing, multilingual accessibility, backup communications, and the politics of who remains reachable when power, fibre, satellites, or ordinary market incentives fail.

## Why this belongs in the archive

The archive already has dossiers on preparedness, human fallback, payment continuity, timekeeping, plain language, and translation. The missing layer was **reachability itself**: the operational fact that institutions increasingly need not just digital systems, but dependable ways to get a message to the right person and dependable ways for that person to reach help back.

OECD material now states the problem in infrastructure language. Its 2025 work says communication networks have become critical infrastructure supporting commerce, healthcare, education, and public services, and that ensuring continuous and stable operation during crises has become a top policy priority [S539]. Its resilience report then frames robust communication networks as a policy problem involving redundancy, diversity, emergency communications, crisis simulations, business continuity, and collaboration across operators and emergency services [S539]. That is already a stronger posture than treating connectivity as a retail convenience.

The baseline is still uneven enough for the shift to matter. ITU’s *Facts and Figures 2025* says almost three-quarters of the world’s population are online, but 2.2 billion people remain offline, while mobile broadband coverage is nearly universal yet quality and affordability gaps persist [S540]. OECD’s 2025 report on connectivity divides sharpens the point: access to high-quality broadband at affordable prices is vital, but territorial gaps persist, and people in metropolitan regions still experience materially better connectivity than those farther away [S541]. The archive should notice the structural implication. A society can look nominally connected while still delivering highly unequal **practical reachability**.

Europe is increasingly treating this layer as strategic. The European Commission’s Digital Networks Act page says the proposal is meant to modernise the legal framework for connectivity and boost investment in advanced and resilient digital infrastructure because such infrastructure is fundamental for Europe’s economy and society [S542]. Its Q&A goes further, saying the framework is meant to foster fast, resilient, and advanced digital connectivity, contribute to preparedness, reduce dependencies, and tackle crisis risks including natural disasters and foreign interference in networks and radio signals [S543]. That is not the language of optional broadband improvement. It is the language of civil preparedness.

Public warning systems show the reachability shift in a more concrete form. The Federal Register notice implementing FCC multilingual Wireless Emergency Alert rules says participating mobile providers must support pre-scripted alert templates in English, the next thirteen most commonly spoken languages in the United States, and American Sign Language for eighteen emergency events [S544]. This matters because it turns what used to be a best-effort translation issue into a defined public-safety delivery obligation. A warning that cannot be understood is operationally close to a warning that never arrived.

The same pattern is now visible in crisis-service routing and emergency calling. FCC material says the Commission adopted additional rules in July 2025 requiring wireless providers to develop georouting solutions for 988 text messages so they can be routed to the closest local crisis center [S545]. FCC’s 2025 NG911 work also frames next-generation emergency networks around reliability, interoperability, and improved support for voice, text, data, and video communications, with improved access for people with disabilities [S546]. That is the archive-worthy threshold crossing: communications policy is being forced to care about not just network uptime, but whether the right plea for help reaches the right institution in a usable way.

The UK signal is equally direct. Ofcom’s October 2025 compliance programme says uninterrupted access to emergency organisations is a critically important function of a communications network and reminds providers that they must take all necessary measures to ensure emergency-call access and provide accurate and reliable caller-location information where technically feasible [S547]. Once caller location and uninterrupted emergency access become enforcement questions, reachability has stopped being a vague service-quality concept and become a regulated duty.

At the international level, warning dissemination is being built as a standing public layer. ITU’s 2025 emergency telecommunications review says thirty countries received support under Early Warnings for All, seven countries were assisted with National Emergency Telecommunication Plans, seven national cell-broadcast feasibility studies were completed, and a technical expert group on mobile early-warning systems was established [S548]. ITU’s NETP guidance says these plans extend beyond the ICT sector and include hazard mapping, operator and service-provider landscapes, service penetration, contingency planning, governance roles, and procedures for information sharing across governments, communities, and public-private organisations [S549]. UNDRR’s 2025 global status report then says that improvements in digital infrastructure, accessibility and affordability of mobile communication, and rollout of cell broadcast and location-based SMS will continue to transform the warning space [S550].

Taken together, these are not just telecom facts. They point toward a broader institutional shift: **states and regulators are beginning to treat practical reachability as a civic layer that must be designed, measured, and governed**.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Reachability gaps become governance failures rather than mere market gaps

Dead zones, unaffordable data, unstable voice service, and low-quality rural coverage may increasingly be treated as failures of public reach rather than merely disappointing telecom competition outcomes.

### 2. Caller-location quality becomes a frontline compliance surface

Emergency access may be judged less by whether a call technically connected and more by whether location, callback, and routing worked reliably enough for real response.

### 3. Multilingual and accessible alerting become baseline public-safety expectations

Language coverage, device compatibility, screen behavior, and disability access may increasingly move from advocacy requests into formal alerting requirements and procurement standards.

### 4. The stable contact point becomes quasi-identity infrastructure

Phone numbers, reachable devices, and durable callback channels may matter more as practical keys to crisis support, benefits contact, fraud response, care coordination, and account recovery.

### 5. Telecom resilience will be judged by social triage, not just headline uptime

A future outage will increasingly raise questions such as: who still received warnings, who could still call for help, which areas lost location accuracy, and which populations were effectively unreachable.

### 6. Emergency services, crisis lines, and telecom regulators converge operationally

The boundaries between communications regulation, public warning, crisis care, and civil protection may blur as they discover they depend on the same routing, location, accessibility, and continuity layers.

### 7. “Universal service” may get reinterpreted around practical reachability

The next generation of universal-service politics may focus less on nominal coverage and more on always-reachable emergency access, continuity under disaster, inclusive warning delivery, and the quality of last-mile contact.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Connectivity quality and affordability improve enough through ordinary market rollout that reachability does not need much new governance.
- App-based and platform-based channels displace phone-number and network dependence without creating comparable new exclusion bottlenecks.
- Emergency access, multilingual alerting, and crisis routing remain siloed specialist issues rather than converging into a broader reachability layer.
- Public warning and crisis-contact improvements remain too patchy, local, or advisory to reshape how states think about communications infrastructure.

## What to watch next

- Whether more regulators publish metrics or enforcement findings on emergency-call continuity, caller-location quality, and outage effects on crisis services.
- Whether multilingual and accessible alerting expands beyond a handful of jurisdictions into default national warning design.
- Whether 988, 911 / 112 / 999, public-warning systems, and telecom-outage policy start sharing more standards, reporting vocabularies, or operating assumptions.
- Whether National Emergency Telecommunication Plans and cell-broadcast programmes become ordinary preparedness infrastructure in more countries.
- Whether universal-service and broadband programmes begin to talk more about *reachable people* than about nominal premises passed.
