# Dossier: Forecasting Functions Reappear Inside Institutions

## Core claim

As more systems become harder to govern with backward-looking administration alone, institutions are beginning to build **anticipation functions** into ordinary operations. The speculative claim is not simply that governments do more long-range thinking. It is that forecasting, horizon scanning, scenario stress-testing, collaborative surveillance, and strategic intelligence start to behave like a **quiet administrative layer**.

Prediction markets may appear in some domains, but they are not the main story. The stronger pattern is that the underlying function — organized advance notice about plausible futures and weak signals — gets institutionalized under calmer names: foresight, risk intelligence, technology watch, scenario planning, collaborative surveillance, preparedness analytics, and policy stress-testing.

## Why this belongs in the archive

This archive already has dossiers on preparedness, epistemic inequality, biological observability, provenance, and hidden chokepoints. The missing layer was the **anticipatory bureaucracy** that tries to connect signals to action before crisis or scarcity becomes fully visible. Once volatility rises, institutions do not only need more data or more models. They need repeatable ways to decide what to watch, how to interpret weak signals, how to test policy under multiple futures, and where to embed that work in ordinary procedures.

OECD now treats anticipatory governance as an explicit capacity problem. Its 2025 guidance says governments face unprecedented pressures and that anticipatory governance — integrating strategic foresight, experimentation, and innovation — is essential, while also noting that the systematic use of anticipatory governance in government is **not widespread** and remains insufficiently connected to policy development and innovation [S170]. A companion OECD report says governments need more than reactive measures and presents efforts in Italy, Lithuania, and Malta to **build and institutionalise anticipatory capacity** inside governance systems [S171]. That is already more than abstract futures talk. It is institutional design.

The European Commission is building the same layer more overtly. Its Competence Centre on Foresight says it exists to provide future-oriented input, develop an anticipatory culture inside the Commission, and continuously experiment with methods and tools that make foresight useful for decision making [S172]. In March 2026, the Joint Research Centre described the Blue Book Foresight Hub as building foresight capacity across the Commission, with trainees contributing to Strategic Foresight Report work, intergenerational-fairness exercises, digital-sovereignty scenarios, polycrisis workshops, and ESPAS horizon-scanning activities [S173]. This is a sign that anticipatory practice is moving from a specialist craft toward a reproducible administrative competency.

The United Kingdom is doing something similar in a more procedural register. The Government Office for Science’s 2025 Futures Toolkit says traditional forecasting is insufficient when technology, society, and environment change in ways institutions do not expect, and it offers practical pathways for horizon scanning, scenario work, and policy stress-testing [S174]. That matters because it turns anticipation from an occasional expert exercise into something closer to a standard operating method.

Health systems make the same move under names that sound less like forecasting. WHO’s EIOS strategy says the initiative is meant to strengthen public-health intelligence and envisions EIOS as a **core part** of how such intelligence is generated and applied so decision-makers can act earlier and more effectively [S175]. ECDC’s 2025 future-challenges work says it ran a multi-method strategic foresight process to inform long-term planning, starting with horizon scanning for megatrends and drivers of change relevant to public health [S176]. EMA’s 2025–2028 Network Data Steering Group workplan says the network will horizon scan and monitor research and innovation projects to keep abreast of advances in data and tools for medicines regulation [S177]. In each case, the institution is not waiting for full certainty. It is formalising the upstream work of noticing, comparing, and preparing.

OECD’s 2025 primer on strategic intelligence for emerging-technology governance pushes the same logic into technology policy, arguing that bold anticipatory governance is essential and introducing strategic-intelligence tools as a policy instrument rather than a luxury [S178]. Its 2025 report on AI in strategic foresight adds that practitioners are already using AI to scan for signals of change, analyse large datasets, and support scenario development, while public-sector adoption is constrained by data security, reliability, and governance limits [S179]. The result is a strong speculative pattern: **the state, and eventually large firms and transnational institutions, may develop hidden forecasting layers before they openly develop prediction-market cultures**.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. More decisions acquire pre-decision intelligence stages

Expect more policy, procurement, and regulatory processes to include mandatory scanning, scenario notes, watchlists, or risk dashboards before money is committed or rules are issued.

### 2. Forecasting becomes bureaucratic rather than charismatic

The centre of gravity may move away from celebrity futurists and toward foresight cells, public-health intelligence teams, regulatory watch functions, and cross-agency stress-testing routines.

### 3. AI expands signal intake before it settles prediction quality

Institutions may first use AI to widen the search space, cluster signals, and accelerate scenario construction before they trust it with narrower high-stakes forecasts.

### 4. Quiet anticipatory capacity becomes a competence premium

States, regulators, and large firms that can ingest weak signals early and route them into real procedures may get more resilient, more governable, and more strategically agile than peers with similar raw resources.

### 5. Forecasting markets may survive mainly as components

Where prediction markets do spread, they may do so as bounded instruments nested inside larger anticipatory stacks — alongside expert elicitation, scenario work, and operational intelligence — rather than as the main public face of forecasting.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Foresight remains mostly ceremonial and fails to change actual budgeting, regulation, procurement, or preparedness decisions.
- Institutions create toolkits and hubs but do not retain staff, routines, or authority to make anticipatory work durable.
- AI-generated scanning floods organisations with low-value signals, causing the hidden forecasting layer to lose legitimacy.
- Crisis response continues to dominate because political systems reward reaction more than preparation.
- Prediction functions stay fragmented across intelligence, compliance, audit, and planning units without forming a recognisable administrative layer.

## Research queue

- Which anticipatory tools most often cross the line from workshop method to standing procedure?
- Which domains institutionalise hidden forecasting first: public health, emerging technology, defence, climate adaptation, or finance?
- What governance arrangements stop horizon scanning from degenerating into signal overload or theatre?
- When do organisations give anticipatory units budgetary or regulatory influence rather than advisory status alone?
- Do prediction markets become useful complements inside institutional stacks, or do other forms of structured forecasting crowd them out?
