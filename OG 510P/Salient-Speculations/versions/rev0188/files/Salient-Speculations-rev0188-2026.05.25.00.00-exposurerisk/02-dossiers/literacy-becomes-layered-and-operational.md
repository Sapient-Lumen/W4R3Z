# Dossier: General Literacy Expands into Model, Risk, Systems, and Proof Literacy

## Core claim

Foundational literacy and numeracy remain essential, but they cease to be socially and economically sufficient on their own. As more knowledge arrives through platform feeds, model outputs, dashboards, synthetic media, automated recommendations, and complex service systems, societies begin to treat literacy as a **layered competence stack**. That stack still includes reading, writing, and quantitative comprehension, but it increasingly adds media and source evaluation, model literacy, adaptive problem solving, risk comprehension, and the ability to decide when an answer, document, or output deserves trust.

The important shift is not that “everyone must become a programmer” or that schools suddenly abandon fundamentals. It is that the scarce layer moves toward **credibility judgement**, **AI and model calibration**, **adaptive reasoning in unfamiliar environments**, and **safe navigation of mediated systems**. In that world, the people and institutions that can distinguish evidence from noise, decide when to rely on a model, and reason through uncertain systems become disproportionately advantaged.

## Why this belongs in the archive

A weak framing says digital literacy matters. A stronger framing says modern societies are quietly redefining what counts as literacy at all.

That shift is now visible in official assessment and policy machinery. OECD’s PISA 2029 MAIL assessment defines Media and Artificial Intelligence Literacy as a set of competences required to engage effectively, ethically, and responsibly with digital content, media platforms, and AI systems, including evaluating the credibility, quality, and purpose of digital content [S130]. The same OECD work on future education frames student agency, well-being, and broader competencies as necessary for thriving in unfamiliar contexts rather than simply absorbing fixed instruction [S111]. UNESCO’s 2025 MIL issue-brief summary says recognition of media and information literacy is high but implementation remains fragmented, and explicitly treats MIL as a public-good capacity that should be embedded across education and society [S131].

The competence stack is also broadening in workforce and adult-skills policy. The World Bank’s 2025 digital-skills review synthesizes competence frameworks for citizens, students, teachers, and workers, emphasizing digital proficiency, information and data literacy, assessment tools, and targeted training as part of a larger skills landscape [S132]. OECD’s adult-skills work now treats adaptive problem solving as a distinct capability: the capacity to achieve goals in dynamic situations, search for information, and monitor one’s progress across changing environments [S133]. OECD reports that proficiency in adaptive problem solving is associated with higher labour-force participation and higher wages, and that roughly 29% of adults across participating OECD countries score at Level 1 or below on that scale [S133]. In parallel, OECD’s AI-skills note says most workers will not need specialised AI skills but will need general AI literacy, ethical awareness, and the ability to critically evaluate AI technologies as they use them [S135].

This widening competence demand does **not** make basic literacy obsolete. It makes basic literacy the floor under a taller structure. The World Bank still frames literacy, numeracy, and socio-emotional competencies as foundational for work and life [S101]. But once information abundance, AI assistance, and digital mediation become everyday conditions, foundational literacy alone stops being an adequate proxy for civic and operational competence.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Assessments begin to test calibration, not just recall

Education systems and employers may increasingly want evidence that people can evaluate sources, detect weak reasoning, compare model outputs, and decide when confidence is unwarranted.

### 2. A new form of hidden illiteracy appears among the credentialed

Some highly educated populations may read well and still perform poorly when asked to judge provenance, interpret uncertainty, or use AI systems without over-trusting them.

### 3. Libraries, public broadcasters, and civic-information institutions regain strategic importance

As credibility judgement becomes more valuable, institutions that teach source evaluation, evidence use, and public reasoning may regain status as democratic infrastructure rather than cultural extras.

### 4. Workplace sorting shifts toward mediated competence

Hiring and promotion may increasingly reward people who can work productively with models, identify when outputs are unsafe or low-quality, and operate effectively in dense information environments.

### 5. Curriculum conflict shifts from “should we use AI?” to “what kind of mind are we trying to produce?”

The real conflict may turn out to be less about devices or software and more about which literacies deserve scarce classroom time once societies accept that mediated judgement has become a practical necessity.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Foundational literacy remains so weak in most systems that the competence stack never broadens in practice.
- AI and media tools become so well-governed and reliable that source evaluation and calibration become less, not more, important for most people.
- Employers continue to reward narrow credentials or task-specific training much more than broader judgement and reasoning capacity.
- New literacy domains proliferate rhetorically but never harden into assessments, curricula, hiring standards, or public-service design.
- The competence bundle fragments into domain-specific training rather than cohering into a general social expectation.

## Research queue

- Which assessment bodies formalize the broadened competence stack first: school systems, licensing regimes, civil-service exams, or workplace certifications?
- Does “proof literacy” emerge more clearly in journalism, courts, procurement, and scientific communication than in schools?
- Which institutions are best at teaching calibration rather than confidence: libraries, schools, broadcasters, online platforms, or employers?
- How much of the new stack can be taught explicitly, and how much depends on slower habits of reading, discussion, and reflective practice?
- Which populations become newly excluded because they cannot navigate mediated systems safely even though they satisfy older definitions of literacy?
