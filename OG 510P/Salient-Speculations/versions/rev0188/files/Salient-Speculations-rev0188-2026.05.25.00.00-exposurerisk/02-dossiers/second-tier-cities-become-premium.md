# Dossier: Competent Second-Tier Cities Become Premium

## Core claim

As superstar metros grow more expensive, congested, climate-exposed, and institutionally brittle, a new urban premium may emerge around a different package: **adequate scale plus competent infrastructure**.

The key shift is not that megacities stop mattering. It is that a growing share of households, firms, lenders, and states begin valuing places that combine decent connectivity, workable housing costs, reliable services, climate adaptation, and administratively usable land. That points toward a wider class of **second-tier and intermediary cities**: places large enough to sustain labor markets and amenities, but small or mid-sized enough to remain governable, expandable, and serviceable.

In that world, the most desirable cities of the next era are not necessarily the largest, richest, or most globally famous. They are the ones that can still deliver **mobility, housing, water, power, drainage, permitting, and basic municipal competence without extreme friction**.

## Why this belongs in the archive

A weak framing says some people may leave expensive big cities. A stronger framing says the geography of desirability may start sorting around **competence** rather than glamour.

OECD’s current programme on intermediary cities says these cities can alleviate the negative effects of large-city agglomeration, including excessive housing costs and congestion, while offering accessible services, urban amenities, quality of life, and affordability [S123]. It also says they can attract people and firms and support a more balanced distribution of population and economic activity across space [S123]. That already implies a meaningful shift: some places become attractive not because they maximize agglomeration, but because they provide a better balance of scale, access, and livability.

OECD/UN-Habitat’s work on intermediary cities and climate change strengthens the point. It says intermediary cities with 50,000 to 1 million inhabitants are often overlooked despite accounting for a large share of the urban population in less developed regions, and that rapid urbanisation is straining their ability to provide services, finance climate action, and avoid weak governance and poor infrastructure [S124]. The importance of these cities lies exactly in that pressure: if they are built or upgraded well, they can absorb growth without simply reproducing the pathologies of larger metros.

The World Bank’s 2025 feature on Africa’s medium-sized cities makes the employment and corridor logic explicit. It says medium-sized cities are central to the continent’s employment future, that nearly half of Africa’s urban growth by 2050 will occur in such cities, and that policy choices there will determine whether urbanisation becomes a ladder or a trap [S125]. Its 2025 *Cities as Growth Hubs* framework similarly argues that one-size-fits-all urban growth strategies do not work and points to serviced land, infrastructure gaps, peri-urban disorder, and financing constraints as core practical barriers [S126]. This is exactly the archive’s terrain: urban value shifting toward the places that can solve mundane but decisive coordination problems.

Housing pressure makes the sorting mechanism more plausible. OECD’s urban housing work says housing in cities has become increasingly unaffordable and notes that, on average, prices per square metre in large metropolitan areas are twice those in small- or medium-sized cities [S127]. If big-city housing remains scarce while climate adaptation and service quality become more important, then competent second-tier cities acquire a structural opening.

This is not merely a conceptual possibility. Development institutions are already acting on it. In 2025, the World Bank approved projects aimed at climate-resilient infrastructure, land services, and flood-risk reduction in targeted urban and secondary cities in Cameroon and Gabon, explicitly to unlock their socio-economic potential as intermediate hubs [S128][S129]. That is a live institutional signal that secondary-city competence is being treated as an economic-development and resilience strategy, not just as a local-planning nicety.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Settlement sorting shifts from prestige toward reliability

Households may increasingly trade some headline metropolitan prestige for places where housing, schools, transport, and utilities still function with lower friction.

### 2. “Growth hubs” become a national strategy category

States and development banks may increasingly target node cities, corridor cities, and intermediary hubs rather than assuming that capitals and megacities alone should absorb growth.

### 3. Municipal competence becomes an investable asset

Investors, insurers, employers, and lenders may place more weight on drainage, land records, utility reliability, local planning speed, and fiscal discipline when judging where expansion is safest.

### 4. Climate adaptation quietly reorganizes urban hierarchy

Cities that can harden basic systems against flood, heat, drought, and service interruption may gain attractiveness relative to larger places whose scale magnifies fragility.

### 5. The winning places may be networks, not isolated towns

The most successful second-tier cities may be those plugged into trade corridors and inter-city systems, able to “borrow” metropolitan functions through rail, roads, logistics, and digital connectivity rather than reproducing every major-city function internally.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Large metropolitan areas remain so overwhelmingly productive and service-rich that affordability and competence advantages elsewhere never overcome agglomeration benefits.
- Many second-tier cities fail to improve land administration, housing supply, or infrastructure reliability, leaving them cheaper but not genuinely more desirable.
- Remote and hybrid work recede, reducing willingness to move away from major metros.
- Climate and insurance pressures spread so broadly that smaller cities lose their resilience advantage.
- National policy continues to starve intermediary cities of finance, technical capacity, and political attention.

## Research queue

- Which second-tier cities are already becoming magnets for firms because they combine corridor access with basic competence?
- Do insurers and lenders start pricing cities by drainage, heat mitigation, cadastral quality, and utility reliability?
- Which countries begin explicitly designing balanced urban systems rather than defaulting to capital-city concentration?
- Is the decisive variable affordability, climate resilience, connectivity, or local administrative competence?
