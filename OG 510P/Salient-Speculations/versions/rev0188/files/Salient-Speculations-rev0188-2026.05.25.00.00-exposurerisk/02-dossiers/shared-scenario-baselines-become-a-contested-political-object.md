# Dossier: Shared Scenario Baselines Become a Contested Political Object

## Core claim

Once simulation capacity spreads and credibility standards harden, a further bottleneck appears.
Institutions increasingly need not only **models** and not only **credible model evidence**, but **shared reference futures**: scenario sets, parameter baselines, and canonical worlds that many actors can use at once.

The stronger version of the thesis is that **shared scenario baselines become a contested political object**.
They stop being merely technical conveniences for analysts and become quiet constitutional instruments that shape what risks are visible, which investments look prudent, which policies appear feasible, and what counts as “reasonable planning” across sectors.

In that world, a deep struggle shifts upstream.
The practical question is no longer only *what should we do?*
It becomes *which baseline future counts as official enough that planning, stress testing, procurement, regulation, insurance, budgeting, or adaptation should take it seriously?*

## Why this belongs in the archive

The archive now has dossiers on **simulation capacity becomes governance infrastructure** and **model credibility standards become a new operating choke point** [S692–S711].
Those dossiers establish that institutions are building maintained counterfactual systems and formalizing admissibility criteria.
But they leave open the next coordination layer: **which scenario worlds are treated as common reference objects across institutions rather than as private assumptions inside separate models?**

That layer is becoming increasingly explicit.
The NGFS says its scenarios are designed to provide a **common reference framework** for analysing the impacts of climate change on the economy and financial sector [S712].
Its updated 2025 guide on climate scenario analysis says the field has matured enough for best practices in scenario design, data, and modelling to be codified and shared [S713].
That is a sign that scenario baselines are no longer just academic exercises. They are becoming reusable policy infrastructure.

European financial supervision shows what this looks like in practice.
The ECB says its analysis of the 2025 EU-wide stress test integrates the NGFS Nationally Determined Contributions scenario into the EBA’s adverse scenario, using the shared trajectories for energy prices, emissions, energy consumption, and macroeconomic variables to assess transition risk amplification [S714].
EIOPA’s one-off 2024 Fit-for-55 climate stress test likewise states that the exercise was conducted against **three scenarios** developed by the ESRB with ECB support [S715].
The important point is not only that climate risk is being analyzed. It is that multiple institutions are converging on common baseline worlds and then building supervisory action on top of them.

The energy-planning world shows the same pattern.
NREL says its Standard Scenarios are designed to capture a range of possible power-system futures [S716].
NREL’s Annual Technology Baseline then provides a **consistent set** of technology cost and performance data and modelling input assumptions for energy analysis, explicitly pairing those assumptions with Standard Scenarios as shared inputs for U.S. energy-sector work [S717].
This is exactly the archive’s structure: once many actors need comparable outputs, baseline assumptions become a governable layer in their own right.

The coastal-adaptation world points the same way.
NOAA’s Sea Level Rise Viewer says its five relative sea-level-rise scenarios are derived from the 2022 technical report, were developed by a U.S. interagency task force, and serve as input into the U.S. Global Change Research Program’s sustained assessment process and the Fifth National Climate Assessment [S718].
Here again, the scarce asset is not just a local model. It is an officially maintained, interagency reference future that other actors can reuse.

Taken together, these signals support a broader speculation: **as more consequential systems rely on shared scenario sets, politics moves upstream into baseline selection, parameter governance, version control, and the legitimacy of the futures that institutions are allowed to treat as normal enough to plan against**.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Politics moves upstream into baseline choice

In more domains, the sharpest disputes may not first be over the final policy decision.
They may be over which scenario families, discount assumptions, technology trajectories, hazard curves, or demand projections are allowed to count as the official reference case.

### 2. Baseline stewards become quiet governors

The institutions that publish, version, document, and retire shared scenarios may gain infrastructural power because they define the worlds other institutions are expected to take seriously.

### 3. Version changes become policy shocks

A baseline update may quietly reprice assets, alter resilience requirements, change stress-test outcomes, invalidate planning documents, or force revisions to local adaptation and procurement assumptions.

### 4. Translation markets gain leverage

Consultancies, labs, software vendors, and sectoral intermediaries that translate official scenarios into local operating models may become strategically important because most institutions will not consume raw baseline worlds directly.

### 5. Baseline pluralism becomes a governance problem

Institutions may discover that one official baseline is too brittle, while too many baselines destroy comparability.
A new design problem appears: how many sanctioned futures are enough for robust planning without collapsing coordination.

### 6. Representational conflict hardens

Communities, sectors, or jurisdictions may increasingly argue that official baselines omit their local hazards, social realities, adaptive capacity, or demand profiles.
Scenario disputes may start to look like fights over whose future was allowed into the model at all.

### 7. Appeals and adversarial review emerge

Once common baselines carry real consequences, some sectors may need consultation windows, appeal routes, challenge procedures, or independent review panels for scenario updates and parameter changes.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Most consequential sectors continue using highly fragmented private assumptions, with little pressure toward shared reference scenarios.
- Shared scenarios remain optional planning aids and do not materially discipline budgeting, supervision, procurement, insurance, or infrastructure decisions.
- Local empirical data dominates decision-making strongly enough that common scenario baselines remain secondary.
- Scenario frameworks stabilize as low-conflict technical standards and do not become meaningful sites of political or administrative contest.
- Cross-institution comparability turns out to matter less than this dossier expects, reducing pressure for shared baseline worlds.

## Research queue

- Which scenario sets become de facto mandatory first: climate-finance, energy-planning, coastal-adaptation, supply-security, or health-risk baselines?
- Who owns baseline versioning in practice: regulators, standards bodies, scientific consortia, infrastructure ministries, or vendors?
- When do official baselines acquire consultation, audit, or appeal procedures?
- How often do procurement terms, insurance rules, supervisory templates, or planning statutes begin naming specific scenario vintages or reference datasets?
- Which institutions can maintain translation capacity in-house, and which become dependent on outside interpreters of the official future?
