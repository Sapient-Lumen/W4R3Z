# Dossier: Legibility Becomes a Hidden Premium

## Core claim

The next major divide will not just be rich/poor, online/offline, or public/private. It will increasingly be **legible/illegible**. People, firms, parcels, products, and jurisdictions that can be **identified, mapped, verified, and integrated into machine-readable systems** will attract more capital, faster service delivery, and more administrative attention. Those that remain hard to identify or hard to integrate will increasingly face friction, exclusion, or higher operating costs.

## Why this belongs in the archive

The ordinary framing treats identification, registries, standards, and metadata as administrative details. The more salient speculative frame is that they are becoming a **selection layer** for development, trade, finance, and state capacity.

World Development Report 2025 argues that standards are the hidden infrastructure of modern economies, notes that nearly 90% of world trade is shaped by non-tariff measures mostly linked to standards, and treats standards, testing, certification, metrology, and accreditation as development capacity rather than technical ornament [S52]. The World Bank’s broader DPI materials likewise frame digital public infrastructure around reusable identity, payments, and data-exchange rails that can support service delivery across sectors [S59].

On the population side, the World Bank’s ID4D program says approximately 850 million people still lack official ID and 3.3 billion do not have access to digital ID for official online transactions [S53]. Its current dataset adds that as of 2024 only 89 countries offer at least one government-recognized digital identity credential for remote authentication, leaving over 2.8 billion people in countries without an option to prove official identity online [S54]. This means the global administrative map is still highly uneven precisely when more services, transfers, and verifications are moving online.

On the territorial side, the World Bank’s 2025 Colombia multipurpose cadaster financing treats updated cadastral information as an investment-climate tool: strengthening land tenure security, planning capacity, property taxation, infrastructure delivery, and access to credit through more accurate and standardized land information [S55]. That is a clean signal that land legibility is not just a legal nicety but a production variable.

On the firm and product side, W3C’s Verifiable Credentials 2.0 standard formalizes tamper-evident digital claims in issuer-holder-verifier ecosystems [S56]. The European Commission’s Digital Product Passport work under the ESPR treats machine-readable product data as a system for sustainability, durability, conformity, and enforcement across consumers, businesses, and public authorities [S57]. GLEIF reports that the active global LEI population grew to more than 2.93 million in 2025, with regulatory initiatives such as DORA helping drive adoption and extend standardized organizational identity beyond narrow capital-markets use cases [S58].

Taken together, these are not isolated bureaucratic reforms. They indicate that **identity, registry quality, standards alignment, and machine-readable evidence** are becoming a hidden premium on top of every other asset or capability.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Registries become investment infrastructure

Countries and cities will increasingly compete not only on tax rates, labor costs, or energy access, but on whether land, firms, utilities, and public records are sufficiently standardized and current to support credit, insurance, construction, and procurement at scale.

### 2. Informality is reframed as low observability

Many activities now described as “informal” may increasingly be treated less as moral or developmental shortcomings and more as observability failures. The key question becomes whether people, firms, and assets can be onboarded into systems of payment, taxation, benefit delivery, lending, and compliance.

### 3. Eligibility replaces universality in more domains

Where systems become machine-speed and document-dependent, access to services may increasingly hinge on possessing the right identifier, credential, parcel record, or product passport. Exclusion may therefore arrive less through explicit denial than through missingness, mismatch, or unverified status.

### 4. Standards strategy becomes territorial strategy

The ability to align with international standards, credential formats, reporting schemas, and product-data requirements may increasingly determine which jurisdictions can export, attract manufacturing, host supply-chain nodes, or integrate into green-transition markets.

### 5. Privacy conflict moves from surveillance rhetoric to service design

As legibility becomes more economically valuable, the central policy fight may shift from whether data systems exist to how they are governed: who can query them, what is mandatory, what is portable, how errors are corrected, and which forms of selective disclosure are technically possible.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Public and private systems remain fragmented enough that ad hoc documents and manual verification continue to work well at scale.
- Privacy, civil-liberties, or sovereignty objections keep registry integration shallow in most jurisdictions.
- Open standards lose to incompatible proprietary stacks, preventing a broad cross-system premium from emerging.
- Capital and state capacity continue to flow mainly on old proxies such as political connections, commodity rents, or brute market size rather than observability.
- Low-tech workarounds prove good enough that missing registries and weak identifiers do not materially block trade, finance, or service delivery.

## Research queue

- In which domains does the legibility premium show up first: credit, procurement, export compliance, insurance, social protection, or urban planning?
- Which registries matter most in practice: civil registration, business registries, cadaster, beneficial ownership, product passports, or utility data?
- When do machine-readable identifiers reduce friction, and when do they merely create new chokepoints?
- Which countries are building interoperable legibility stacks without over-centralizing power?
- Where does a “right to be legible” begin to emerge alongside rights to privacy, correction, and appeal?
