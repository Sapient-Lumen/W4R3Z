# Dossier: Re-review Trigger Grammars Become Procurement Language

## Core claim

Once conditioned places are governed through searchable restrictions, machine-readable rule objects, action clearances, replay-grade logs, preserved source snapshots, reliance-grade source attestations, and reliance-scope matrices, the next bottleneck is no longer simply **what a determination is good enough for**. It becomes **what events force that determination to be re-run, refreshed, escalated, or refused**.

The stronger version of the thesis is that the archive’s conditioned-place lane naturally hardens into a sixth object above the scope matrix: a **re-review trigger grammar**. Institutions can tolerate imperfect data longer than they can tolerate undefined freshness. A lender, buyer, regulator, insurer, contractor, landowner, or permitting office may accept that a given determination was valid for a named transaction class while still needing an explicit answer to a second question: *what has to happen before we stop trusting it?* Once that question becomes common, the scarce object is no longer just the determination or the scope table. It is the **codified trigger set** that says which combinations of elapsed time, source supersession, new information, altered conditions, changed activities, or rule changes require a fresh determination.

## Why this belongs in the archive

The archive already has a coherent lifecycle-governance sequence running from **institutional controls become a shadow zoning layer**, through **restriction-search infrastructure becomes routine conveyancing**, **machine-readable restriction objects become transaction middleware**, **action-clearance objects become field-work middleware**, **replay-grade clearance logs become insurance evidence**, **source-snapshot escrow becomes liability-tail infrastructure**, **reliance-grade source attestations become a service tier**, and **reliance-scope matrices become procurement exhibits**. That sequence explains how conditioned places remain governed, how work proceeds, how evidence is preserved, how another institution begins accepting a package as sufficient, and how that sufficiency is bounded by transaction class. It still leaves one practical bottleneck under-described: **when a previously sufficient package stops being safe to reuse**.

EPA’s all-appropriate-inquiries rule already contains an official trigger grammar. It requires all appropriate inquiries to be conducted within one year before acquisition, but separately requires several components — including interviews, lien searches, government-record reviews, visual inspections, and the environmental professional declaration — to be conducted or updated within 180 days before acquisition. EPA also says previously collected information must be updated to include relevant changes in site conditions and specialized knowledge [S1120]. That is already a formal rule saying that one determination does not simply remain valid until someone happens to feel uneasy; it has named time windows and named refresh obligations.

The Corps uses a different but equally explicit grammar. Corps materials state that an Approved Jurisdictional Determination is generally valid for five years, but that new information can warrant revision before that expiration date [S1121; S1122]. In other words, the object is not just scope-bound; it is also governed by a mixed trigger logic of elapsed time plus material new information.

NRCS does something similar in agricultural wetland governance. NRCS says a certified wetland determination stays in effect as long as the land remains in agricultural use, or until the producer requests review after an error or a natural event that altered the site’s hydrology [S1123]. NRCS separately says the producer’s AD-1026 compliance certification remains effective unless changes to the agricultural operation occur or new activities arise, such as bringing new land into production or installing new drainage structures [S1124]. That is a richer grammar still: use-state continuity, natural-event change, self-reported operational change, and new activity class can all become review triggers.

FEMA’s flood-governance stack shows the same pattern from the map side. FEMA’s Flood Map Service Center says it is the official public source for NFIP flood-hazard information, but warns that effective information downloaded or printed from the site may change or become superseded by new maps over time [S1125]. FEMA’s flood-hazard mapping updates fact sheet says flood hazards are dynamic, FEMA periodically updates flood maps, a new or revised effective map can change mapped hazard and building or insurance requirements, and users should check the current effective information. The same fact sheet also defines a revalidation letter as the instrument identifying previously issued LOMCs that remain valid after a revised FIRM [S1126]. That is not just a map product. It is a public example of a live supersession and revalidation grammar.

Taken together, these sources imply the next bottleneck above scope matrices: **re-review trigger grammars become procurement language**. Once institutions already know what a determination is good for, they still need to know when it must be rechecked, who carries that monitoring duty, what events count as supersession, and whether the proper response is a lightweight refresh, a human escalation, or a full re-determination. The scarce object stops being the one-time report. It becomes the **portable trigger grammar** attached to that report.

So this belongs in the archive because it names the layer above transaction-class sufficiency: **re-review trigger grammars become procurement language**. The actors who can standardize, monitor, and encode those grammars may increasingly decide which transactions keep moving without hidden staleness risk, which ones are paused for review, which liabilities remain insurable, and which decision systems become trusted enough to automate around.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. “Fresh enough” becomes a machine-checked field, not a vague judgment

Determination products may increasingly carry explicit freshness logic with named dates, event classes, source-watch obligations, and escalation thresholds rather than relying on open-ended professional caution.

### 2. Staleness splits into a richer liability taxonomy

Disputes may increasingly distinguish between elapsed-time staleness, superseded-source staleness, condition-change staleness, activity-change staleness, and rule-change staleness instead of treating “out of date” as one undifferentiated failure.

### 3. Trigger-detection services become valuable

A new layer of vendors, agencies, or internal controls may increasingly watch for map revisions, new information, changed parcel conditions, new drainage work, altered site use, or changed regulations and push re-review events into downstream workflows.

### 4. Contracts start buying monitoring duties, not just one-time determinations

Buyers may increasingly procure ongoing trigger surveillance, notice service levels, and rerun rights rather than only a report delivered on one date.

### 5. Replay systems preserve the trigger state as well as the decision state

Later incident or claim review may increasingly require proof not only of what determination existed, but also whether any relevant trigger had already fired before the action occurred.

### 6. Public systems remain current-state surfaces while private wrappers sell trigger clarity

Agencies may continue publishing dynamic source systems, while higher-margin private and quasi-public actors increasingly sell the trigger logic that turns those moving surfaces into operationally safe reuse rules.

### 7. Routine operations gain “tripwire constitutions”

Work-ordering, lending, redevelopment, acquisition, and compliance systems may increasingly encode explicit tripwires that force a pause, rerun, or higher-grade review before an old determination can be reused.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Institutions continue accepting old determinations with minimal concern about elapsed time, source supersession, or changed conditions.
- Official determination products converge on near-perpetual validity with few named review triggers.
- Refresh becomes so cheap and automatic that separate trigger grammars add little operational or contractual value.
- Most disputes keep centering on original source error rather than missed rerun conditions.
- Change events remain too bespoke for portable trigger taxonomies or procurement clauses to stabilize.

## Research queue

- What are the minimal fields of a re-review trigger grammar: validity window, source-watch set, event taxonomy, review threshold, escalation path, responsible monitor, and notice deadline?
- Which trigger classes stabilize first across domains: time expiry, superseding map or registry revision, new field observations, operational change, natural event, or legal-rule change?
- Who owns the monitoring duty in practice: agency, consultant, lender, insurer, buyer, seller, contractor, or an independent trigger-watcher?
- When is a lightweight refresh enough, and when do institutions insist on a full re-determination?
- Do procurement templates, insurer endorsements, lender overlays, or public forms become the main carriers of trigger grammar first?
