# Dossier: Intergenerational Bargaining Becomes a Primary Political Surface

## Core claim

As societies age, intergenerational tension is shifting from a background demographic fact into a **primary bargaining surface** in politics, fiscal policy, housing, and welfare-state design.

The strongest version of the claim is not that young and old will simply "fight more." It is that more domains will be interpreted through an age-structured distributive lens: who gets housing access first, who bears tax burdens, whose benefits are protected, who inherits late rather than early, who is expected to work longer, and which public systems are implicitly optimised for the preferences of larger older electorates.

The bottleneck therefore moves toward **fiscal room**, **benefit incidence**, **housing entry**, and the institutional ability to renegotiate age-structured claims without destroying legitimacy.

## Why this belongs in the archive

A weak framing says populations are ageing. A stronger framing says ageing changes the default bargaining map of the state.

OECD’s Employment Outlook 2025 is unusually explicit that demographic change is now tied to **intergenerational inequalities** rather than to ageing alone. It says the old-age dependency ratio across OECD countries rose from 19% in 1980 to 31% in 2023 and is projected to reach 52% by 2060, while the working-age population declines and GDP-per-capita growth slows absent policy action [S149]. The same OECD chapter also says older generations have generally enjoyed higher income growth than younger adults since the mid-1990s, that poverty risk has shifted away from older people toward children, and that public social spending is already heavily concentrated on seniors [S149].

The wealth side of the bargain is diverging too. OECD’s 2025 wealth-distribution paper says rising house prices have made homeownership more difficult for young and low-wealth households and are exacerbating wealth gaps between younger and older generations [S150]. OECD’s Employment Outlook 2025 adds that households headed by people aged 55-65 and 65+ hold, on average across 19 OECD economies, net wealth about six times higher than households headed by 25-34 year-olds, and that the share of people in their 30s who own their main residence has fallen in 17 of 24 OECD countries over the last three decades [S149]. When housing access, inheritance timing, and asset appreciation increasingly separate generations, disputes over tax, planning, rents, pensions, and family transfers acquire a more explicit age structure.

The fiscal surface is also becoming harder to ignore. OECD’s 2025 paper on ageing and fiscal policy says ageing is already raising pressure on pensions, health, and long-term care spending and will continue affecting revenues and debt dynamics [S151]. The IMF’s 2025 G20 note similarly says ageing raises pension and healthcare spending, shrinks the tax base, and can crowd out public investment more conducive to long-run growth without substantive reform [S152]. That means generational bargains no longer live only inside family narratives; they increasingly live inside budget baselines.

Public expectations are moving as well. OECD’s 2024 Risks That Matter wave added population ageing and fertility preferences, and OECD’s 2025 analysis says 65% of respondents worry about population ageing, while large majorities favour labour-supply and productivity responses over simple pronatalist exhortation [S153]. Meanwhile OECD’s 2025 *Cities for All Ages* treats housing, transport, and urban design as explicitly age-structured policy problems, including affordable housing for younger households and age-friendly adaptation for older adults [S154]. This suggests that intergenerational bargaining is ceasing to be a niche pension debate and becoming a **general policy grammar** that can reorganise labour policy, housing policy, urban policy, social protection, and public-finance legitimacy.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Budgets get read as age maps

Tax, pension, housing, transport, and health decisions may increasingly be interpreted less as neutral technocratic allocations and more as choices about which generation is being asked to wait, pay, or sacrifice.

### 2. Housing becomes the decisive fairness arena

Where younger households are locked out of ownership and face high rents while older cohorts sit on appreciated assets, intergenerational conflict may appear first through planning rules, mortgage guarantees, inheritance timing, and tenure policy rather than through pension debates alone.

### 3. Retirement-age politics gets harder, not easier

As fiscal pressure rises, governments may push later retirement and higher participation among older workers, but those reforms will be judged against whether younger cohorts are also getting housing access, wage growth, and family-formation support.

### 4. Family transfers matter more because public bargains feel weaker

If inheritances arrive later in life and public redistribution remains tilted toward older cohorts, private family support may become more decisive for whether younger adults can buy homes, have children, or absorb shocks.

### 5. Cities become laboratories for generational settlement

Municipal governments may increasingly mediate the bargain through age-inclusive transport, housing design, co-housing, public space, and land-use choices that determine whether different age groups can remain in the same place without mutual displacement.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Productivity, migration, and later-life employment offset ageing pressure enough that age-structured fiscal conflict remains muted.
- Housing affordability improves materially for younger cohorts without major distributive conflict across generations.
- Political coalitions successfully recast ageing as a broad social-insurance challenge rather than an age-group bargaining problem.
- Asset-price appreciation slows or reverses enough that wealth gaps between generations stop widening.
- Public policy shifts strongly toward children, youth, and family support before age-structured conflict hardens.

## Research queue

- Which policies make intergenerational conflict sharper versus converting it into a renegotiated social contract?
- Is the decisive fairness variable pensions, housing, inheritance timing, taxation, or care burdens?
- Which countries first make explicit use of age-incidence analysis in budgeting or long-term planning?
- Do cities, not national governments, become the first institutions to build workable intergenerational bargains?
- When people say they are anxious about ageing, are they mostly anxious about fiscal sustainability, labour shortages, housing exclusion, or political unfairness?
