# Dossier: Simulation Capacity Becomes Governance Infrastructure

## Core claim

A stronger version of the modelling story is emerging. The important shift is not simply that institutions can simulate more things more cheaply. It is that **maintained counterfactual systems — digital twins, scenario engines, model-informed regulatory pipelines, and simulation-linked planning environments — are becoming part of ordinary operating infrastructure**.

That matters because the governing question changes. The issue stops being only *can experts model this system?* and becomes *which institutions have access to trusted, updateable, interoperable counterfactuals before they intervene in the real world?*

## Why this belongs in the archive

The source base has crossed from experimentation into institutionalization.

At the planetary layer, the European Commission describes Destination Earth as a flagship effort to build a highly accurate digital model of the Earth that will **monitor, simulate, and predict** interactions between natural phenomena and human activity, and explicitly links it to disaster preparedness, adaptation strategy, and socioeconomic impact analysis [S692]. ECMWF’s February 2026 update sharpens the point further: phase three, running from June 2026 to June 2028, will focus on **operating and further evolving** the Climate and Extremes Digital Twins and on turning digital-twin outputs into AI-ready datasets [S693]. That is no longer a pilot mentality. It is an operating mentality.

At the urban layer, the European Commission’s smart-cities programme now treats local digital twins as reusable public tooling. Its Local Digital Twins Toolbox is meant to help cities build AI-based twins that evaluate scenarios affecting traffic, pollution, and public health, and it explicitly frames these simulations as inputs to real-time emergency response and long-range planning [S694]. In U.S. environmental operations, NOAA’s 2025 digital-twin study says a twin environment allows researchers to rapidly build and examine a wide variety of potential weather scenarios, while exploring how such systems could improve data products and last-mile insight for users [S695].

The same pattern appears in infrastructure and industrial control. The U.S. Department of Energy’s 2025 AI Strategy says advanced nuclear work includes creating AI digital twins for real-time simulations and describes the GEMINA program as developing digital-twin technology for advanced reactors, predictive maintenance, and model-based fault detection [S696]. The UK’s 2025 infrastructure strategy says the National Digital Twin Programme is developing standards, frameworks, methodologies, and tools that can provide the foundation of a functioning market in digital twins [S697]. Its Clean Flexibility Roadmap then connects this directly to live energy-system governance by describing a data-sharing pilot carried out with the National Digital Twin Programme and by requiring more transparent, coherent modelling and shared data arrangements for strategic energy planning [S698].

Regulatory science is moving in the same direction. FDA now says **design-by-simulation is a common practice** in new drug development at the IND stage and points sponsors toward formal support programmes for model-informed approaches [S699]. FDA’s paired-meeting programme explicitly exists to advance and integrate computational models into drug development and regulatory review [S700]. EMA’s February 2026 adoption of ICH M15 provides a harmonized assessment framework for model-informed drug-development evidence and defines MIDD as the use of computational modelling and simulation to generate evidence for developers, regulators, and other stakeholders [S701].

Finally, the standards layer is thickening. NIST says digital-twin standardization is critical if the technology is to move beyond isolated bespoke deployments, and its 2025 security-and-trust report frames digital twins as broad capabilities that depend on interoperable definitions, tools, standards, and explicit trust considerations [S702, S703]. Once standards and assurance questions become central, simulation capacity is no longer just an expert-side analytic convenience. It becomes governable infrastructure.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Model governance becomes an operational bottleneck

The scarce asset may not be raw data or raw compute alone, but the combination of validated models, synchronized inputs, acceptable assumptions, version control, and institutional trust.

### 2. Shared scenario baselines become political objects

As more planning, regulation, and investment depend on simulation, disputes will increasingly shift toward baseline assumptions, boundary conditions, confidence intervals, and which scenarios count as official enough to act on.

### 3. Counterfactual access stratifies institutions

Some cities, firms, ministries, and regulators will be able to test interventions before making them, while others still govern largely by lagging indicators, static reports, or outsourced expertise. That gap may become a form of practical power.

### 4. Simulation starts to fuse analysis and control

Digital twins will not remain purely advisory in every domain. In some sectors they will increasingly sit close to optimization, dispatch, maintenance, dosage, traffic management, fault detection, or other live operating loops.

### 5. Validation and interoperability regimes expand

A larger share of institutional energy may go into proving that models are credible, comparable, secure, interoperable, and fit for a named decision context.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Digital twins and scenario engines remain expensive showcases with little effect on real operational decisions.
- Institutions continue to collect data but fail to keep models current enough for live use.
- Regulatory, legal, or political distrust sharply limits reliance on simulation in high-stakes domains.
- Domain-specific modelling grows, but without enough standardization or interoperability to become a reusable infrastructure layer.
- Human judgment, local discretion, or political bargaining remain overwhelmingly more decisive than counterfactual tooling.

## Research queue

- Which sectors cross first from simulation as advisory support to simulation as a routine precondition for action?
- Which bottleneck dominates in practice: data quality, model validation, interoperability, cybersecurity, or institutional trust?
- When do official scenario baselines become contested enough to require appeals, audits, or adversarial review?
- Which public institutions can afford to maintain high-quality counterfactual infrastructure in-house, and which become dependent on vendors or consortia?
- Where does simulation most clearly change distributive outcomes: infrastructure planning, disaster adaptation, medicine, transport, energy, or environmental enforcement?
