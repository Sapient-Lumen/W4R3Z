# Dossier: Representation-Risk Telemetry Becomes a Supervisory Surface

## Core claim

The important shift is not merely that modern institutions allow representatives, helpers, attorneys, brokers, fiduciaries, or trusted contacts to act on someone else’s behalf. It is that **the risk generated by those delegated roles is becoming measurable enough to supervise**. Once systems begin logging misuse allegations, unauthorized-enrollment complaints, suspicious-authority events, temporary holds, disciplinary actions, revocations, reimbursement totals, and representative replacements, proxy risk stops looking like scattered casework and starts looking like a distinct governance surface.

The stronger version of the thesis is not simply that delegated authority can be abused. It is that **representation-risk telemetry becomes a supervisory surface**. Institutions increasingly need to know which representatives generate repeated friction, which authority relationships create safeguarding risk, which channels attract impersonation or abuse, and which warning signals should trigger investigation before losses compound. That pushes organizations toward dashboards, case taxonomies, sanction lists, review queues, fraud referrals, and cross-case pattern detection focused on the authority layer itself.

## Why this belongs in the archive

The archive already contains dossiers on delegated representation, safeguarded delegation, complaint telemetry, resolution metrics, and remedy budgets. The missing layer was **risk sensing around the representative role itself**. Those earlier dossiers explain why proxy authority is spreading and why its scope, revocation, and auditability matter. This dossier asks what happens once institutions start treating misuse reports, suspicious-authority events, and delegated-access failures as a measurable stream that can drive oversight.

Guardianship and attorney systems already show the pattern clearly. The Office of the Public Guardian’s 2024 to 2025 annual report says concerns raised with OPG increased to 11,266, up from 10,577 the year before, and says OPG is proactively exploring causes, identifying process improvements, and using data to predict future trends [S404]. The same report says 98% of safeguarding concerns were risk assessed within two working days and 96% received a decision within five working days on whether OPG could investigate or signpost the case [S404]. GOV.UK’s standing concern-reporting page makes the intake surface explicit: members of the public can report concerns about registered attorneys, deputies, or guardians, including misuse of money or decisions not in the person’s best interests, and OPG then checks whether it has legal authority to investigate [S405]. The point is no longer merely that abuse can happen. It is that abuse reports are becoming routinised administrative signal.

Social Security’s representative-payee program shows an even clearer operational telemetry layer. SSA tells beneficiaries it will investigate all allegations of misuse, gather evidence, decide whether misuse occurred, and try to recover misused money [S406]. Its FY 2024 annual report on representative payee reviews says that out of 4,018 completed reviews, SSA initiated new misuse investigations in 55 cases, carried 152 pending misuse cases from the prior year, closed 49 cases with all actions complete, removed the payee in 13 closed cases, and made misuse determinations totaling $812,833 [S407]. It also reports that $1,115,191 of misused funds were repaid or reissued to affected beneficiaries [S407]. SSA’s regulations then tie telemetry to consequence: representative payees who misuse benefits are liable to repay them, and SSA requires regular accounting for use of benefits [S408][S409]. That is a mature example of a delegated-authority system that already depends on intake, review, classification, recovery, and supervisory action.

Veterans’ benefits show the same logic in another federal setting. VA says fiduciaries must report changes affecting entitlement or their ability to continue as payee and must meet periodic onsite reviews designed to protect beneficiaries from misuse and strengthen oversight [S410]. VA’s fiduciary definitions page makes the monitoring layer more explicit: FAST exists to submit accountings and fund-usage reports electronically, fund-usage reviews collect statements and supporting documents for irregular purchases, and misuse is defined as using any part of VA benefits for a purpose not for the use and benefit of the beneficiary or dependents [S411]. In other words, the fiduciary relationship is increasingly governed through recurring data production, review, and exception analysis rather than trust alone.

Health-insurance marketplaces show the same phenomenon under a different name: agent and broker misconduct telemetry. CMS reported that from January through August 2024 it received 90,863 complaints of unauthorized plan switching and 183,553 complaints of unauthorized enrollment, while resolving more than 99% of both categories [S412]. It also said that from June through October 2024 it suspended 850 agents’ and brokers’ Marketplace Agreements for reasonable suspicion of fraudulent or abusive conduct [S412]. CMS’s January 2026 integrity fact sheet then framed the same issue as an ongoing supervisory program, describing rapid complaint resolution, targeted oversight, strong enforcement, and the ending of premium subsidies for nearly 1.5 million people found ineligible for assistance or enrolled without authorization on the federal platform [S413]. The 2025 Marketplace Integrity and Affordability final rule adds the legal-hardening layer by adopting a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard for terminating agent, broker, and web-broker Marketplace Agreements for cause [S414]. This is not just a fraud story. It is a case where representative-risk traces have become a regulatory operating surface.

Financial regulation shows the same shift in a more mature supervisory vocabulary. FINRA’s 2025 Annual Regulatory Oversight Report says firms must make reasonable efforts to obtain trusted-contact information, regulate when associated persons may hold positions of trust or powers of attorney for customers, and can place temporary holds when they reasonably believe financial exploitation of specified adults is occurring or has been attempted [S415]. The report also highlights supervisory findings such as failure to obtain trusted-contact information, failure to make required disclosures, lack of documented training, and lack of documented internal review for temporary holds [S415]. FINRA’s Regulatory Notice 26-02 goes a step further by proposing more informative notice requirements to authorized parties and trusted contacts when a temporary hold is placed [S416]. Here the representative layer is clearly no longer informal help. It is a monitored risk domain with specific data, controls, review artifacts, and communications duties.

Tax administration reinforces the same trend from another angle. IRS maintains an intake channel for complaints about tax return preparers and says it investigates improper conduct [S417]. It also now publishes a searchable disciplinary look-up covering censures, suspensions, disbarments, and other restrictions on practice before the IRS, updated when disciplinary announcements are published and retained for up to 25 years [S418]. That means delegated tax authority is increasingly governed not just through appointment forms and revocations, but through a visible history of complaint intake, sanctioning, and long-tail pattern memory.

Taken together, these sources support a stronger claim than “proxies can be risky.” They suggest that **representation risk is becoming observable enough to supervise across sectors**. Once complaint streams, misuse investigations, accountings, hold notices, sanction lists, and authority-change events are gathered into durable systems, institutions gain a new way to see where delegated authority is safe, brittle, or repeatedly exploited.

## Speculative consequences worth tracking

### 1. Representatives may acquire risk profiles rather than simple authorization states

Institutions may increasingly ask not only whether a person is authorized to act, but whether their pattern of complaints, revocations, suspicious events, or audit findings makes them higher risk.

### 2. Safeguarding units may become more central than frontline enrollment or service teams

As delegated-action risks accumulate, specialist teams that triage misuse, exploitation, impersonation, or unauthorized-change cases may become key bottlenecks in keeping ordinary services trustworthy.

### 3. Delegated authority may become tiered by monitorability

Some representatives may receive faster or broader permissions because they are easier to audit, easier to revoke, better trained, or attached to lower-risk histories.

### 4. Public and quasi-public sanction lists may matter more

Suspension lists, disciplinary look-ups, or complaint histories may become part of the practical trust infrastructure that determines who can continue acting for others at scale.

### 5. Representative-risk data may be fused with fraud and vulnerability systems

Misuse allegations, trusted-contact activations, exploitative-transfer holds, and unauthorized-change complaints may increasingly feed the same analytic environment as elder-abuse monitoring, fraud detection, and consumer-protection oversight.

### 6. Institutions may optimize for safe delegation, not just more delegation

The value of proxy access may increasingly depend on whether representative-risk telemetry is good enough to make delegated action scalable without making abuse unmanageable.

## What could falsify or weaken the thesis

- Misuse, complaint, and revocation data remain too fragmented or local to influence supervision outside individual cases.
- Institutions continue authorizing representatives but do not build durable intake, review, or sanction mechanisms around them.
- Telemetry produces mostly noise, false positives, or symbolic dashboards rather than real intervention.
- Representative-risk data remain legally inaccessible, operationally incomparable, or too privacy-constrained to support cross-case learning.
- The pattern remains confined to a few fiduciary-heavy sectors rather than spreading across health, finance, tax, benefits, and civic digital services.

## Research queue

- Which signals are the strongest leading indicators of harmful representation: complaints, revocations, restitution events, temporary holds, or repeated documentation anomalies?
- When should representative-risk data stay internal, and when should sanction or safeguarding metrics become publicly visible?
- How can institutions distinguish harmful representatives from high-volume representatives who simply serve more complex populations?
- Which sectors are most likely to converge on shared taxonomies for misuse, exploitation, unauthorized action, and authority failure?
- What governance prevents telemetry from collapsing into blanket suspicion of carers, relatives, or frontline agents who are legitimately helping others navigate difficult systems?
