# Anti-Legibility Model

This archive began by tracking how bottlenecks migrate toward legibility: standardized records, registries, credentials, validation reports, product passports, status labels, appeal states, and reusable proof objects. That lens remains powerful, but it has a structural bias. It can make every new proof surface look like progress.

rev0182 makes anti-legibility a standing model rather than a caveat.

## Core distinction

Anti-legibility is not the same as secrecy.

It includes at least five different moves:

1. **privacy-preserving proof** — prove eligibility without over-disclosure;
2. **strategic opacity** — avoid exposing relationships, recipients, suppliers, or vulnerabilities to counterparties;
3. **local trust override** — keep a service functioning when a central trust source is wrong, stale, or unreachable;
4. **informal refuge** — transact outside formal proof systems when compliance infrastructure is inaccessible or predatory;
5. **illegible abuse** — hide fraud, evasion, coercion, laundering, or unsafe practice.

The archive should defend the first three, analyze the fourth, and expose the fifth.

## Anti-legibility is part of infrastructure maturity

A naive proof system says: disclose more.

A mature proof system says:

- disclose the minimum sufficient claim;
- bind the claim to a purpose;
- publish acceptable proof profiles;
- make over-collection appealable;
- support revocation without persistent surveillance;
- allow local fallback with logged accountability;
- preserve redress for people or firms who cannot produce the preferred digital proof.

## Standing test

Every proof-object dossier should now answer:

> What is the minimum sufficient proof, and who decides that more cannot be demanded?

That question links:

- data-minimization proofs;
- recipient-graph privacy proofs;
- public proof-profile registries;
- small-supplier evidence brokers;
- informal-market refuges;
- local trust overrides;
- human fallback;
- redaction-boundary ledgers;
- repair-event privacy profiles.

## Legitimate anti-legibility examples

- A wallet proves age-over-threshold without disclosing exact birthdate.
- A broker proves that all required recipients were notified without revealing acquisition counterparties.
- A supplier proves EUDR compliance through a public tool without handing an entire farm network to every buyer.
- An AI provider exposes evaluation evidence to a trusted reviewer while giving buyers a signed redaction-boundary ledger.
- A repair event proves recall clearance without revealing unrelated diagnostics.
- A local operator temporarily trusts a known root during a central trust-list outage, with logged expiry and later reconciliation.

## Illegitimate anti-legibility examples

- A marketplace lets sellers hide recall status under privacy rhetoric.
- A vendor refuses source-witness requests to exhaust an appeal clock.
- A supplier masks commodity origin to avoid due-diligence checks.
- A broker hides recipient graphs to avoid sending correction notices.
- A platform uses opaque scoring to deny redress.
- A resolver points verifiers to a stale or friendly passport view.

## Design rule

> The archive should prefer proof systems that make legitimate anti-legibility operational and illegitimate anti-legibility harder.

This turns privacy into a design layer, not a veto.

## Editorial consequence

When a new dossier celebrates legibility, ask for its paired anti-legibility artifact:

| Legibility artifact | Needed anti-legibility pair |
|---|---|
| recipient graph | privacy proof / escrowed roster |
| product passport | purpose-scoped disclosure profile |
| AI model documentation packet | redaction-boundary ledger |
| repair event | privacy-filtered repair proof |
| due-diligence statement | small-supplier evidence public good |
| validation report | public proof profile and overrequest appeal |
| authority check | human fallback and local trust override |
| incident report | correction-afterlife and privileged-source handling |

Without this pair, the dossier is probably under-specified.
