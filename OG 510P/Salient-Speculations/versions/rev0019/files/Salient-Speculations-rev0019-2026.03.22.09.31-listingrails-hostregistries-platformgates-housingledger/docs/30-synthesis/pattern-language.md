# Pattern language

The archive is now coherent enough to describe its recurring mechanism families.
This is a compression device, not a replacement for the notes.

## 1. Scarce complements

Some things get more valuable when cognition gets cheaper because they are still hard to scale.
Examples in the canon:

- electricity and interconnection (`001`)
- care labor and embodied attention (`003`)
- trusted performance time in assessment (`007`)

## 2. Gatekeepers and permission layers

Some systems matter because they decide whether normal participation remains possible.
Examples in the canon:

- insurers as territorial permission layers (`002`)
- synthesis-screening and vendor controls (`004`)
- low-altitude airspace restrictions and counter-UAS authority (`006`)
- pre-travel authorisation, carrier-side checks, and upstream mobility permission (`018`)
- prior-authorisation workflows that decide whether a specific clinical service may proceed or be reimbursed (`019`)
- pre-start work-right checks and labour-compliance workflows that decide whether paid work may begin (`020`)
- model-based building-permit validation that decides whether construction or retrofit can enter the approval pipeline (`021`)
- instrumented extraction requirements that increasingly decide whether large water diversions or fishing effort remain licit (`023`)
- insurability-conditioned collateral rules that increasingly decide whether a home can clear origination, servicing, or secondary-market finance (`024`)
- short-term-rental registration and platform-verification stacks that increasingly decide whether a lodging listing may be marketed or transacted at all (`025`)

## 3. Proof surfaces

As generation, forgery, and remote participation get cheaper, proof of origin, eligibility, compliance, or authentic performance becomes more valuable.
Examples in the canon:

- provenance and chain-of-custody for media (`005`)
- audit trails and attestation in biology (`004`)
- certification and supervised performance in education (`007`)
- threshold credentials and derived-attribute proofs for access decisions (`009`)
- organisational authority credentials that prove who may act for or bind a firm (`013`)
- object passports that make goods carry machine-readable compliance and traceability surfaces (`010`)
- title-bearing electronic originals whose control can substitute for possession in trade and warehousing (`012`)

Some proof surfaces do more than certify facts. Some determine who may exercise a right because authority to represent an organisation is itself the operative question; others determine who can exercise a right because exclusive control of the digital original is itself the operative commercial act.

## 4. Status surfaces

Some systems matter because institutions stop treating issuance-time validity as enough and instead check whether a credential, provider, verifier, endpoint, product, or delegated role is still in force at the moment of use.
Examples in the canon:

- trusted lists and verifier-authentication chains in wallet and signature ecosystems (`016`)
- passport-linked travel permissions and journey-readiness checks that must still be valid when the trip starts (`018`)
- participant discovery, metadata updates, and certificate revocation in networked transaction infrastructure (`015`, `016`)
- role-credential revocation and public status updates in organisational identity systems (`013`, `016`)
- customs-facing registry checks that confirm a passport or identifier still exists and matches current registry data (`010`, `016`)
- payee-validation and sanctions-state screening that increasingly determine whether a payment counterparty still counts as safe enough to pay now (`022`)

Status surfaces are different from proof surfaces, query surfaces, and relay surfaces. A status surface is not primarily about what can be shown, where source evidence comes from, or which operator carried the message. It is about whether the relevant thing still counts **now**.

## 5. Query surfaces

Some systems matter because institutions stop asking users to assemble and re-submit evidence, and instead retrieve current evidence from authoritative sources under trusted interoperability, consent, and logging rules.
Examples in the canon:

- authoritative evidence exchange and once-only administrative retrieval (`014`)

Query surfaces are different from proof surfaces. Proof surfaces change what a person, firm, or object can present. Query surfaces change whether presentation is needed at all.

## 6. Observability layers

Some shifts are not about blocking or certifying action but about making activity newly legible.
Examples in the canon:

- persistent sensing of land disturbance, emissions, and disaster damage (`008`)
- remote visibility that erodes strategic depth in warfare (`006`)
- insurance and territorial repricing that depend on ever-richer risk maps (`002`)
- operator-side telemetry and electronic monitoring that make water take and fishing effort legible at the point of extraction (`023`)

## 7. Transaction rails

Some systems matter because institutions stop relying on delayed summaries and instead want economically meaningful events to be generated, routed, and checked on standardised digital rails.
Examples in the canon:

- structured e-invoicing and continuous transactional reporting that turn invoices into born-reportable events (`011`)
- prior-authorisation request and decision exchanges that make treatment access increasingly born-adjudicated rather than argued by fax (`019`)
- payment initiation flows that increasingly include born-validated counterparty checks rather than blind account addressing (`022`)

## 8. Relay surfaces

Some systems matter because institutions do not trust every endpoint directly and instead rely on certified, listed, supervised, or otherwise governed intermediary operators to carry high-trust messages.
Examples in the canon:

- qualified trust service providers sitting inside wallet, signature, and attestation flows (`013`, `015`)
- certified Peppol access points routing transactional data (`011`, `015`)
- eDelivery access points and third-party relay operators carrying once-only evidence exchange (`014`, `015`)

Relay surfaces are different from gatekeepers, proof surfaces, query surfaces, and transaction rails. A relay surface is not primarily about who is eligible, what is proven, what is queried, or which event format is used. It is about which operators are trusted to move the message at all.

## 9. Allocation surfaces

Some systems matter because institutions stop offering one generic form of access to a scarce shared system and instead sort participants into differentiated service classes with different prices, obligations, and fallback conditions.
Examples in the canon:

- large-load electricity classes, minimum-pay obligations, and special cost-allocation terms for system-shaping demand (`017`)
- queue-priority and reliability-focused service design for large new loads in stressed power systems (`001`, `017`)

Allocation surfaces are different from gatekeepers. A gatekeeper decides whether participation is possible at all. An allocation surface decides **under what class of service, with what burden-sharing terms, and with what obligations** participation is allowed.

Gatekeepers increasingly come in two forms: broad front-door permission layers and narrower episode-specific adjudication loops.
The latter do not mainly decide whether someone belongs to the system. They decide whether a particular journey, procedure, or request may proceed under standardised data, timing, and explanation rules.

## Working claim

A good candidate note often succeeds because it shows one of these nine shapes in a place where most people are still seeing only the local story.

## Why this matters

This pattern language gives the archive a sharper admission test.
A new note should usually do at least one of three things:

1. add a genuinely new branch,
2. materially strengthen an existing branch,
3. or reveal a better umbrella law that compresses several notes at once.

If it does none of these, it probably does not belong.
