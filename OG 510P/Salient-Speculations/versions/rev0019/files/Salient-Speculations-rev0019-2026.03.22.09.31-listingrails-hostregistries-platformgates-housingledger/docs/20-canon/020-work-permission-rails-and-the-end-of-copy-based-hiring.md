# 020 — Work permission rails and the end of copy-based hiring

**Status:** canon

## Thesis

As right-to-work, employment-eligibility, and posted-worker declaration duties move into online services, certified digital verification services, share codes, remote examination rules, and pre-start compliance software, the practical ability to begin paid work will increasingly depend on passing a machine-readable onboarding stack rather than mainly on signed contracts, photocopied documents, or ex post inspection.

The scarce asset is no longer only labour demand or a willing employer.
It is the ability to present the identity, residency, work-right, and declaration signals that a hiring, platform, sponsor, or labour-compliance workflow will accept quickly enough for work to begin.

## Why it matters

This changes where practical power sits in labour markets.
The decisive moment is no longer only the interview, the contract, or the manager’s decision.
It is increasingly the earlier workflow in which an employer, platform, agency, contractor, or regulator decides whether the person can be cleared onto payroll, onto a shift, onto a subcontracting chain, or onto a cross-border service assignment.

That matters because delays, mismatches, unavailable digital evidence, failed remote checks, or missing declarations become forms of labour exclusion.
The bottleneck is not merely a legal right in the abstract.
It is increasingly **pre-start admissibility on work-permission rails**.
That pulls HR software, digital-identity providers, government checking services, E-Verify-linked procedures, labour platforms, sponsor compliance teams, and cross-border declaration systems deeper into the ordinary act of starting paid work.

This is related to `009`, `014`, `016`, and `018`, but it is not reducible to them.
`009` is about threshold proofs in general.
`014` is about authoritative-source retrieval.
`016` is about runtime standing.
`018` is about upstream mobility permission.
This note is about a different shift: labour-market entry itself increasingly moving into pre-start digital compliance flows.

## Mechanism sketch

- The UK’s March 2026 digital-ID consultation now states the move explicitly. It proposes that evidence for prescribed right-to-work checks must be checked digitally, that this should become the only acceptable way to obtain the statutory excuse for the narrowed evidence set, and that paper alternatives such as birth certificates would be removed from that route.
- The UK’s digital-ID explainer makes the labour implication even clearer. It says digital ID will be required for right-to-work checks, that employers will be required to check it as evidence of the right to work, and that the scheme is expected to be rolled out to UK citizens and legal residents by the end of the Parliament.
- The same UK stack is also building a governed intermediary layer. The new supplementary code for digital right-to-work checks sets out rules for digital verification services that support employers conducting those checks, creating a more standardised market for compliant onboarding rather than leaving employers with ad hoc document judgment.
- Current Home Office guidance already shows the operating logic. For people whose status is evidenced online, employers use a Home Office service, a share code, and date of birth to establish a statutory excuse before employment starts, and eVisa holders can use only the online service rather than paper immigration documents.
- In the United States, DHS already allows a remote alternative procedure for Form I-9 document examination, but only for employers enrolled in E-Verify and only at E-Verify hiring sites. That makes remote work-right onboarding a governed software-linked compliance path rather than a general freedom to inspect less carefully.
- The EU is pushing the same shift for cross-border labour mobility. The Commission and Member States are developing a common electronic e-Declaration for posted workers through IMI, and the Council’s 2025 general approach says the regulation should fully digitalise the submission of posting declarations while making compliance monitoring easier for authorities.
- The UK is also moving to widen the labour surface, not just digitise the old one. Its 2025 impact assessment says right-to-work duties will extend to workers under worker’s contracts, individual sub-contractors, agency and gig-economy arrangements, and online matching services, turning work-permission checks into a broader labour-market gate rather than a traditional employer-only paperwork duty.
- Taken together, these pieces point to a deeper pattern: some labour markets are moving from **hire and later prove eligibility** toward **clear digitally, then let work begin**.

## What this speculation predicts

1. More employers, staffing firms, and labour platforms will treat work-right clearance as an API- or service-mediated onboarding step embedded inside recruitment and payroll software rather than as a manual document side process.
2. Labour-market exclusion will increasingly appear upstream as failed digital checks, unresolved share-code issues, unavailable identity signals, or missing platform-side declarations before a first shift starts, not only later as enforcement or payroll disputes.
3. Gig-economy, subcontracting, and agency-work regimes will be pulled into work-right compliance stacks that were once aimed mainly at standard employment relationships.
4. Cross-border service provision will increasingly depend on machine-readable worker-declaration flows and digital evidence exchange before work begins, especially in sectors where posting rules, sponsor duties, or worker notifications are being standardised.
5. Digital verification providers, HR software vendors, and government checking services will become a more visible choke point in labour-market access because they will increasingly mediate whether a willing worker can become an admissible worker.

## Watchpoints

- more governments making digital right-to-work or work-authorisation checks the only or clearly preferred route to safe-harbour employer compliance
- HR, staffing, payroll, and labour-platform software marketing built-in work-right checking, share-code handling, E-Verify-linked workflows, or contractor/gig compliance modules as core onboarding features
- expansion of work-right duties beyond conventional employees into agencies, subcontractors, platform workers, or cross-border service chains
- evidence that workers with lawful status are being delayed or excluded because the digital proof, remote-check, or declaration workflow fails even when the underlying legal right exists
- evidence that employers continue to rely mainly on photocopies, manual visual inspection, and ex post spot checks, with digital work-permission rails remaining marginal or optional

## What would weaken this

- governments retreating from mandatory or preferred digital checking and preserving broad paper-based alternatives as equally acceptable long-term routes
- digital work-right systems remaining too fragmented, unreliable, or sector-specific to become a general labour-market control surface
- cross-border worker declarations and platform-side labour checks staying administratively marginal rather than becoming routine pre-start infrastructure
- employers continuing to treat hiring and contract formation as practically sufficient, with eligibility and work permission still managed mainly after the fact
