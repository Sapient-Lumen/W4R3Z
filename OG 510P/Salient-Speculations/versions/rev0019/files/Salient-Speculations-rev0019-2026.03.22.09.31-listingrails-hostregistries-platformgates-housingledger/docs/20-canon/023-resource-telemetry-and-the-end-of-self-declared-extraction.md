# 023 — Resource telemetry and the end of self-declared extraction

**Status:** canon

## Thesis

As water, fisheries, and other common-pool resource regimes move toward approved meters, telemetry-capable loggers, vessel-position feeds, electronic logbooks, onboard cameras, and standardised machine-readable submissions, the practical ability to keep taking from a regulated resource will increasingly depend on emitting admissible telemetry rather than mainly on holding a licence and filing retrospective declarations.

The scarce asset is no longer only the legal entitlement to extract.
It is increasingly the ability to operate an extraction point that can be measured, transmitted, reviewed, and trusted inside the governing system.

## Why it matters

This changes where control sits in resource governance.
The decisive moment is no longer only the annual report, the random patrol, the dockside inspection, or the after-the-fact enforcement action.
It is increasingly the earlier and more continuous workflow in which a pump, diversion point, reservoir, vessel, or fishing trip becomes legible enough for extraction to count as compliant while it is happening or soon after.

That matters because common-pool resources are politically explosive when monitoring remains sparse and self-reported.
If withdrawals, catches, bycatch, or vessel locations need to be governed at finer temporal and spatial resolution, then measurement devices, approved telemetry hardware, transmission standards, app-based reporting, video review, analyst capacity, exemption logic, and upload cadence become part of the right itself.
The bottleneck is not merely whether a permit exists.
It is increasingly **instrumented admissibility for resource take**.

This is related to `002`, `008`, `015`, and `016`, but it is not reducible to them.
`002` is about territorial repricing through insurance.
`008` is about persistent remote sensing that replaces self-reporting from the outside.
`015` is about trusted relays.
`016` is about runtime standing.
This note is about a different shift: extraction rights increasingly becoming conditional on **approved measurement and machine-readable telemetry at the point of take**.

## Mechanism sketch

- California now makes the move explicit for large water diversions. Starting **1 October 2026**, large diversion requirements replace the prior telemetry framing for qualifying rights, and large diverters generally must submit measurement data on a **weekly** basis with no more than a **7-day** lag, using standardised templates, direct transmission to CalWATRS, or another approved site.
- The California rule also shows how the entitlement becomes entangled with instrumentation and format. Large-diversion status is triggered by thresholds such as maximum allowable diversion amount or rate, only CalWATRS is currently approved for large diversion submissions, and the rule was revised in 2026 partly because non-standardised files prevented efficient and systematic analysis.
- NSW shows the same pattern in a second water regime. Under the amended non-urban metering rules, covered surface and groundwater works need a pattern-approved meter and a telemetry-capable local intelligence device, telemetry is required for cumulative entitlements of **100 ML or more** unless exempt, tamper-evident seals are part of the compliance stack, and the device layer is assessed against published specifications and listed by government.
- EU fisheries governance is now moving the same way. The revised control regulation is built around digital technology and modern data management, mandates electronic recording of catches, expands digital traceability, and requires remote electronic monitoring with onboard cameras for certain vessels above **18 m** with a high risk of non-compliance.
- The EU’s monitoring architecture makes the runtime aspect explicit. Vessel Monitoring System tracking is mandatory for EU vessels over **12 m**, exemptions for vessels under **15 m** run only until **January 2026**, and by **January 2028** all vessels under **12 m** are expected to be equipped and tracked, with mobile-app pathways for smaller operators.
- U.S. fisheries administration shows that this is not merely a legislative aspiration. NOAA’s 2026 Alaska deployment plan assigns at-sea and shoreside observers and electronic monitoring to vessels and processing plants in the North Pacific, making instrumented monitoring part of normal annual operations rather than a distant pilot.
- The Western and Central Pacific Fisheries Commission now describes electronic monitoring in even broader terms: cameras, GPS, and sensors can potentially extend coverage to every trip, while electronic reporting sends data directly into databases and pushes member states toward shared standards and compatibility.
- Put together, these moves suggest a deeper pattern: some resource regimes are shifting from **licensed extraction later explained by reports** toward **licensed extraction that must remain machine-legible while it occurs or shortly after**.

## What this speculation predicts

1. More water, fisheries, and other resource-governance regimes will tie legal compliance to approved device stacks, upload timing, and standardised telemetry formats rather than treating those as optional enhancements to a paper permit.
2. Smaller operators will increasingly be pulled into lighter but still digital monitoring paths — such as app-based reporting, simplified device classes, or managed exemptions — instead of being left entirely outside telemetry-based governance.
3. Device approval, vendor listing, seal integrity, blackspot exemptions, and reviewer accreditation will become important secondary chokepoints because extraction rights will depend on whether the measurement stack itself is admissible.
4. Disputes over overuse, bycatch, curtailment, or illegal take will increasingly turn on data latency, device uptime, signal coverage, file format, and auditability rather than only on witness testimony or coarse periodic reports.
5. Resource governance will become more operationally comparable across domains: the same archive grammar will appear in rivers, reservoirs, vessels, and possibly other regulated extraction systems once the right to take is coupled to persistent machine-readable evidence.

## Watchpoints

- more jurisdictions making continuous or near-real-time telemetry a normal condition of large water diversion, fishing effort, or other resource extraction rather than a pilot requirement
- growth of government-maintained device lists, approved data standards, or certified monitoring/review vendors as ordinary prerequisites for lawful extraction
- evidence that smaller operators are moved onto app-based or simplified digital reporting rather than remaining permanently exempt from telemetry-conditioned governance
- cases where enforcement or dispute resolution turns materially on upload delay, telemetry gaps, device tampering, or incompatible data formats rather than on substantive extraction limits alone
- evidence that governments retreat from telemetry-conditioned resource governance because cost, coverage gaps, privacy objections, or political resistance make the systems too brittle to sustain

## What would weaken this

- telemetry-heavy rules staying confined to a few unusually stressed jurisdictions or sectors, with most resource extraction still governed mainly through periodic self-reporting and occasional inspection
- governments continuing to tolerate non-standardised files, delayed submissions, or weak device requirements without trying to make machine-readable monitoring operationally decisive
- monitoring stacks proving too expensive, too easy to evade, or too politically fragile to become durable conditions of lawful extraction
- electronic monitoring remaining an auxiliary science tool or grant-funded experiment rather than a real compliance condition for ordinary operators
