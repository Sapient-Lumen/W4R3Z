# 001 — Grid-constrained intelligence and the politics of power

**Status:** canon

## Thesis

The pace and geography of advanced AI deployment will be shaped at least as much by **electricity, interconnection, and load-management capacity** as by model research.
The compute race is turning into a grid-governance race.

## Why it matters

This changes what counts as strategic capacity.
The decisive bottleneck may not be the next model idea but the ability to energize large clusters of chips quickly, legally, and repeatedly.
That pushes utilities, regulators, queue managers, transmission planners, and local permitting regimes into the center of AI history.

## Mechanism sketch

- AI training and inference intensify demand for dense, reliable power.
- The IEA is already describing an “Age of Electricity,” with data centres named as a material demand driver.
- DOE now explicitly links interconnection stress to AI, data centres, large loads, and broader electrification.
- LBNL’s queue data show that U.S. grid interconnection remains slow, backlogged, and failure-prone.

Put differently: intelligence may be software, but industrialized intelligence is **energized hardware in contested networks**.
As cognition gets cheaper, connection rights, queue priority, and power quality become more valuable.

## What this speculation predicts

1. Regions with faster interconnection and generation buildout will gain disproportionate AI advantage.
2. Utilities will start designing load classes, contracts, or curtailment structures specifically for large compute customers.
3. Compute firms will increasingly behave like energy developers, not just software companies.

## Watchpoints

- AI-specific utility tariffs, demand-response contracts, or interruptible-load regimes
- more compute campuses co-locating with dedicated generation or storage
- queue reform framed explicitly around data-centre or large-load urgency
- local politics shifting from “should this data centre exist?” to “what does it owe the grid?”

## What would weaken this

- sustained algorithmic efficiency improvements that sharply decouple capability growth from electricity growth
- queue reform and grid buildout becoming much faster than current evidence suggests
- large compute becoming meaningfully portable across low-power environments without major capability loss
