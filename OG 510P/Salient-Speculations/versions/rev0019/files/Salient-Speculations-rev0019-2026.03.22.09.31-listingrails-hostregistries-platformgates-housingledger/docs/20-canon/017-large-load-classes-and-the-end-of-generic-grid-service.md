# 017 — Large-load classes and the end of generic grid service

**Status:** canon

## Thesis

As very large electricity users proliferate, utilities, grid operators, and regulators will increasingly stop treating them as just bigger versions of ordinary customers.
They will become a separately governed class with bespoke tariffs, minimum-take obligations, collateral, queue discipline, flexibility requirements, self-supply expectations, and special interconnection rules.

The scarce asset is no longer only megawatts.
It is admissibility into a tolerable **service class**.

## Why it matters

This changes the politics of infrastructure.
When exceptionally large loads arrive faster than supply, network institutions stop pretending that one generic retail or wholesale service model fits everyone.
They begin sorting users by system risk, bargaining power, flexibility, and cost causation.

That matters because AI and data-centre growth now hits the grid not as smooth background demand but as lumpy, forecast-distorting, infrastructure-forcing requests.
Once that happens, the real question is not just whether power exists in the abstract.
It is which customer class gets what rights, on what terms, with whose money at risk, and with what duty to curtail, prepay, or self-supply.

This note is related to `001`, but it is not the same note.
`001` says intelligence becomes constrained by power and interconnection.
`017` says the governance response is increasingly to abandon generic service and create special classes for exceptional demand.

## Mechanism sketch

- FERC has opened a rulemaking focused specifically on how **large loads**, generally defined there as demand greater than 20 MW, interconnect to the interstate transmission system.
- FERC separately directed PJM to revise its tariff because the existing tariff did not adequately account for co-located load service, and it explicitly linked the reform agenda to enhanced load forecasting and demand-flexibility measures.
- PJM’s 2025 long-term forecast now describes dramatic demand growth driven primarily by data centres and warns that a capacity shortage could affect the system as early as the 2026/2027 Delivery Year.
- PJM’s large-load process then moved from generic concern to explicit class design: 32 GW of peak-load growth from 2024 to 2030 with all but 2 GW coming from data centres, proposals for new large-load service categories, incentives to bring generation, and demand-response-style flexibility for unbacked load.
- Pennsylvania’s model large-load tariff proposal goes further by spelling out cost-causation measures, contributions-in-aid-of-construction, tiered collateral, minimum contract terms, clustered study timelines, and interruptible or flexible service options.
- Virginia has already approved a new rate class for customers at 25 MW or more and imposed minimum-pay requirements to shield ordinary ratepayers from rapid infrastructure buildout.
- New York is now explicitly reviewing interconnection, cost-allocation, and tariff structures for large loads under a public standard that these projects must pay their fair share or supply their own energy.

The deeper pattern is that scarce shared infrastructure is increasingly governed through **classification**.
When demand becomes unusually large, volatile, or system-shaping, the network stops offering one generic product and starts offering differentiated access packages.
Price is only one lever.
The real levers become queue credibility, collateral, minimum bills, interruptibility, self-supply, and whether the customer is allowed to impose system costs on everyone else.

## What this speculation predicts

1. More jurisdictions will create explicit large-load or special-service classes for data centres and other unusually large customers rather than relying on ordinary commercial tariffs.
2. Minimum contract terms, collateral rules, study fees, exit charges, and minimum-take obligations will increasingly matter as much as the posted electricity rate.
3. Interruptible service, bring-your-own-generation structures, demand-response commitments, or self-supply conditions will become common bargaining terms for very large loads.
4. State competition for AI and data-centre investment will increasingly turn on governance packages — who offers the fastest studies, the most permissive class design, the most forgiving minimums, or the cleanest co-location rules — not just cheap land and headline power prices.
5. Political conflict will intensify around cross-subsidy, stranded infrastructure risk, speculative queue reservations, and whether ordinary customers are underwriting private load booms.

## Watchpoints

- new utility or commission proceedings creating explicit large-load classes, thresholds, or model tariffs
- official rules requiring collateral, contributions-in-aid-of-construction, minimum demand payments, or long minimum service terms for very large customers
- more offers of interruptible, flexible, curtailable, or non-capacity-backed service for data centres and similar loads
- policies that require self-supply, co-located generation, or dedicated resource commitments as a condition of fast connection
- evidence that jurisdictions retreat from special treatment and continue serving very large loads under mostly generic tariff structures

## What would weaken this

- the large-load wave proving overstated, speculative, or too temporary to justify durable class redesign
- rapid supply expansion making bespoke tariffs and allocation fights unnecessary in most jurisdictions
- regulators consistently refusing differentiated treatment and insisting that large new loads remain on largely standard service terms
- large-load governance remaining confined to a few headline cases without becoming a reusable pattern across major electricity jurisdictions
