
# 002 — Insurance as climate zoning

**Status:** canon

## Thesis

Before many legislatures enact honest climate retreat policy, **insurance markets will partially do the zoning for them**.
Premium spikes, nonrenewals, exclusions, and residual-market dependence will quietly redraw where middle-class property ownership remains stable.

## Why it matters

This is politically explosive because it shifts territorial governance into a hybrid public-private channel.
People may encounter climate adaptation first not as a statute, but as a renewal notice, an unaffordable premium, a mortgage problem, or a shrinking buyer pool.

## Mechanism sketch

- Climate-driven losses raise insurer costs and uncertainty.
- Treasury’s granular U.S. data already show faster premium growth, higher nonrenewal in higher-risk ZIP codes, and deteriorating availability.
- WMO and NOAA point to intensifying and costly climate disruption rather than a temporary blip.
- OECD’s 2026 catastrophic-risk report emphasizes that many countries already face large protection gaps and that high-risk regions can experience coverage limits or unaffordable premiums.
- Once insurability weakens, lending, municipal finance, and household mobility all begin to feel second-order effects.

The key move is that insurance is not just compensation after damage; it is a **permission layer for property normality**.
When that layer thins, territorial value changes before politics catches up.

## What this speculation predicts

1. Insurance data become one of the earliest practical maps of climate retreat.
2. States will expand backstops and residual pools, delaying but not removing repricing pressure.
3. Property politics will increasingly turn on whether a place remains normally insurable rather than merely habitable.

## Watchpoints

- growth of FAIR plans and other residual-market mechanisms
- mortgage or appraisal changes tied to insurability rather than just floodplain maps
- municipal fiscal stress or out-migration in areas with repeated insurance instability
- political fights over cross-subsidy versus actuarial realism

## What would weaken this

- dramatic public reinsurance backstops that make private repricing much less salient
- unexpectedly rapid adaptation measures that materially stabilize loss ratios in high-risk areas
- broad legal suppression of risk-based repricing without major insurer withdrawal
