# 024 — Insurable collateral and the end of geography-blind housing finance

**Status:** canon

## Thesis

As climate-linked insurance costs rise, coverage becomes harder to obtain in some places, and mortgage systems keep tightening around flood determinations, remap monitoring, condo-project insurance standards, and secondary-market eligibility rules, the practical ability to buy, refinance, securitize, or keep financing a home will increasingly depend on whether the address remains legibly insurable.

The scarce asset is no longer only borrower creditworthiness.
It is increasingly **financeable collateral whose hazard and insurance profile can survive underwriting, servicing, and secondary-market sale**.

## Why it matters

This changes where housing exclusion happens.
The decisive friction is not only the mortgage rate, the borrower’s income, or the sticker price of the home.
It is also whether the property can clear the insurance-and-hazard filters embedded in mortgage origination, project approval, servicing, and securitisation.

That matters because housing finance is a huge coordination layer, not a narrow insurance niche.
When insurers retreat or reprice, the consequence is not merely a higher monthly escrow bill.
The consequence can be delayed closings, failed condo eligibility, forced-placement risk, remap-triggered coverage hunts, secondary-market ineligibility, or public pressure to loosen standards so transactions can keep clearing.
The pressure point is no longer simply “can this house be insured?”
It is increasingly **can this house still function as admissible collateral inside the housing-finance stack?**

This note is related to `002`, but it is not the same note.
`002` says insurance repricing becomes territorial zoning.
`024` says the same repricing increasingly changes who can borrow, refinance, sell, securitize, or keep a property mortgage-eligible once insurance, flood maps, and hazard-aware collateral rules are inside the financing chain.

## Mechanism sketch

- Treasury’s Federal Insurance Office says homeowners insurance is becoming more costly and harder to procure for millions of Americans, based on a ZIP-code-level dataset spanning more than **246 million** policy observations from over **330** insurers.
- Freddie Mac’s borrower data then shows the financing-side burden. The average annual homeowners’ insurance premium for the covered borrower population rose from **$1,081** in 2018 to **$1,522** in 2023, and by August 2024 the average had reached **$1,761**, with insurance taking a larger share of income and hitting lower-income borrowers hardest.
- OECD’s 2025 real-estate work makes the strategic implication explicit: climate-related risks are highly location-specific, should be made visible in planning and investment decisions, and climate-risk disclosure at moments such as **mortgage issuance** can improve transparency.
- OECD’s 2026 catastrophic-risk report then tightens the link to financeability: because mortgage lenders often require property insurance, reduced access to affordable coverage can have direct implications for housing markets.
- The U.S. mortgage stack already operationalises this. Fannie Mae requires lenders and servicers to determine whether a property sits in a **Special Flood Hazard Area**, **Coastal Barrier Resources System**, or **Otherwise Protected Area** using the FEMA Standard Flood Hazard Determination form; properties in non-participating NFIP communities are in some cases ineligible for purchase.
- The servicing layer keeps the hazard check alive after closing. Fannie Mae servicers must actively monitor flood-map and community-status changes, maintain required coverage with no lapse, and obtain required flood coverage within **120 days** if a property is remapped into an SFHA.
- HUD’s floodplain rule clarifies that HUD or the responsible entity may require flood insurance beyond statutory minimums to minimise financial risk and that mortgagees in HUD assistance or mortgage-insurance programmes may impose additional flood-insurance requirements.
- The March 2026 FHFA/Fannie/Freddie insurance rollback shows why this belongs in the canon. Policymakers are now openly treating insurance requirements as a housing-finance choke point whose tightening or loosening can re-open or re-close parts of the mortgage market, especially in condos and rural areas.

The deeper pattern is that climate risk enters housing not only through damages and premiums but through **collateral admissibility**.
Once insurance availability, hazard maps, remapping rules, project standards, and securitisation eligibility become operational bottlenecks, geography stops being a background attribute of the house and becomes a financing variable that is continuously re-checked.

## What this speculation predicts

1. Mortgage originators, servicers, GSE sellers, and public housing-finance programmes will embed property-level hazard and insurability checks more deeply into routine underwriting, project approval, and servicing workflows rather than treating insurance as a mostly external afterthought.
2. Insurance cost and coverage quality will increasingly function as quasi-underwriting variables for marginal buyers, condo projects, and rural or high-risk properties, even when formal credit models do not treat them as standalone borrower scores.
3. More housing-market disruptions will show up as **financeability cliffs** rather than simple price increases: failed closings, suspended refinance activity, project ineligibility, force-placed coverage, or properties that remain physically usable but become much harder to mortgage.
4. Governments and housing-finance institutions will oscillate between tougher hazard discipline and politically motivated relief, waiver, or standards-softening when insurance scarcity begins threatening transaction volume, affordability optics, or local property values.
5. Over time, housing-market geography will sort more visibly by whether a property remains legibly insurable to the institutions that fund and securitize mortgages, not only by school quality, commute, or headline home price.

## Watchpoints

- new GSE, FHA, VA, state-housing-finance, or insurer-linked rules that make insurance type, deductible structure, master-policy quality, or property-level hazard determinations more decisive for loan eligibility
- mortgage or affordability dashboards that start explicitly surfacing homeowners-insurance cost, hazard exposure, or remap risk alongside rate and payment information
- condo projects, rural markets, or newly remapped properties losing ordinary mortgage access because insurance cannot be obtained or no longer satisfies programme standards
- public interventions that socialise, subsidise, or relax insurance requirements specifically to keep mortgage markets functioning in exposed geographies
- evidence that insurance availability stabilises enough that hazard-linked financeability stops becoming a major sorting mechanism in housing markets

## What would weaken this

- private and public insurance markets stabilising enough that coverage availability and cost cease to be meaningful constraints on ordinary mortgage finance
- lenders and secondary-market institutions continuing to treat property insurance as a minor servicing detail rather than as a recurring collateral-admissibility question
- large-scale public backstops or legal reforms broadly decoupling mortgage access from private-market insurability
- property-level hazard and remap data remaining too inconsistent, too weakly integrated, or too contested to shape everyday origination and servicing decisions in practice
