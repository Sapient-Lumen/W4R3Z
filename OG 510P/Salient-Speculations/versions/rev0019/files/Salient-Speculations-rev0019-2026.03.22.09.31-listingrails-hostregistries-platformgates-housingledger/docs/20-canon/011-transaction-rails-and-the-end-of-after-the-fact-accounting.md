# 011 — Transaction rails and the end of after-the-fact accounting

**Status:** canon

## Thesis

As structured e-invoicing, digital reporting, and accounting-software integration spread, more economically important transactions will be **born as machine-readable events on standardised rails** rather than reconstructed later from private books and submitted in batches.
The transaction becomes reportable by design.

## Why it matters

This is broader than tax compliance.
If invoice data is standardised, routed through interoperable networks, and reused for customs, procurement, financing, and sustainability workflows, then ordinary commercial operations start to run on quasi-public document infrastructure.
That turns accounting software, access points, APIs, and schema governance into control surfaces.

## Mechanism sketch

- The OECD now treats digital continuous transactional reporting as a live global policy family: jurisdictions increasingly require the near-real-time reporting of invoices or transactional data to tax authorities, with digital invoicing as the foundation.
- The OECD’s broader Tax Administration 3.0 work frames the deeper ambition more clearly: tax is to be embedded into the software and devices taxpayers already use, with more than 80% of tax administrations developing APIs and over three quarters of those APIs made public for third-party use.
- In the EU, public-procurement e-invoicing is already established as baseline infrastructure: public authorities must be able to receive and process EU-standard-compliant electronic invoices, and the European standard defines machine-readable semantic and syntactic requirements.
- The VAT in the Digital Age package, adopted in March 2025, lets Member States introduce mandatory domestic e-invoicing under specified conditions and sets an EU timetable in which cross-border B2B digital reporting begins in 2030 and domestic real-time systems converge on EU standards by 2035.
- The Commission’s current eInvoicing programme now goes further than invoicing alone: it explicitly treats eInvoicing as a single-market enabler, plans a new regulation to make the European standard mandatory in public procurement, and is piloting the reuse of invoice data for customs processes and sustainability reporting.
- The Commission’s March 2026 consultation on revising the eInvoicing Directive shows that the frontier is now interoperability and uptake, not whether the underlying direction is real.

The deeper pattern is that once economically meaningful transactions are generated inside approved digital channels, the old boundary between a firm’s internal books and the state’s reporting surface starts to erode.
A firm increasingly needs not just compliant transactions, but compliant transaction plumbing.

## What this speculation predicts

1. More jurisdictions will treat structured invoice data and near-real-time transaction reporting as normal economic infrastructure rather than as exceptional tax-tech projects.
2. Smaller firms will be pushed toward certified or quasi-certified accounting, ERP, and access-point vendors because the ability to transact cleanly becomes partly a software-compliance question.
3. Verified invoice data will increasingly be reused beyond tax for customs, procurement, fraud controls, trade finance, and sustainability disclosure.
4. Political conflict will move toward schema governance, routing identity, outage handling, error correction, data retention, privacy, and vendor lock-in.

## Watchpoints

- new e-invoicing or digital continuous transactional reporting mandates with concrete public timetables
- accounting-software certification, API publication, or access-point accreditation becoming conditions of routine compliance
- invoice-data reuse entering customs, procurement, sustainability, or finance workflows rather than remaining tax-only
- tax and customs systems embedding more deeply into ordinary business software and ERP products
- backlash focused on surveillance, SME burden, interoperability failures, or excessive dependence on a small vendor layer

## What would weaken this

- e-invoicing mandates staying narrow, delayed, or largely optional outside a few jurisdictions or public-procurement settings
- firms continuing to rely mainly on PDFs or hybrid workflows that do not materially change how transaction data is checked or reused
- fragmentation across formats, networks, and national regimes preventing the rails from becoming cross-domain infrastructure
- successful political resistance forcing tax administrations back toward periodic summary reporting and looser post hoc audit models
