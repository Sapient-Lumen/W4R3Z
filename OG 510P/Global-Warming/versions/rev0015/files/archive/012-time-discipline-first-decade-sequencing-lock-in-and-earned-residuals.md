# 012 — Time discipline: first-decade sequencing, lock-in, and earned residuals

Tags: sequencing, timing, priorities, lock-in, triage, residuals

## One-line thesis

The ideal climate solution is not only a portfolio but a sequence: the first decade must be dominated by the fastest, highest-confidence emissions cuts and by decisions that avoid new fossil and infrastructure lock-in, while slower hard-sector transformations and residual cleanup are developed without being allowed to delay near-term action.

## Why this matters

The archive now has the portfolio, the delivery bargain, the managed-decline rule, the scoreboard, the throughput layer, and the observability layer. What it still lacked was a compact answer to a practical question: **what belongs in the first move, what must start early because it has long lead times, and what should wait for residual use rather than governing the whole strategy?**

## Core claim

The timing constraint is brutal. The Global Carbon Budget's 2025 update says the remaining carbon budget for 1.5°C is about 170 GtCO2, equivalent to roughly four years at current emissions levels and gone before 2030 if emissions do not fall quickly.[1] UNEP's Emissions Gap Report 2025 likewise says that annual emissions in 2035 would need to be 55% below 2019 levels for a 1.5°C-aligned pathway, and that higher exceedance of 1.5°C is very likely within the next decade unless emissions fall faster.[2] In other words: **sequence is substance**. A plan that saves its biggest claimed reductions for later decades is not solving the actual time problem.

IPCC's mitigation synthesis explains why near-term order matters so much. Deep GHG cuts by 2030 and 2040, especially methane reductions, lower peak warming, reduce the chance and extent of overshoot, and reduce reliance on later net-negative CO2.[3] IEA's 2023 Net Zero Roadmap update says delayed action to 2030 would make returning to 1.5°C depend on the massive deployment of carbon-removal technologies that are expensive and unproven at scale, requiring nearly 5 GtCO2 removal per year in the second half of the century.[4] The archive implication is straightforward: **residual cleanup must be earned by prior fast mitigation, not assumed up front**.

The first-decade package should therefore be dominated by levers that are already deployable at scale or can cut emissions quickly once enforced:

1. **Triple renewables, connect them, and build grids now.** COP28's stocktake called for tripling renewable capacity and doubling the rate of energy-efficiency improvement by 2030, along with faster coal phase-down and fossil-subsidy reform.[5] IEA's latest electricity outlook says demand is now growing by 3.6% per year through 2030 and that grid projects can take 5-15 years to plan, permit, and complete, with more than 2 500 GW already stalled in connection queues.[6][7] That means long-lead grid work belongs to the first move even if some benefits arrive later.
2. **Push efficiency and electrification before demand growth outruns clean supply.** IEA's COP28 tracking shows that meeting the efficiency goal would require annual energy-intensity improvement above 4% through 2030, yet the world managed only 1% in 2024.[8] Efficient cooling, buildings, motors, and electrified end uses are therefore not a follow-on refinement; they are part of keeping the near-term problem from compounding.
3. **Cut methane and stop deforestation early.** IPCC and IEA both indicate that methane cuts are among the fastest ways to reduce near-term warming pressure, and IEA says around 70% of fossil-fuel methane emissions could be avoided with existing technologies, often at low cost.[3][9] Forest protection likewise beats later restoration in time-sensitive terms because avoided loss preserves an existing carbon stock immediately.
4. **Prevent new lock-in while managing decline.** Coal phase-down, subsidy reform, and retirement sequencing belong to the first decade because every new long-lived unabated asset raises the later removal and political-cleanup burden.[5]
5. **Start hard-sector and CDR learning early, but keep them in their lane.** Clean industrial plants, shipping fuels, aviation fuels, long-duration storage, and durable CDR need demonstrations, standards, procurement, and infrastructure now. But their slower maturity is a reason to start learning early, not a reason to let them displace the fast stack in budgets or attention.

The note's sequencing rule can be stated simply:

- **Do first what cuts fastest.**
- **Start early what takes longest.**
- **Do not let slower residual tools govern first-decade policy.**
- **Judge every later-stage claim against the shrinking cumulative budget.**

IEA's Net Zero Roadmap adds one more important corrective: universal access need not wait. In its net-zero pathway, full access to modern energy by 2030 can be achieved with annual investment of nearly USD 45 billion, a little over 1% of total energy-sector investment.[4] So the archive should say explicitly that time discipline is not permission for development austerity. The first decade must prioritize both rapid emissions cuts and access-compatible clean development.

## Failure modes

- treating every climate lever as if it were equally urgent, regardless of lead time or time-to-abatement;
- using long-run residual tools or speculative removals to justify slower gross cuts this decade;
- starting grid, transmission, and institutional reforms too late because their benefits arrive after electoral cycles;
- funding hard-sector prestige projects while mature methane, efficiency, and clean-power opportunities stay underbuilt;
- confusing "eventual net zero" with a sequence that actually respects the remaining carbon budget.

## Pattern pack

- The first decade is different.
- Fast cuts beat elegant lateness.
- Start long-lead work before it becomes the bottleneck.
- Residuals must be earned.
- Access is part of the first move.
