# 015 — Lead markets, standards, procurement, and coordinated trade rules

Tags: industry, trade, procurement, standards, carbon-accounting, lead-markets, competitiveness

## One-line thesis

An ideal climate solution has to create **lead markets for clean materials and fuels** — with public procurement, advance offtake, interoperable product standards, credible carbon accounting, and coordinated trade rules — so first movers in steel, cement, chemicals, and other hard sectors are rewarded for decarbonising rather than undercut by cheaper high-emissions production.

## Why this matters

The archive already says that hard sectors need special tools, that policy and finance are part of the solution, and that development-compatible delivery matters. What it still lacked was a compact rule for **how clean industrial output becomes commercially normal across borders**. Heavy industry is capital-intensive, trade-exposed, and slow to turn over. If the archive does not specify how early clean materials win real demand and fair treatment in international markets, then its hard-sector story stays technologically plausible but commercially fragile.

## Core claim

IEA’s *Demand and Supply Measures for the Steel and Cement Transition* makes the coordination problem explicit. Steel and cement together account for 14% of global energy- and process-related emissions on a direct basis, yet announced 2030 near-zero-emissions capacity for iron-based steel and cement amounts to only about 10 Mt and 35 Mt respectively — roughly 10% of what would be needed in 2030 on a pathway to net zero by mid-century.[1] On the demand side, publicly disclosed offtake agreements for near-zero steel account for a little under 2 Mt per year by 2030, with a further roughly 3 Mt of signalled demand through buyer initiatives; early production still carries estimated cost premiums of about 10% to 125% over conventional production depending on region and technology.[1] In archive terms, this means hard-sector decarbonisation is not blocked only by invention. It is blocked by the absence of **durable markets**.

IEA’s 2025 Breakthrough Agenda work on steel and cement says those markets will not form cleanly by accident. It says interoperable definitions, standards, and emissions-measurement methodologies are needed so governments and buyers can compare products across borders, and it says public procurement commitments can be a key lever because aggregated demand and firm offtakes help projects reach final investment decisions.[2][3] The archive implication is that standards are not bureaucratic garnish. They are **market infrastructure**.

OECD’s 2025 public-governance work shows why public demand matters so much. Public procurement expenditure across the OECD was 12.7% of GDP in 2023, and OECD says green public procurement frameworks are now in place across a very large majority of countries. But it also says implementation is weak: half of OECD and accession countries do not signal future green procurement opportunities in procurement plans, and only a small minority measure environmental impact in practice.[4][5] In archive terms, this means governments are big enough to move markets, but only if procurement becomes legible, forward-signalled, and accountable rather than declarative.

Trade policy is now part of the same operating problem. OECD’s 2025 carbon-pricing update says countries are increasingly exploring border carbon adjustments and that the EU CBAM moves from transition to its definitive phase in 2026 while the UK plans to start its CBAM in 2027.[6] OECD’s IFCMA programme page adds that the forum is explicitly exploring interoperable approaches to measuring the carbon intensity of products, which matters because border measures, procurement systems, and buyer standards all become more brittle when jurisdictions cannot compare product emissions cleanly.[7] The archive implication is straightforward: **trade shields without standards and cooperation risk turning climate policy into industrial conflict**.

That yields five operating rules for this archive:

1. **Create lead markets on purpose.** Public procurement, advance purchase commitments, product mandates, contracts-for-difference, and pooled buyer alliances should be used to create dependable early demand for clean steel, cement, chemicals, shipping fuels, and other hard-sector outputs.
2. **Converge on product-level accounting.** Interoperable definitions, measurement methods, and chain-of-custody rules are needed so buyers, regulators, and border measures are working from comparable product signals.
3. **Pair demand pull with supply push.** Clean-material markets need both early demand certainty and project-enabling support for plants, infrastructure, and verification systems.
4. **Coordinate trade measures, do not just nationalise them.** Border adjustments, product standards, and competitiveness tools need transparent coordination and comparability or they will raise friction, reporting burdens, and diplomatic resistance.
5. **Keep EMDE participation inside the design.** If lead markets become rich-country clubs without finance, verification support, and fair access to market entry, they will slow global diffusion and deepen inequity.

## Failure modes

- assuming a generic carbon price by itself will deliver global markets for clean materials quickly enough;
- allowing incompatible standards and carbon-accounting rules to splinter the market into non-comparable claims;
- using border measures as a substitute for domestic procurement, industrial policy, and genuine emissions accounting;
- building lead markets only in advanced economies without project pipelines, finance, or verification support for emerging and developing economies;
- treating “competitiveness” as a cover for indiscriminate protectionism rather than for disciplined climate-aligned market formation.

## Pattern pack

- Hard-sector markets must be made, not awaited.
- Standards are industrial infrastructure.
- Public buyers can move first.
- Trade coordination beats fragmented carbon mercantilism.
- Development access is part of market design.
