# 008 — Managed fossil decline: retirement, sequencing, and subsidy reform

Tags: fossil-retirement, coal, oil-and-gas, subsidy-reform, sequencing, political-economy

## One-line thesis

Solving global warming requires not only building clean systems fast but also explicitly managing the decline of coal, oil, and gas so clean additions displace fossil use rather than merely accompany it.

## Why this matters

The archive already has a strong abundance story. What it still needed was a sharper retirement story. Without that, clean deployment can coexist with rising electricity demand, new coal approvals, fossil subsidies, and politically panicked reliability responses.

## Core claim

IEA's Global Energy Review 2025 reports that total energy-related CO2 emissions still increased by 0.8% in 2024 to a record 37.8 GtCO2, even though rapid deployment of solar PV, wind, nuclear, electric cars, and heat pumps is now avoiding around 2.6 GtCO2 per year.[1] That is the archive's warning against complacency: clean buildout is working, but not yet fast enough or politically hard enough to guarantee fossil decline.

The investment picture is similarly mixed. IEA's World Energy Investment 2025 says clean-energy investment is set to reach USD 2.2 trillion in 2025, around double the USD 1.1 trillion going to oil, natural gas, and coal.[2] Yet the same source says China approved almost 100 GW of new coal-fired plants in 2024 and India approved another 15 GW, pushing global coal approvals to their highest level since 2015.[2] OECD's 2025 inventory further reports that the fiscal cost of government support for fossil fuels was still about USD 0.92 trillion in 2024 and remained elevated relative to historical norms.[3]

IEA's net-zero roadmap provides the strategic implication: in a 1.5°C-consistent pathway there is no need for new long-lead-time upstream oil and gas projects, new coal mines or mine extensions, or new unabated coal plants, but the decline in fossil supply investment must be sequenced with the rise in clean energy investment to avoid damaging price spikes or supply gluts.[4]

The archive should therefore treat **managed fossil decline** as a canonical solution component with six operating rules:

1. **No new long-lead fossil lock-in** where the asset would depend on decades of continued high emissions to pay out.
2. **Retirement schedules matter as much as build schedules**, especially for coal power and the most methane-intensive upstream assets.
3. **Subsidy reform should be paired with protection**, using targeted cash support, lifeline tariffs, or public-service compensation rather than bluntly exposing poor households to volatility.
4. **Reliability replacement must precede or accompany closure**, through grids, storage, demand response, efficiency, and dispatchable low-emissions capacity where needed.
5. **Workers and regions need transition compacts**, because unmanaged place-based losses are one of the fastest ways to produce climate backlash.
6. **Success should be measured in fossil displacement**, not only in clean additions or headline investment totals.

## Failure modes

- adding renewables while coal, oil, and gas systems keep expanding underneath them;
- declaring "no new fossil" without a reliability, affordability, and worker-transition plan;
- reforming fossil-fuel subsidies without compensating vulnerable households or firms that provide essential services;
- assuming declining fossil demand automatically produces orderly decline rather than political conflict over stranded assets and regional employment.

## Pattern pack

- Abundance without retirement is incomplete.
- Retirement without replacement is brittle.
- Subsidy reform must recycle political pain into visible protection and public goods.
- Climate success depends on managed decline, not just managed growth.
