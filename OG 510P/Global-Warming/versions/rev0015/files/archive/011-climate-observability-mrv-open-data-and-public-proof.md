# 011 — Climate observability: MRV, open data, and public proof

Tags: observability, transparency, MRV, enforcement, open-data, accountability

## One-line thesis

The ideal climate solution needs public observability as well as good policy: emissions, deforestation, methane leaks, and delivery claims have to be measured quickly enough and openly enough to drive correction before another year is lost.

## Why this matters

The archive now has a portfolio, a delivery bargain, a managed-decline rule, an outcome scoreboard, and a throughput layer. What it still lacked was a compact account of **how the world would know, in time, whether the transition is actually happening**.

## Core claim

UNFCCC's Enhanced Transparency Framework has made national reporting more regular and comparable: Parties to the Paris Agreement are required to submit biennial transparency reports every two years, with the first submission due by 31 December 2024, and those reports cover inventories, progress toward NDCs, policies and measures, support, and adaptation information.[1] That is a necessary floor for climate accountability.

But treaty-grade reporting is not enough on its own. IEA's Global Methane Tracker 2025 says most national inventories still underreport energy-related methane emissions, with the IEA estimate about 80% higher than the total reported by countries to the UNFCCC.[2] In other words, the archive cannot treat self-reported annual inventories as the whole truth when measured data and satellites are already showing otherwise.

The good news is that a stronger observability layer now exists. UNEP says its Methane Alert and Response System became fully operational in January 2024, now shares public data through the Eye on Methane platform, and has catalyzed and verified mitigation across four continents.[3] UNEP's 2025 methane update adds that IMEO has issued more than 3,500 satellite-based alerts across 33 countries, while OGMP 2.0 now covers 42% of oil and gas production and nearly one-third of global production is reporting, or soon will report, at Gold Standard measurement levels.[4] Those facts make a practical point: climate observability is no longer a futuristic extra; for some major sources, it is already operational infrastructure.

Forests show the same pattern. Global Forest Watch says users can view and subscribe to weekly GLAD deforestation alerts showing where tree-cover loss is happening right now in the tropics, and describes cases where timely alerts enabled action within days.[5] WRI's latest forest-loss analysis says tropical primary forest disappeared at a rate of 18 football fields per minute in 2024, nearly double the 2023 rate.[6] That combination means forest governance should be treated as an alert-and-response problem as much as a target-setting problem.

Cross-sector emissions visibility is also getting faster. Climate TRACE says it began releasing monthly emissions data in 2025 with a two-month lag, and by late 2025 its database covered more than 745 million sources of greenhouse gas emissions worldwide.[7] The archive implication is straightforward: an ideal solution should combine **national inventories, independent atmospheric and satellite observation, facility-level and asset-level datasets, and public response loops**.

The archive should therefore add five operating rules:

1. **Use layered observability.** Combine treaty reporting, administrative reporting, satellites, sensors, and independent source-level estimates rather than trusting any one layer alone.
2. **Default to open and frequent data.** Climate information should be public, machine-readable, and updated fast enough to change decisions this year rather than merely explain failure next year.
3. **Tie alerts to named responders.** Methane plumes, forest-loss alerts, and retirement failures should route to specific agencies, operators, or regulators with a response path.
4. **Reconcile inventories against measured reality.** Where national or company reporting diverges materially from observed emissions, the divergence should trigger review, methodology updates, and enforcement.
5. **Use public proof to steer money and permission.** Subsidy reform, concessional finance, permitting priority, and industrial support should increasingly depend on verified outcomes and credible data systems.

## Failure modes

- treating national self-reporting as sufficient when measured emissions clearly diverge;
- publishing climate data too slowly or opaquely to affect operations and enforcement;
- building alert systems without named institutions that must respond;
- counting unverifiable retirements, forest protection, or methane cuts as solved;
- allowing climate finance and policy claims to remain disconnected from public evidence.

## Pattern pack

- Public proof beats paper promises.
- Measure from the sky and the stack.
- Alerts need responders.
- Open data compresses delay.
- Verification is climate infrastructure.
