# 007 — Delivery architecture: abundance, retirement, and development

Tags: delivery, international-cooperation, development, grids, access, implementation

## One-line thesis

The ideal solution is not just a technology portfolio but a delivery bargain: wealthy countries move first on fossil retirement and finance, while emerging and low-income economies get rapid clean-development buildout, energy access, and institutional support.

## Why this matters

rev0001 named most of the tools but left the global implementation architecture implicit. That gap matters because a portfolio can be technically correct and still fail if it does not say who moves first, who pays, what gets built fastest, and how development needs are handled.

## Core claim

The first global stocktake agreed at COP28 already provides a partial skeleton for the bargain: tripling renewable energy capacity and doubling energy-efficiency improvements by 2030, accelerating the phase-down of unabated coal power, phasing out inefficient fossil-fuel subsidies, and driving the transition away from fossil fuels in a just, orderly, and equitable manner, with developed countries continuing to take the lead.[1]

The need for such a bargain is sharpened by current system conditions. IEA projects global electricity demand growth of 3.6% annually through 2030 and says more than 2,500 GW of renewable, large-load, and storage projects are stuck in grid queues worldwide.[2][3] CPI argues that EMDEs need almost USD 4 trillion per year in climate finance through 2030.[4] At the same time, the latest SDG 7 tracking report says over 666 million people still lack electricity access and around 2.1 billion still lack access to clean cooking, which means the climate solution cannot be designed as energy austerity for the poor.[5]

## Canonical operating design

An archive-level ideal solution should therefore allocate responsibilities roughly as follows:

1. **High-income countries** decarbonize fastest at home: retire coal, stop locking in new unabated fossil dependence, build grids and clean power aggressively, and create lead markets for clean steel, cement, fuels, and industrial equipment.
2. **High-income countries and MDBs** provide concessional capital, guarantees, debt relief where needed, and procurement certainty so capital costs fall in developing economies.
3. **Emerging economies** build clean power, transmission, storage, transit, and industrial capacity at development scale rather than being asked to choose between growth and climate.
4. **Low-income and energy-poor regions** get distributed renewables, grid extension where viable, and clean-cooking deployment treated as central climate-development infrastructure.
5. **All countries** pursue methane abatement, because it is fast, cheap in many cases, and globally beneficial regardless of development level.

## 2030 delivery tests

A proposed global solution should fail this archive's standard unless it can show a credible path to:

- much faster growth in clean electricity than electricity demand;
- large-scale grid expansion and queue reduction;
- visible fossil retirement or displacement rather than additive clean buildout;
- scaled concessional finance and lower cost of capital in EMDEs;
- expanding electricity access and clean cooking rather than treating them as separate from mitigation.

## Failure modes

- Asking developing economies to suppress energy use rather than leapfrog to cleaner systems.
- Treating climate finance as a side promise rather than deployment infrastructure.
- Celebrating renewables additions while grids, access, and fossil retirement lag.
- Using trade or industrial policy to hoard supply chains without compensating cooperation.
- Confusing national net-zero narratives with a global burden-sharing strategy.

## Pattern pack

- Development-compatible decarbonization beats carbon austerity.
- Abundance and retirement must move together.
- Grid buildout is geopolitics, not just engineering.
- Access and clean cooking are climate strategy, not charity annexes.
- The ideal solution is a bargain, not a sermon.
