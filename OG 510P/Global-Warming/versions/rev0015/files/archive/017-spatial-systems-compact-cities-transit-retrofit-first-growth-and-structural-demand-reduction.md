# 017 — Spatial systems: compact cities, transit, retrofit-first growth, and structural demand reduction

Tags: cities, urban-form, mobility, buildings, demand-side, land-use, transit, retrofit

## One-line thesis

An ideal climate solution must shape **where people live, how they move, and what gets built** — through compact urban growth, public and active transport, retrofit-first building policy, and whole-life carbon rules — because electrification alone cannot outrun sprawl, floor-area expansion, and car dependence.

## Why this matters

The archive already says to build clean power, electrify fast, improve efficiency, and make clean switching easy. But it still left one powerful emissions driver too implicit: **structural demand**. Cities, transport systems, and buildings do not merely consume energy; they determine how much energy and material throughput a society needs in the first place.

If the archive lacks a rule for urban form and mobility design, it risks assuming that cleaner supply can always compensate for bad spatial choices. That is not true. A city that locks in long commutes, weak transit, detached low-density growth, oversized floor area, and demolition-first construction can overwhelm otherwise strong climate policy with durable demand growth.

## Core claim

IEA's *Empowering Urban Energy Transitions* makes the scale of the operating problem clear. Cities account for around 75% of global energy consumption and 70% of global greenhouse gas emissions, and almost 10% of the increase in global emissions since 2015 can be attributed to urbanisation.[1] The same report says the urban share of world population is expected to rise from 56% in 2024 to around 70% by 2050, adding roughly 1.8 billion urban residents, while urban land area expands by about 1 million km².[1] In archive terms, this means the climate transition is partly a **city-shaping problem**.

The transport side is equally structural. IEA's *Energy Efficiency Policy Toolkit 2025* says private cars and vans were responsible for more than 25% of global oil use and around 10% of energy-related CO2 emissions in 2023.[2] Its *Energy Efficiency 2025* transport chapter adds that transport accounts for around 30% of global energy demand, that road transport makes up nearly 90% of domestic transport energy demand, and that buses move people far more efficiently than private cars.[3] In archive terms, the lesson is not just "electrify vehicles". It is **reduce forced car dependence and shift access toward more efficient modes wherever possible**.

Buildings make the same point from the other side. UNEP's *Global Status Report for Buildings and Construction 2024/2025* says the sector consumes 32% of global energy and contributes 34% of global CO2 emissions.[4] UNEP's 2025 buildings explainer adds that global building floor space is expected to double by 2050 and that emerging and developing economies added 51 billion square metres of new floor space in 2023 alone.[5] If that growth is delivered through weak codes, carbon-intensive materials, demolition-first practice, and sprawling urban layouts, the archive's clean-power and adoption gains become harder and more expensive.

OECD's current city work makes the design logic explicit. Its 2025 report on intermediary cities says compact urban forms, strong public transport, and safe non-motorised mobility reduce emissions and enhance quality of life, while uncoordinated expansion tends to produce car dependence, congestion, and rising emissions.[6] OECD's broader 2025 net-zero summary adds that demand-side policies across buildings, land transport, and food could reduce GHG emissions by 40-70% globally by 2050, yet remain underused.[7] The archive implication is straightforward: **ideal climate solutions must cut structural demand, not only clean up energy supply**.

That yields six operating rules for this archive:

1. **Plan for access, not just vehicle flow.** The right metric for urban mobility is whether people can reach jobs, services, and social life with low energy and low cost.
2. **Treat compactness and transit as climate infrastructure.** Public transport, safe walking and cycling, and transit-oriented development reduce both emissions and household transport burdens.
3. **Prefer retrofit and reuse over demolition and expansion where feasible.** Existing buildings and neighbourhoods are climate assets when upgraded well.
4. **Use whole-life carbon rules in buildings.** Operational efficiency is necessary but insufficient if materials, construction, and demolition emissions stay invisible.
5. **Avoid locking in sprawl during growth.** Fast-growing cities, especially in emerging economies, need support for serviced land, connected housing, grid-ready districts, cooling-efficient buildings, and public transport before high-carbon patterns harden.
6. **Make structural demand reduction a fairness tool, not an austerity story.** Better urban form, shorter trips, safer streets, and efficient housing can improve daily life while lowering energy demand.

## Failure modes

- treating electrification as a substitute for good land-use and transport policy;
- measuring transport success by traffic speed or vehicle sales rather than access, mode share, and energy use;
- allowing building-floor-area growth and demolition cycles to swamp efficiency gains;
- financing roads, parking, and peripheral sprawl while underfunding transit, public space, and infill services;
- framing demand reduction as moral sacrifice instead of as a design problem about convenience, cost, health, and access.

## Pattern pack

- Access beats traffic.
- Compact growth is climate policy.
- Transit, walking, and cycling are energy infrastructure.
- Retrofit first, then build clean.
- Stop structural demand before it locks in.
