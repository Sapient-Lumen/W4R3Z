# 009 — Outcome metrics: absolute cuts, displacement, and access

Tags: metrics, evaluation, emissions, displacement, access, accountability

## One-line thesis

The archive should judge climate solutions by a short scoreboard of physical outcomes and access conditions, not by pledge counts, dollars announced, or intensity improvements alone.

## Why this matters

The datacube now has a fairly strong portfolio, delivery bargain, and managed-decline story. What it still needed was a compact way to tell whether future proposals are actually solving warming or merely sounding climate-aligned.

## Core claim

UNEP's Emissions Gap Report 2025 says the latest round of NDCs has only slightly lowered projected warming this century, with full implementation of NDCs still implying roughly 2.3-2.5°C and current policies around 2.8°C.[1] The same report says emissions in 2035 would need to be 55% below 2019 levels for a 1.5°C-aligned pathway.[1] That is the first warning against scoreboard failure: **announcements do not equal solved warming**.

The physical signal is harsher. The Global Carbon Budget's 2025 update projects fossil CO2 emissions of about 38.1 GtCO2 in 2025 and says the remaining 1.5°C carbon budget is about 170 GtCO2, or roughly four years at current emissions levels.[2] IEA likewise reports that total energy-related CO2 emissions still rose to 37.8 GtCO2 in 2024 even though the CO2 intensity of global economic activity improved by 2.1% and clean-energy technologies are already avoiding about 2.6 GtCO2 per year.[3][4] That is the second warning: **intensity improvement and clean deployment can coexist with record absolute emissions**.

A workable archive therefore needs a compact evaluation layer. Proposals should be judged first by whether they move the following outcome metrics in the right direction, fast enough:

1. **Absolute emissions**: global and sectoral CO2 and total GHG emissions must fall, not merely rise more slowly.
2. **Fossil displacement**: clean additions must be paired with declining unabated coal, oil, and gas use, plant output, or retirements rather than additive growth.
3. **Power-system cleanliness**: the power sector's emissions intensity and unabated fossil share must fall quickly enough to support broad electrification. IEA notes that getting on track requires power-sector emissions to fall by nearly 9% per year to 2030, reaching around 165 g CO2/kWh.[5]
4. **Methane decline**: fossil methane should drop rapidly because about 70% of methane emissions from the fossil fuel sector can be avoided with existing technologies, often at low cost.[6]
5. **Access and affordability**: progress counts as climate success only if electricity access and clean cooking keep improving. The 2025 SDG 7 tracking update still finds over 666 million people without electricity and around 2.1 billion without clean cooking.[7]

The archive should also distinguish **leading indicators** from **real outcomes**. Clean-energy investment, grid spending, queue reductions, standards, and permitting reform matter, but only as evidence that the core scoreboard above is likely to improve. IEA's 2025 investment update makes this distinction vivid: clean investment is rising strongly, yet grid spending remains stuck around USD 400 billion per year and below the pace needed for electricity security.[8]

## Operational consequence

A future proposal should be treated as weak or incomplete unless it can answer six questions plainly:

- How many tonnes fall, by when?
- Which fossil systems shrink, retire, or lose market share?
- Does clean electricity growth outpace demand growth and reduce power-sector carbon intensity?
- Are methane leaks, venting, and flaring visibly declining?
- Does the plan preserve or improve affordability and energy access?
- Are money, permitting, and industrial policy being used as means to those ends rather than substitutes for them?

## Failure modes

- counting pledges, targets, or investment headlines as success while absolute emissions remain flat or rising;
- celebrating cleaner GDP or cleaner electricity shares while total fossil use keeps expanding;
- reporting deployment wins without showing displacement of coal, oil, and gas;
- treating access, reliability, and affordability as separate from climate evaluation;
- confusing leading indicators with the actual atmospheric problem.

## Pattern pack

- Score by tonnes, not vibes.
- Intensity gains are secondary if totals still rise.
- Buildout only counts fully when it displaces fossil use.
- Climate success that deepens energy poverty is not success.
- Leading indicators matter, but atmospheric outcomes rule.
