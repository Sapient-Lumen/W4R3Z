# 016 — Innovation discipline: demonstrations, first-of-a-kind projects, and no-delay learning

Tags: innovation, demonstration, foak, learning, hard-sectors, pilots, r-and-d

## One-line thesis

An ideal climate solution must pair **deployment of mature tools at full speed** with a disciplined public pipeline for demonstrations and first-of-a-kind projects in the technologies that are still not ready at commercial scale — without letting frontier innovation become an excuse to delay fast cuts.

## Why this matters

The archive already says that hard sectors, clean firming, durable carbon removal, and some industrial processes need more than today's mass-market toolkit. But it still lacked a compact rule for how those frontier pieces should be handled. Without that rule, the archive risks two opposite mistakes at once: underinvesting in technologies the world will probably need later, or overhyping them so they displace urgent deployment of cleaner options that are already ready.

## Core claim

IEA's *The State of Energy Innovation 2025* says public and corporate energy R&D spending have been rising by around 6% per year in real terms in recent years, and that direct government spending on energy R&D globally grew again in 2024 to above the USD 50 billion of the previous year.[1] But the same report stresses that the path from prototype to early market adoption can be long, that cost and performance gaps persist after first adoption, and that well-functioning innovation ecosystems do not happen automatically.[1] In archive terms, this means innovation is not a side quest. It is **part of the transition's operating system** for the residual problems the fast stack cannot solve alone.

IEA's *The State of Energy Innovation 2026* makes the next problem clearer: frontier activity is real, but the valley of death is real too. The report identifies more than 150 significant energy-innovation highlights in 2025, 50 upgrades of technology-readiness levels, and more than 80 new innovation policies across 32 countries and jurisdictions.[2] Yet it also says several major first-of-a-kind projects in areas such as near-zero-emissions steel and direct air capture needed emergency funding packages or job cuts to cope with higher costs and policy uncertainty.[2] In archive terms: **the frontier is moving, but it is still fragile**.

IEA's *Races to First in Energy Innovation* is useful because it gives the archive a better mental model than generic pilot boosterism. It tracks 19 technology-neutral demonstration milestones and treats the path to scale as phased: small-scale testing, fund-raising and preparation, construction, and then a finish line that proves a milestone has actually been met.[3] That is a better rule than cheering every announcement equally.

The sectoral stakes are large enough to canonize this. IEA's heavy-industry work for the G7 says technologies at prototype and demonstration stage today account for about 60% of emissions reductions by 2050 in the Net Zero Emissions by 2050 Scenario, while material and energy efficiency account for around 25%.[4] It also says push and pull measures are both needed: direct public support for demonstrations, early commercial projects, and infrastructure, alongside procurement, carbon contracts for difference, mandates, and quotas.[4] And IEA's *Global Hydrogen Review 2025* shows why discipline matters: low-emissions hydrogen is still below 1% of global hydrogen production, demand from new applications is below 1% of total hydrogen demand, and announced 2030 production potential has fallen after delays and cancellations, even though more than 200 low-emissions hydrogen projects have reached final investment decision since 2020.[5] The archive implication is straightforward: **frontier technologies need active learning systems, not narrative inflation**.

That yields six operating rules for this archive:

1. **Deploy mature levers first and hardest.** Clean power, grids, efficiency, electrification, methane abatement, and forest protection stay ahead in money, attention, and political priority.
2. **Run frontier work as a milestone pipeline.** Demonstrations should be tied to concrete technical and commercial milestones, not generic innovation branding.
3. **Fund first-of-a-kind projects as public learning infrastructure.** Some early losses and overruns are normal; the public purpose is to buy down uncertainty, not to pretend the first plant will look like the hundredth.
4. **Pair push with pull quickly.** Demonstration without later procurement, standards, offtake, or infrastructure leaves projects stranded in pilot purgatory.
5. **Require open learning from subsidised projects.** Public support should buy publication of performance, cost, failure, and interoperability lessons wherever commercially possible.
6. **Cull weak pathways honestly.** Not every frontier technology deserves indefinite support; the archive should privilege options that show improving evidence on cost, performance, safety, and system fit.

## Failure modes

- using frontier technology stories to justify slower deployment of mature solutions;
- subsidising demonstrations without standards, procurement, infrastructure, or offtake pathways that let learning continue;
- confusing announcements or small pilots with commercially relevant milestones;
- keeping weak technology pathways alive indefinitely because of political prestige or sunk-cost bias;
- treating innovation as proprietary theatre rather than as a partially public learning process.

## Pattern pack

- Deploy what is ready.
- Learn fast on what is not.
- First-of-a-kind is public learning, not proof of failure.
- Pilots need pull, not applause.
- Frontier tools must earn their lane.
