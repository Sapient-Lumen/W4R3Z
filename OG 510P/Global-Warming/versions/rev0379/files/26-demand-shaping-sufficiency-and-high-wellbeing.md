# 26 — Demand Shaping, Sufficiency, and High-Wellbeing Climate Design

## Claim

The ideal solution is not only a cleaner way to supply the same wasteful demand. It is also a **lower-energy, lower-material, high-wellbeing way of meeting human needs**.

The archive already contained pieces of this claim in buildings, cities, industrial materials, affordability, and transport. This revision makes the doctrine explicit: **demand shaping is not a soft afterthought to supply-side decarbonization. It is one of the main ways to make supply-side decarbonization physically easier, cheaper, and more politically durable.**

IPCC says demand-side mitigation can reduce emissions in key end-use sectors by **40–70% globally by 2050**, and that more efficient end-use energy conversion in illustrative low-demand scenarios can reduce the need for upstream energy by **45% by 2050** relative to 2020 while improving services [S98]. OECD’s 2025 synthesis says demand-side climate policies remain **underutilised** even though they can deliver significant emissions reductions across all sectors, and that the highest-impact policies often work by transforming systems and infrastructure rather than by asking for isolated virtue from households [S97]. UNEP’s Global Resources Outlook 2024 sharpens the material boundary: extraction and processing account for **over 60%** of climate impacts, high-income countries use **six times** more materials and generate **ten times** the climate impacts of low-income countries, and policy shifts that cut excessive resource demand can lower global resource use while improving well-being [S99].

So the archive’s conclusion is now clearer: **good climate strategy should reduce unnecessary demand for energy, materials, land pressure, and high-carbon services while preserving or improving what people actually need — comfort, mobility, nutrition, safety, and dignity.**

For the archive’s shortest distributional version of that rule, see `61-ideal-solutions-service-floors-waste-ceilings-progressive-restraint.md`: the point is to cut waste ceilings in high-throughput settings without confusing that with keeping underserved populations below a decent service floor.

## Why the archive upgrades this lane

The archive already had strong notes on power, buildings, urban form, materials, affordability, and transport. But one cross-cutting hinge still remained under-specified: the difference between **meeting needs well** and **locking in resource-hungry ways of meeting them**.

That gap now matters because:
- supply-side buildout alone can still be overwhelmed by wasteful growth in final energy and material demand [S58][S97][S98]
- compact cities, efficient buildings, repairable products, food-waste reduction, and smaller / shared mobility systems often reduce the amount of new infrastructure that must be financed and built [S97][S98][S99]
- high-income overconsumption and low-income deprivation are not mirror images; a serious climate doctrine should reduce excess where it is high while allowing demand to grow where basic provisioning is still inadequate [S98][S99]
- well-designed demand-side policy can improve affordability, air quality, health, biodiversity, and public legitimacy rather than trading them off [S58][S97][S99]

The archive therefore now treats **demand shaping and sufficiency** as part of the climate solution itself.

## House doctrine

### 1. Service first, throughput second
The right objective is not maximum energy use, maximum floor area, maximum vehicle size, or maximum material throughput. The objective is good service: warm or cool homes, mobility, nutrition, safety, and durable goods.

That means preferring, where context allows:
- efficient, comfortable buildings rather than permanently larger heating and cooling loads
- compact and connected urban growth rather than auto-forced sprawl [S77][S78][S79][S80]
- walking, cycling, buses, and rail where they replace expensive car dependence [S30][S31][S97]
- refurbishment, reuse, repair, and longer product lifetimes where they avoid new material demand [S26][S97][S99]
- food systems that reduce waste and excessive high-emissions consumption without worsening nutrition [S21][S22][S97][S98]

### 2. Avoid / shift / improve is better than improve alone
OECD’s 2025 demand-side review explicitly uses the **avoid / shift / improve** logic and stresses that avoid options are often first-best because they reduce the need for later substitution [S97]. The archive now states that more clearly.

This means:
- **avoid** unnecessary energy and material demand where good planning can prevent it
- **shift** demand toward lower-carbon ways of providing the same service
- **improve** the emissions intensity of what remains

Electrification still matters enormously. But electrifying a badly designed system is not the same as designing a lower-demand clean system from the start.

### 3. High-income overconsumption and basic provisioning gaps require different treatment
IPCC says some regions and socioeconomic groups still require additional energy and resources for human well-being, while UNEP’s resource outlook says high-income countries consume far more materials and generate far larger climate impacts than low-income ones [S98][S99].

So the archive rejects one-size-fits-all “consume less” rhetoric. The doctrine is:
- reduce wasteful, high-carbon, high-material demand where it is excessive
- expand access to shelter, mobility, electricity, cooling, and clean cooking where they are still inadequate [S69][S70][S71]
- design policies so demand reduction among affluent users helps create fiscal, spatial, and ecological room for dignified provisioning elsewhere [S97][S98][S99]

### 4. Infrastructure and system design matter more than moralizing
OECD’s 2025 review says the most impactful demand-side policies are those that transform the systems that drive demand, and that demand-side policies focusing on infrastructure or technology can hold the greatest potential [S97].

The archive therefore favors:
- compact-city and land-use reform over generic anti-car messaging [S77][S79][S97]
- strong building envelopes, passive design, and retrofit delivery over telling households to simply “use less” [S16][S17][S51][S52][S53][S54]
- right-to-repair, durability rules, and reuse systems over hoping markets will spontaneously lengthen product lifetimes [S97][S99]
- food-waste systems, procurement reform, and better information over private guilt campaigns alone [S21][S22][S97]

### 5. Demand-side policy should make supply-side decarbonization easier
Lower and smarter demand means:
- fewer gigawatts that must be financed and connected [S55][S58][S97][S98]
- less mineral and material pressure [S47][S50][S99]
- lower household bills when paired with efficiency and flexibility [S58][S69]
- less land conflict and less political backlash from always-expanding infrastructure footprints [S59][S60][S97]

The archive therefore treats demand shaping as a force multiplier for the rest of the program rather than as a competing ideology.

## Minimum package for this lane

A serious climate program should include at least:
- compact and connected land-use policy where appropriate, so housing, jobs, and services do not force high-carbon travel [S77][S79][S97][S98]
- retrofit-first, envelope-first building policy and passive cooling before defaulting to permanently higher energy demand [S16][S17][S51][S52][S53][S54]
- procurement, standards, and circular-economy rules that increase durability, reparability, reuse, and refurbishment [S26][S27][S97][S99]
- food-loss and food-waste reduction plus support for sustainable healthy diets where compatible with nutrition and local context [S21][S22][S97][S98]
- transport systems that make walking, cycling, public transit, and smaller vehicles genuinely usable rather than theoretically available [S30][S31][S77][S79][S97]
- affordability design so low-demand options are also low-cost and high-quality rather than second-class services [S58][S69][S97]

## What this note rules out

The archive rejects:
- climate strategies that assume supply-side substitution alone will solve unlimited growth in wasteful demand [S97][S98][S99]
- austerity-style language that collapses sufficiency into deprivation [S98][S99]
- moralizing household behaviour while leaving cities, buildings, products, and transport systems badly designed [S97]
- “green growth” storytelling that ignores the material intensity of how services are currently delivered [S99]
- demand reduction framed as universal when many populations still need more energy and material access for dignified living [S69][S70][S98][S99]

## Compression

**The ideal climate solution is not only cleaner supply. It is a better-designed society that needs less wasteful energy, less wasteful mobility, less wasteful floor area, less wasteful material throughput, and less wasteful food loss to deliver the same or better well-being. Demand shaping is not anti-growth asceticism. It is one of the main ways to make decarbonization physically easier and socially livable.**
