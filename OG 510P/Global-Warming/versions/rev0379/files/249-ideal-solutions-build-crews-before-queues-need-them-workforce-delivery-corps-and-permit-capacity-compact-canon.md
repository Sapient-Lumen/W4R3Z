# 249 — Ideal Solutions: Build Crews Before Queues Need Them — Workforce, Delivery Corps, and Permit Capacity Compact Canon

## Claim

Rev0239 turned the climate answer into a governed work queue.
Rev0240 added overshoot discipline and an implementation ledger.
Rev0241 added scarce-capacity allocation.
Rev0242 added ready project shelves and procurement platforms.

The next missing layer is human and institutional capacity before the work arrives.
A shelf can be full, a procurement platform can be well designed, a finance window can be open, and a scarce-capacity allocation can be correct — and the programme can still stall because there are not enough electricians, line workers, installers, welders, engineers, planners, permit reviewers, inspectors, procurement officers, community liaisons, operators, maintainers, data staff, caseworkers, and supervisors to turn the packet into a working clean state.

This is not a soft social add-on.
It is physical climate capacity.
A transformer is not installed by a target.
A building is not retrofitted by a subsidy alone.
A methane alert is not repaired by a dashboard.
A heat-health plan is not implemented by a PDF.
A public procurement platform is not a platform if no one can write, run, inspect, enforce, maintain, and improve the contracts.

Current evidence makes the constraint concrete. IEA's 2025 employment work says energy-sector job growth remains positive, but all scenarios depend on firms' ability to secure skilled labour; in a net-zero-aligned pathway total energy-workforce needs would increase by nearly 15 million by 2035 [S176]. More than half of surveyed firms, trade unions, and educators reported critical hiring bottlenecks, applied technical roles now account for over half of the energy workforce, and around 60% of companies reported labour shortages that put timelines, reliability, and cost control at risk [S176]. IEA also says the number of graduates entering energy would need to rise by around 40% globally by 2030 to prevent the skills mismatch from worsening, at an estimated cost of roughly USD 2.6 billion per year — less than 0.1% of global public education spending [S176]. IEA's transmission-grid work adds a concrete grid version: around 8 million people already construct, maintain, and operate grids worldwide, and that workforce needs to rise by about 1.5 million by 2030 under today's policy settings [S178]. IRENA and ILO estimate at least 16.6 million renewable-energy jobs globally in 2024, while warning that the human side of renewable deployment is too often overlooked or taken for granted [S63]. OECD's 2025 employment-and-skills review shows the policy shape: strong green-transition workforce strategies link climate goals to skills systems, adapt vocational education and apprenticeships, support worker mobility, target regions and high-emission sectors, consult social partners, and monitor implementation [S259]. ILO's 2025 green-employment diagnostic guidance adds the developing-country and informal-economy discipline: labour-market evidence, sector prioritisation, task-based occupation analysis, geospatial targeting, and social dialogue are needed before countries can plan just transition pathways honestly [S260].

So the next discipline is simple: **build the crews and delivery institutions before the queue calls for them.**

## Fast rule

**A serious climate programme should carry a crew-and-capability plan beside every work queue, shelf, procurement platform, and bottleneck allocation. It should know which occupations, public offices, inspectors, operators, maintainers, suppliers, training seats, certifications, safety systems, and local delivery partners are needed; which are already scarce; how fast they can be grown; and which packets must be sequenced, simplified, or redesigned because the necessary human capacity is not yet real.** [S62][S63][S132][S165][S170][S176][S178][S179][S197][S228][S247][S248][S259][S260]

## Why this note is not redundant

- `18` says **delivery capacity, permitting, workforce, and social licence are part of the climate solution**.
- `170` says **solutions should prefer broad, growable capability and short training ladders**.
- `228` says **planning, permitting, procurement, and workforce throughput set climate speed at mass scale**.
- `247` says **keep high-value packets ready before finance or capacity windows open**.
- `248` says **make repeated needs buyable through procurement platforms and service contracts**.
- this note says **none of those layers is executable unless the programme has a living crew and public-capacity plan**.

A project shelf is not enough if the shelf assumes labour that does not exist.
A procurement platform is not enough if it creates demand spikes that destroy quality.
A finance facility is not enough if it cannot find inspected projects, qualified installers, or operating teams.
A fast permitting reform is not enough if permit offices, environmental reviewers, grid planners, and inspectors are understaffed.

House rule: **do not call a packet ready if the needed people are imaginary.**

## The compact canon

### 1. Count person-capacity as real capacity
The programme should count labour, institutional staff time, inspection capacity, and community-interface capacity the way it counts megawatts, transformers, dollars, and tonnes.

For each major queue, ask:
- which occupations are required
- how many person-hours are needed
- which certifications or licenses are required
- how long training takes
- which workers can transfer from adjacent trades
- which roles are already scarce
- which public offices are a throughput gate
- which contractors or suppliers can actually perform
- which operating and maintenance roles continue after installation

House rule: **the schedule is not real until the person-hours are real.**

### 2. Distinguish five capacity pools
A climate programme needs at least five different kinds of human capacity:

| Capacity pool | Examples | Failure if missing |
|---|---|---|
| Build crews | electricians, line workers, welders, installers, civil works, HVAC, retrofit crews | projects approved but not built |
| Public delivery offices | planners, permit reviewers, procurement staff, inspectors, grid-queue staff, local authorities | files stall or quality collapses |
| Operating crews | control-room operators, grid operators, building managers, transit operators, water-system operators | assets exist but service is unreliable |
| Maintenance and repair | technicians, spare-parts logistics, warranty teams, methane-response crews, refrigerant recovery, retrofit callbacks | first-year gains decay |
| Social and user support | caseworkers, tenant support, community liaisons, trainers, translators, grievance staff | technically correct work loses legitimacy or uptake |

House rule: **installation labour is only one part of delivery capacity.**

### 3. Attach training seats to the project shelf
A project shelf should not merely list projects.
It should produce a rolling forecast of the skills, certifications, and crews implied by those projects.

For each shelf item, the owner should know:
- whether the needed labour exists locally
- whether existing trades can be upskilled
- whether training providers have capacity
- when apprentices or trainees can become productive
- whether procurement calendars will cause stop-start hiring
- whether wages, safety, and working conditions are sufficient to retain people
- whether disadvantaged groups and affected workers have a real pathway in

House rule: **a shelf item should create a training signal before it creates a labour panic.**

### 4. Use short ladders and transferable certification where honest
Do not design routine decarbonization around rare specialists when a safe broader pathway exists.
Prefer:
- add-on modules for existing trades
- stackable credentials
- recognition of prior learning
- common regional skills standards
- train-the-trainer systems
- supervised pathways from assistant to certified role
- cross-certification among adjacent occupations
- mobile training units where local access is weak

But do not fake qualification where safety, reliability, or public trust requires deep expertise.

House rule: **short ladders should lower friction, not lower competence.**

### 5. Make public delivery staff part of the buildout budget
Permit reviewers, grid planners, environmental-review staff, procurement officers, inspectors, and data teams are not clerical leftovers.
They are climate infrastructure.

Capital budgets should carry a delivery-capacity line for:
- permit and review staffing
- queue-management reform
- inspection and commissioning
- digital case management
- procurement templates and legal support
- local-authority technical assistance
- community engagement and grievance handling
- measurement and public reporting

House rule: **an understaffed approval system is a hidden veto.**

### 6. Build local delivery corps and surge teams
Many climate tasks recur across small places that cannot each maintain full specialist capacity.
Use shared or mobile capacity for:
- school and clinic resilience packages
- low-income retrofits
- efficient cooling and clean heat
- methane and refrigerant response
- municipal energy management
- disaster-recovery safer-rebuild packets
- small utility planning
- farm nitrogen and manure services
- local procurement support

A national or regional programme can maintain roving expert teams, train local crews, and leave behind operating routines.

House rule: **small places need shared capacity, not merely devolved responsibility.**

### 7. Make job quality a speed policy
A programme that relies on poor wages, unsafe work, unstable contracts, or thin training will lose people just when scale is needed.
The point is not only fairness.
Poor job quality becomes delay, rework, accidents, low trust, and political backlash.

Procurement and finance should therefore require credible labour standards, safety, supervision, training time, complaint channels, and retention incentives.

House rule: **bad jobs are bad climate infrastructure.**

### 8. Use workers from shrinking systems without pretending transfer is automatic
Workers in fossil, industrial, construction, utility, automotive, and adjacent sectors can carry valuable skills.
But transfer requires mapping, bridge training, wage and pension protections where needed, geographic realism, recognition of experience, and early planning before closure.

Do not tell a worker that a future clean job exists somewhere else in an abstract economy.
Name the occupation, training route, employer, timing, wage floor, and relocation or local-development support.

House rule: **just transition is a placement system, not a slogan.**

### 9. Let software remove paperwork, not pretend to replace field skill
Digital tools and AI can help with scheduling, permit triage, training support, safety, inspection records, grid modelling, and user service.
They should be used.
But current evidence does not support treating them as substitutes for manual applied technical roles in construction, operations, and maintenance [S176].

House rule: **automate friction; do not hallucinate electricians.**

### 10. Score the workforce layer publicly
A serious scoreboard should track:
- training seats by needed occupation
- completion and placement rates
- time from training to productive work
- permit-office staffing and case backlogs
- inspector availability
- contractor performance and rework rates
- O&M staffing and spare-parts availability
- worker safety and retention
- entry pathways for affected workers and underrepresented groups
- geographic distribution of capacity

House rule: **what is not tracked will be discovered as delay.**

## Crew-and-capability ledger in one view

| Climate lane | Crew risk | Capability packet | First proof |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grid expansion | line workers, engineers, protection staff, substation crews | apprenticeship seats, utility training academies, regional crews, long-term plans | queue time and outage risk fall while buildout rises |
| Building retrofits | installers, auditors, inspectors, callbacks | one-stop delivery, approved contractor panels, add-on certifications, quality audits | installations work, bills fall, callbacks are resolved |
| Methane response | detection without repair crews | response contracts, field teams, enforcement clock | confirmed repairs and lower recurrence |
| Cooling access | cheap inefficient units and no service | efficient bulk procurement, installers, refrigerant recovery, demand-response enrolment | safer homes, lower peak load, controlled leakage |
| Public facilities | capex without operations | facility managers, maintenance budgets, backup-power drills | schools and clinics operate during heat and outages |
| Local government | responsibility without staff | shared technical assistance and delivery corps | small jurisdictions complete packets without bespoke reinvention |
| Fossil-region transition | closure without placement | bridge training, wage support, local project shelf, employer commitments | workers move into named jobs or protected retirement |

## What this note demotes

This note demotes:
- project shelves that ignore labour and public-office capacity
- procurement platforms that create demand spikes without quality control
- headline training announcements not tied to the actual project queue
- assuming worker transfer without named occupations, timing, pay, and location
- treating permit offices and inspectors as overhead instead of climate infrastructure
- digital optimism that ignores manual applied technical bottlenecks
- building assets without operators, maintainers, spare parts, and user support

## Compression rule

**Build the crews before the queue needs them. Every climate work packet should carry a workforce and public-capacity plan: occupations, certifications, training seats, public-office staffing, inspectors, operators, maintainers, local delivery partners, job-quality rules, and correction triggers. Otherwise the project shelf is only a shelf, not a delivery system.**

## Rev0261 addendum — crews must be protected from climate hazards while they deliver the transition

Rev0261 adds a safety-and-livelihood condition to the crew doctrine. It is not enough to expand training slots, apprenticeships, permit staff, public delivery corps, installers, inspectors, lineworkers, retrofit crews, and maintainers. The queue also needs to keep those workers safe and paid through heat, smoke, flood, unsafe commutes, school closures, care interruptions, and disaster recovery pressure.

Crew packets should therefore cross-check `285` and `286`: work-rest rules, paid breaks, stop-work authority, anti-retaliation, housing and transport, childcare, PPE, water, shade, sanitation, clean air, wage continuity, procurement deadlines, and after-action correction should be built before the queue depends on heroic labour [S418][S419][S421][S423][S428].
