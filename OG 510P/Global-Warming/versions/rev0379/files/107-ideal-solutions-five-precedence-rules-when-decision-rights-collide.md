# 107 — Ideal Solutions: Five Precedence Rules When Decision Rights Collide

## Claim

The archive already has:
- a one-page answer (`58`)
- an execution-lanes note (`100`)
- a steering-levers note (`101`)
- a governing-clocks note (`102`)
- a tripwires note (`103`)
- a response-modes note (`104`)
- an ownership-map note (`105`)
- and a decision-rights note (`106`)

What it still lacked was the shortest **precedence note** for the question:

**Once the roles and decision rights are named, what should happen when those rights collide under real pressure?**

That matters because many programmes still fail after naming roles and authorities.
They know who owns delivery, truth, protection, or escalation in the abstract, but they do not know which one should lead when speed clashes with safety, when delivery disputes the scoreboard, when a tripwire fires but lane owners still want normal autonomy, or when a cross-lane bottleneck cannot be solved from inside one silo.
A compact archive needed one short bridge from **decision rights** to **order of precedence**.

This note is a **compression note**, not a new doctrinal branch.
Its job is to reduce the archive to five precedence rules that keep a serious climate state governable under conflict rather than only on calm days [S111][S114][S129][S130][S131][S147][S181][S182][S183].

## Fast rule

**When decision rights collide, facts outrank delivery comfort, stop-harm outranks routine speed, triggered escalation outranks normal lane autonomy, mission direction outranks local optimisation on cross-system trade-offs, and return to routine requires independent proof rather than self-release.**

## Why this note is not redundant

- `105-ideal-solutions-five-accountable-roles-in-a-serious-climate-state.md` answers **who should be accountable**
- `106-ideal-solutions-five-decision-rights-in-a-serious-climate-state.md` answers **which decisions belong to which role**
- `103-ideal-solutions-five-escalation-tripwires-for-a-serious-climate-state.md` answers **when routine governance is no longer enough**
- `104-ideal-solutions-five-escalation-responses-after-a-tripwire-fires.md` answers **what operating mode should begin after a trigger**
- this note answers **which right should lead when two valid rights are pulling against each other**

## Five precedence rules

### 1. Public truth outranks delivery comfort on the question of what is happening
When delivery and the truth function disagree about performance, the independent truth function should control the published score, the metric definition, and the statement of current conditions.

What this usually means:
- delivery can challenge methods through a visible process, but should not be able to edit the scoreboard in order to preserve managerial comfort
- metric revisions should be explicit, versioned, and comparable across time rather than smuggled in during underperformance
- bad news should surface early enough to change action, not after failure has already hardened [S111][S113][S114][S129][S130][S131]

Why it matters:
- if delivery can decide what counts as failure, the system learns too late
- governance without an independent truth signal turns escalation into opinion

### 2. Stop-harm protection outranks routine delivery speed on exposed people and places
When the clean path is becoming unaffordable, illegible, unsafe, or plainly exclusionary for exposed users or communities, the protection backstop should be able to force shielding, safer fallback, or temporary procedural adjustment even if delivery teams want to push ahead unchanged.

What this usually means:
- affordability, safety, and legibility failures should trigger protective correction rather than being treated as acceptable collateral damage
- shielding should preserve the clean direction where possible, not casually reopen a permanent dirty side door
- the protection right is strongest under visible hardship, exclusion, or acute risk, not as a general veto over the whole mission [S105][S108][S109][S117][S141][S143]

Why it matters:
- if routine speed always wins, climate delivery manufactures backlash
- if protection never has operational force, fairness becomes commentary instead of governance

### 3. Triggered escalation outranks normal lane autonomy once a tripwire fires
Once a formal tripwire is crossed, routine lane autonomy should narrow until the incident is stabilised or a correction plan is accepted.
The operations-and-escalation lead should be able to convene the relevant actors, assign an initial response mode, and force a short proof window.

What this usually means:
- lane owners still run their lane, but they no longer get to treat the failure as a private scheduling issue
- cross-lane coordination becomes mandatory rather than optional when the incident has system implications
- escalation is a temporary operating condition with visible entry and exit, not a mood [S111][S114][S147][S181][S182][S183]

Why it matters:
- if routine autonomy survives unchanged after a trigger, the trigger was not real governance
- if escalation has no precedence over normal autonomy, delay remains the path of least resistance

### 4. Mission direction outranks local optimisation on cross-system trade-offs
When a conflict spans lanes, agencies, regulators, or levels of government, the mission sponsor should settle the trade-off.
Lane optimisation should not silently become system direction by default.

What this usually means:
- lane owners optimise inside their lane, but the sponsor decides on cross-lane priorities, ratchets, and unresolved trade-offs
- the centre should not micromanage routine work, but it should decide when one lane’s convenience is blocking whole-program progress
- cross-system conflicts should be surfaced quickly rather than hidden inside local optimisation stories [S59][S60][S65][S103][S147][S180]

Why it matters:
- if no one can settle cross-system trade-offs, the programme fragments into local maxima
- if lane owners effectively set overall direction, strategy gets written by bottlenecks rather than by mission

### 5. Return to routine requires independent proof, not self-release
The role that was under pressure should not be the sole authority that declares the incident over.
De-escalation, reopening of suspended discretion, or removal of protections should require evidence that the problem has actually been corrected.

What this usually means:
- the truth function should verify recovery claims on the relevant metric
- the protection backstop should confirm that acute harm or exclusion has genuinely eased where that was part of the incident
- exceptions, emergency workarounds, and restored autonomy should have explicit off-ramps rather than drifting back by fatigue or political pressure [S111][S114][S129][S130][S141][S145][S148]

Why it matters:
- without proof-based exit, emergency modes either end too early or never really end at all
- self-release is one of the easiest ways for recurring failure to relabel itself as recovery

## Minimal precedence map

The smallest serious precedence map is:

- on **facts**, the truth function leads
- on **acute harm, affordability, safety, and exclusion**, the protection backstop leads
- on **triggered incidents**, the operations-and-escalation lead leads
- on **cross-lane direction and unresolved trade-offs**, the mission sponsor leads
- on **return to routine**, independent proof leads over self-declaration

This does not mean every disagreement requires a constitutional crisis.
It means the archive now has a small answer to the question:
**when two valid authorities clash, who should usually have the stronger claim?**

The most important separations are these:
- do not let delivery define its own score
- do not let speed outrank stop-harm by default
- do not let normal autonomy survive unchanged after a visible trigger
- do not let local optimisation quietly write whole-program strategy
- do not let the pressured actor alone declare recovery
