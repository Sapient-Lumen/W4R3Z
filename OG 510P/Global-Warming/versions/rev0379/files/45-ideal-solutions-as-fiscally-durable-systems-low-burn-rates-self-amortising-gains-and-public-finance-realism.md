# 45 — Ideal Solutions as Fiscally Durable Systems: Low Burn Rates, Self-Amortising Gains, and Public-Finance Realism

## Claim

The archive now has strong answers to what ideal climate solutions should build, what they should exclude, how they should allocate scarcity, how they should stay fail-soft, how they should avoid one-way loss, how they should package measures, how they should compound, how they should correct themselves, how they should move through many venues, how they should stay low-friction, persistent, interoperable, buffered, hard to capture, fast enough to matter, physically real, incentive-compatible, and legitimacy-preserving.

One more rule belongs inside the canon:

**ideal solutions should be fiscally durable.**

That means the climate portfolio should be able to survive lean budgets, electoral turnover, interest-rate stress, and normal Treasury scrutiny.

The archive should therefore prefer climate designs that:
- use public money to create durable capital-stock improvement, future bill reduction, or institutional capability,
- use targeted support to cross affordability or adoption barriers rather than to preserve bad baseline demand forever,
- replace large recurring fossil support with cleaner and better-targeted support,
- and move mature climate action toward standards, procurement, regulated investment, savings-backed finance, or competitive low-subsidy deployment rather than indefinite open-ended subsidy burn.

IEA’s current fossil-subsidy work says governments still spent **USD 620 billion** in 2023 subsidising fossil-fuel use, far above the roughly **USD 70 billion** spent on consumer-facing clean-energy investments, and that many subsidies remain poorly targeted [S139]. OECD’s current fossil-fuel-support inventory sharpens the point: the 2025 Inventory documents **over 1 700** support measures in 52 economies and says the fiscal cost of fossil-fuel support remained around **USD 916.3 billion** in 2024 even after emergency measures eased [S145]. IEA’s 2025 affordability work adds that countries are already using standards, top-up financing, and targeted cash support to replace blunter fiscal support with cheaper long-run structures [S143]. And IEA’s 2026 ESCO work shows that savings-backed delivery models can reduce upfront barriers and shift technical and performance risk away from consumers instead of forcing the public purse to carry every project directly [S144].

This note therefore adds a simple rule to the archive:

**prefer climate systems that can keep winning after the first burst of subsidy enthusiasm is gone.**

## Why fiscal durability belongs inside the ideal solution

### 1. Good climate policy still loses if it cannot survive contact with real budgets

The archive already treats public finance as a hard constraint in many countries, especially in EMDE [S13][S91][S92][S95][S96]. This note adds the general rule:

**an ideal climate solution should not require permanently exceptional fiscal generosity just to avoid relapse.**

There are times when large public subsidy is justified: emergency deployment, infant-market support, worker protection, social housing, public transit, resilience, and access for poor households.

But a portfolio that needs endless broad subsidy simply to make the basics work is fragile by design. It becomes vulnerable to elections, austerity rounds, fuel-price swings, and lobbying campaigns that portray climate policy as a permanent fiscal drain.

### 2. Structural cost reduction beats permanent compensation for a bad baseline

The archive already prefers efficiency, passive design, better urban form, and cleaner electricity because they reduce recurring operating cost as well as emissions [S16][S17][S26][S58][S69]. This note makes the fiscal implication explicit:

**the state should prefer paying to change the structure of demand and supply over paying forever to cushion the costs of an unchanged dirty system.**

IEA’s 2025 affordable-transition work says governments have used minimum energy performance standards and top-up financing to make high-efficiency appliances more accessible, and that Mexico’s standards improved efficiency **without pushing up costs**, while more modest support in Ghana and Senegal can make best-in-class models competitive with less efficient ones [S143]. IEA’s appliance-efficiency toolkit adds the longer-run lesson: **long-term appliance policies can halve consumption without raising purchase cost** [S146].

That is the pattern the archive should generally prefer:
- standards that lift the floor,
- temporary support that helps people reach the better option,
- and system redesign that lowers future public and private burn rates.

### 3. Scarce public money should cross valleys, not live there forever

Public money is often most valuable at the points where markets are stuck:
- early deployment of still-learning technologies,
- low-income households facing liquidity barriers,
- public infrastructure with diffuse returns,
- grid upgrades and public systems,
- project preparation and de-risking,
- and worker or regional transition support where real losses are concentrated [S13][S69][S91][S94][S96].

That does **not** mean the state should lock itself into permanently subsidising mature wasteful demand, broad fossil-price suppression, or high-income adopters who would have moved anyway.

OECD’s 2025 climate-growth work says revenue from carbon pricing or subsidy reform can be recycled to limit distributive harm and support low-carbon investment, and that redirecting funds from fossil-subsidy reform can produce significant gains where fiscal costs are high [S138]. OECD’s fossil-fuel-support work adds that inefficient fossil support should be phased out as soon as possible and better targeted to those most in need so public resources can be freed for functioning energy markets and the clean transition [S145].

So the archive should prefer climate finance that looks like:
- **bridge support**,
- **targeted protection**,
- **de-risking**,
- **institution building**,
- and **capital formation that lowers later costs**,

rather than permanent blanket compensation for an unchanged system.

### 4. Savings-backed and performance-backed delivery deserves more weight

Some of the strongest climate measures do not only reduce emissions. They create an income stream or cost saving that can finance part of the transition itself.

IEA’s 2026 ESCO commentary says energy service companies develop projects financed through **verified energy savings**, reducing upfront investment barriers and shifting technical and performance risks away from consumers [S144]. It also says ESCO markets scale where policy is durable, procurement aligns with performance contracting, and projects use standardised processes [S144].

The archive should read this broadly, not only as an ESCO note.

Ideal solutions should generally value delivery models that:
- recover cost through lower energy bills, avoided fuel purchases, or paid system services,
- tie support to measured performance,
- and lower the amount of pure grant dependence over time [S56][S58][S69][S144].

This is one reason the archive keeps returning to efficiency, demand flexibility, passive design, public transit, grid upgrades, methane repair, and better appliances: many of these measures pay back socially or financially instead of remaining pure recurring fiscal obligations [S11][S16][S17][S56][S58][S69][S146].

### 5. Fiscal durability is part of political durability

The archive already argues that legitimacy and incentive design matter [S138][S140][S141][S142]. This note adds a harder budgetary corollary:

**a climate policy that is always easy to attack as “expensive forever” is politically weaker than one that can credibly say it lowers bills, reduces exposure, shifts spending from bad subsidies to better support, and becomes cheaper to run over time.**

That is why the archive generally prefers:
- bill-lowering retrofits over perpetual energy-bill compensation,
- competitive clean-power deployment over indefinite fuel-price suppression,
- targeted social tariffs and cash support over universal consumption subsidies,
- and public procurement or standards that change future markets over support schemes with no glide path [S69][S107][S138][S139][S143][S145][S146].

## What fiscal durability means in practice

The archive’s fiscal-durability rule does **not** mean “small government,” “no subsidy,” or “let markets solve climate change.”

The rule is narrower and more practical:

**when public money is used, does it build durable capacity, lower future costs, protect those who need protection, and create a pathway toward lower recurring fiscal burden — or does it mainly postpone the reckoning while locking in ongoing public burn?**

That can imply different instruments in different cases:
- grants where upfront affordability is the real barrier,
- guarantees or concessional tools where risk pricing is the problem,
- standards where technology is already good enough,
- public ownership or regulated investment where networks are the constraint,
- and targeted transfers where justice requires compensation [S13][S53][S69][S91][S94][S143][S145][S146].

## The archive’s fiscal-durability screen

A future proposal should usually be promoted only if it passes most of the following tests.

### 1. Treasury-burn test
Does the proposal mainly require recurring subsidy outlays year after year, or does it create durable assets, standards, or capabilities that lower future support needs?

### 2. Structural-savings test
Does the proposal reduce future fuel costs, energy bills, repair costs, peak demand, or disaster losses in a way that improves public-finance resilience as well as private welfare?

### 3. Targeting test
If subsidy is used, is it concentrated on low-income households, access gaps, first-mover risk, or genuine hard cases — or is it mostly broad support for everyone including high-income users?

### 4. Sunset-or-glidepath test
Is there a credible route by which support can shrink, become more targeted, or be replaced by standards / market finance / regulated returns as costs fall and markets mature?

### 5. Leverage test
Does each public dollar crowd in durable private, municipal, utility, or development-bank capital, or is the public sector expected to fund the whole recurring cost stack itself?

### 6. Relapse test
If budgets tighten or politics turns hostile for two or three years, does the underlying physical progress keep delivering anyway, or does the system immediately fall back into dirty dependency?

Measures that pass four or five of these tests deserve promotion. Measures that pass only one or two should usually be demoted unless they solve an exceptional justice or emergency-protection need.

## What fiscally durable portfolios overweight

The archive’s fiscal-durability rule pushes the ideal portfolio toward:
- efficiency standards, appliance standards, and building codes that lock in lower future energy use without permanent per-unit subsidy [S16][S17][S51][S143][S146]
- social-housing-first retrofits, public-building upgrades, and targeted retrofit support where public money buys lasting bill reduction and heat protection [S53][S69][S137]
- grids, interconnection, storage, and flexibility markets that lower future system costs and reduce congestion or fossil fallback [S14][S55][S56][S58]
- competitive renewable deployment and clean procurement with declining support intensity as technologies mature [S6][S107][S132]
- performance-contracting, on-bill, savings-backed, or utility-funded models that reduce upfront barriers without requiring the Treasury to carry every project indefinitely [S53][S143][S144]
- fossil-subsidy swaps: redirecting broad support toward targeted household protection, access, and clean capital formation [S69][S138][S139][S145]
- methane abatement where captured gas value or avoided waste can help fund implementation, provided measurement is real [S11][S66][S67]

## What fiscally durable portfolios distrust

The archive therefore distrusts:
- permanent blanket fossil-price suppression as a substitute for structural reform [S139][S145]
- large clean-tech support schemes with no targeting, no sunset logic, and no pathway to lower public cost as deployment scales [S107][S138]
- tax-credit-heavy household strategies that mostly reward actors with the cashflow and tax appetite to act anyway [S69][S137]
- climate programs whose main defence is that governments will always find more money later [S12][S91][S95]
- public compensation that keeps paying for wasteful demand instead of helping households escape it [S69][S137][S143]
- procurement and support systems that privatise upside while socialising long-run losses and performance risk [S129][S130][S131][S144]

## Bottom line

The ideal solution to global warming is not only fast, fair, robust, measurable, and physically real.

It is also **fiscally durable**.

It uses public money where public money is uniquely powerful: to protect vulnerable people, build enabling infrastructure, de-risk the right investments, accelerate learning, and lock in lower future costs.

It tries not to build a climate program that survives only while the subsidy taps are fully open.
