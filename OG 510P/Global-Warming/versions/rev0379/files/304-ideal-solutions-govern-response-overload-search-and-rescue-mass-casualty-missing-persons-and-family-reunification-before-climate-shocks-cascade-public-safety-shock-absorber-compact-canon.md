# 304 — Ideal Solutions: Govern response overload, search and rescue, mass casualty, missing persons, and family reunification before climate shocks cascade

## Claim

The public-safety service floor fails in recognizable cascades.
A flood traps people while roads and cell towers fail.
A wildfire outruns evacuation timing while shelters fill and hospitals receive smoke exposure.
A heatwave raises emergency calls while power, elevators, care staff, and cooling access fail.
A storm produces mass injury, missing people, dead bodies, damaged records, rumor, fraud, and legal deadlines at once.

If the absorber is not pre-commanded, the public-safety system learns the cascade by suffering it.

## Fast rule

**Every high-consequence climate hazard should have a response-overload absorber that pre-assigns search and rescue, triage, accessible evacuation, dispatch fallback, family reunification, mortality management, public-order safeguards, responder protection, and correction ledgers before the incident.**

## Public-safety shock-absorber canon

### 1. Trigger overload early

Do not wait for collapse.
The absorber should open on forecast, warning, threshold, infrastructure failure, call-volume surge, hospital diversion, evacuation order, shelter saturation, utility outage, wildfire spread, flood depth, road isolation, air-quality threshold, or missing-persons surge.

Shock rule: **a late trigger is a decision to make the field discover the emergency first.**

### 2. Pre-rank rescue by survivability and isolation

Search and rescue should combine hazard mapping, call data, local knowledge, disability / care dependency, building type, flood depth, fire spread, road status, shelter-in-place feasibility, and last-known-location information.

The ranking should be revisable as conditions change.

Shock rule: **rescue priority should be explainable after the event.**

### 3. Keep emergency calls from becoming the only intake path

People without phones, charge, signal, language access, hearing access, speech access, trust, or safe disclosure may not call.
The absorber should use door-to-door checks, community organizations, home-care rosters, schools, shelters, pharmacies, utilities, OPDs, older-persons networks, tenant groups, public housing, encampment outreach, and field reports.

Shock rule: **silence is not proof of safety.**

### 4. Separate evacuation from rescue

Evacuation is movement before entrapment.
Rescue is extraction after entrapment.
They need different triggers, vehicles, workers, maps, liability rules, destination capacity, family protocols, and communications.

Shock rule: **a rescue plan is not an evacuation plan, and an evacuation order is not transportation.**

### 5. Pre-build accessible evacuation and shelter-in-place support

The absorber should cover wheelchair-accessible vehicles, paratransit, school buses, medical transport, oxygen / dialysis / device movement, pet and service-animal rules, caregiver travel, elevator outage, high-rise checks, language access, sensory accessibility, and no-car households.

It should also support safe shelter-in-place where evacuation is more dangerous.

Shock rule: **accessibility cannot be improvised after the window closes.**

### 6. Use triage without abandoning dignity

Mass-casualty triage must be trained, transparent, documented, and connected to transport and receiving facilities.
WHO's mass-casualty guidance frames preparedness as a system capacity problem for policy makers, decision makers, and emergency managers; the archive's shock rule is that triage protocols must be rights-aware, disability-aware, and connected to after-action review. [S539]

Shock rule: **scarcity triage is a governance act, not only a clinical act.**

### 7. Keep family information separate from rumor control, but connect them

Family assistance needs verified, privacy-protected channels.
Rumor control needs public information.
They should coordinate without exposing survivors, children, domestic-violence survivors, migrants, detainees, or families of the dead.

Shock rule: **families need answers; the public needs clarity; neither need unverified names circulating.**

### 8. Make missing-persons intake easy, safe, and durable

The absorber should allow missing-persons reports through multiple channels, protect sensitive data, avoid immigration or policing misuse, include shelters and hospitals, support cross-border and cross-jurisdiction inquiries, and track resolution status.

ICRC describes restoring family links as preventing separation, tracing or reuniting relatives, restoring contact, and clarifying fate in conflict, migration, violence, and disasters. [S540]

Shock rule: **missing-persons systems must be designed for uncertainty, not for perfect paperwork.**

### 9. Pre-govern child reunification

Children may be separated from parents, caregivers, schools, transport, shelters, hospitals, foster systems, or institutions.
The absorber should include child-safe intake, identity verification without excessive proof, trusted adults, school records, custody safeguards, anti-trafficking protocols, domestic-violence protections, and child-friendly communication.

Shock rule: **speed in child reunification must not erase safety.**

### 10. Manage deaths as truth, dignity, and legal continuity

Deaths create grief, public-health duties, legal status, benefits, inheritance, custody, insurance, worker-compensation, criminal investigation where relevant, cultural duties, and political accountability.
The absorber should include recovery, documentation, identification, storage, notification, death certification, cause-of-death coding, public reporting, and family support. [S540]

Shock rule: **fatality management is part of recovery infrastructure.**

### 11. Protect responders from overload as a system risk

Responder exhaustion changes decisions.
The absorber should set work-rest cycles, rehab locations, hydration, cooling, clean air, backup crews, mental-health support, family support, decontamination, injury tracking, and demobilization rules.

Shock rule: **fatigued systems become dangerous systems.**

### 12. Use public order to preserve access, not punish exposure

Traffic control, shelter rules, curfews, anti-looting operations, and restricted zones should be narrowly tailored, time-limited, appealable where possible, non-discriminatory, and tied to life-safety access.

Shock rule: **the first public-order question is whether the rule helps people reach safety and services.**

### 13. Keep emergency procurement auditable

Emergency response often needs fast procurement.
The absorber should pre-approve contracts, thresholds, price checks, conflict-of-interest rules, beneficial-ownership checks where feasible, receipts, public logs, and after-action audits.

Shock rule: **speed is not the same as opacity.**

### 14. Track the invisible failures

The public dashboard should not count only rescues, calls, and shelter beds.
It should also track missed calls, welfare checks not completed, doors not reached, inaccessible alerts, paratransit failures, delayed death certificates, unresolved missing-persons cases, responder injuries, public-order complaints, and communities that received no field contact.

Shock rule: **the absorber must measure who was not reached.**

### 15. Close only after families, records, and remedies have a path

Public-safety closure is not when the hazard passes.
It is when missing-persons cases have a route, families have information channels, death records are processed, legal and benefit pathways are open, responders have follow-up, public-order complaints are reviewable, and correction actions are funded.

Shock rule: **the end of response is not the end of accountability.**

## Minimum public-safety shock-absorber ledger

| Question | Evidence required |
|---|---|
| What triggered overload? | Forecast, warning, call volume, hospital status, road isolation, fire spread, flood depth, outage, shelter saturation, missing-persons surge. |
| Who could not call? | Telecom outage, device loss, language barrier, disability, trust barrier, domestic-violence risk, detention, age, institutional setting, encampment. |
| Who was searched or rescued? | Areas, buildings, rosters, local reports, last-known locations, rescue attempts, delays, unsuccessful searches, re-search triggers. |
| How was triage governed? | Protocol, training, transport destinations, disability safeguards, documentation, family notification, review. |
| Who was separated or missing? | Intake channels, reports, shelters / hospitals checked, child cases, privacy safeguards, resolved / unresolved cases. |
| How were deaths handled? | Recovery, identification, storage, certification, notification, public reporting, benefits / legal linkage, cultural handling. |
| Who protected the responders? | Work-rest cycles, injuries, exposure, PPE, decontamination, mental-health follow-up, family support. |
| What changed? | Budgeted corrections, route fixes, roster updates, contracts, training, equipment, data standards, public-order safeguards. |

## What this rules out

It rules out search-and-rescue systems that begin with a blank map.
It rules out evacuation orders that become abandonment for people without cars or assistance.
It rules out family-reunification theatre with no privacy, child safety, or missing-persons tracking.
It rules out mass-casualty triage unconnected to rights, transport, receiving capacity, and review.
It rules out fatality undercounting as administrative convenience.
It rules out public-order measures that protect property symbolism while delaying life-safety access.

## Compression rule

**A public-safety shock absorber opens before response overload, ranks rescue by survivability and isolation, protects people who cannot call, separates evacuation from rescue, governs accessible transport, triage, missing persons, family reunification, deaths, responder safety, emergency procurement, public order, invisible failures, and closure — then funds corrections before the next shock.**
