# 36 — Ideal Solutions as Low-Friction Systems: Defaults, Standardization, and Administrative Simplicity

## Claim

The archive now has strong answers to what ideal climate solutions should build, what they should exclude, how they should allocate scarcity, how they should stay fail-soft, how they should avoid one-way loss, how they should arrive as packages, how they should compound, how they should correct themselves, and how they should move through many venues at once.

One more delivery rule belongs inside the canon:

**ideal solutions should be low-friction.**

That means the clean, resilient, lower-emissions option should usually be the **easier** option to understand, finance, approve, install, and keep using.

It should not depend on millions of people repeatedly doing difficult things under time pressure: finding trustworthy contractors, comparing confusing products, navigating multiple agencies, paying high upfront costs and waiting months for reimbursement, negotiating with landlords, or mastering dynamic tariffs and paperwork just to reach the option society already knows is better.

OECD’s 2025 regulatory work says governments have improved interactions through **one-stop shops** that bring together information requirements, approvals, and payment in a single place, and through the **once-only principle**, where people do not repeatedly provide information government already holds [S118]. IEA’s efficiency-policy toolkit says one-stop shops, renovation passports, expedited administrative procedures, standardised contracts, and streamlined digital processes can reduce transaction costs and barriers to investment in efficiency upgrades [S119]. UNEP’s 2025 cooling work points to the same deeper lesson from another angle: a **passive-first** pathway reduces the amount of equipment, peak demand, and user burden required in the first place, with roughly **two-thirds** of cooling mitigation coming from passive and low-energy solutions [S120].

This note therefore adds a low-friction rule to the canon:

**prefer climate measures that work by defaults, standards, simplification, passive design, and one-stop delivery over measures that only work if households, firms, or local officials repeatedly overcome high hassle, high search costs, and high administrative burden.**

## Why low friction belongs inside the ideal solution

### 1. Friction kills uptake even when the long-run economics are good

Many climate measures look attractive in lifetime-cost terms and still underperform because the path from interest to action is too hard.

Heat pumps are a clean example. The archive already cites IEA evidence that governments need stringent standards, one-stop shops, installer training, targeted rebates, procurement support, and electricity-price reform to accelerate heat-pump uptake [S53]. That package is telling. If clean technologies needed only good engineering and long-run savings, such delivery layers would be optional. They are not optional.

The same general problem appears in retrofits, efficient appliances, flexibility programs, rooftop solar, battery adoption, flood-proofing, clean-cooking conversion, and concessional project finance. High transaction costs, fragmented approvals, split incentives, complex eligibility rules, and high upfront payments repeatedly block measures that are otherwise sensible [S53][S69][S91][S119].

So the ideal solution cannot be defined only by the physical technology stack.
It must also be defined by the **human effort required to traverse the stack**.

### 2. Low-friction systems scale farther because they ask less of attention and expertise

A strategy that works only for well-informed owner-occupiers with spare cash, reliable internet, legal confidence, and time to chase forms is not ideal. It is socially selective.

OECD’s 2025 regulatory work says people and businesses should not have to become experts in government operations to complete necessary tasks, and describes one-stop shops and interoperable systems as ways to save users time, effort, and frustration [S118]. That public-administration lesson applies directly to climate delivery.

If the clean option requires:
- more forms
- more agencies
- more quotes
- more waiting
- more cash up front
- more knowledge of tariffs, codes, and subsidy windows

then the archive should expect uptake to skew toward the already advantaged.

That is not only inequitable. It is slower.

### 3. Defaults and standards often beat information-heavy voluntarism

There are many cases where the best climate design is not to persuade each actor one by one, but to change the baseline so the better option becomes normal.

IEA’s efficiency-policy toolkit says minimum energy performance standards remove the least efficient products from the market, and it highlights information tools such as one-stop shops and renovation passports alongside expedited procedures and standardised contracts [S119]. UNEP’s 2025 cooling work sharpens the same point for heat: making passive cooling mandatory through building codes is described as the **single strongest lever** to shift from purely mechanical cooling toward lower-energy cooling, especially where future cooling demand will grow fastest [S120].

This is a core house judgment:

**where standards, codes, public procurement, passive design, or default enrollment can reliably deliver better outcomes, they are usually preferable to strategies that rely on constant high-attention private optimization.**

That does not eliminate choice. It changes which choice architecture society defaults to.

### 4. Administrative simplicity is also a capital-allocation strategy

Fragmented procedures waste more than patience. They also waste capital.

IEA says standardised contract templates and terms help create trust, reduce transaction costs, and simplify replication, while streamlined and digitised administrative processes and one-stop shops reduce barriers to investment [S119]. This matters because climate action increasingly depends on thousands or millions of repeatable projects, not only a few flagship megaprojects.

Retrofits, heat pumps, distributed storage, resilience upgrades, district cooling, social-housing renovation, and adaptation projects often fail not because the first project is impossible, but because the **hundred-thousandth project is too bespoke**.

The ideal solution therefore prefers:
- standard contracts over handcrafted paperwork when quality can be preserved
- pre-approved designs and vendor lists where appropriate
- financing structures that move at point of sale or on-bill rather than months later by reimbursement
- interoperable data and documentation that do not have to be recreated at every step [S53][S118][S119]

### 5. Passive-first design is low friction by subtraction

Some of the best low-friction climate policy does not simplify a later decision. It removes the need for the decision or shrinks its stakes.

Passive cooling is exemplary. UNEP’s 2025 cooling work says a passive-first, low-energy pathway could lower cooling emissions sharply, save large sums in energy and avoided grid investments, and reduce projected cooling-equipment stock in 2050 by **41%** [S120]. The archive’s broader demand-shaping and building notes already argue for envelope-first, passive-first, compact-form, and shade-first design [S16][S17][S26][S51][S58].

The deeper rule is wider than cooling:
- a well-insulated building reduces the number of later heroic decisions households must make [S51][S53]
- a compact neighborhood reduces the transport burden the household must solve privately [S58][S77][S78][S79]
- a safer floodplain rule avoids later emergency adaptation scramble [S81][S82][S83][S84]
- a cleaner grid reduces the need for downstream compensatory accounting [S4][S5][S55][S56]

Ideal solutions should therefore value **friction removed upstream** at least as much as friction managed downstream.

## Low friction is not the same as paternalism

The archive is **not** claiming that every climate decision should be automatic or compulsory.

Some measures require active consent, local deliberation, or context-specific tailoring. Communities should be able to contest siting, households should be protected from bad products or abusive contractors, and automated systems should not become opaque denial machines.

The low-friction rule is narrower and harder-edged:

**do not make the clean, safe option needlessly difficult.**

Use opt-outs where appropriate. Use guardrails and consumer protection. Keep appeals and human support available. But where government already knows the desired direction, do not express that knowledge as a maze.

**does this climate solution require too much hassle, paperwork, search, expertise, or upfront cash from the people who must actually carry it?**

## The archive’s low-friction screen

A future proposal should usually be promoted only if it passes most of the following tests.

### 1. Default-path test
If people do nothing special, does the system still tend toward the cleaner or safer outcome because of codes, standards, procurement, tariffs, or other defaults?

### 2. Paperwork test
How many approvals, forms, portals, audits, quotes, or reimbursement steps are required before action can happen?

### 3. Upfront-cash test
Does the proposal ask the actor with the least liquidity to pay first and recover later, or is finance embedded close to the point of decision?

### 4. Replication test
Can the proposal be repeated at large scale using standard contracts, standard data fields, standard technical packages, or pre-approved pathways?

### 5. Split-incentive test
Will the design still work for renters, landlords, multi-family buildings, informal settlements, small firms, municipalities, and other actors who cannot solve the problem alone?

### 6. Passive-substitution test
Can better design remove future equipment demand, peak load, travel demand, or exposure rather than only greening a bad baseline later?

Measures that pass four or five of these tests deserve promotion. Measures that pass only one or two should usually be demoted unless they solve an exceptional hard constraint.

## What low-friction portfolios overweight

The archive’s low-friction rule pushes the ideal portfolio toward:
- product and equipment standards that remove the dirtiest or least efficient options from routine sale [S16][S17][S51][S119]
- passive-cooling and passive-building-code requirements that reduce later mechanical demand and peak load [S16][S17][S51][S120]
- one-stop shops for retrofits, heat pumps, resilience upgrades, or clean-energy access where users would otherwise face multiple agencies and contractors [S53][S118][S119]
- standardised contracts, procurement templates, and interoperable data that reduce project-by-project reinvention [S27][S68][S119]
- targeted support delivered at point of sale, on-bill, or through trusted public intermediaries rather than rebate mazes that favor the cash-rich [S53][S69][S71]
- public and social-housing-first delivery, where governments can bundle upgrades and protect households who cannot act alone [S53][S69]
- urban and land-use design that reduces forced car dependence, exposure, and cooling burden before those burdens must be financed privately [S58][S77][S78][S79][S80][S120]

## What low-friction portfolios distrust

The archive therefore distrusts:
- rebate systems that require high upfront payment, multiple quotes, and long reimbursement delays when simpler support structures are feasible [S53][S69][S119]
- tax-credit-only strategies for mass household uptake in contexts where many target households have low tax liability or poor cashflow [S69][S71]
- information campaigns standing in for standards or defaults when the better option is already well known [S16][S17][S119]
- retrofit and appliance programs that assume owner-occupiers are the universal user model [S53][S69]
- adaptation plans that shift too much burden to private vigilance instead of making places, buildings, and infrastructure safer by design [S81][S82][S83][S84]
- climate policy that mistakes complexity for seriousness [S118][S119]

## Bottom line

The ideal solution to global warming is not only low-carbon, high-welfare, robust, corrigible, polycentric, and compounding.

It is also **easy enough to use**.

It wins by making the clean and resilient path the default path more often: through standards, passive design, one-stop delivery, standardised contracts, targeted support that arrives where decisions are made, and administrative systems that do not require citizens to become climate-policy experts just to do the right thing.

That is not a stylistic improvement.
It is a speed, equity, and scale advantage.
