# 72 — Ideal Solutions by Eligibility Form: Presumptive Inclusion, Existing Data, and No Proof Mazes

## Claim

The archive already says ideal climate solutions should be low-friction, service-first, actor-aware, affordable, and realistic about how payment and contract form shape uptake.
It says the cleaner option should usually come through simpler delivery, stronger local channels, standard offers, and support that reaches constrained users rather than rewarding only the liquid and the patient [S69][S71][S80][S118][S119][S137][S143].

One more compact rule belongs in the canon:

**ideal climate solutions should usually make eligible people and institutions easy to identify, easy to include, and hard to exclude by paperwork.**

In plain terms:
- use existing data, existing programme relationships, and once-only information flows where possible rather than making users prove the same facts repeatedly [S118][S119]
- prefer presumptive or default inclusion for clearly eligible groups when exclusion errors are more damaging than modest over-inclusion [S69][S71][S118][S137][S143]
- keep application steps short, local, and close to the point of decision rather than turning climate support into a navigation contest [S80][S118][S119][S137]
- do not confuse “the programme exists” with “the target population can realistically get through it” [S69][S118][S137][S143]

So the archive’s shortest eligibility-form rule is:

**use presumptive eligibility, existing records, once-only proof, and simple local channels wherever possible; do not build climate access around repeated forms, uncertain qualification, and proof mazes.**

## Why this belongs in the canon

### 1. A large share of exclusion comes from administrative design, not disagreement with the goal

Many climate programmes fail long before anyone rejects the underlying service.
The user may want the warmer home, lower bill, safer cooling, efficient appliance, clean cooking connection, or better public service.
But the path is blocked by multiple forms, uncertain documentation, repeated data entry, landlord signatures, fragmented agencies, or unclear eligibility rules [S69][S118][S119][S137][S143].

That means some climate underperformance is really an **eligibility-design failure**.
The support exists on paper, but the route into it is too ambiguous, too repetitive, or too effort-intensive for the target user.

So the archive needs a compact rule against confusing **formal availability** with **practical inclusion**.

### 2. Once-only design is usually better than repeated proof

OECD’s 2025 regulatory work says one-stop shops and interoperability support the once-only principle, meaning governments should not keep asking users for the same information when systems can share it appropriately [S118].
IEA’s efficiency toolkit likewise highlights streamlined and digitised administrative processes, one-stop shops, and standardised pathways as ways to cut transaction costs and investment barriers [S119].

The lesson for this archive is straightforward:
when eligibility depends on facts the system already knows — income bracket, housing type, meter status, tariff class, social-housing residence, participation in another means-tested programme, or basic location / building data — the default should usually be **reuse, not re-prove** [S118][S119][S137].

This is not only a convenience principle.
It is a scale principle.
Repeated proof wastes user time, local administrative time, and political patience.

### 3. Targeting is not enough if the target cannot get through the door

IEA’s current household-affordability work says many governments used affordability policies in 2025, but only about one-third specifically targeted households most in need [S69].
IEA’s 2025 affordability design guidance also says major barriers include high upfront costs, split incentives, and administrative complexity, and that good policy design uses simplified programme design and strong local delivery models [S137].

That means “targeted support” is not yet a complete answer.
A programme can be well-targeted in theory and still badly designed in practice if the intended beneficiaries face high proof burdens, low awareness, or repeated application failure.

So this note adds a narrower rule:

**support should not merely point toward the right population; it should usually meet that population through a low-burden path.**

### 4. Eligibility form is part of justice because burdens are not evenly distributed

Proof mazes do not fall equally on everyone.
They favor actors with:
- more spare time,
- more digital confidence,
- better paperwork,
- stronger landlord leverage,
- more language support,
- and more confidence that a failed application is merely annoying rather than costly [S69][S71][S118][S137][S143].

That means bad eligibility form quietly sorts in favor of the already advantaged.
A climate programme can claim fairness in its budget tables while reproducing exclusion through the application pathway.

The archive therefore needs a plain justice rule:

**a climate measure is weaker when the people most meant to benefit are asked to bear the highest administrative burden.**

### 5. Eligibility form scales from households to cities and smaller institutions

This is not only a household problem.
Small firms, schools, clinics, municipalities, social-housing providers, and weaker-capacity local governments also lose out when support is fragmented across agencies, deadlines, proofs, portals, and bespoke conditions [S80][S118][S119][S137].

A stronger climate design often uses:
- one-stop shops,
- default pathways,
- pre-filled or short forms,
- standard eligibility bands,
- trusted local intermediaries,
- and routes that combine information, approval, and payment rather than scattering them [S80][S118][S119][S137].

That is often better for inclusion, speed, and administrative capacity all at once.

## The archive’s eligibility-form hierarchy

A future proposal is usually stronger when it fits most of this hierarchy.

### 1. Presumptive-inclusion layer
Where the eligible group is already identifiable through existing public or regulated systems, use presumptive or default inclusion where feasible rather than forcing full re-application from zero [S69][S71][S118][S137][S143].

### 2. Once-only-data layer
Ask for information once when possible, reuse existing verified records, and reduce repeated proof requests across agencies and programme stages [S118][S119].

### 3. Point-of-decision layer
Let eligibility be checked and support delivered close to the purchase, upgrade, enrolment, housing, utility, or municipal decision point instead of through a distant, delayed, or fragmented process [S80][S118][S119][S137][S143].

### 4. Local-channel layer
Use strong local delivery models, one-stop shops, utilities, social-housing channels, schools, clinics, or other trusted intermediaries where target users are least likely to navigate centralised complexity alone [S80][S118][S119][S137].

### 5. Soft-failure layer
Where routines are low-risk and the social value of inclusion is high, use short forms, default continuation, or silent-acceptance style approaches for routine steps rather than letting delay and procedural failure act as hidden denial [S118].

## The archive’s eligibility-form tests

### 1. Doorway test
Can the intended user or institution tell quickly whether they qualify, or is eligibility so ambiguous that many drop out before trying? [S118][S137][S143]

### 2. Repeated-proof test
Does the design ask for facts the system already holds or could verify through existing channels? [S118][S119]

### 3. Burden-distribution test
Who bears the administrative burden — the agency or provider that wants the transition to happen, or the constrained user who is supposed to benefit? [S69][S118][S137][S143]

### 4. Local-access test
Can low-capacity users reach the programme through trusted local delivery or familiar channels, or only through remote and fragmented processes? [S80][S118][S119][S137]

### 5. Inclusion-error test
If the design makes a mistake, is it more likely to exclude the people the programme is for than to include a modest number who could have been screened later? [S69][S71][S137][S143]

### 6. Persistence test
Once a user is enrolled or verified, do they have to restart the proof burden repeatedly, or does the design preserve continuity where circumstances have not changed materially? [S118][S119]

## What this rules in

This note rules **in**:
- one-stop shops, once-only information flows, and interoperable programme design that reduce repeated proof burdens [S118][S119]
- point-of-sale or point-of-upgrade support with fast eligibility checks rather than long post-hoc application chains [S119][S137][S143]
- strong local delivery through utilities, municipalities, social-housing systems, community channels, or other trusted intermediaries when target users are least able to navigate complexity alone [S80][S118][S137]
- simplified programme design and targeted support that are actually reachable by low-income households and energy-burdened users [S69][S137][S143]
- climate support designs that piggyback appropriately on existing social-protection, tariff, housing, or regulated-service relationships instead of rebuilding proof from zero each time [S69][S71][S118]

## What this demotes

This note demotes:
- programmes that are technically available but practically unreachable because qualification is unclear and administrative steps are too many [S118][S137][S143]
- designs that ask households or small institutions to repeatedly prove facts already held elsewhere in the system [S118][S119]
- targeted-support schemes that reach mostly the time-rich, cash-rich, or administratively sophisticated because the doorway is too narrow [S69][S137][S143]
- climate support that is fragmented across agencies, portals, contractors, and deadlines without a clear local route through [S80][S118][S119]
- eligibility processes that quietly use delay, uncertainty, or documentation friction as a rationing device [S118][S137]

## One-paragraph compression

**Ideal climate solutions should usually make the cleaner option easy to qualify for, not merely desirable after a bureaucratic obstacle course. Prefer presumptive inclusion, once-only proof, interoperable records, strong local channels, and simple point-of-decision eligibility checks. Do not make households, renters, small firms, schools, clinics, or municipalities repeatedly prove the same facts just to reach support that was supposedly designed for them [S69][S71][S80][S118][S119][S137][S143].**
