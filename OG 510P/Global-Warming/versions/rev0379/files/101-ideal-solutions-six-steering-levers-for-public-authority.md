# 101 — Ideal Solutions: Six Steering Levers for Public Authority

## Claim

The archive already has:
- a one-page answer (`58`)
- a compact package note (`57`)
- an action-grammar note (`99`)
- and an execution-lanes note (`100`)

What it still lacked was the shortest **control-panel note** for the question:

**What few levers should governments, regulators, utilities, public financiers, and other public authorities actually pull to steer a serious climate program?**

That matters because many readers do not first need more sectors, more verbs, or more tests.
They need the smallest reusable picture of **how public authority moves the system**.

This note is a **compression note**, not a new doctrinal branch.
Its job is to reduce the archive to six steering levers that public institutions can actually wield.

## Fast rule

**A serious climate state mostly does six things: build the clean backbone, change the defaults, finance the clean path, retire dirty dependence, protect people and places, and measure then ratchet real performance.**

## Six steering levers

### 1. Build the backbone
Use public authority to build, permit, connect, and govern the core clean systems that private choice alone will not assemble fast enough.

What this usually means:
- transmission, distribution, interconnection, storage, and other grid capacity
- public, regulated, or coordinated buildout where network logic and universal service dominate
- queue reform, siting, procurement, and backbone institutions that let cleaner supply actually connect and spread [S100][S101][S102][S103][S117]

Why it matters:
- serious climate programs fail when the substitute backbone never arrives or arrives too late

### 2. Change the defaults at control points
Use rules, standards, planning, procurement, and institutional defaults to make the cleaner path ordinary rather than exceptional.

What this usually means:
- appliance, vehicle, building, and industrial standards
- zoning, street design, building codes, and public procurement that stop reproducing high-carbon lock-in
- default tariffs, standard offers, interconnection rules, and service designs that make the clean option easier to choose and easier to deliver [S100][S102][S112][S118][S119][S146]

Why it matters:
- public authority should not rely mainly on persuasion when it can reset the default at concentrated control points

### 3. Finance the clean path and lower the burden of adoption
Use public finance, regulation, and payment design so the cleaner option is actually reachable for households, firms, landlords, cities, and poorer countries.

What this usually means:
- concessional finance, guarantees, and lower cost of capital where needed
- bill-based repayment, on-bill recovery, social-housing-first delivery, and other low-upfront-burden structures
- public de-risking tied to public value capture, lower bills, open competition, or other shared gains rather than one-way subsidy transfer [S117][S143][S145][S146][S147]

Why it matters:
- even strong technologies stall when the clean path is cash-heavy, coordination-heavy, or politically read as unfair

### 4. Retire and discipline dirty dependence
Use law, regulation, and public bargaining power not only to add clean capacity but also to close, constrain, clean up, or phase down high-emissions systems.

What this usually means:
- coal retirement, no-new-lock-in rules where feasible, and fossil subsidy reform
- cleanup liability, decommissioning rules, and clear exit logic for temporary dirty bridges
- transition arrangements for workers, regions, and exposed users so retirement is socially and politically workable [S100][S102][S108][S109][S110]

Why it matters:
- the state is not serious if it funds the replacement while quietly protecting the incumbent dirty baseline

### 5. Protect people and places directly
Use public authority as a protection function, not only as an emissions planner.
Treat adaptation, affordability, health, and ecosystem protection as core climate work.

What this usually means:
- heat-health plans, cooling safety, flood and fire protection, resilient water systems, and public-health readiness
- targeted affordability support so the transition lowers exposure rather than deepening hardship
- protection and restoration of forests, wetlands, peatlands, and other living systems that reduce risk and avoid avoidable emissions [S104][S105][S106][S107][S117][S143]

Why it matters:
- legitimacy and human survival depend on visible protection during the transition, not only on future emissions curves

### 6. Measure, correct, and ratchet
Use scoreboards, verification, remedy, and feedback so the program can tell whether it is working and can tighten where it is not.

What this usually means:
- physical-outcome metrics rather than announcement metrics alone
- simple verification where possible and stricter checks where needed
- corrective mechanisms, visible remedies, and ratchets that turn weak performance into adjustment rather than denial [S103][S111][S113][S114][S147]

Why it matters:
- a climate program without correction loops becomes ceremony, subsidy drift, or paper compliance

## What this note changes in practice

When a proposal claims to be an ideal climate solution, ask not only what sector or lane it belongs to.
Ask **which steering lever makes it move**.

If it depends on no identifiable lever, it is probably wishful.
If it sits on one lever while ignoring the others, it is probably incomplete.
If one move activates several levers at once — for example backbone buildout plus default change plus affordability plus measurement — that is a strong sign.

This note therefore sits in a distinct place:
- `99` says what the program mostly **does**
- `100` says how the program is best split into parallel **lanes**
- `101` says what public authority mostly **uses to steer those lanes**
