# 183 — Ideal Solutions: Corrigibility, Feedback, Measurement, and Ratcheting Compact Canon

## Claim

The archive already has strong notes for:
- corrigibility as a deeper design rule (`34`)
- truth, measurement, and enforcement (`133`)
- robustness under uncertainty (`166`)
- incentive compatibility (`180`)
- legitimacy and visible fairness (`178`)
- and the whole-system correction cycle (`150`)

What it still lacked was one joined first-stop note for a front-door question that keeps deciding whether a climate programme can improve fast enough once reality starts talking back:

**what should ideal climate solutions look like when evidence must be able to reveal underperformance, trigger correction, and tighten the better path without reopening the whole strategy from zero?**

This note is not a new branch.
It is a compression note.
Its job is to gather the archive’s corrigibility rule into one first-stop answer so the programme does not keep reopening the deeper corrigibility doctrine, the truth canon, the robustness canon, the legitimacy canon, and the whole-system correction-cycle notes every time a pathway looks promising on paper but needs fast learning, explicit review, and a credible ratchet in live operation.

## Fast rule

**Among physically serious options, prefer climate pathways whose real-world performance can be measured on useful timescales, reviewed by named institutions on a visible cadence, corrected when evidence goes bad, and tightened when substitutes, capability, and public protection improve. Demote pathways that mainly generate paperwork, vague promises, or one-shot commitments with no clear trigger for redesign, escalation, or ratcheting** [S11][S59][S65][S66][S67][S68][S112][S113][S114].

## The compact canon

### 1. Good climate strategy should be adjustable without becoming directionless
The archive does not treat correction as mission drift.
It treats correction as one of the ways a serious programme stays loyal to mission under live conditions.
OECD’s green-governance work centers monitoring and institutional capability as part of effective climate administration, not as optional add-ons [S59].
UNFCCC’s transparency architecture is now explicitly framed as implementation infrastructure that helps Parties refine climate policies and prepare more ambitious NDCs over time [S113].

House rule: **hold direction firm, but keep instruments corrigible.**

### 2. Measurement is only fully valuable when it changes what happens next
The archive already prefers hard MRV, interoperable definitions, and public reporting.
This note compresses the harder rule: information should feed correction, enforcement, or ratcheting rather than just decorate dashboards.
That is why methane measurement tied to response, BTR-style reporting tied to planning, and auditable product definitions tied to standards matter more than reporting without consequence [S11][S65][S66][S67][S68].

House rule: **prefer climate information systems that can cause action, not only documentation.**

### 3. A serious climate programme needs visible review cadences and named triggers
OECD’s Climate Action Monitor 2025 says climate action expanded by only 1% in 2024 across CAPMF-covered countries, continuing the slowdown seen in 2022 and 2023 and staying well below the average pace seen from 2010 to 2021 [S114].
When progress is that slow, leaving weak designs unreviewed becomes costly.
Ideal solutions therefore need explicit review windows, named reviewers, and known triggers for redesign, tightening, or escalation.

House rule: **do not trust “adaptive management” unless the archive can point to who reviews what, on what cadence, and what evidence changes the rule.**

### 4. Ratchets should be legible, not technocratic surprise
Many climate instruments become more valuable when they can tighten as alternatives improve: building standards, appliance standards, methane obligations, procurement thresholds, fleet standards, or managed-decline schedules.
But tightening that arrives as surprise punishment can destroy legitimacy.
The archive therefore prefers ratchets that are announced in advance, tied to visible evidence, and paired with real substitutes and protection where needed [S59][S112][S113].

House rule: **prefer named next steps with visible trigger conditions over vague promises that ambition will somehow rise later.**

### 5. Corrigibility should shorten the distance between weak signal and better rule
A pathway that underperforms for ten years before the system notices is not highly rankable just because the original policy story sounded elegant.
Ideal solutions rank higher when they can detect delivery problems, integrity problems, drift, rebound, or public-burden failures early enough that correction still matters [S59][S65][S113][S114].

House rule: **promote pathways whose weak signals arrive soon enough to be governable.**

### 6. Correction should be compatible with public trust
A pathway that can only improve through opaque technocratic revision, hidden threshold changes, or unexplained burden shifts is politically brittle.
The archive therefore treats procedural clarity, reasons-giving, visible metrics, and practical substitutes as part of corrigibility rather than as separate courtesy layers [S59][S112][S113].

House rule: **prefer corrigibility that is publicly legible, not only technically elegant.**

### 7. The point is not endless fluidity; it is ratcheting without rupture
Some programmes fail by being frozen.
Others fail by changing so often that nobody believes the system has direction.
The archive’s corrigibility rule sits between those errors: long-run direction stays steady, but instruments, thresholds, sequencing, and support can improve as evidence accumulates.

House rule: **make the better path easier to tighten than to abandon.**

## The archive’s corrigibility screen

A future proposal should usually score higher when it passes most of these tests:

1. **Feedback test** — real-world performance can be observed on a useful timescale rather than only inferred years later [S11][S65][S66][S67].  
2. **Review-cadence test** — a named institution is required to review that performance on a visible timetable [S59][S113].  
3. **Trigger test** — the evidence that would tighten, redesign, or escalate the policy is already named rather than left to ad hoc discretion [S112][S113].  
4. **Consequence test** — monitoring feeds enforcement, redesign, or ratcheting instead of producing stand-alone paperwork [S65][S66][S67][S68].  
5. **Legibility test** — affected households, workers, firms, and places can understand why the rule changed and what evidence justified it [S59][S112].  
6. **Stability test** — the instrument can improve without reopening the whole mission or destroying continuity [S59][S113].  
7. **Next-step test** — if the measure works, the next tightening step is already plausible enough to name.  

## Preferred use

Open this note first when the live question is any of the following:
- what ideal climate solutions should look like when learning, review, and tightening matter as much as first-round policy choice
- whether a proposal is too static, too paper-heavy, or too weakly governed to improve when evidence turns against it
- how to think about feedback, public metrics, review cycles, named triggers, and ratchets in one joined rule
- whether a transparency or data system actually changes programme behaviour or only generates compliance theatre
- how corrigibility differs from generic robustness, generic measurement, or generic anti-whiplash design

Then reopen the deeper note that matches the surviving sub-question.

## Compression rule

**Ideal climate solutions should be measurable enough to reveal weak performance, reviewable enough to trigger timely correction, and structured enough to ratchet the better path without strategic rupture or public confusion.**
