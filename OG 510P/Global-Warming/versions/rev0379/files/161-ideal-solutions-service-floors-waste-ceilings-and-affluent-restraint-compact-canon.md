# 161 — Ideal Solutions: Service Floors, Waste Ceilings, and Affluent Restraint Compact Canon

## Claim

The archive already has strong notes for:
- demand shaping and high-wellbeing design (`26`)
- service floors, waste ceilings, and progressive restraint (`61`)
- the political-economy / social-contract layer (`131`)
- funding and burden allocation (`156`)
- and lived experience / everyday fit (`160`)

What it still lacked was one joined first-stop note for a front-door question that keeps reappearing whenever fairness, restraint, and practicality collide:

**what should ideal solutions protect and make easier, and what should they constrain first?**

This note is not a new doctrinal branch.
It is a compression note.
Its job is to gather the archive’s distributive rule into one place so the programme does not keep rediscovering, file by file, that climate seriousness should usually mean stronger protection for essential clean service and stronger restraint on affluent waste, luxury throughput, and dirty excess.

## Fast rule

**A serious climate programme should protect and expand decent service floors first — cooling safety, clean energy, mobility access, shelter, water, health, and developmental catch-up — while putting earlier and harder restraint on wasteful, high-throughput, fossil-intensive, and luxury-heavy demand in affluent settings; design the cleaner path to feel easier for necessities and tighter for excess; and recycle any broad climate revenues visibly back into protection, substitution, and public clean backbones rather than using the transition to squeeze those already below a decent floor** [S97][S99][S138][S141][S143][S145].

- this note answers **the joined front-door question: what should stay protected and what should be pushed back first when climate design has to distinguish need from excess?**

## The compact canon

### 1. Start from protected necessities, not from uniform sacrifice
The archive’s moral starting point is not that everyone should be asked to cut in the same way.
It is that everyone should be brought above a decent floor of climate-safe and materially decent provision.
That means protecting or expanding basic cooling, clean cooking, reliable electricity, warmth, water, health protection, dignified mobility, and developmental catch-up before treating restraint as a symmetric duty across all households and countries [S97][S99][S141][S143].

House rule: **raise people to a decent floor before celebrating uniform restraint.**

### 2. Put earlier restraint on affluent excess, luxury throughput, and dirty convenience
The archive should usually push hardest first against demand that is both high-impact and weakly tied to basic welfare: oversized and wasteful energy use, luxury-heavy material throughput, fossil-intensive convenience where clean substitutes exist, and other forms of affluent excess.
OECD and UNEP both point toward the same direction: high-income groups and richer countries generally have the largest room to cut excessive resource use, while poor and underserved groups often still need more access, not less [S97][S99].

House rule: **constrain excess before necessity.**

### 3. Make the clean necessity path easier than the dirty baseline
It is not enough to say essentials should be protected.
The programme should usually make them easier to secure in clean form: lower recurring bills, lower exposure to fossil-price shocks, less administrative friction, better public and regulated service, and simpler default pathways into efficient homes, clean mobility, and resilient cooling [S138][S141][S143][S145].
If the clean path is still the harder path for basic needs, the archive should treat the design as unfinished.

House rule: **the protected floor should be reachable through the easier path, not only the worthier one.**

### 4. Use pricing and restraint more aggressively where substitutes and slack already exist
The archive does not reject pricing, standards, or hard limits.
It rejects using them crudely where people lack substitutes or are already below decent service floors.
The stronger default is to apply tighter pricing, standards, procurement rules, and ceilings where there is clear waste, visible luxury, better alternatives, or publicly subsidised dirty lock-in to unwind [S97][S138][S143][S145].

House rule: **be toughest where the harm is discretionary and the alternative is already within reach.**

### 5. Recycle broad climate revenues into floors, substitution, and shared gain
Where carbon pricing, subsidy reform, congestion charging, or other broad instruments are used, the archive’s stronger rule is to route the resulting fiscal space back into visible household protection, cleaner substitutes, and long-lived public backbones.
That keeps the programme from looking like generalized extraction and makes the distributive logic legible: necessities are protected, the cleaner path gets easier, and the dirty baseline loses public support at the same time [S138][S143][S145].

House rule: **if broad charges are politically necessary, visibly turn them into cleaner floors and better options.**

### 6. Developmental catch-up is on the protected side of the ledger
At world scale, the archive should treat many forms of additional energy and material use in poorer countries as part of dignified development rather than as evidence of failure.
The climate design task is to make that catch-up cleaner, cheaper, and less lock-in-heavy — not to freeze poorer populations below adequate housing, cooling, mobility, nutrition, or infrastructure while rich-country excess continues on softer terms [S97][S99][S138][S141].

House rule: **the programme must cut rich excess without blocking poor sufficiency.**

### 7. This is a legitimacy rule, not only a distribution rule
When people experience climate policy as tighter rules on luxuries, waste, and dirty rents alongside better protection for essential service, the programme is easier to defend.
When they experience it as abstract sacrifice that leaves high-consuming actors relatively untouched while basics get pricier, legitimacy weakens.
So service floors and waste ceilings are part of implementation quality and political durability, not merely an ethical appendix [S138][S141][S143][S145].

House rule: **fairness becomes credible when protection and restraint are visibly sorted.**

## Preferred use

Open this note first when the live question is any of the following:
- what should stay protected and get easier in an ideal climate transition
- what should be constrained first when fairness and restraint are both necessary
- how to distinguish necessities from affluent excess in climate design
- whether a proposed climate package is squeezing basics instead of cutting waste
- how service floors, waste ceilings, and revenue recycling fit together

Then reopen the deeper note that matches the surviving sub-question.

## Compression rule

**Ideal solutions should make clean necessity easier and high-impact excess harder: protect decent service floors, tighten early against affluent waste and dirty luxury throughput, and use public finance plus visible recycling to move people upward into clean sufficiency rather than downward into austerity.**
