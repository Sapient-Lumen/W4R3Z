# 354 — Ideal Solutions: Protect multifamily, manufactured housing, mobile-home parks, condos, and HOAs before private governance breaks recovery

## Claim

Much housing recovery is written as if a single owner controls a single detached house on a clear parcel. Real housing is messier: apartments, manufactured homes, mobile-home parks, condos, cooperatives, HOAs, shared roofs, common walls, elevators, private roads, wells, septic systems, park utilities, landlord incentives, and association reserves.

FEMA P-85 provides technical guidance for protecting manufactured homes from floods and other hazards [S632]. HUD's CDBG-DR FAQ and FEMA's building-code work show why program design and safe-rebuild rules must be adapted to real housing types rather than abstract households [S620][S627].

House rule: **housing recovery is not ready until it knows who controls the asset, who lives with the risk, who pays for repair, and who can force action.**

## Fast rule

Every housing packet should classify the dwelling governance form: owner-occupied detached, rental, multifamily, manufactured home on owned land, manufactured home in a park, condo, HOA, cooperative, informal settlement, institutional housing, or temporary housing. Each form needs its own recovery path.

## The compact canon

### 1. Residents often lack control over the broken part

A tenant cannot repair the roof. A condo unit owner may not repair common elements. A manufactured-home owner in a park may own the home but not the land or utility infrastructure. A disabled resident may be trapped by an elevator or generator controlled by a board or landlord. Aid that ignores control structure will stall.

### 2. Common elements are service floors

Roofs, stairs, elevators, fire alarms, hallways, drainage, electrical rooms, pumps, private roads, wells, septic, park utilities, laundry, cooling rooms, and mailboxes can determine whether individual units are habitable. They should be inspected and financed as recovery infrastructure.

### 3. Manufactured homes need specific hazard treatment

Siting, elevation, anchoring, foundations, utility connections, wind / flood / seismic exposure, replacement eligibility, park infrastructure, and title / chattel finance issues can all decide whether a manufactured-home resident recovers or is displaced.

### 4. Association and landlord finance can displace people

A repair assessment, rent increase, park closure, insurance gap, or landlord abandonment can convert disaster into displacement. The packet should include repair finance, rent and assessment protections, code enforcement, legal aid, and relocation support when repair is impossible.

### 5. Private infrastructure must be visible to public plans

Private roads, private utilities, mobile-home park systems, condo pumps, and association drainage can break public service floors. Emergency managers need a shared-housing infrastructure map before the event.

## Minimum packet

A shared-housing packet includes: housing-governance classification; resident roster and access needs; common-element and private-infrastructure inventory; insurance and reserve status; landlord / board / park-owner contacts; habitability inspection; repair finance path; rent / assessment protection; manufactured-home anchoring / elevation / replacement path; legal-aid route; and relocation plan if the private governance structure fails.

## Bottom line

The climate housing floor cannot be single-family by default. The cube should force every housing recovery plan to say who controls the damaged asset and who is harmed if that controller does nothing.
