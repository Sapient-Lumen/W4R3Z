# 40 — Ideal Solutions as Hard-to-Capture Systems: Competition, Procurement Integrity, and Low Rent-Seeking

## Claim

Ideal climate solutions should be **hard to capture**.

They should not depend on unusually clean politics, unusually patient taxpayers, or unusually benevolent incumbents.
They should be designed so that large flows of climate money, permits, contracts, and subsidy eligibility are **contestable, transparent, auditable, and hard to monopolise or collude around**.

This is not the same as the archive’s low-friction rule.
`36-ideal-solutions-as-low-friction-systems-defaults-standardization-and-administrative-simplicity.md` asks whether the clean option is easy to use.
This note asks whether the delivery machine itself is easy to **loot, steer, freeze, or rig**.

It is also not the same as the interoperability rule.
`38-ideal-solutions-as-interoperable-systems-open-interfaces-common-metrics-and-non-bespoke-scale.md` asks whether systems can connect and scale.
This note asks whether the same systems stay **competitive, legible, and publicly defensible** as they scale.

## Why this belongs in the canon

Climate transition money is now large enough that bad political economy is no longer a side risk.
If climate delivery creates opaque contracts, stop-go subsidy arbitrage, bid-rigging opportunities, monopoly choke points, conflict-of-interest fog, or hand-built exemptions for insiders, then the transition becomes slower, costlier, and politically easier to discredit.

OECD’s 2025 bid-rigging update states the problem plainly: competitive tendering delivers value for money only when bidders prepare offers honestly and independently, while bid rigging raises prices or lowers quality and is especially harmful in public procurement because it wastes public money and diminishes trust in the public sector [S129].
OECD’s 2025 procurement-implementation review adds that pre-tender and post-contract transparency remain limited, competition in tenders is hard to sustain, risk-based approaches are stagnating, emergency-procurement accountability still needs strengthening, and frequent legal or policy changes can weaken system stability and reduce the attractiveness of procurement opportunities [S130].
The World Bank’s 2025 procurement framework makes the corresponding delivery principle explicit: procurement should achieve value for money with integrity, fit the realities on the ground, include conflict-of-interest and beneficial-ownership tools, and maintain complaint and anti-corruption channels rather than treating speed as a licence for opacity [S131].
IEA’s policy guidance for tripling renewables adds the climate-specific procurement lesson: clean-energy buildout works better with **regular competitive auctions, long-term visibility, realistic price ceilings, and avoidance of retroactive or stop-go policy design** [S132].

So the archive needs a tighter rule:

**prefer climate portfolios that are difficult to capture by incumbents, insiders, and gatekeepers — and distrust portfolios that depend on bespoke discretionary deals, opaque subsidy allocation, or concentrated control over essential transition lanes.**

## What “hard to capture” means here

This note is not anti-state and not anti-industrial policy.
It is anti-**sloppy discretion**.

A hard-to-capture climate system usually has some combination of:
- competitive procurement or auctioning where standardisable goods or services are being bought [S129][S130][S132]
- clear and stable eligibility rules rather than constantly rewritten deal-by-deal exceptions [S130][S132]
- stage-appropriate transparency before tender, at award, and during contract management [S130][S131]
- conflict-of-interest controls, beneficial-ownership disclosure, and credible complaint pathways [S130][S131]
- standard documents, centralised platforms, and repeatable procedures that reduce room for hidden favouritism [S130][S131]
- oversight that extends into contract modification, emergency procurement, and lifecycle delivery rather than stopping at announcement or award [S130][S131]

The point is not moral purity.
The point is to stop climate policy from quietly turning into a transfer system for whoever already controls market access, paperwork, political brokerage, or critical bottlenecks.

## Why ideal portfolios need anti-capture design

### 1. Climate scale multiplies rent opportunities

As public and publicly-enabled climate investment grows, so does the incentive to steer contracts, permits, interconnection rights, subsidies, concession terms, and exemptions toward favoured actors.
That can show up as cartel behaviour, fabricated scarcity, padded prices, under-specified contracts, or retroactive rule changes that reward incumbents and punish honest new entrants [S129][S130].

A solution is not ideal if it needs everyone in the chain to behave well by default.
It is more ideal when ordinary safeguards make bad behaviour harder and easier to detect.

### 2. Procurement and auctions can either discipline costs or institutionalise waste

Public procurement and auctions are not merely administrative tools.
They are climate-shaping institutions.

OECD’s 2025 bid-rigging guidance says tender design matters: authorities should be informed before designing tenders, maximise participation by genuinely competing bidders, define requirements clearly, reduce communication among bidders, choose award criteria carefully, and watch for red flags in bidding patterns, pricing, tender documents, and bidder behaviour [S129].
IEA’s 2025 renewables policy guidance adds that regular competitive auctions, realistic ceiling prices, and monitoring of awarded-project realisation help keep clean-energy procurement investable without drifting into consumer- or taxpayer-hostile overpayment [S132].

So the archive should prefer climate delivery lanes that are:
- schedulable rather than one-off
- standardisable rather than handcrafted each time
- open to genuine new entrants rather than quietly reserved for a small club
- monitored after award rather than judged complete at contract signature [S129][S130][S132]

### 3. Stability matters as much as ambition

Bad climate political economy is not only corruption in the narrow sense.
It is also policy design that invites rent extraction because it is erratic, bespoke, and impossible to plan around.

OECD’s 2025 procurement report says frequent legal and policy changes can reduce system stability and the attractiveness of procurement opportunities [S130].
IEA’s renewables guidance says countries should provide long-term auction schedules and avoid stop-go or retroactive policy changes [S132].

That means ideal portfolios should prefer:
- long-term auction or procurement calendars
- transparent capacity-allocation rules
- durable criteria that do not change without notice
- pre-announced community-benefit and local-content rules where used
- fewer ad hoc exemptions and emergency-only pathways becoming the normal route [S130][S132]

### 4. Integrity has to cover the whole lifecycle

A climate project can be clean at award and dirty in execution.
Contract amendments, emergency exceptions, conflict-of-interest failures, thin audit trails, or poor complaint handling can still turn the project into a value-for-money and legitimacy failure.

OECD’s 2025 procurement review says pre-tender and post-contract transparency remain limited, emergency-procurement oversight needs strengthening, and quality data across the lifecycle is still needed to support performance measurement and better choices [S130].
The World Bank’s 2025 framework similarly bakes integrity into the broader procurement system through anti-corruption rules, conflict-of-interest guidance, beneficial-ownership disclosure requirements, complaints processes, and procurement planning tools [S131].

So the archive should treat integrity as a **lifecycle property**, not a press-release property.

### 5. Anti-capture design protects legitimacy and speed together

A climate program that looks captured becomes easier to stall.
Once people think climate procurement is a racket, opposition can recruit from many directions at once: fiscal conservatives, anti-corruption advocates, communities that feel excluded, and households that mainly see rising costs.

A hard-to-capture design therefore helps with more than ethics.
It helps preserve the coalition needed to keep building.

## The archive’s anti-capture screen

A future proposal should usually be promoted only if it passes most of the following tests.

### 1. Contestability test
Can more than a tiny set of incumbent actors realistically bid, build, supply, finance, or interconnect — or has the design quietly created a narrow gatekeeper class?

### 2. Transparency test
Can outsiders see the main rules, award logic, key contract terms, change orders, progress, and complaint channels without needing insider access?

### 3. Stability test
Are the rules predictable enough that honest entrants can invest and plan, or is the scheme mainly a series of discretionary exceptions and retroactive rewrites?

### 4. Integrity-controls test
Are there conflict-of-interest rules, beneficial-ownership disclosure, complaint paths, and anti-collusion safeguards where money or permits are being allocated? [S129][S131]

### 5. Lifecycle-oversight test
Does scrutiny continue through implementation, amendment, operation, and audit — or does oversight stop once the ribbon-cutting photo is taken? [S130][S131]

### 6. Public-defensibility test
Could a reasonable outsider understand why this supplier, project, subsidy, or tariff treatment won — and why the public should believe the process was fair?

Measures that pass these tests deserve promotion. Measures that depend on hidden discretion, insider translation, or permanent exception-handling should be demoted.

## What hard-to-capture portfolios overweight

The anti-capture rule pushes the ideal portfolio toward the following lanes.

### Competitive and regular procurement where feasible
Use regular auctions, framework agreements, or competitive tenders when the state is buying standardisable capacity, services, or equipment — especially in power, retrofit programs, public fleets, public buildings, and some adaptation contracts [S129][S130][S132].

### Standard documents and common platforms
Use standard procurement documents, common disclosure formats, centralised portals, and repeatable complaint channels so every deal is not reinvented in a fog [S130][S131].

### Clear public-interest conditions
Where local-content rules, resilience requirements, or community-benefit provisions are justified, define them transparently and prospectively rather than bargaining them privately project by project [S130][S132].

### Open complaint and audit channels
Make it easy for bidders, communities, journalists, watchdogs, and oversight bodies to detect and contest collusion, conflicts, non-performance, and unjustified amendments [S129][S130][S131].

### Procurement professionalism
Treat procurement as a skilled climate institution, not clerical back office. OECD’s 2025 implementation review says procurement professionalisation is expanding but still incomplete [S130].
A climate program run through weakly trained procurement systems will leak money and legitimacy.

## What this note rules out

The archive rejects:
- climate delivery architectures that depend mainly on bespoke negotiated deals when competitive or standardised routes are available
- retroactive policy changes and stop-go auction design that reward insiders and scare off honest entrants [S130][S132]
- public procurement with weak conflict-of-interest controls, poor complaint handling, or limited post-award transparency [S130][S131]
- emergency exemptions becoming the normal operating model for transition delivery [S130]
- concentration and gatekeeping treated as harmless so long as project headlines remain impressive

## Compression

**Ideal climate solutions should be hard to hijack: buy and allocate through competitive, transparent, auditable, low-discretion systems wherever feasible, so the transition scales as public infrastructure rather than as a long-lived rent machine.**
