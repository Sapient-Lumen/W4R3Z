# 239 — Ideal Solutions: At Rising-Load Scale, Efficiency, Retrofits, and Passive Demand Reduction Set a Material Part of Grid Size, Bill Pressure, and Capital Needs Compact Canon

## Claim

The archive already has:
- a deeper buildings note (`16`)
- a deeper clean-power note (`17`)
- a deeper cooling note (`07`)
- a demand-shaping note (`26`)
- a biophysical-thrift front-door note (`173`)
- and, more recently, front-door tempo notes for grids, finance, institutions, uptake, protection, hardware, methane, nitrogen, cooling, flexibility, water, retreat, and managed fossil decline (`226`–`238`)

That is already enough to say efficiency matters.
What it still lacked was one smallest front-door note for a now-practical question:

**once electricity demand is rising because of cooling, electrification, and new digital loads, what determines how much generation, grid, storage, and bill burden the programme actually has to carry?**

This note is not a new branch.
It is a compression note.
Its job is to make one practical truth harder to miss at the front door:

**at rising-load scale, efficiency, retrofits, and passive demand reduction set a material part of grid size, bill pressure, and capital needs.**

## Fast rule

**Do not treat efficiency as downstream housekeeping after supply planning. Treat efficient equipment, building envelopes, passive cooling, appliance standards, retrofits, and other same-service demand reductions as capacity resources that shrink peak growth, lower bills, and reduce how much clean generation, wires, and backup the programme has to build to deliver the same human service** [S237][S238][S239][S240].

## The compact canon

### 1. Avoided load is now a real climate-speed resource
IEA’s 2025 efficiency work says global energy-efficiency progress is set to improve to **1.8%** in 2025, up from around **1%** in 2024, but still far below the COP28 ambition to reach **4%** annual improvement by 2030 [S237].
IEA also says that without efficiency gains since 2010, today’s greenhouse gas emissions would be **20% higher**, and that efficiency actions since 2000 have reduced household energy bills in advanced economies by up to **20%** [S237].
That means efficiency is not just a side benefit of good design.
It is already doing work that the atmosphere, households, and energy systems would otherwise have to absorb.

House rule: **treat avoided load as a real climate-speed resource, not as optional polish after the “real” infrastructure is chosen.**

### 2. Slow efficiency progress now enlarges every other bottleneck
The same IEA report says global efficiency progress has averaged only **1.3%** per year since 2019, barely above half of the COP28 target, while electricity demand has been growing much faster than overall energy demand [S237].
In other words, when efficiency underperforms, the programme does not merely miss a nice extra.
It makes the grid lane, the clean-generation lane, the flexibility lane, and the affordability lane all work harder.

House rule: **every unit of demand you do not have to serve is also grid, capital, and delivery pressure you do not have to create.**

### 3. Cooling now makes efficiency and passive relief impossible to treat as secondary
IEA says energy for space cooling has seen the fastest growth of any end-use in buildings since 2000, growing over **4% per year** [S237].
It adds that if every air conditioner bought since 2019 had been the most efficient model available, the world could have avoided electricity-demand growth equivalent to the demand growth from data centres over the same period [S237].
IEA’s 2025 cooling commentary sharpens the household and peak-load point: the average new air conditioner sold globally is only about **half as efficient** as the best models available, and in a well-insulated building in Singapore an average-efficiency unit set to **26 °C** and used with a fan consumes about **30% less** energy than one set to **24 °C** [S240].
This is why the archive now treats passive cooling, envelopes, setpoints, fans, and appliance efficiency as joined demand-relief design rather than as private virtue.

House rule: **no serious cooling lane without an efficiency and passive-relief doctrine.**

### 4. Buildings remain one of the biggest same-service leverage points in the whole programme
UNEP’s 2025 buildings update says the sector still consumes **32%** of global energy and contributes **34%** of global CO2 emissions [S238].
It also says the sector’s energy intensity fell by almost **10%** between 2015 and 2023, while over **85** countries now have building energy codes — yet more than **50%** of newly constructed floor space in emerging and developing economies remains uncovered by such codes [S238].
So the archive should not talk about buildings only as a long-run decarbonization sector.
Buildings are also a live control point where better envelopes, codes, retrofits, appliances, and commissioning can reduce how much downstream energy-system buildout is needed at all.

House rule: **treat buildings as demand infrastructure, not only as places that later consume whatever the energy system provides.**

### 5. Rising building load is already a grid and affordability problem in the present tense
IEA’s 2025 global energy review says global electricity consumption in buildings increased by more than **600 TWh** in 2024 — about **5%** growth — and accounted for nearly **60%** of total growth in electricity consumption [S239].
The main drivers were air conditioning under severe heat and rising demand from data centres [S239].
So when the archive says efficiency, retrofits, and passive demand reduction belong near the front door, it is not making a philosophical point.
It is responding to a concrete operating fact: building-side demand is now expanding fast enough to shape grid timing, system cost, and political exposure.

House rule: **treat building-side load growth as a front-door system variable, not as a downstream sector detail.**

### 6. Efficiency is also an affordability and public-legitimacy lane
If the same comfort, lighting, refrigeration, industrial output, or digital service can be delivered with less energy, households face less bill pressure and governments face less need for emergency relief, oversized grid spending, or politically fragile subsidy packages.
IEA’s 2025 efficiency work explicitly ties efficiency to affordability, emissions reduction, and competitiveness [S237].
This means efficiency belongs inside the public bargain, not outside it: it is one of the few climate tools that can simultaneously reduce pressure on bills, grids, import dependence, and capital budgets.

House rule: **treat efficiency as part of the affordability packet, not merely part of the engineering packet.**

### 7. Every serious climate programme now needs a demand-relief packet
The archive already had the deeper buildings, cooling, and demand-shaping doctrines.
This note adds the operating corollary: every serious climate programme should now carry an explicit **demand-relief packet** — tighter appliance and equipment standards, retrofit pipelines, envelope upgrades, passive cooling and shading, commissioning and controls, industrial motor and process optimisation, public-building performance upgrades, lower-loss distribution where relevant, and planning rules that count demand reduction alongside new supply, flexibility, and wires [S237][S238][S239][S240].

House rule: **no serious clean-buildout strategy without a demand-relief packet specific enough to inspect and ordinary enough to scale.**

### 8. What this note demotes
This note demotes eight recurring mistakes:
- treating efficiency as worthy but secondary once the supply buildout is already planned [S237][S239]
- talking about building demand growth as an external fact rather than as something design can materially change [S238][S239]
- scaling cooling access without high-efficiency standards, passive measures, or better operating norms [S237][S240]
- assuming more wires and generation can substitute indefinitely for bad envelopes and inefficient equipment [S237][S238][S239]
- treating bill relief only as a subsidy question rather than also as a same-service demand question [S237]
- treating building codes as symbolic while leaving large shares of new floor area uncovered [S238]
- ignoring the fact that efficient equipment can often be bought at similar upfront cost to much worse equipment [S240]
- leaving efficiency outside the main climate scoreboard even when it materially changes how much infrastructure must be built [S237][S239]

## Compression rule

**At rising-load scale, efficiency, retrofits, and passive demand reduction set a material part of grid size, bill pressure, and capital needs. Treat same-service demand reduction as a real capacity resource and a real public-offer lane; demote build-more-only strategies that ignore avoidable load.**

## Rev0267 efficiency bridge — the cheapest emergency power is avoided critical load

Efficiency and passive demand reduction are now outage-resilience measures. Weatherization, efficient cooling / heating, shading, cool roofs, envelope retrofits, refrigeration efficiency, thermal storage, and efficient water systems reduce the emergency load that batteries, microgrids, fuel, and generators must serve.
