# 349 — Ideal Solutions: Govern buyouts, retreat, land banks, and no-rebuild areas before recovery locks in risk

## Claim

Some places should not be rebuilt in the same way, and some should not be rebuilt at all. But retreat without rules becomes abandonment, and rebuilding without risk exit becomes subsidized repetition.

FEMA's Hazard Mitigation Assistance guidance and HMGP materials support mitigation activities designed to reduce future disaster losses [S622][S623]. UN-Habitat's land-and-disasters guidance shows why land, property, tenure, and recovery cannot be separated [S621]. The archive needs a compact rule for buyouts and no-rebuild decisions: they are not side deals after catastrophe; they are a governed recovery service.

House rule: **do not offer a buyout without a destination, and do not fund a rebuild without a risk test.**

## Fast rule

A buyout or retreat packet must name: eligibility, valuation, tenant and nonowner support, appeals, relocation destinations, receiving-community services, open-space restrictions, stewardship funding, tax-base effects, and cultural-continuity loss.

## The compact canon

### 1. Buyout speed is a justice issue

A buyout that arrives years late can trap a household in mortgage payments, rent, temporary housing, and uncertainty. The readiness ledger should track application date, appraisal date, offer date, closing date, relocation payment, appeal, and whether the household had safe housing while waiting.

### 2. Tenants and nonowners must not vanish

Property acquisition often compensates owners. Climate recovery must also protect renters, mobile-home residents, informal occupants, small businesses, farmworkers, service workers, and people whose community anchor disappears. Otherwise risk reduction becomes wealth protection for title holders only.

### 3. Open space needs stewardship

Acquired parcels are not automatically safety. They need recorded restrictions, debris clearance, contamination checks, floodplain / wetland / fire-buffer management, invasive-species control, maintenance budgets, and public accountability.

### 4. Rebuilding should carry a risk lockout

If the same site is repeatedly repaired, the burden of proof should shift. Why is rebuilding safer this time? What code, elevation, defensible space, drainage, relocation, buyout, or land-use change prevents repeat loss? If the answer is only insurance, FEMA reimbursement, or political pressure, the archive should classify it as risk repetition.

### 5. Retreat is also receiving-community policy

Relocation requires housing, schools, jobs, health care, water, transport, records, cultural anchors, and legal portability. A buyout that empties one risky place and overloads another service-poor place is not adaptation.

## Minimum packet

A retreat packet includes a repeated-loss and future-risk map; voluntary buyout criteria; appraisal / compensation rules; tenant and nonowner assistance; interim housing; relocation destination capacity; receiving-community service check; open-space covenant and maintenance plan; land-bank governance; appeal process; and after-action review of who was left in limbo.

## Bottom line

Managed retreat is not an anti-place doctrine. It is a rule against forcing people to relive foreseeable loss. The cube should treat retreat as a safety, property-rights, housing, culture, and receiving-capacity problem at once.
