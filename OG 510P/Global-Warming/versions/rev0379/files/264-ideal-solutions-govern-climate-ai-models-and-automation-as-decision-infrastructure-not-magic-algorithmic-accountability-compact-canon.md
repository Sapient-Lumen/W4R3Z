# 264 — Ideal Solutions: Govern Climate AI, Models, and Automation as Decision Infrastructure, Not Magic — Algorithmic Accountability Compact Canon

## Claim

The archive now relies on a work queue, implementation ledgers, proof records, data spines, claims checks, remedy ladders, stress tests, project shelves, procurement platforms, and critical-service continuity.
Many of those systems will increasingly use models and automation: load forecasting, grid optimization, interconnection screening, methane detection, satellite triage, heat-risk targeting, grant eligibility, building retrofit routing, procurement analytics, product-passport checks, disaster-recovery intake, insurance-risk assessment, digital twins, predictive maintenance, transition-plan review, and public dashboards.

That can help.
But climate AI can also misallocate scarce capacity, automate denial, hide value judgments behind proxy scores, hallucinate claims, launder weak data into confident outputs, overfit to yesterday's hazards, disadvantage renters or informal residents, steer public money toward easier cases, and create a new form of unaccountable delay.

So the doctrine is:

**climate AI, models, digital twins, scoring tools, and automation should be treated as decision infrastructure: registered, bounded, tested, monitored, appealable, overrideable, and retired when they cannot support the duty they are being asked to carry.**

## Fast rule

**No AI system, model, digital twin, or automated rule should allocate climate money, grid priority, support, enforcement, permits, recovery, retreat offers, risk classifications, claims credibility, or critical-service control unless the packet states the decision use, authority, data sources, model owner, affected parties, human review point, performance metric, bias and proxy-risk test, uncertainty handling, contest route, override rule, monitoring cadence, cybersecurity / privacy controls, fallback mode, and retirement trigger. Advisory tools may be lighter; high-consequence tools need full algorithmic accountability.**

## Why this note is not redundant

- `261` says records need to link.
- `263` says connected systems need cyber resilience and fallback.
- `257` says high-decision-weight claims need proof.
- `259` says duties need remedy ladders.
- `245` says scarce capacity must be allocated by public climate value.
- `251` says packets must survive compound shocks.

This note adds the model-governance rule:

**automation may assist climate delivery, but it should not become an unowned authority.**

## The compact canon

### 1. Register climate AI and models by decision consequence

Do not begin with the label “AI.”
Begin with what the tool changes.

Typical climate-model / automation uses include:
- eligibility routing for rebates, retrofits, recovery funds, social tariffs, or relocation support
- grid queue triage, load interconnection, congestion forecasting, and dispatch
- methane-alert detection and repair prioritization
- heat, flood, fire, smoke, drought, and exposure targeting
- building-stock segmentation and retrofit recommendation
- product-passport validation and green-claims triage
- procurement risk, price-benchmarking, and fraud screening
- digital twins for infrastructure planning and stress tests
- predictive maintenance for critical services
- financial-risk, insurance, and affordability scoring
- public dashboards, chatbots, and drafting tools

Classify each by consequence:
- low consequence: drafting, summarization, internal search, non-binding support
- medium consequence: triage, prioritization, alerts, recommendations
- high consequence: money, eligibility, enforcement, service, permit, grid priority, recovery, relocation, or public-rights effect
- critical consequence: life safety, critical-service control, grid stability, public health, or emergency response

House rule: **govern the consequence, not the marketing name of the tool.**

### 2. State the authority before using the tool

An automated system should not quietly create law, policy, or discretion.
For each high-consequence use, specify:
- what legal, contractual, tariff, grant, procurement, or operational authority permits the tool
- whether the output is advisory, presumptive, binding, or automatically actionable
- who can accept, reject, override, or pause it
- what rights or duties are affected
- what notice is owed to affected people or counterparties

House rule: **automation should execute a named duty or support a named decision; it should not invent its own mandate.**

### 3. Keep humans responsible without pretending every human review is meaningful

“Human in the loop” is not enough if the human lacks time, information, authority, or courage to override.

A meaningful review point needs:
- clear accountability
- understandable output
- access to underlying evidence or uncertainty
- authority to change the result
- time to review high-consequence cases
- documentation of overrides and non-overrides
- escalation route when the tool repeatedly fails

House rule: **human review is real only when the human can actually stop, change, explain, and repair the decision.**

### 4. Test for proxy harm and uneven access

Climate data often proxy for race, income, tenancy, disability, informality, neighbourhood disinvestment, insurance access, digital access, immigration status, or historic under-service.
A tool that optimizes for easiest delivery, lowest default risk, fastest payback, or cleanest paperwork can quietly route support away from the people who need it most.

Test for:
- under-selection of renters, informal residents, rural households, disabled people, and low-income households
- over-selection of easy-to-serve properties
- false negatives in under-monitored regions
- insurance, credit, or property-value proxies that intensify retreat or abandonment
- language, broadband, documentation, and digital-ID barriers
- unequal error correction or appeal success rates

House rule: **a climate model that efficiently serves the already-legible is not a justice tool.**

### 5. Make uncertainty visible enough to govern

Climate tools often combine incomplete records, stale maps, remote sensing, forecasts, engineering assumptions, user inputs, and modelled baselines.
False precision can be more dangerous than acknowledged uncertainty.

Require high-consequence tools to disclose:
- data vintage
- coverage gaps
- confidence or uncertainty range where material
- whether the tool uses proxy data
- whether the output is out-of-domain
- known blind spots
- monitoring cadence
- conditions that require manual review

House rule: **uncertainty should route review; it should not be hidden behind a single score.**

### 6. Validate against physical outcomes, not only prediction accuracy

A model can be statistically impressive and climate-useless.
The relevant test is whether it improves the real work:
- emissions actually fall
- dirty defaults are retired
- scarce capacity goes to higher public value
- critical services stay operational
- vulnerable people receive support
- methane leaks get repaired faster
- retrofits perform better
- recovery reduces repeat loss
- claims become easier to verify
- procurement fraud or capture falls

House rule: **optimize for the climate duty, not for the neatness of the model output.**

### 7. Keep generative AI away from unsupported climate authority

Generative tools can help draft, search, summarize, translate, compare, explain, and prefill.
They should not, without a governed decision path, become the source of:
- legal eligibility decisions
- public warnings
- safety instructions
- climate claims
- technical approvals
- enforcement findings
- grant denials
- evacuation, cooling, shelter, or health instructions
- official statements of emissions, risk, or remedy

When used, require source grounding, human verification, logging, version control, citation to authoritative records, and clear indication of draft status.

House rule: **a plausible sentence is not a climate fact.**

### 8. Use digital twins as stress-test instruments, not as reality substitutes

Digital twins can help test heat, flood, grid, water, evacuation, and retrofit scenarios.
But a twin can also become a theatre of precision if it is not maintained, validated, and connected to field evidence.

Good twin governance requires:
- known boundary and purpose
- current asset data
- calibration against observed events
- stated assumptions
- scenario range, including compound shocks
- version history
- known missing systems
- operator and community review where decisions affect people
- trigger for field inspection or design change

House rule: **a digital twin should reveal what must be checked in the real world, not replace checking.**

### 9. Give affected people a usable contest route

If an automated tool denies support, lowers priority, flags a claim, alters a bill, changes a risk class, or shapes a relocation offer, affected people need a route that works.

A usable contest route includes:
- notice that automation or a model was used where appropriate
- plain-language reason
- evidence the person can see or correct
- human review by someone with authority
- deadline for response
- protection against benefit loss during good-faith contest where stakes are essential
- record correction that propagates to linked systems

House rule: **automation without contest rights turns administrative friction into hidden coercion.**

### 10. Keep model records in the data spine

A high-consequence model should itself have a proof record:
- model name and version
- owner and steward
- decision use
- authority
- training / input data classes
- prohibited uses
- validation results
- monitoring metrics
- known limitations
- human review point
- appeal route
- incident history
- retirement trigger

House rule: **if a model affects the climate ledger, the model belongs in the ledger.**

### 11. Retire, quarantine, or downgrade bad tools

Do not let a tool survive because it is embedded in workflow.
Retire or quarantine models that:
- repeatedly produce biased or harmful outcomes
- cannot be explained enough for the decision at issue
- rely on stale or unavailable data
- cannot be monitored
- cannot support contest or correction
- cannot be secured
- turn advisory convenience into de facto binding authority
- fail during compound shocks
- optimize for proxy targets that conflict with the climate duty

House rule: **automation should lose authority when it cannot remain accountable.**

### 12. Use AI to reduce burden, not move burden downstream

The best climate automation should make ordinary users do less paperwork, not more.
It should help:
- prefill applications from already-held records
- find eligible support
- translate and explain offers
- detect missing records before denial
- route cases to human help
- flag under-served groups
- speed repair and warranty service
- help small public offices maintain project ledgers

House rule: **use automation to move complexity upstream, not to make weaker parties argue with machines.**

## Minimum climate-AI / model-governance checklist

Before a climate AI, model, digital twin, or automated rule is used in a high-consequence packet, ask:

1. What decision or duty does it support?
2. What authority permits it?
3. Who owns the tool and the decision?
4. Is the output advisory, presumptive, binding, or automatically actionable?
5. What data are used, how current are they, and what gaps matter?
6. Who is affected and how could they be harmed?
7. What tests check bias, proxy harm, uncertainty, and out-of-domain use?
8. Where is the meaningful human review point?
9. What notice, contest, correction, and appeal route exists?
10. What monitoring, incident, fallback, and retirement triggers apply?

## What this note demotes

This note demotes:
- AI theatre in climate dashboards
- black-box eligibility scoring for essential support
- unreviewable grid or recovery triage
- generative climate claims with no source evidence
- automated denials dressed up as efficiency
- digital twins that are never calibrated
- predictive tools that worsen under-service
- “human in the loop” labels without real override authority
- model records stored outside the audit and remedy system

### Rev0251 bridge: climate AI can quietly optimize around invisible supply-chain assumptions

Rev0251 adds a model-governance warning. AI, digital twins, procurement analytics, grid-queue tools, risk screens, and project-prioritization systems should not assume away supply-chain reality. If a model ranks packets without seeing component lead times, export-control exposure, certification delays, quality-infrastructure gaps, supplier concentration, repairability, spares, or market-access proof, it may optimize for a plan that cannot be built or maintained [S311][S312][S313][S314][S315][S316][S317].

House rule: **model outputs are not delivery truth unless the model can see the delivery dependencies that can stop the work.**

## Compression rule

**Climate AI should help people build, protect, verify, repair, and prioritize real work; it should not become an unowned authority over money, service, risk, capacity, claims, recovery, or rights. Register high-consequence tools, bound their authority, test their data and proxy harms, show uncertainty, keep meaningful human override, preserve contest rights, monitor outcomes, and retire tools that cannot support the climate duty they claim to serve.**

## Rev0265 addendum — crisis communication automation must be accountable

AI-generated warnings, automated translation, chatbots, social-media listening, impact forecasts, and targeted alerts can help public communication, but they can also hallucinate, exclude, surveil, expose sensitive groups, or amplify panic. Crisis automation should be human-supervised, tested with affected groups, logged, privacy-bounded, and backed by non-digital routes.

Automation rule: **no climate chatbot or automated warning should be the only route to lifesaving information or remedy.** Use `294` with `264` [S482][S484].

## Rev0266 AI / automation bridge — optimize access, do not automate exclusion

Routing algorithms, evacuation models, demand-responsive transit tools, freight optimization, charger routing, and AI public-information systems can help during shocks. They can also misroute disabled people, ignore informal settlements, overload shelters, hide low-income detour costs, hallucinate route status, or optimize average delay while sacrificing high-risk users. Automation in transport-continuity packets should be human-supervised, equity-tested, auditable, and backed by manual fallback [S488][S494][S500].

## Rev0267 algorithm bridge — automated energy decisions need accountability

Automated outage prediction, vegetation prioritization, load shedding, demand response, restoration sequencing, shutoff risk scoring, arrears targeting, and medically dependent outreach must be treated as decision infrastructure. They need explainability, appeal, bias testing, human override, privacy controls, and after-action correction. [S517]

## Rev0268 AI bridge — damage and debris models need accountability

AI or remote-sensing tools used to estimate damage, debris volumes, unsafe homes, route clearance, contractor priority, or pickup eligibility must be auditable, appealable, and checked against local conditions. Automation may accelerate cleanup triage; it must not erase households, informal settlements, contamination, or worker risk. Pair with `299` and `300`.

## Rev0269 AI / cube addition — automation must not outrun service floors

AI and automation should help populate ledgers, detect exposure, route cases, translate warnings, inspect delivery packets, and surface missing service-floor fields. They should not become a substitute for ownership, staffed operations, rights access, or physical redundancy. A model that improves prediction while leaving dispatch, shelters, power, cooling, family reunification, or complaint channels underbuilt is not a climate solution [S194][S536][S537].

Large new digital loads also have to pass the same service-floor test as other infrastructure: additional clean power, grid flexibility, water awareness, resilience without diesel dependency, cyber-resilient fallback, and interruptibility during stress must be visible before the load is treated as harmless.
