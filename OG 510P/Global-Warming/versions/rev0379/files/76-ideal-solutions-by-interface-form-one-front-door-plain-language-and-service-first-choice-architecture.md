# 76 — Ideal Solutions by Interface Form: One Front Door, Plain Language, and Service-First Choice Architecture

## Claim

The archive already says ideal climate solutions should be low-friction, service-first, actor-aware, affordable, and realistic about how payment, contract, eligibility, verification, remedy, and continuity shape uptake.
It says the clean path should usually come through defaults, standardisation, stronger local channels, and delivery systems that do not require ordinary users to become climate-policy experts or project managers [S69][S80][S118][S119][S137][S140][S141][S143].

One more compact rule belongs in the canon:

**ideal climate solutions should usually be easy to find, easy to understand, and easy to start.**

In plain terms:
- present the clean option through a clear front door rather than scattered portals, acronyms, and contractor lore [S118][S119]
- describe the offer in the language of service, comfort, reliability, health, safety, and monthly cost, not only technical specifications and programme jargon [S140][S141][S143]
- let users compare a small number of intelligible pathways rather than forcing them to assemble their own transition plan from disconnected pieces [S118][S119][S137]
- do not treat discoverability and comprehension as soft communication extras; they are part of delivery quality [S118][S140][S141]

So the archive’s shortest interface-form rule is:

**use one clear front door where possible, plain language by default, and service-first choice architecture; do not hide the clean path inside fragmented programme maps or technical shopping puzzles.**

## Why this belongs in the canon

### 1. Many climate failures happen before the form is even opened

A household, landlord, school, clinic, or small firm may never reject the clean option on the merits.
They may never get far enough.
They cannot tell where to start.
They cannot tell which programme fits.
They cannot tell whether the relevant unit is the appliance, the bill, the building, the tenancy, the tariff, or the contractor.
They encounter a maze of portals, agencies, offers, and technical categories before any eligibility, payment, or contract question is even reached [S118][S119][S137].

That means some climate underperformance is an **interface-design failure**.
The solution exists, but the intended user cannot locate or parse it at ordinary effort.

### 2. User-centred regulation means the state should not require expert navigation just to do the right thing

OECD’s 2025 regulatory work says people and businesses should not have to become experts in government operations to complete necessary tasks, and points to one-stop shops and interoperable arrangements that reduce time, effort, and frustration [S118].
IEA’s efficiency-policy toolkit says one-stop shops, renovation passports, standardised pathways, and digitised procedures cut transaction costs and barriers to investment [S119].

The lesson here is slightly earlier than eligibility and payment design.
Before the system asks for proof or money, it should usually answer three ordinary questions clearly:
- what is being offered,
- why this is the relevant pathway,
- and where to start.

A climate programme is weaker when that first layer is left to consultants, contractor folklore, or patient trial and error.

### 3. People compare in service language, not only technical language

OECD’s 2025 work on public acceptability says technically strong reform still fails when policymakers ignore how people perceive burden, fairness, and reform credibility [S140].
IEA’s people-centred transition work says successful energy transitions should engage people as active participants and beneficiaries, not treat fairness and inclusion as afterthoughts [S141].
IEA’s affordability work reinforces the practical side: the relevant household question is often whether the cleaner choice feels accessible and manageable, not whether it wins a technical spreadsheet in abstract [S143].

That implies a simple interface rule:

**present climate action in the language ordinary actors actually use to decide** — warmth, cooling safety, lower bills, quieter streets, reliable mobility, cleaner air, simpler service, and less hassle.

Technical detail still matters.
But it should usually sit behind a usable front layer, not replace it.

### 4. Interface form is part of justice because confusion is not evenly distributed

Bad interfaces sort by time, confidence, language, digital fluency, and prior familiarity with state or utility systems.
They advantage actors who already know the jargon, already know the suppliers, or can afford to spend evenings deciphering the pathway [S118][S140][S141][S143].

That means a climate programme can be generous on paper and still exclusionary in practice if the clean path is legible mainly to the already organised and well-advised.
The archive therefore needs a plain justice rule:

**the cleaner path should not depend on whether the intended user already speaks programme language.**

### 5. Interface form is not the same as eligibility, contract, or delivery form

This note is not the same question as:
- `67-ideal-solutions-by-delivery-form-clean-service-performance-and-less-asset-shopping.md`, which asks **what the user should receive**,
- `71-ideal-solutions-by-contract-form-standard-offers-prequalified-delivery-and-performance-backed-terms.md`, which asks **how the agreement should usually be written, compared, and enforced**,
- `72-ideal-solutions-by-eligibility-form-presumptive-inclusion-existing-data-and-no-proof-mazes.md`, which asks **how the intended user should qualify without repeated proof burdens**,
- or `74-ideal-solutions-by-remedy-form-fast-recourse-automatic-correction-and-no-appeals-mazes.md`, which asks **how ordinary failures should usually be corrected**.

This note asks the earlier question:

**when the intended user first encounters the clean pathway, can they tell what it is, why it is for them, and where to begin without specialist help?**

For the archive’s companion note on **what should usually happen after that first contact so likely-fit users are directly invited, pre-filled, and helped into the pathway instead of being left to self-start from a blank page**, see `77-ideal-solutions-by-enrolment-form-default-invitation-prefilled-paths-and-assisted-opt-in.md`.

### 6. Good interface form improves copyability and delivery capacity

A system that is easy to explain is easier to replicate.
A system that has one usable front door and a small number of intelligible pathways is easier for local officials, utilities, schools, social-housing providers, and community intermediaries to carry into routine practice [S118][S119][S141].

So interface form is not cosmetic.
It is part of scale.
It reduces drop-off before enrolment, reduces bad referrals, and lets trusted intermediaries carry the clean path further at lower cognitive cost.

## The archive’s interface-form hierarchy

A future proposal is usually stronger when it fits most of this hierarchy.

### 1. Front-door layer
Use one clear starting point where possible — one-stop shop, unified portal, standard referral path, or a small number of well-signposted channels — instead of scattering the user journey across multiple bodies and hidden entry points [S118][S119].

### 2. Service-language layer
Describe the offer in terms of service and lived outcomes first: comfort, cooling safety, lower bills, resilience, cleaner air, reliability, access, and convenience. Put technical detail behind that layer rather than making it the first thing the user must decode [S140][S141][S143].

### 3. Small-pathway layer
Reduce the number of distinct pathways ordinary users must compare. Bundle complements where sensible and present a manageable menu instead of a combinatorial maze [S118][S119][S137].

### 4. Trusted-channel layer
Let utilities, municipalities, schools, clinics, social-housing systems, employers, and other trusted local intermediaries carry the clean offer where that improves comprehension and uptake [S80][S118][S141].

### 5. Start-now layer
At first contact, ask only for the information needed to route the user correctly. Do not make the opening interaction as heavy as the full backend process [S118][S119].

## The archive’s interface-form tests

### 1. Findability test
Can the intended user tell quickly where to begin, or do they have to search across agencies, websites, contractors, and funding streams before they can even orient themselves? [S118][S119]

### 2. Comprehension test
Does the first description use service language an ordinary user can understand, or mainly technical categories, acronyms, and programme jargon? [S140][S141][S143]

### 3. Pathway-count test
How many distinct decisions or branches must the user understand before being routed to the right option? [S118][S119][S137]

### 4. Trusted-channel test
Can the clean pathway arrive through a familiar channel the target group already uses, or only through a remote expert-facing interface? [S80][S118][S141]

### 5. False-complexity test
Is the complexity real, or has the system exposed backend distinctions the end user did not need to navigate personally? [S118][S119]

### 6. Early-drop-off test
At what point do typical users disappear — before they apply, before they compare, or before they can even tell that the offer is relevant? [S118][S140][S143]

## What this rule promotes

Promote:
- one-stop shops, unified portals, standard referral routes, and clear front doors for retrofits, clean heat, cooling access, appliance upgrades, efficient mobility, resilience support, or municipal delivery [S118][S119]
- plain-language descriptions centred on bills, comfort, safety, reliability, health, and service outcomes rather than raw technical jargon [S140][S141][S143]
- small menus of intelligible pathways with sensible bundling where complements belong together [S118][S119][S137]
- trusted local intermediaries and existing public-service channels that help people recognise that the offer is for them [S80][S118][S141]
- opening interactions that route users quickly before demanding full documentation, technical choices, or bespoke project assembly [S118][S119]

## What this rule demotes

Demote:
- climate offers hidden across multiple agencies, portals, acronyms, or contractors with no clear front door [S118][S119]
- first-contact interfaces dominated by product specs, engineering categories, or programme jargon rather than user-relevant service language [S140][S141][S143]
- fragmented pathway maps that force ordinary users to build their own package from disconnected offers and rules [S118][S119][S137]
- designs that treat discoverability and comprehension as marketing problems to be solved later instead of core delivery problems [S118][S140][S141]
- programmes whose intended beneficiaries disappear before application because the clean path was not legible enough to start [S118][S140][S143]

## One-paragraph compression

**Ideal climate solutions should usually be easy to find, easy to understand, and easy to start. Use one clear front door where possible, describe the offer in service language ordinary people and small institutions actually use, route users through a small number of intelligible pathways, and let trusted channels carry the offer. Do not hide the clean path inside fragmented programme maps, technical shopping puzzles, or jargon that assumes the user already knows how to translate climate policy into action [S80][S118][S119][S137][S140][S141][S143].**
