# 156 — Ideal Solutions: Funding and Burden Allocation Compact Canon

## Claim

The archive already has joined notes for:
- political economy / social contract (`131`)
- fiscal durability (`45`)
- service floors and waste ceilings (`61`)
- upside allocation (`69`)
- payment form (`70`)
- and EMDE finance (`25`)

What it still lacked was one joined first-stop note for the funding question that keeps returning in practice:

**who should pay, what should be taxed or charged down, what public finance should buy, and which burdens should be kept off people who are least able to carry them?**

This note is not a new doctrinal branch.
It is a compression note.
Its job is to gather the archive’s rules on progressive restraint, revenue recycling, fossil-subsidy reform, public-value capture, targeted protection, infrastructure finance, and concessional burden-sharing into one place.

## Fast rule

**A serious climate programme should usually put new burden first on pollution, waste, fossil dependence, excess resource throughput, and publicly created windfalls; finance long-lived public backbones like infrastructure; recycle broad climate revenues into household protection and clean buildout; and avoid making low-income households, cash-poor tenants, or poorer countries pre-finance the transition through upfront cash, complexity, or expensive capital** [S13][S97][S99][S138][S139][S141][S143][S145].

## The compact canon

### 1. Protect essential service floors before imposing broad price discipline
The archive’s distributive starting point is not abstract price purity.
It is whether people can actually secure cooling safety, clean energy, mobility, shelter, water, and basic resilience.
So a serious funding design should usually protect or expand those floors first, especially for low-income households and other exposed groups, and should distrust designs that raise the price of basic necessity without visible compensation, targeted support, or better substitutes already in place [S141][S143].

House rule: **do not prove seriousness by making essential clean service harder to afford.**

### 2. Put the first burden on dirty defaults, waste, and high-throughput excess
When the programme needs to raise money or impose stronger restraint, the archive should usually aim first at:
- fossil dependence and inefficient fossil support
- methane leakage and other avoidable waste
- excess resource throughput in high-consuming settings
- and other forms of affluent or lock-in-heavy excess before basic need

This follows the archive’s service-floor / waste-ceiling logic and the wider inequality signal that high-income countries and groups still have much larger scope to cut excessive resource use and climate impact than poorer ones [S97][S99][S139][S145].

House rule: **tax and charge the bads, not the basics.**

### 3. Recycle broad climate revenues visibly and progressively
The archive does not treat carbon pricing or subsidy reform revenue as a generic fiscal pool by default.
If broad pricing instruments are used, the stronger design is usually to recycle revenues into:
- targeted household protection
- lower bills and cleaner substitutes
- public transit, grids, retrofits, or other climate backbones
- and other visible uses that improve fairness, credibility, and political durability

OECD now stresses that revenue recycling can both support low-carbon investment and limit distributive harms, while IEA and OECD both show that replacing broad fossil support with targeted support for vulnerable households can free fiscal space for better social and climate priorities [S138][S143][S145].

House rule: **if you raise broad climate revenue, show people where it goes and why it is fairer than the old baseline.**

### 4. Finance long-lived public backbones like infrastructure, not like luxury purchases
Grids, resilient public buildings, social-housing retrofits, district systems, public transport, water-security systems, early warning, and other backbone assets should usually be financed over their useful life through public, regulated, or concessional capital structures that match their public value and long operating life.
The archive should distrust programme shapes that expect ordinary households, small municipalities, or cash-poor institutions to solve infrastructure-scale transition costs with large upfront cash payments, reimbursement mazes, or expensive short-term finance [S13][S96][S143].

House rule: **pay for long-lived climate backbones the way serious societies pay for infrastructure.**

### 5. Condition public support so publicly created value stays partly public
When climate value is being created by public permits, public de-risking, public procurement, subsidy reform, standard setting, regulated privilege, or other state-made market structure, the gains should not flow out as one-way private windfalls by default.
The archive’s stronger default is to attach conditions that return some share of that value as lower bills, stronger service, host benefit, open replication, performance accountability, or clearer public leverage over future pricing and conduct [S138][S141][S143][S145].

House rule: **public help should buy public-facing gain, not only private upside.**

### 6. Treat EMDE finance as burden allocation, not as charity after the main design is finished
At world scale, burden allocation is not only a domestic tax question.
It is also a financing question.
When the same solar plant, grid line, or public-transit investment faces much higher financing costs in poorer countries, the burden of transition is being allocated unfairly through capital markets.
So guarantees, concessional finance, local-currency tools, and MDB / public-balance-sheet risk reduction are part of moral burden allocation, not an optional kindness layer after the “real” programme is designed [S13][S96].

House rule: **if capital is mispriced, burden-sharing is mispriced too.**

### 7. Prefer funding instruments that weaken dirty lock-in while they raise or recycle money
The archive should usually prefer funding designs that do double duty:
- weaken dirty defaults,
- reduce wasteful demand,
- or shift investment toward better substitutes,

rather than merely collecting money while leaving the old system intact.
That is why fossil-subsidy reform, targeted replacement support, and revenue recycling into clean alternatives usually dominate designs that preserve the dirty baseline and then try to compensate everyone forever [S138][S139][S143][S145].

House rule: **the best funding instrument helps close the bad path while funding the better one.**

## Preferred use

Open this note first when the live question is any of the following:
- who should pay for the climate transition
- what climate taxation or charging should try to do
- how carbon-pricing or subsidy-reform revenues should be recycled
- how to think about burden-sharing between high-consuming groups and those still below decent service floors
- how to fund grids, retrofits, public backbones, or other long-lived climate assets
- how rich countries and public financial institutions should think about burden-sharing in EMDE

Then reopen the deeper note that matches the surviving sub-question.

## Compression rule

**Tax and charge the dirty, wasteful, rent-rich, and windfall-prone parts of the system first; finance durable public backbones over their life; recycle revenues visibly into protection and clean substitution; and keep necessity, dignity, and developmental catch-up on the protected side of the ledger.**
