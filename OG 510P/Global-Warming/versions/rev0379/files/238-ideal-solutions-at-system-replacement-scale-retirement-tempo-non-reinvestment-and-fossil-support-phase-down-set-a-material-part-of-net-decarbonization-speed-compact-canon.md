# 238 — Ideal Solutions: At System-Replacement Scale, Retirement Tempo, Non-Reinvestment, and Fossil-Support Phase-Down Set a Material Part of Net Decarbonization Speed Compact Canon

## Claim

The archive already has:
- a deeper note on managed fossil decline (`09`)
- a transition-mechanism canon on **build, switch, retire, protect, and prove** (`198`)
- a replacement-proof canon (`199`)
- and, more recently, front-door tempo notes for electricity, finance, institutions, uptake, protection, hardware, methane, nitrogen, cooling, flexibility, water, and exposure discipline (`226`–`237`)

That is already enough to say that climate programs do not win by buildout alone.
What the archive still lacked was one smallest front-door note for a now-practical question:

**once clean supply is scaling faster, what determines whether gross emissions actually fall fast enough rather than coexisting with re-upped fossil defaults, extended dirty assets, and public support that keeps the old system cheap to preserve?**

This note is not a new doctrinal branch.
It is a compression note.
Its job is to make one practical truth harder to miss at the front door:

**at system-replacement scale, retirement tempo, non-reinvestment, and fossil-support phase-down set a material part of net decarbonization speed.**

## Fast rule

**Do not treat fossil retirement, non-reinvestment, subsidy phase-down, and decommissioning discipline as downstream cleanup after clean buildout. Treat them as core climate infrastructure. If dirty assets keep being life-extended, refinanced, rebuilt, and subsidized, clean additions do not translate into net decarbonization at the speed the programme claims** [S233][S234][S235][S236].

## Why this note is not redundant

- `09-managed-fossil-decline.md` answers **the deeper doctrine on deliberate fossil phase-down, worker protection, and anti-chaos rules**
- `198-ideal-solutions-transition-mechanism-build-switch-retire-protect-and-prove-compact-canon.md` answers **the smallest joined mechanism for how cleaner systems displace dirtier ones**
- `199-ideal-solutions-replacement-proof-what-counts-as-real-displacement-compact-canon.md` answers **what counts as actual replacement rather than parallel addition**
- this note answers something smaller and more front-door:

**why the archive now treats dirty-default exit itself as a tempo-setting lane rather than as a later bookkeeping exercise once enough clean kit has shown up.**

## The compact canon

### 1. Buildout without retirement is not the same thing as fast net decarbonization
IEA’s 2025 World Energy Investment report says clean-energy investment is set to reach about **USD 2.2 trillion** in 2025, roughly **double** the **USD 1.1 trillion** going to oil, gas, and coal, and that electricity-sector investment is set to reach **USD 1.5 trillion**, around **50% higher** than the amount spent bringing fossil fuels to market [S233].
That is real progress.
But IEA’s Global Energy Review 2025 also says world energy demand rose in 2024 and demand increased for **all** major fuels, while energy-sector CO2 emissions still rose, even though rapid clean-tech deployment avoided about **2.6 Gt** of additional annual CO2 emissions [S234].
So buildout can be necessary and still not be enough.

House rule: **judge speed by dirty service displaced, not only by clean assets added.**

### 2. Net-zero-consistent pathways do not leave room for routine new long-lead fossil expansion
IEA’s updated net-zero roadmap says that in a pathway consistent with global net zero emissions by 2050, oil and gas demand falls fast enough that **no new long lead-time conventional oil and gas projects are required**, while low-emissions electricity rises fast enough that **no new unabated coal plants** beyond those already under construction at the start of 2023 are built [S236].
That does not mean all fossil use disappears instantly.
It means the programme cannot claim 1.5°C seriousness while treating new long-lived fossil expansion as routine infrastructure.

House rule: **make proponents of new long-lived fossil assets carry a much heavier burden of proof than proponents of clean replacement, efficiency, flexibility, and demand reduction.**

### 3. Some existing production must decline faster than incumbents prefer
IEA’s oil-and-gas transition work says that in a 1.5°C scenario some existing production would need to be shut in, with more than **7 million barrels per day** of oil production pushed out of operation in 2040 before the end of technical lifetime; it also says new project developments face major commercial risks and can lock in emissions that push the world over 1.5°C [S236].
So managed decline is not a rhetorical add-on to clean buildout.
It is part of whether installed fossil capital stops governing the emissions ledger by inertia.

House rule: **treat retirement schedules, closure triggers, and non-reinvestment rules as part of the physical programme, not just its politics.**

### 4. Fossil support keeps the dirty baseline easier to preserve than public language admits
OECD’s current fossil-fuel-support page says its 2025 Inventory documents over **1 700** support measures across 52 OECD, G20, EU, and Eastern Partnership economies [S235].
It also says the fiscal cost of global support for fossil fuels was still about **USD 916.3 billion** in 2024, that such support can increase emissions and undermine incentives for the energy transition, and that as currently designed it tends to disproportionately benefit higher-income households [S235].
So a programme that adds clean capacity while still making fossil use artificially easy is not yet aligned with its own replacement logic.

House rule: **phase out broad fossil support quickly while replacing it with tighter, better-targeted protection for households and firms that actually need help.**

### 5. Retirement tempo is also a fiscal-honesty lane
If public money keeps extending the old system while also paying to build the new one, the programme burns capital twice: once on replacement and once on preserving what should be leaving [S233][S235].
That weakens fiscal durability and creates political confusion about why bills, public budgets, and emissions do not improve as quickly as the buildout narrative promised.
The archive therefore treats fossil-support reform, closure planning, and non-reinvestment as a public-finance discipline, not only an emissions discipline.

House rule: **prefer public spending that speeds replacement, worker protection, remediation, and targeted relief over public spending that prolongs the dirty baseline as a mass default.**

### 6. The right comparison is not “clean added” versus “nothing”
The real comparison is **clean added plus dirty retired** versus **clean added next to a still-growing or still-protected fossil baseline** [S233][S234][S235][S236].
That is why the archive now needs a smallest front-door note for retirement tempo itself.
Without it, readers can easily mistake clean investment headlines for sufficient progress even when emissions, lock-in, and fiscal exposure are still being prolonged by the old system.

House rule: **run the programme as replacement accounting, not addition accounting.**

### 7. What this note demotes
This note demotes seven recurring mistakes:
- treating clean buildout headlines as proof of sufficient decarbonization even when fossil use and emissions remain too sticky [S233][S234]
- treating retirement and decommissioning as later housekeeping instead of part of the main programme [S236]
- approving new long-lived fossil assets as if climate pathways had left abundant room for them [S236]
- keeping broad fossil-support regimes in place while calling the programme technology neutral or socially necessary by default [S235]
- using untargeted fossil support instead of tighter household protection that does not preserve the dirty baseline [S235]
- assuming incumbents will wind down on their own timetable without formal closure, non-reinvestment, or burden-of-proof rules [S236]
- reporting progress mainly as clean capacity added rather than dirty service retired and emissions actually displaced [S233][S234]

## Preferred use

Open this note first when the live question is any of the following:
- why clean buildout alone can still leave emissions too high
- why managed fossil decline belongs near the front door rather than deep in the archive
- what lane links subsidy reform, retirement schedules, and non-reinvestment rules
- why new long-lived fossil approvals now require a heavier burden of proof
- why serious climate strategy has to count dirty defaults exiting, not only clean assets arriving

Then reopen `09`, `198`, or `199` if the surviving question is mainly about the deeper managed-decline doctrine, the joined displacement mechanism, or the stricter test for what counts as real replacement.

## Compression rule

**At system-replacement scale, retirement tempo, non-reinvestment, and fossil-support phase-down set a material part of net decarbonization speed. Treat closure schedules, subsidy phase-down, non-reinvestment rules, and remediation obligations as core climate infrastructure; demote addition accounting, routine life-extension of dirty assets, and broad fossil-support regimes that keep the old baseline easier to preserve than the new one is to install.**

## Rev0254 addendum — retirement tempo without closure proof creates ghost liabilities

This note treats retirement tempo, non-reinvestment, and fossil-support phase-down as material to net decarbonization speed. Rev0254 adds the afterlife rule: **retirement tempo must not outrun closure proof**.

A faster closure schedule is not mature if it leaves unfunded wells, abandoned mines, fugitive methane, ash ponds, contaminated land, worker obligations, local-government revenue holes, and unclear ownership behind. Conversely, cleanup and reuse should not be used to delay retirement when replacement service is ready. The proper sequence is to pair retirement with closure finance, methane control, worker and community terms, and reuse passports at the same control point.

House rule: **count dirty assets as retired only when output, permission, support, liability, and residual hazard all have a closure path.** See `271` and `272` [S341][S342][S344][S347][S349].
