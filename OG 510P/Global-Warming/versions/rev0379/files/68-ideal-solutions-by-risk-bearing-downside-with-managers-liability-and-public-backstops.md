# 68 — Ideal Solutions by Risk-Bearing: Keep Downside with Managers, Use Liability Honestly, and Reserve Public Backstops for True System Risk

## Claim

The archive already says ideal climate solutions should be physical-first, low-friction, actor-aware, service-first, and institutionally well matched.
It says they should put obligations on concentrated control points, deliver clean service rather than user hassle, and use public or regulated backbones where coordination and universal-service duties dominate [S13][S69][S80][S91][S96][S137][S143][S144].

One more compact rule belongs in the canon.
For the archive’s companion note on **who should carry the hardest implementation burden**, see `63-ideal-solutions-by-actor-concentrated-control-points-and-no-heroic-consumers.md`.
For the companion note on **which institutional form** should hold climate backbones, see `66-ideal-solutions-by-institutional-form-public-backbones-regulated-service-and-competitive-edges.md`.
For the companion note on **what end users should actually receive**, see `67-ideal-solutions-by-delivery-form-clean-service-performance-and-less-asset-shopping.md`.

**ideal climate solutions should usually place controllable downside on the actor that designs, owns, profits from, or operates the risky system — not on households, tenants, workers, or the general public by default.**

In practice that means:
- providers should usually bear performance risk when they claim savings, comfort, flexibility, or reliability gains [S56][S58][S144]
- polluters and asset owners should usually bear cleanup, closure, storage-integrity, and end-of-life obligations rather than socializing them after profit has been taken [S23][S24][S25][S123][S135][S136]
- public guarantees should usually be used to reduce financing frictions for socially valuable clean buildout, especially in EMDE, rather than to preserve the dirty baseline or erase ordinary commercial discipline [S13][S91][S94][S95][S96][S153]
- public insurance or disaster backstops should usually be reserved for correlated system risks that private markets cannot absorb alone, and paired with risk reduction, open risk information, and no-build / retreat discipline where warranted [S40][S81][S82][S83][S84]

So the archive’s shortest risk-bearing rule is:

**put downside with the party that can manage it and is choosing it; use public balance sheets to unlock public-good buildout or absorb true system risk, not to hide preventable failure or subsidize reckless lock-in.**

## Why this belongs in the canon

### 1. Bad downside allocation quietly rewards bad system design

Many climate failures persist because the actor who chooses the risky design is not the actor who pays when it fails.
A landlord can leave a tenant in an inefficient building.
A fossil system can run without full closure or cleanup discipline.
A programme can celebrate equipment sales while the user absorbs underperformance, maintenance trouble, and bill volatility.
A development pipeline can be deemed “too risky” in EMDE even when the broader social value is high, because financing systems price the project class badly rather than the project logic well [S23][S24][S69][S91][S93][S96][S137][S143][S144].

That is not just unfair.
It also biases the whole system toward the wrong technologies, contracts, and locations.

### 2. Clean buildout often needs public risk reduction, but dirty lock-in needs more private accountability

IEA’s cost-of-capital work says clean-energy finance in EMDE faces much higher financing costs than in advanced economies, even for mature technologies [S13][S91].
OECD’s blended-finance guidance says public instruments should mobilize commercial finance rather than replace it wholesale, which points toward selective guarantees, first-loss tools, and other disciplined risk-reduction devices where markets systematically underprovide socially valuable investment [S153].

That implies an archive rule with two sides:
- be more willing to use public guarantees to make clean buildout bankable where the barrier is financing architecture rather than weak technology [S13][S91][S94][S95][S96][S153]
- be less willing to use public money to absorb ordinary downside from fossil continuation, weak cleanup planning, or avoidable lock-in [S23][S24][S25][S139][S145]

Public balance sheets matter, but they should usually be used to **accelerate desirable replacement**, not to immunize incumbent damage.

### 3. Service-first delivery works better when providers keep performance risk

The archive’s delivery-form note already says users should usually receive clean service and measurable performance rather than a pile of assets to manage alone.
That delivery logic only really works when the provider, utility, ESCO, landlord, or programme carries a meaningful share of the risk that the promised service will not materialize [S53][S56][S58][S69][S137][S143][S144].

IEA’s ESCO work says performance-contracting models can integrate project development, finance, and verification around delivered savings [S144].
IEA’s flexibility work says demand-response programmes are stronger when users are compensated for a real grid service rather than left with manual altruism and opaque value capture [S56][S58].

So this note adds the missing corollary:
**service-first climate delivery usually requires risk-first provider discipline.**

### 4. Disaster backstops are real public duties, but they cannot replace exposure discipline

UNDRR says disaster losses now impose very large macro-financial burdens, while OECD says the sustainable path to insurance affordability is risk reduction rather than simply suppressing premiums [S40][S81].
Swiss Re’s 2025 catastrophe-loss work says a large share of global disaster losses remains uninsured, which means the public and households often absorb the residual anyway [S82].
UNDRR’s risk-information work and the World Bank’s adaptation principles both point to the same implication: public protection should be paired with open risk information, land-use realism, and avoidance of continued buildout in the most dangerous places [S83][S84].

So the archive should reject two bad extremes at once:
- **no backstop at all**, where households and local governments are abandoned to correlated disasters they cannot self-insure against
- **backstop without discipline**, where the public keeps absorbing repeated losses while exposure keeps rising [S40][S81][S82][S83][S84]

### 5. Honest liability is part of truth infrastructure

The archive already says climate claims need auditable MRV, measured methane, and credible rules for carbon accounting [S11][S65][S113][S123][S135][S136].
But truthful measurement alone is not enough.
If no actor is clearly responsible when a storage claim fails, a methane event is ignored, a contaminated site is abandoned, or an efficiency promise does not materialize, then the metric layer remains half-built.

That means liability, guarantees, warranties, bonds, and clear fallback responsibilities are part of climate governance itself, not merely legal detail [S23][S24][S81][S123][S129][S130][S131][S135][S136].

## The archive’s risk-bearing hierarchy

A future proposal is usually stronger when it fits most of this order.

### 1. Prevent-and-reduce layer
First use standards, better siting, maintenance, resilience investment, and cleaner design to avoid the downside in the first place [S14][S40][S81][S83][S84][S121][S128].

### 2. Manager-bears-performance-risk layer
Where a provider claims savings, comfort, flexibility, reliability, or service quality, it should usually bear a meaningful share of the risk if those claims fail [S56][S58][S123][S137][S143][S144].

### 3. Polluter / owner-bears-cleanup layer
Where a dirty or hazardous asset creates closure, cleanup, storage-integrity, or end-of-life obligations, those duties should usually remain attached to the owner, operator, or beneficiary rather than drifting to the public by default [S23][S24][S25][S123][S135][S136].

### 4. Public-guarantee-for-clean-buildout layer
Where socially valuable clean infrastructure is blocked mainly by financing cost, political-risk perception, currency risk, or weak project-preparation capacity, public and concessional instruments should usually reduce those barriers in a disciplined way [S13][S91][S92][S93][S94][S95][S96][S153].

### 5. Public-backstop-for-system-tail-risk layer
Where risks are correlated, society-wide, or too large for private balance sheets alone — major disasters, systemic insurance withdrawal, or backbone-infrastructure failure — public backstops may be necessary, but they should usually be tied to exposure reduction, better standards, open risk information, and retreat or no-build discipline where warranted [S40][S81][S82][S83][S84].

## The archive’s risk-bearing tests

### 1. Manager-of-risk test
Who is best placed to prevent, price, monitor, and correct the risk?
Does the proposal keep downside with that actor, or does it dump it on weaker parties?

### 2. Upside-downside symmetry test
Does the actor that captures the upside also keep a serious share of downside, or are profits private while failure is socialized? [S23][S129][S130][S131]

### 3. Cleanup-and-afterlife test
Does the design specify who pays for decommissioning, cleanup, storage monitoring, warranty support, maintenance, and end-of-life obligations, or are these left vague for future publics to absorb? [S23][S24][S25][S121][S123][S135][S136]

### 4. Public-balance-sheet test
If public guarantees or backstops are used, are they reducing a real public-interest bottleneck such as EMDE financing cost or correlated disaster risk, or simply insulating a weak private design from deserved discipline? [S13][S40][S81][S91][S96][S153]

### 5. User-protection test
Can a renter, low-income household, small firm, or municipality get the cleaner or safer outcome without becoming the de facto insurer of underperformance, fuel volatility, hidden defects, or recurrent loss? [S69][S81][S137][S143][S144]

### 6. Exposure-discipline test
If a public backstop exists, does the proposal also reduce exposure through standards, maintenance, land-use rules, or retreat where needed, or does it merely subsidize repeated loss? [S40][S81][S82][S83][S84]

## What this rules in

This note rules **in**:
- performance-backed retrofit, flexibility, and energy-service models where providers are rewarded for verified outcomes and keep meaningful delivery risk [S56][S58][S144]
- landlord, utility, and public-provider obligations that prevent weaker users from carrying the full burden of inefficient assets or unreliable service [S53][S69][S80][S137][S143]
- clear cleanup, closure, and long-tail responsibility for fossil and carbon-management assets rather than vague public inheritance of the mess [S23][S24][S25][S123][S135][S136]
- disciplined public guarantees, concessional windows, and risk-sharing tools that make mature clean infrastructure bankable in constrained settings [S13][S91][S92][S93][S94][S95][S96][S153]
- public disaster backstops tied to open risk information, stronger resilience standards, and exposure reduction rather than endless rebuilding of the same vulnerability [S40][S81][S82][S83][S84]

## What this demotes

This note demotes:
- household-facing programmes that celebrate uptake while leaving users to absorb underperformance, maintenance failure, contractor risk, or tariff complexity on their own [S53][S69][S137][S143][S144]
- fossil continuation without serious closure, cleanup, worker-transition, and liability discipline [S23][S24][S25][S139][S145]
- public guarantees used mainly to preserve the dirty baseline rather than speed replacement [S23][S139][S145]
- disaster insurance or compensation systems that repeatedly socialize loss while allowing exposure to deepen unchecked [S40][S81][S82][S83][S84]
- carbon-management or crediting claims that are measured loosely, with unclear long-term responsibility if the claim fails [S123][S135][S136]

For the matching rule on the other side of the ledger — what should happen to gains created partly by public authority, public de-risking, or regulated privilege — see `69-ideal-solutions-by-upside-allocation-public-value-capture-lower-bills-and-shared-gains.md`.

## One-paragraph compression

**Ideal climate solutions should usually keep downside with the party that controls it, profits from it, or is best placed to manage it. Providers should bear performance risk when they promise savings or service; polluters and asset owners should bear cleanup and long-tail obligations; public guarantees should be used mainly to unlock socially valuable clean buildout where financing systems are the bottleneck; and public backstops should be reserved for genuine system risks such as disaster losses that private markets cannot absorb alone, paired with exposure discipline rather than repeated socialization of avoidable harm [S13][S23][S24][S25][S40][S56][S58][S69][S81][S82][S83][S84][S91][S96][S123][S135][S136][S143][S144][S153].**
