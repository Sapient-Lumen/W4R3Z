# 269 — Ideal Solutions: Treat Land, Water, Seabed, and Corridors as Governed Climate-Siting Capacity, Not Empty Space — Place-Based Siting Compact Canon

## Claim

Rev0252 made clean assets durable through repair, reuse, recovery, decommissioning, and end-of-life infrastructure.
The next missing layer is **place**.

A climate programme can have finance, permits, crews, standards, cybersecurity, supply chains, circular design, and recycling plans, and still fail if the land, water, seabed, corridors, interconnection points, roads, ports, rights, ecology, viewsheds, cultural sites, and host settlements needed for deployment are treated as empty space.

Clean buildout needs real places.
Those places are already doing work: growing food, storing carbon, holding water, supporting biodiversity, carrying culture, hosting homes, absorbing recreation, bearing past pollution, or giving communities identity and power.
So siting capacity is not discovered by drawing shapes on a map after a project is already sold.
It is governed as scarce public infrastructure capacity: mapped early, screened by rights and ecology, prioritised by low-conflict value, linked to grids and roads, stress-tested against cumulative impacts, and updated as climate risk changes [S60][S64][S329][S330][S331][S332][S338][S339].

## Fast rule

**Every footprint-bearing climate packet needs a place-capacity ledger before it is called ready.**

That ledger should say:

1. what physical footprint the packet needs;
2. whose land, water, seabed, corridor, view, access, livelihoods, culture, or service conditions are affected;
3. what sites were screened out before the preferred site was chosen;
4. whether the packet uses already-modified places before higher-conflict places;
5. what grid, road, port, water, telecom, waste, emergency, and workforce dependencies the site creates;
6. what biodiversity, water, soil, food, heritage, Indigenous, community, and cumulative-impact filters were applied;
7. what alternatives, co-location, repowering, rooftop, brownfield, degraded-land, agrivoltaic, floating, corridor-sharing, undergrounding, or demand-reduction options were considered;
8. what monitoring, mitigation, restoration, decommissioning, and end-of-life obligations travel with the site;
9. who can correct the site decision if evidence changes;
10. what trigger pauses, reroutes, shrinks, redesigns, or cancels the packet.

No place ledger, no mature delivery packet.

## Compact canon

### 1. Siting is not a leftover permitting step

Bad siting turns a good climate technology into delay, litigation, resentment, biodiversity loss, water conflict, cultural harm, or stranded infrastructure.

Good siting does not mean giving every project a blank cheque.
It means doing enough upstream public planning that the highest-value projects can move faster because obvious no-go zones, low-conflict zones, interconnection logic, community conditions, ecological constraints, and cumulative-impact rules are already visible [S60][S330][S332][S338].

### 2. Treat place capacity like grid capacity

Grid capacity, transformers, finance, staff, and critical minerals are not allocated honestly if the place capacity under them is imaginary.

A serious siting system tracks:

- low-conflict land and roofs;
- brownfields, parking lots, mines, landfills, degraded land, reservoirs, rail / road / transmission corridors, public buildings, and repowering sites;
- high-biodiversity, high-carbon, high-cultural-value, high-water-stress, high-food-value, and high-exposure places;
- corridors needed for transmission, distribution, pipelines for truly hard residuals, rail, ports, evacuation, and emergency service;
- cumulative burden on communities that already host extraction, generation, waste, traffic, heat, flood, or pollution;
- future climate exposure at the site itself.

### 3. Start with avoid, not compensate

The first question is not “how do we offset damage?”
It is “what damage should be avoided because a lower-conflict path exists?”

The hierarchy is:

1. avoid no-go and high-conflict places;
2. minimise footprint and service demand;
3. co-locate with existing infrastructure;
4. restore and improve where disturbance is unavoidable;
5. compensate only for residual impacts that survive the first four steps;
6. refuse projects whose residual harms break rights, ecology, water security, or legitimacy tests [S330][S331][S332].

### 4. Use already-modified places before expanding conflict

The first shelf should usually be roofs, parking lots, warehouses, public buildings, brownfields, mine lands, landfills, wastewater sites, canals, reservoirs, rights-of-way, industrial zones, repowering sites, and degraded lands.

That will not solve every siting problem.
But it changes the politics and physics of the queue: many packets can move without asking new communities or ecosystems to absorb all the burden of delayed planning.

### 5. Co-location is a climate-speed tool

Where safe and legitimate, co-location should become a default design question:

- solar plus storage;
- solar plus parking shade;
- solar plus irrigation canals;
- solar plus buildings;
- wind plus compatible farming or grazing;
- transmission plus transport corridors;
- charging plus depots and logistics nodes;
- district cooling plus public buildings and shelters;
- resilience hubs plus schools, clinics, libraries, and community centres.

Co-location does not erase rights or local impacts.
It reduces the number of separate footprint fights the programme must win.

### 6. Biodiversity, water, food, and cultural filters are throughput filters

A packet that ignores biodiversity, water, food, heritage, sacred places, customary use, or community identity is not faster.
It is late in a way that has not happened yet.

Target 1 of the Global Biodiversity Framework requires participatory, integrated, biodiversity-inclusive spatial planning and effective management processes addressing land- and sea-use change while respecting Indigenous and local community rights; Target 3 strengthens the same point for conserved areas and equitable governance [S331][S332].

### 7. Marine and coastal siting need their own ledger

Offshore wind, ports, coastal transmission, desalination, cooling-water intakes, hydrogen / ammonia export terminals, subsea cables, and coastal protection projects use waters that already support fisheries, shipping, habitats, recreation, cultural practice, security, and storm buffering.

Marine siting needs the same discipline as land siting:

- navigation and fisheries conflict mapping;
- seabed and habitat filters;
- cumulative noise and collision assessment;
- port and grid readiness;
- storm, sea-level, corrosion, and maintenance access tests;
- decommissioning and repowering duties;
- benefit and grievance rules for affected coastal communities.

### 8. Permitting should be fast because planning is better, not because reality is ignored

The programme should reject two false choices:

- slow, bespoke, opaque permitting for every project;
- blanket acceleration that suppresses evidence, rights, or local knowledge.

The better answer is pre-planned, evidence-rich, rights-respecting acceleration: clear maps, pre-consulted zones, model conditions, standard monitoring, transparent trade-offs, appeal routes, and correction triggers [S60][S338].

### 9. Cumulative impact is a first-order variable

Each project can look tolerable alone while the sequence becomes intolerable.

The place ledger should track cumulative effects from:

- repeated construction traffic;
- visual, noise, heat, dust, and flood changes;
- water use;
- habitat fragmentation;
- land-price pressure;
- cultural loss;
- emergency-access burden;
- tax and service changes;
- old fossil, mining, waste, and industrial burdens that are not gone simply because the next asset is clean.

### 10. Completion condition

A footprint-bearing packet is mature only when the chosen place has passed:

- rights screen;
- ecological and water screen;
- cumulative-impact screen;
- alternatives screen;
- grid / access / service screen;
- climate-exposure screen;
- host-community and grievance screen;
- decommissioning / restoration / end-of-life screen;
- public evidence and correction screen.

Installed megawatts, batteries, heat pumps, transmission lines, mines, ports, roads, hydrogen terminals, and recycling plants do not count as stable climate progress if their place ledger is missing or fraudulent.

## Preferred use

Use this canon when the live question is about:

- renewable siting;
- transmission corridors;
- ports, depots, mines, recycling plants, hydrogen or CCS facilities;
- land conflict;
- offshore wind or coastal infrastructure;
- biodiversity and water conflicts;
- cumulative impacts;
- project delays caused by local opposition;
- “go faster” permitting claims that do not say what planning work has moved upstream.

## Compression rule

**Place is climate infrastructure. Govern land, water, seabed, corridors, ecological limits, cumulative impacts, and decommissioning duties before the project arrives, or the clean queue becomes a place-conflict queue.**

## Rev0254 addendum — old dirty places are place capacity only after repair

This file says place is climate infrastructure. Rev0254 adds the legacy-site qualifier: brownfields, mine lands, landfills, ash sites, refineries, terminals, well pads, and other already-modified places are often the first places to examine, but they are not automatically low-conflict.

A legacy site should enter the place-capacity map only with a remediation-and-reuse passport that identifies contamination, methane, hydrology, subsidence, climate exposure, residual restrictions, community vision, long-term monitoring, and liability owners. Already-modified land is a speed tool only when the old harm is governed rather than hidden [S344][S345][S348].
