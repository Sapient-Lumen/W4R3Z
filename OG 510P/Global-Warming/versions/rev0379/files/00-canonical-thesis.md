# 00 — Canonical Thesis

## Claim

The ideal solution to global warming is an **emergency buildout and managed phase-down program**.

Ideal, in this archive, means **minimizing overshoot, minimizing future dependence on large-scale removals, and maximizing the chance that decarbonization is experienced as safer, healthier, more affordable daily life rather than abstract sacrifice** [S58][S85][S90][S100][S101].

“Buildout” means cheap clean power, grids, storage, efficiency, electrification, public transit, passive cooling and efficient appliances, **deep building retrofits, efficient new construction, clean heat, clean cooking where relevant**, resilient buildings, sustainable agrifood systems, ecosystem protection, peatland and wetland restoration where damage is already severe, adaptation capacity, early warning, resilient public systems, **secure mineral and battery supply chains**, **water-security and hydrological-stability measures** such as watershed protection, drought planning, floodplain discipline, groundwater governance, and selective water reuse, and a power-system-integration layer that includes queue reform, demand flexibility, interconnection, and selected clean firming resources for residual reliability needs. It also means a **delivery layer**: better spatial planning, faster and more predictable permitting, staffed permit authorities and grid operators, workforce pipelines, and credible community participation so projects can actually move [S59][S60][S61][S62][S63][S64][S72][S73][S74][S75][S76]. It further means a **truth layer**: measured methane, interoperable product definitions, public progress reporting, standardised data exchange where climate systems need to talk to each other, **gross-emissions-first accounting**, and enforceable MRV / liability rules where carbon management is claimed so paper claims do not substitute for real decarbonization [S11][S65][S66][S67][S68][S113][S123][S124][S135][S136]. It also needs an **affordability-and-access layer**: targeted support for vulnerable households, social-housing-first retrofits, access to electricity and clean cooking where still absent, and tariff / market design that lowers bills structurally rather than preserving fossil dependence through blunt subsidies [S58][S69][S70][S71]. It also needs a **fiscal-durability layer**: public money should generally buy durable assets, lower future operating cost, targeted bridge support, and risk reduction rather than permanent blanket subsidy burn for an unchanged dirty baseline [S137][S138][S139][S143][S144][S145][S146]. It also needs an **urban-form layer**: compact growth where appropriate, transit-supportive land use, hazard-aware siting, urban heat reduction, and municipal procurement / planning strong enough that housing, mobility, cooling, and water systems reinforce rather than sabotage decarbonization [S77][S78][S79][S80]. It also needs a **demand-shaping layer**: lower-energy and lower-material ways of delivering mobility, shelter, comfort, food, and durable goods through better design, compact form, repairability, reuse, and waste reduction rather than endless clean substitution for wasteful baseline demand [S58][S97][S98][S99]. It also needs an **exposure-discipline layer**: open risk information, insurance realism, repeat-loss limits, no-build rules where warranted, and public buyout / relocation capacity so silent market exit does not become the only retreat mechanism [S81][S82][S83][S84]. It also needs a **health layer**: fast combustion-pollution reduction, heat-health protection, and climate-resilient low-carbon care systems so the transition is experienced as cleaner air and safer daily life rather than abstract future benefit [S85][S86][S87][S88][S89][S90]. It also needs a **finance-architecture layer** in EMDE: lower cost of capital, more local-currency finance, stronger guarantee capacity, and enough concessional / blended support that mature clean technologies can actually scale where future demand growth is concentrated [S13][S91][S92][S93][S94][S95][S96].  
“Managed phase-down” means stopping new long-lived unabated fossil lock-in and retiring existing high-emitting assets on deliberate schedules rather than hoping demand falls by itself [S1][S4][S9][S36][S37][S38][S47][S48][S49][S50].

## Why this is the right center of gravity

The current situation is contradictory:

- the world has strong clean-energy momentum: clean energy is attracting about **USD 2.2 trillion** in 2025, roughly **double** fossil investment [S6]
- clean energy is meeting virtually all growth in aggregate energy demand in IEA’s stated-policies case, and IEA sees demand for all three fossil fuels peaking before 2030 in that case [S4]
- yet emissions are still too high: 2024 energy-related CO2 rose again [S7], and the 2025 Global Carbon Budget says fossil CO2 is reaching another record high while the remaining 1.5°C carbon budget is “virtually exhausted” [S10]

So the ideal solution is **not** merely “more transition.” It is to convert transition momentum into **absolute decline in total greenhouse-gas emissions**, fast enough to matter. And it must do so without assuming natural sinks will stay stable by themselves: the Global Carbon Budget 2025 says climate change is already weakening land and ocean sinks and that 8% of the rise in atmospheric CO2 since 1960 is due to this weakening [S39].

## Rev0269 cube and service-floor corollary

The archive now treats the service-continuity family as a datacube problem, not merely as a sequence of additional notes. A serious climate packet should declare its object type, domain, service floor, hazard tags, clock, owning actors, instruments, bottlenecks, proof ledgers, evidence grade, and failure modes before it is admitted as canon [S40][S83][S536][S537].

This does not demote mitigation. It protects mitigation from becoming abstract. Clean power, electrification, methane cuts, ecosystem protection, finance, and managed fossil decline still form the core physical transition; the cube layer asks whether those transitions keep water, food, health, shelter, mobility, communications, energy, waste, public safety, cooling refuge, legal access, family reunification, and recovery records functioning under degraded conditions [S40][S536][S537][S539][S540].

It also adds a claims-discipline corollary: carbon-market, offset, neutralization, and credited-mitigation claims are not substitutes for gross emissions cuts, fossil-asset decline, or physical service protection unless additionality, permanence, accounting, rights, leakage, and liability tests are explicit enough to survive independent audit [S135][S136][S280][S281][S546].

## Design rules

### 1. Prefer options that cut a lot before 2035
Near-term reduction speed matters because cumulative emissions govern peak warming and overshoot risk [S1][S2]. The archive now adds a tighter latency corollary: where two serious measures are both worth doing, give extra weight to the one whose physical or lived benefits begin arriving sooner, because overshoot reduction, bill relief, and political durability are all path-sensitive during the 2025-2035 window [S101][S133][S134].

### 2. Prefer options that improve multiple systems at once
The best interventions also improve air quality, affordability, energy security, resilience, or public health. Cooling policy is exemplary here: done well, it protects people from heat while avoiding unnecessary peak load and emissions [S16][S17]. Buildings policy is exemplary too: good envelopes, clean heat, and cleaner cooking can cut emissions while also improving comfort, indoor air quality, and energy affordability [S28][S51][S52][S53][S54]. Demand-shaping policy is exemplary in the same way: compact urban form, repairability, reuse, and food-waste reduction can lower emissions while also reducing bills, congestion, air pollution, and material pressure [S58][S97][S98][S99]. These co-benefits make climate policy politically durable.

### 3. Prefer infrastructure, low-friction defaults, flywheels, corrigible systems, and polycentric delivery over virtue signaling
Infrastructure compounds. Build decisions today shape emissions for decades. But buildout speed is also institutional, financial, and political. OECD’s 2025 green-transition work argues governments need a broader transformation of institutional, policy, and regulatory arrangements if they are to design, deliver, and execute climate policy at the speed Paris-consistent transitions require [S59]. The archive now adds a practical delivery corollary: the best measures should usually arrive through **low-friction defaults** — standards, passive design, one-stop delivery, standardised contracts, targeted support at the point of decision, and administrative systems that do not force citizens to become experts in order to participate [S53][S118][S119][S120]. It adds an interoperability corollary too: the best measures should rely on common metrics, open or mutually recognised interfaces, and non-bespoke data / assurance architectures so they can connect and travel across markets rather than stalling in translation [S68][S119][S123][S124][S125]. It now adds a copyability corollary as well: the best measures should usually be expressible as reference designs, checklists, model codes, train-the-trainer packages, and transparent finance / procurement templates that can be adapted across jurisdictions without forcing each place to start from a blank page [S150][S151][S152][S153]. It now adds a modularity corollary as well: where serious substitutes exist, the best measures should usually scale through many repeatable units and stepwise additions so useful capacity, learning, and public benefit can arrive without waiting for a few synchronized mega-bets [S154][S155][S156]. It now adds a platform-assets corollary as well: the best measures should usually build general-purpose infrastructure, interoperable systems, and credibly repurposable assets — especially stronger electricity systems, efficient buildings, adaptable corridors, ecological restorations, and truth infrastructure — that remain useful across several plausible decarbonized futures rather than only under one brittle technology story [S51][S55][S68][S72][S73][S157][S158][S159][S160]. It now adds a turnover-aware corollary as well: the best measures should usually attach themselves to natural decision points — sale, rental, replacement, renovation, procurement, fleet renewal, and post-disaster rebuild — so the cleaner or safer option wins when capital stock is already changing rather than missing the moment and locking in another dirty or fragile cycle [S81][S82][S83][S84][S161][S162][S163][S164]. It now adds a dependency-light corollary as well: the best measures should usually solve the largest possible share of the problem with the fewest missing prerequisites, clear near-term signals, and minimal need for many new infrastructures, offtakers, standards, and business models to mature simultaneously; direct electrification and efficiency should generally outrank more dependency-heavy chains in easier uses, while the harder chains are reserved for genuinely no-substitute cases [S112][S160][S165][S166][S167]. It now adds a physicality corollary as well: climate success should be counted first in actual gross emissions, measured methane, verified durable storage, asset retirement, hectares protected, and exposure reduction rather than mainly in certificate volumes or paper-neutrality claims [S113][S123][S135][S136]. It adds an anti-capture corollary as well: the best measures should usually move through competitive, transparent, auditable, low-discretion procurement and allocation systems rather than bespoke insider deals, stop-go subsidy bargains, or opaque exceptions that invite collusion and rent extraction [S129][S130][S131][S132]. It now adds an incentive-compatibility corollary too: the best measures should usually make the cleaner, more flexible, less wasteful, and more protective action financially normal through aligned prices, targeted support, payment for useful system services, and explicit compensation where visible losses would otherwise block progress [S23][S53][S56][S69][S137][S138][S139]. It now adds a legitimacy corollary as well: the best measures should be publicly justifiable, visibly fair, open to meaningful participation and redress, and legible as public-interest projects rather than as expert impositions or insider bargains [S59][S71][S138][S140][S141][S142]. It now adds a fiscal-durability corollary as well: the best measures should usually lower future public and private burn rates by building durable assets, standards, savings-backed finance, and targeted bridge support rather than depending on indefinite blanket subsidy politics [S137][S138][S139][S143][S144][S145][S146]. It now adds a political-durability corollary as well: the best measures should generally be hard to reverse abruptly because they are anchored in law, plans, standards, institutions, contracts, physical assets, and broad beneficiary groups rather than in one temporary governing coalition’s enthusiasm [S147][S148][S149]. It now adds a buffering corollary as well: the best measures should retain enough reserve, redundancy, storage, diversity, and surge capacity that stressful periods do not force abrupt fossil fallback, service failure, or emergency improvisation [S16][S17][S76][S108][S126][S127][S128]. It also adds a stewardship corollary: the best measures should be designed for **whole-of-life performance**, with credible operating budgets, maintenance plans, trained operators and maintainers, and service-level monitoring rather than capex-only success stories [S121][S122]. IRENA, IEA, OECD, and the archive’s newer ideal-solution notes add the wider set of delivery-side consequences: the best measures do not only cut emissions once, they create learning curves, stronger supply chains, better routines, and wider constituencies that make the next round cheaper and easier; they also generate feedback that lets governments correct weak performance and tighten working measures over time rather than waiting for strategic failure; they can be advanced through multiple venues — national, subnational, regulatory, financial, and non-state — so progress is less likely to die in one blocked lane; and they should keep performing after installation rather than quietly decaying through maintenance neglect or operating undercapacity; and they should be difficult to hijack through collusion, opaque discretion, or rent-seeking procurement design as they scale [S30][S48][S59][S77][S80][S107][S111][S113][S114][S115][S116][S117][S121][S122][S129][S130][S131][S132]. The archive now adds a timing corollary as well: the best measures should usually include a thick layer of short-latency gains — methane abatement, efficiency, passive design, repair, and protection work whose benefits begin arriving quickly enough to matter for overshoot, peak load, bills, and trust while slower backbone investments are still being built [S101][S106][S133][S134]. It now adds a place-sensitivity corollary as well: the best measures should travel as stable principles with adaptable edges, fitting climate zone, settlement pattern, resource endowment, exposure, and institutional reality in each place rather than arriving as rigid one-size-fits-all templates or as bespoke exceptions with no common core [S172][S173][S174][S175]. It now adds a capability-abundance corollary as well: where serious substitutes exist, the best measures should usually run on broad, expandable workforce fit, short training ladders, transferable certifications, and standard playbooks rather than on narrow pools of rare specialists or unusually strong institutions, reserving scarce expert labour for the genuinely hard bottlenecks [S176][S177][S178][S179]. It now adds a routinization corollary as well: the best measures should usually run on visible, recurring calendars — auction rounds, standards updates, procurement pipelines, training intakes, reporting cycles, and review windows — so the transition becomes boring repeatable administration rather than a chain of one-off campaigns and announcement bursts [S180][S181][S182][S183]. It now adds a repairability corollary as well: the best measures should usually be designed for maintenance, component replacement, software support, refurbishment, and safe second-life use where relevant, so serviceable assets are kept useful longer instead of being discarded early through sealed-box design, short support windows, or blocked repair pathways [S184][S185][S186][S187][S188][S189].

### 4. Reduce unnecessary demand first where possible, then electrify what remains
Power decarbonization is the master key because it unlocks cleaner road transport, buildings, cooling, and portions of industry [S4][S5][S17]. But the archive now sharpens the ordering rule: avoid and shift unnecessary demand where system design can do so cleanly, then electrify whatever remains practical, while reserving scarce low-emissions fuels for aviation, shipping, and only the genuinely hardest freight applications [S30][S33][S34][S35][S58][S97][S98].

### 5. Build the wires, the flexibility, and the reliability architecture
The transition fails if generation races ahead while grids, interconnection, demand flexibility, and system balancing lag behind. IEA now expects solar PV and wind to rise from **17%** of global generation today to **27%** by 2030, while more than **2 500 GW** of projects remain stuck in connection queues worldwide [S55]. Grid planning is climate policy, not back-office utility policy [S5][S14]. It is now also a materials policy problem: copper, transformers, batteries, and refined inputs cannot be treated as someone else’s downstream issue [S47][S48][S50]. Buildings are part of the same systems problem: inefficient envelopes, fossil heating, and unmanaged peak demand can waste clean power as fast as the grid adds it [S28][S51][S52][S53][S54]. Demand flexibility is part of the fix, not a side issue: IEA says it can improve the use of existing generation and network assets by up to **30%**, yet only around **100 GW** is currently utilised globally [S56][S58].

### 6. Hit methane as an emergency lane
Methane cuts act faster on near-term warming than CO2 cuts alone; methane from the energy sector is also materially undercounted in many official inventories [S8][S11].

### 7. Treat agrifood, ecosystems, water security, and material demand as coupled stability problems
Stopping deforestation is necessary but not sufficient. A real solution must also protect peatlands, wetlands, mangroves, and other carbon-rich ecosystems, while cutting livestock and rice methane, improving nitrogen use efficiency, reducing food loss and waste, extending product lifetimes where possible, and protecting watersheds and aquifers, because agrifood and land-use emissions remain large, resource extraction and processing drive a major share of climate impact, and hydrological instability now directly threatens food systems, ecosystems, and settlement resilience [S10][S18][S19][S20][S21][S36][S37][S38][S39][S72][S73][S74][S75][S97][S98][S99].

### 8. Treat water security as civilizational infrastructure
Only about one-third of river basins had normal conditions in 2024, glacier losses continued worldwide for a third straight year, and global freshwater reserves have been falling over the past two decades [S72][S75]. The archive therefore treats watershed protection, drought planning, groundwater discipline, selective reuse, and floodplain rules as part of the climate stack itself rather than background adaptation context [S72][S73][S74][S75].

### 9. Treat buildings as a first-order delivery lane
Buildings are where climate strategy becomes lived reality: heat, cold, bills, cooking, comfort, and peak demand. Advanced economies need mass retrofits and clean heat because most of the 2050 stock already exists; fast-growing economies need efficient new construction, passive cooling, and stronger codes because most future floor area has not yet been locked in [S28][S51][S52][S53][S54]. The archive now makes the timing rule explicit too: building strategy should capture sale, rental, major renovation, refinancing, and equipment-failure moments, because those are when standards, finance, and disclosure can most credibly redirect the next long-lived choice [S161][S162].

### 10. Treat cities and urban form as climate infrastructure
Cities are where buildings, transport, cooling, water, and affordability meet. UNDP’s 2024 urban-NDC review says cities are responsible for about **67%** of global energy consumption and **70%** of greenhouse-gas emissions, while UN-Habitat adds that urban areas host about **57%** of the world’s population on roughly **1.7%** of its surface [S77][S78]. The archive therefore treats compact and connected growth, transit-supportive land use, flood-aware siting, urban heat reduction, and city-capable finance as climate policy rather than municipal garnish.

### 11. Treat delivery capacity as climate infrastructure
Permitting, inspection, grid connection, vocational training, and local administrative competence are not implementation afterthoughts. They are part of the climate stack itself. IEA says around **60%** of companies in the energy sector reported labour shortages, already affecting timelines, reliability, and cost control, while OECD’s 2025 permitting work describes complex procedures, staffing shortages, and weak coordination as live barriers to deployment [S60][S62]. IRENA and ILO estimate at least **16.6 million** renewable-energy jobs globally in 2024, which means labour and training policy now shape the transition directly rather than indirectly [S63].

### 12. Finance the Global South or fail globally
A large share of future energy demand and infrastructure growth is in EMDE. If concessional finance, guarantees, local-currency finance, and lower cost of capital do not scale, the transition remains too geographically narrow to solve the problem [S4][S13][S91][S92][S93][S94][S95][S96]. The archive now treats this as a distinct design rule: mature clean technology is not enough if money stays expensive, FX risk stays high, and debt space stays thin where future buildout is largest.

### 13. Treat affordability and access as design constraints, not PR garnish
A climate program that arrives as higher exposure, higher hassle, and poorly targeted bills will eventually lose legitimacy even when the technology stack is correct. IEA’s 2026 affordability work says more than **120 million** households in advanced economies already spend over **10%** of their income on residential heating, cooling, and appliances, and that in many emerging and developing economies affordability is still a binding constraint on electricity, clean cooking, and cooling access [S69]. The archive therefore treats targeted support, energy access, clean cooking, social-housing-first retrofit policy, and bill-lowering efficiency as part of the core solution rather than compensatory afterthoughts [S58][S69][S70][S71].

Insurance strain now sharpens the same legitimacy point from another angle. OECD’s 2026 catastrophe-finance work says increasing wildfire losses are already affecting the availability of affordable insurance in some wildfire-exposed countries, with possible housing-market implications because mortgage lenders often require insurance [S81]. The archive therefore now treats open risk maps, no-build rules in repeated-loss zones, and public buyout / relocation capacity as part of the solution itself rather than late-stage emergency improvisation [S81][S82][S83][S84].

### 14. Treat truth infrastructure as climate infrastructure
Methane measurement, interoperable product definitions, public emissions reporting, and credible MRV / liability rules are not clerical extras. They determine whether methane cuts, industrial procurement, and carbon-management claims are governable in practice. UNFCCC’s first BTR synthesis explicitly frames transparency as part of the evidence base for policymaking and accountability, while IEA and UNEP now show that methane undercounting and non-response to measured large emissions events remain live implementation failures [S11][S65][S66][S67][S68].

### 15. Treat adaptation as civil protection, not surrender
As climate impacts intensify, mitigation without visible protection will lose public legitimacy. WMO still reports that only about half of countries have adequate multi-hazard early warning systems, while UNDRR says disaster costs now exceed USD 2.3 trillion annually when cascading and ecosystem costs are counted [S15][S40]. The ideal solution therefore protects grids, hospitals, schools, water systems, transport links, and vulnerable households as explicitly as it protects the climate system. As electricity becomes the backbone of buildings, transport, cooling, and parts of industry, power-system reliability is part of that protection rather than a separate technical afterthought.

### 16. Keep removals subordinate and bounded
CDR is needed for residual emissions, but it is not the main engine of stabilization and should not be allowed to displace gross emissions cuts [S1]. The archive now makes the boundary more explicit: current novel CDR is still tiny relative to what many pathways assume, and durable storage infrastructure remains far from scaled, so removals doctrine must stay residual-first, durability-aware, and hostile to fossil-delay rhetoric [S42][S43][S44][S45][S46].

### 17. Treat clean-energy supply chains as part of the solution, not background plumbing
A world that electrifies fast but accepts extreme concentration in refining, processing, battery manufacturing, and copper supply has not fully solved the transition problem. IEA’s 2025 minerals outlook says the top three refining countries for key energy minerals raised their average market share from about 82% in 2020 to 86% in 2024, while recycling can materially reduce new mining and financing needs if scaled [S47][S50]. The archive therefore favors mineral realism with diversification, recycling, and stronger social license rather than either naive extraction boosterism or magical dematerialization rhetoric.

### 18. Treat health as a core outcome and delivery lane
Climate policy is not only about tonnes, technologies, and treaties. It is also about cleaner lungs, safer work, lower heat stress, and care systems that can still function during floods, fires, outages, and heat waves. WHO says combined ambient and household air pollution is associated with about **7 million premature deaths** annually, while heat stress is the leading cause of weather-related deaths [S85][S86]. ILO says at least **2.41 billion** workers are exposed to excessive heat each year, and the Lancet Countdown now estimates **USD 1.09 trillion** in potential income losses from labour-capacity reduction due to extreme heat in 2024 [S87][S88]. The archive therefore treats clean air, occupational heat protection, and climate-resilient low-carbon health systems as part of the climate stack itself rather than only downstream co-benefits [S89][S90].

## What the archive rejects

- net-zero rhetoric without gross near-term cuts
- offsets as substitutes for actual decarbonization
- fossil expansion justified by hypothetical future carbon capture or future removals accounting
- adaptation treated as surrender rather than a parallel duty
- solar radiation modification treated as a core solution set
- “technology neutrality” when some tools are clearly faster and higher leverage than others

## Working summary

**Ideal climate strategy = build clean systems faster than demand grows, shape cities and urban growth so housing, transport, cooling, and water systems reinforce low-emissions daily life, build the delivery state that lets clean systems land in the real world, integrate power systems so the clean electricity actually counts, make the transition visibly affordable and accessible to ordinary households, measure and enforce the transition well enough that climate claims can be trusted, stop repeated-loss zones from becoming permanent public and household exposure traps, retire fossil systems faster than incumbents can entrench, cut methane immediately, protect forests and carbon-rich wetlands before they become new sources, slash wasteful agrifood emissions, cut dirty-air exposure fast, protect workers and households from dangerous heat, protect people and critical systems from now-locked-in damage, and use removals only for the residue, while making the clean and resilient option easier by default than the dirty and fragile one.**

## Rev0270 interdependency and atmospheric-risk corollary

The cube layer now requires not only service floors but **interdependency maps**. A climate packet is incomplete if it protects a named service while leaving its upstream dependencies, downstream consequences, degraded modes, restoration conflicts, and correction ledgers invisible. This is the difference between a list of lifelines and an operating system for cascades [S536][S537][S563].

rev0270 also fills four evidence gaps that were too thin in earlier revisions: long-tail physical commitments such as cryosphere, permafrost, and sea-level risk [S547][S548]; short-lived climate pollutants beyond the methane-only frame [S549][S550]; clean-air continuity during smoke, pollution, heat, and outage [S551][S553][S555]; and plastics / petrochemical growth as a fossil-demand, waste, and health system [S556][S557].

Finally, it adds two proof lenses that should apply across all service floors: gendered safety / maternal and reproductive-health continuity [S558][S560], and custodial / closed-institution protection for people who cannot freely self-evacuate, self-cool, or self-filter air [S561][S562].


## rev0271 hidden-rails corollary

rev0271 adds the hidden rails beneath service floors: medical products and oxygen, digital infrastructure and cyber recovery, payment rails and cash access, humanitarian logistics, displacement / receiving capacity, climate-conflict integrity, and readiness assurance. The archive should not treat these as support functions. They are the difference between a service floor that exists on paper and a service floor that works under outage, heat, smoke, flood, conflict, cyber disruption, aid scarcity, or displacement [S564][S565][S567][S568][S570][S571][S573][S574][S575].

New rule: **every continuity packet must identify hidden rails, degraded modes, and assurance tests before it can be marked ready.**

## Rev0272 cube extension — recovery rails and proof capacity

rev0272 adds a recovery-rail layer to the canonical thesis. A climate program does not protect people merely by naming service floors; it must protect the rails that make return and restoration possible: emergency energy inputs and black-start readiness, repair and inspection throughput, claims and disaster-assistance cashflow, floodwater management, local-market continuity, trusted community bridges, agrifood input cycles, and lab / inspection / sampling proof capacity.

This changes the test for adaptation and recovery. The question is no longer only “was there a plan?” or “was money appropriated?” The sharper question is: **can the service become real under bad-day conditions — with fuel, spares, contractors, claims, records, inspectors, labs, local businesses, community trust, and degraded-mode operation actually available?** See `323`–`330` [S499][S576][S579][S580][S581][S582][S583][S584][S586].

## Rev0273 addendum — scarcity priority and civic legitimacy are now part of continuity

rev0273 adds the next operating layer beneath service floors: **scarce restoration priority, mutual aid, critical spares, maintenance debt, behavioral health, civic continuity, reentry access, and scenario loadcases.** A service floor is not ready if it cannot decide restoration priority under shortage, cannot borrow help when local capacity is exhausted, cannot find the parts that restoration requires, has hidden maintenance debt, ignores trauma and grief, suspends public legitimacy during shocks, blocks essential care / repair / resident return through unmanaged reentry controls, or passes only easy tabletop scenarios [S587][S590][S592][S595][S598][S600][S602].

## Rev0274 addendum — service floors fail at human interfaces

rev0274 adds a protective-interface layer. By this stage the archive could name services, dependencies, recovery rails, scarcity rules, mutual aid, spares, maintenance, civic continuity, reentry, and drills. The remaining defect was that people meet the system through shelters, registries, aid queues, contractors, checkpoints, animals, death records, cultural anchors, and last-mile geography.

The new rule is: **a service floor is not real if the point of access creates danger, surveillance, extraction, indignity, or geographic invisibility.** Safe refuge, animal continuity, wildfire / WUI risk reduction, protective data, recovery-market consumer protection, dignified fatality management, safeguarding, remote access, and cultural continuity are now first-class routing objects [S603][S605][S607][S610][S612][S614][S616][S618].


## Rev0275 addendum — rebuild authority is now part of the minimum doctrine

The archive should now treat rebuilding as an authority-and-proof system, not only a construction or finance problem. A climate recovery programme is incomplete until it can answer eight questions: who can prove home, title, tenancy, occupancy, or loss; who can exit risk through buyout or relocation; who can contract fast without capture; who fronts costs and documents reimbursement; which code governs rebuilding; who sustains the workers; who controls shared housing assets; and which unmet needs remain after official closeout. Route to `348`–`355` [S619][S624][S626][S635].

## Rev0276 datacube discipline: score, own, refresh, finance, and boundary-test the floor

The archive should no longer treat a canon note as proof of readiness. A service floor becomes operational only when it has a scenario-relative readiness grade, owner of record, freshness class, fiscal / market stress check, and excluded-user path. Rev0276 adds this discipline through `356`–`363`: score the floor, name the owner, demote stale evidence, test fiscal liquidity, expose insurance / mortgage market exit, preclear cross-border help, govern shared basins, and test informal / worker-camp access paths [S637][S638].

## Rev0277 datacube discipline: query, observe, verify, contest

The archive should no longer treat a service-floor row as operational merely because it is scored and owned. The next proof layer is observability: can the claim be queried, dashboarded, sensed, challenged, corrected, and defended against rumor, cyber-physical failure, toxic release, disease blind spots, and false precision?

Rev0277 adds `364`–`371`: query views, public dashboards, telemetry and ground truth, rumor control, OT / SCADA safety, Natech and toxic legacy sites, climate-sensitive disease surveillance, and contestable public ledgers. These do not replace mitigation or adaptation. They make the service-floor state legible enough to govern [S653][S654][S655][S661][S663].

## Rev0278 addition — warning is not action

The archive now treats forecasts, dashboards, telemetry, alerts, and public ledgers as incomplete unless they release owned pre-impact action. The new forecast-to-action layer routes from observation and warning into triggers, authority, anticipatory finance, protective action, heat and smoke thresholds, interoperable alerts, false-alarm learning, pre-positioning, and shock-responsive household support. Route first to `372`, then to `373`–`380` for operating packets.

## Rev0279 addendum — impact-to-intake is now part of climate continuity

rev0278 made forecasts actionable before impact. rev0279 closes the next gap: once impact occurs, the system must convert incident status, damage, needs, building safety, survivor intake, commodities, volunteers, and emergency authority into routed action. The archive should no longer accept a service-floor claim that has no EOC / common-operating-picture path, no rapid-assessment revision rule, no lifeline stabilization clock, no safe-return / placard gate, no accessible intake / grievance route, no commodity distribution posture, no volunteer / donations management, and no rights-preserving emergency-powers guardrail [S687][S688][S689][S691][S692][S693][S695][S696].

New rule: **early warning is incomplete unless the post-impact intake and assessment loop can prove what happened, who was missed, what action was assigned, what restriction was imposed, and how the record can be corrected.**

## Rev0280 addition — stabilization-to-recovery pathways

rev0280 adds the rule that impact-to-intake is not recovery. A person can be registered, sheltered, assessed, or referred and still remain stranded. Climate service floors therefore need stabilization pathways: interim-to-durable housing, displaced-student stability, chronic-care continuity, nutrition-benefit continuity, utility-arrears protection, deadline tolling, and no-wrong-door benefit sequencing [S698][S699][S712].

The cube should now ask not only whether a service exists, but whether a household can move from first contact to a durable outcome without losing school, medicine, food, utilities, legal rights, or case status along the way.

## rev0281 addendum — recovery must be measured by household function, not case closure

rev0281 adds a household-function and recovery-closeout layer. The archive had reached stabilization pathways: intake, housing transition, school continuity, chronic care, nutrition, utility arrears, deadline tolling, and no-wrong-door benefit sequencing. The remaining defect was that recovery could still be declared while the household could not function.

The cube now asks whether safe housing, income or benefit continuity, credit/debt protection, tenancy stability, livelihood return, mobility, essential property and devices, document / account recovery, and nonrecurrence closeout are visible after case closure. A recovery programme that closes cases while households lose credit, jobs, leases, phones, documents, vehicles, devices, medicine, or appeal routes is not finished. It has merely transferred the climate shock to private balance sheets and hidden ledgers [S714][S715][S717][S719][S720][S721][S723].

## rev0282 addendum — closeout must become risk reduction, not restored exposure

rev0282 adds the next rule after household-function recovery: **do not close recovery back into the same hazard geometry.** A programme that restores documents, income, housing, utilities, and case status but then rebuilds the same repeated-loss pattern has stabilized harm, not reduced it.

The cube now asks whether closeout findings become a funded mitigation pipeline, whether repetitive-loss and chronic-exposure places are visible, whether maps and design values use future conditions, whether projects have finance stacks and equity-aware benefit-cost tests, whether land-use and infrastructure-extension decisions prevent new risk, whether nature-based solutions have lifecycle performance ledgers, and whether completed projects are monitored for actual risk reduction. Route to `405`–`412` [S726][S727][S733][S736][S738].

## Rev0283 addendum — adaptation must be path-governed

The canon now treats adaptation and resilience as pathway-governed rather than project-governed. A mature programme must expose signposts, thresholds, model assumptions, option value, lock-in risks, portfolio stress tests, service-level contracts, unsafe-asset exits, and professional duties. Otherwise recovery can rebuild, mitigation can fund projects, and dashboards can look current while the system still commits to a brittle future [S741][S743][S745].

## Rev0284 addendum — maturity must be auditable

The canon now treats the datacube itself as a governed object. A service floor is not mature because it appears in the index; it is mature when its claim can be decomposed into normalized cube rows, source provenance, internal controls, corrective-action closure, independent challenge, civil-rights access, procurement transparency, workforce competency, and an assurance case for high-stakes claims. Route to `421`–`428` [S756][S761][S763][S768].

## Rev0289 nuclear-positive thesis amendment

The canonical thesis now explicitly favors nuclear energy as a clean-firm, reliability-preserving decarbonization path where safety, siting, waste, water, delivery, finance, emergency-planning, workforce and public-challenge gates can be met. This does not erase renewables, efficiency, storage, transmission, geothermal, hydro or demand flexibility; it changes the default question for firm clean energy from "why nuclear?" to "why not nuclear, and which gates remain unproven?"

The preference is justified as a transparent policy orientation, not as a hidden score. Deep energy-system decarbonization requires rapid emissions reductions [S774], nuclear power is a low-carbon dispatchable source [S773], and current nuclear outlooks emphasize both rising policy momentum and hard delivery risks [S771][S776]. Lifecycle and system comparisons must remain explicit [S775][S779].


## Rev0294 nuclear integrated-energy propagation note

Rev0294 extends the nuclear-positive preference into cogeneration, district heating, process heat, desalination, hydrogen and clean molecules, data-center/AI infrastructure, critical loads, and co-product public-value scorecards. These additions are explicitly bounded by new gates NG_53–NG_66 and by sector-coupling exception rules; preference is not treated as maturity without local evidence.

## Rev0297 nuclear geopolitical-resilience overlay

The nuclear-positive orientation now includes an explicit geopolitical-resilience burden of proof. Clean-firm nuclear preference is strengthened when it reduces fossil/import/coercion exposure, but maturity is capped by unresolved fuel-cycle concentration, vendor lock-in, sanctions exposure, export-finance/debt risk, safeguards capacity, export-control boundaries, and sensitive-publication controls.


## Rev0298 nuclear human-capital refactor note

The cube's pro-nuclear orientation is now explicitly conditioned by workforce depth, operator licensing, regulator capacity, safety/quality culture, knowledge retention, craft labor, and public training pathways. Nuclear remains favored for clean firm power and system benefit, but human-capital and institutional-capacity gaps are binding maturity caps rather than implementation footnotes. Sources: [S864], [S868], [S871], [S873], [S875].


## Rev0300 upstream uranium accountability note

This revision keeps the cube explicitly nuclear-positive while adding a responsible uranium supply constraint: pro-nuclear planning must not erase mining, milling, tailings, radon, groundwater, host-community, Indigenous-rights, legacy-cleanup, transport, material-provenance, or cumulative public-health burdens. Nuclear remains preferred in relevant clean-firm contexts, but upstream uranium evidence now caps maturity when missing.

## Rev0303 physical-security nuclearization note

Rev0303 keeps nuclear energy explicitly favored while adding a physical-security maturity cap: design-basis-threat protection, force-on-force or equivalent performance testing, insider mitigation, drone/perimeter posture, transport security, law-enforcement coordination, and security-safe publication must be traceable before nuclear security claims can mature.
