# 34 — Ideal Solutions as Corrigible Systems: Feedback, Measurement, and Ratcheting

## Claim

The archive now has strong answers to what ideal climate solutions should build, exclude, prioritize under scarcity, protect against one-way loss, assemble as packages, and prefer for compounding delivery.

One further rule belongs inside the canon:

**ideal solutions should be designed as corrigible systems.**

That means they should be measurable enough to reveal whether they are working, adjustable enough to improve when they are not, and ratchetable enough to tighten as cleaner substitutes, institutional capability, and political confidence grow.

This is not the same as mere flexibility. A vague plan that can be changed at any time is often only weakly committed. Corrigibility means something firmer:
- strong direction, but revisable instruments,
- explicit goals, but honest feedback,
- durable commitment, but built-in course correction,
- ratchets that tighten with evidence rather than slogans.

UN Climate Change’s transparency system is now fully operational, first BTR cycles have begun to generate review and multilateral consideration, and UNFCCC now treats transparency as a driver of implementation and continuous improvement rather than only a reporting duty [S65][S113]. OECD’s 2025 Climate Action Monitor adds the warning signal on the other side: climate action across covered countries expanded by only **1%** in 2024, far below the average annual pace seen in 2010–2021 [S114]. That combination strengthens the archive’s conclusion: in a world where progress is too slow and conditions keep moving, ideal climate solutions cannot rely on one-shot policy design. They need **feedback loops that improve action over time** [S59][S113][S114].

This note therefore adds a corrigibility rule to the canon:

**prefer measures and policy architectures that can be measured, reviewed, corrected, and progressively tightened without losing legitimacy or direction.**

## Why this belongs inside the ideal solution

### 1. The problem is moving while the solution is being built

Demand changes. Grid queues change. Technology costs change. Finance conditions change. Heat, drought, fire, flood, and migration pressures change. Some measures outperform expectations while others stall.

A climate strategy that assumes one perfect ex ante design is therefore not ideal. It is brittle planning disguised as confidence.

OECD’s green-transition governance work says effective climate delivery depends on commitment, capabilities, and consensus, and that progress monitoring supports evidence-based policy adjustment over time [S59]. UNFCCC’s current transparency cycle makes the same idea more operational: BTRs, technical expert review, and facilitative multilateral consideration are now part of the working machinery through which countries learn what is real, where support is needed, and how future plans should become more ambitious [S113].

### 2. Slow progress makes error correction more valuable, not less

If the world were rapidly over-delivering, weak feedback architecture would matter less.

But OECD says climate action across CAPMF-covered countries expanded by only **1%** in 2024, after similarly slow growth in 2022 and 2023, well below the roughly **10%** annual average increase seen from 2010 to 2021 [S114]. In that environment, the cost of leaving underperforming policies uncorrected becomes high.

Ideal solutions therefore value not only first-round policy quality, but the speed with which weak designs are noticed and improved.

### 3. Truth infrastructure is not enough unless it changes behaviour

The archive already treats methane measurement, interoperable product definitions, public reporting, and hard MRV as core transition infrastructure [S11][S65][S66][S67][S68].

This note sharpens the point.

Measurement is only fully valuable when it changes what happens next:
- methane alerts should trigger repair and enforcement, not only public awareness [S66][S67]
- BTRs should sharpen planning, finance allocation, and NDC design, not only satisfy procedural compliance [S65][S113]
- building, appliance, and industrial-product data should tighten standards and procurement, not only decorate dashboards [S27][S68]
- resilience and exposure data should change siting, retreat, and infrastructure rules, not only improve storytelling [S40][S41][S81][S82][S83][S84]

So the archive now states the harder rule explicitly: **good climate information should feed correction, not merely documentation.**

## Corrigibility is different from neighboring rules

This note is adjacent to several recent notes, but it is not redundant with them.

- `30-ideal-solutions-robustness-failure-tolerance-and-no-regrets.md` asks whether the package still helps when assumptions break.
- `31-ideal-solutions-under-irreversibility-option-value-and-lock-in-avoidance.md` asks which choices preserve future options and prevent one-way loss.
- `32-ideal-solutions-as-packages-complementarity-sequencing-and-bottleneck-release.md` asks which complements and enabling conditions must arrive together and in what order.
- `33-ideal-solutions-as-flywheels-compounding-learning-curves-and-self-reinforcing-delivery.md` asks whether repeated deployment makes later action cheaper and easier.

This note asks something different:

**when evidence shows a gap between intention and reality, can the system notice, learn, and tighten without starting from scratch?**

## Five kinds of corrigibility the archive cares about

### 1. Measurement corrigibility

Ideal solutions prefer interventions whose real-world performance can be observed on a useful timescale.

That upgrades:
- measured methane and methane-response protocols [S11][S66][S67]
- building programs with real delivered-energy and comfort metrics, not only budget accounting [S51][S53][S106]
- grid-planning systems that track queue time, curtailment, transformer shortages, and flexibility uptake [S56][S103][S111]
- product standards and procurement with auditable emissions definitions [S27][S68]
- adaptation programs that track protection coverage, outage time, mortality, repeat-loss exposure, and early-warning reach rather than only money spent [S40][S41][S81][S82][S83][S84][S108]

The archive therefore prefers **observable delivery** over climate policy that mainly reports intentions.

### 2. Institutional corrigibility

Ideal solutions should create institutions that can review and improve decisions without collapsing credibility.

UNFCCC’s ETF now provides a real example of this logic: reporting, technical expert review, facilitative multilateral consideration, and public climate data are becoming part of an iterative implementation architecture rather than an occasional diplomatic ritual [S113]. OECD’s green-governance work similarly treats clear rules, monitoring, and institutional capability as prerequisites for adjustment [S59].

That upgrades:
- recurring review cycles,
- program dashboards tied to budget and regulatory revision,
- independent audit or expert-review functions,
- public procurement systems that can revise specifications as learning accumulates,
- agencies staffed to adjust delivery rules instead of merely defend legacy process.

### 3. Ratchet corrigibility

Some instruments are especially valuable because they can become more stringent as substitutes improve.

This is one reason the archive keeps upgrading:
- building codes that can be periodically strengthened [S51][S54]
- appliance standards that tighten with efficiency improvements [S16][S17][S106]
- fleet standards and charging systems that can ratchet as EV cost and availability improve [S30][S48]
- methane rules that can move from better reporting to faster response and stricter obligations once detection improves [S11][S66][S67]
- managed-decline schedules that can accelerate as replacement capacity, worker protection, and regional transition plans strengthen [S23][S24][S25]

OECD’s sequencing work supports the general design rule: enabling conditions often need to precede or accompany harder obligations, but that does not imply leaving the obligation static forever [S112].

### 4. Social corrigibility

Ideal solutions should be adjustable without feeling like bait-and-switch politics.

That usually means:
- visible review points,
- clear public metrics,
- targeted protection for households and workers when policies tighten,
- practical substitutes in place before rules harden,
- explanation of why the ratchet is occurring and what evidence triggered it [S59][S69][S71][S112][S113]

The archive therefore prefers **legible ratchets** over technocratic surprise.

### 5. Strategic corrigibility

A good climate portfolio should be able to shift emphasis when facts change without losing the overall mission.

Examples:
- if transmission buildout lags, more near-term effort may need to shift toward efficiency, flexible demand, distributed storage, and transformer production rather than pretending the queue does not exist [S56][S103][S111]
- if heat risk rises faster than expected, cooling access, passive design, and labour-heat protection may need to move up the stack [S16][S17][S88][S104]
- if finance conditions worsen in EMDE, guarantees, local-currency finance, and concessional risk-sharing deserve more weight rather than leaving bankable projects stranded [S91][S95][S96]

This is not mission drift. It is mission fidelity under changing conditions.

## What this rule changes in research judgment

When two proposals look similarly climate-positive, the archive should usually promote the one that:
- produces faster and cleaner feedback on whether it is working,
- can be tightened without redesigning the whole system,
- creates a stable review-and-revision cycle,
- turns monitoring into enforcement or policy improvement,
- lets governments correct weak performance without destroying public trust,
- preserves strategic direction while allowing instrumental adjustment.

This tends to upgrade:
- transparent standards with scheduled revisions,
- methane measurement tied to response obligations,
- building codes and retrofit programs with real performance feedback,
- climate-data systems linked to budget and planning decisions,
- public ledgers for retirements, storage, exposure, and resilience delivery,
- policies whose next tightening step is already named and conditioned on evidence [S11][S59][S65][S66][S67][S68][S106][S112][S113][S114]

## A simple corrigibility screen

A future proposal should usually be promoted when it can answer most of these questions well.

### 1. Feedback test
What would tell us within a few years whether this is actually working?

### 2. Review test
Who is required to review those results, and on what cadence?

### 3. Trigger test
What evidence would cause the policy to tighten, redesign, or shift emphasis?

### 4. Legibility test
Can affected households, workers, firms, and local governments understand why adjustments are happening?

### 5. Stability test
Can the instrument improve without reopening the entire strategic commitment every time?

### 6. Escalation test
If the measure succeeds, is there a named and plausible next step that deepens the gain?

Measures that pass four or more of these tests deserve extra weight even when a static annual-abatement comparison underrates them.

## What the archive distrusts more strongly after this note

This note makes the archive more skeptical of:
- one-shot climate promises with no review architecture,
- reporting systems that do not feed decisions,
- metrics chosen for presentation rather than correction,
- policies that can only tighten through major political crisis,
- plans whose failure will be discovered too late to matter,
- vague “adaptive management” claims with no triggers, no public metrics, and no accountable reviewer.

The archive does **not** conclude that everything must be endlessly adjustable. Long-term direction should remain firm: rapid emissions decline, managed fossil exit, lower overshoot, high wellbeing, and protection against irreversible harm stay non-negotiable [S100][S101][S109][S110].

But within that direction, ideal solutions should be built to **learn in public, correct in time, and ratchet without rupture**.

## One-paragraph compression

Ideal climate solutions should be designed as **corrigible systems**: measurable enough to reveal performance, institutionalized enough to review it, politically legible enough to adjust it, and structured enough to ratchet ambition as substitutes, capability, and evidence improve. That is why the archive should keep preferring truth infrastructure tied to enforcement, standards with revision cycles, methane observation tied to response, BTR-style transparency tied to planning and finance, and policy architectures whose next tightening step is already visible — over one-shot plans, static promises, and reporting systems that generate paperwork but not correction [S11][S59][S65][S66][S67][S68][S112][S113][S114].
