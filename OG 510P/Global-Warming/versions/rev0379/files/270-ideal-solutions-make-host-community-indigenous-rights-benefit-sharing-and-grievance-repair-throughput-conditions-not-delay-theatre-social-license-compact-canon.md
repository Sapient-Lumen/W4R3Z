# 270 — Ideal Solutions: Make Host-Community, Indigenous Rights, Benefit-Sharing, and Grievance Repair Throughput Conditions, Not Delay Theatre — Social-License Compact Canon

## Claim

Rev0253 has two halves.
File `269` says clean deployment needs governed place capacity.
This file says maps are not enough.

A technically good site can still fail if affected people are treated as obstacles, if Indigenous Peoples are given consultation theatre instead of rights-respecting consent processes, if host communities receive vague promises instead of enforceable benefits, if grievance routes are inaccessible, or if burdens are concentrated while value leaves the place.

Social licence is not a public-relations add-on.
It is a throughput condition.
For many packets it is also a rights condition, an enforcement condition, a finance condition, and a durability condition [S140][S141][S162][S333][S334][S335][S337][S339][S340].

## Fast rule

**Every land-, water-, seabed-, mineral-, waste-, corridor-, or large-facility packet needs a host-community and rights ledger before it is called ready.**

That ledger should name:

1. affected people, workers, landholders, tenants, users, Indigenous Peoples, traditional authorities, local governments, public-service systems, and neighbouring communities;
2. rights that may be affected, including land, territory, resource, cultural, livelihood, water, health, safety, housing, labour, and participation rights;
3. whether free, prior, and informed consent is required, and how that requirement is being met through the affected peoples' own representative institutions;
4. what independent technical, legal, translation, facilitation, travel, childcare, and accessibility support affected people need to participate on fair terms;
5. what benefits are promised, who receives them, who decides, whether they are enforceable, and how they survive ownership change;
6. what harms are prevented, minimised, restored, compensated, or refused;
7. what grievance route exists before, during, and after construction;
8. what remedy, stop-work, redesign, benefit-reset, or cancellation trigger applies when evidence shows consent, rights, or promises have failed.

No rights and host ledger, no mature packet.

## Compact canon

### 1. Opposition is not proof of ignorance

Some opposition is misinformation, status quo defence, incumbent manipulation, or local free-riding.
But some opposition is accurate local knowledge about flooding, fire, roads, soils, birds, fisheries, sacred sites, cumulative burden, safety, traffic, housing pressure, corruption, or broken promises.

A serious climate programme must be able to tell the difference.
Dismissing all local resistance as “NIMBYism” is as crude as giving every local objection a veto over global public goods.

### 2. Consent is not generic engagement

For Indigenous Peoples and other rights-bearing communities, participation cannot be collapsed into a town hall after the deal is structured.
UNDRIP requires consultation and cooperation in good faith through Indigenous Peoples' own representative institutions in order to obtain free, prior, and informed consent before measures or projects that affect their rights, lands, territories, or resources [S333][S334].

“Free” means no coercion, intimidation, manipulation, or retaliation.
“Prior” means before authorisation, financing lock-in, construction, or irreversible site commitment.
“Informed” means evidence is understandable, complete enough, culturally accessible, and given in time for real deliberation.
“Consent” means the process is organised to obtain agreement where the right applies, not merely to record objections.

### 3. The host bargain has to be concrete

Community benefit language is weak unless it says:

- benefit type;
- recipient;
- governance;
- timing;
- enforceability;
- inflation adjustment;
- transferability across ownership changes;
- audit path;
- remedy if not delivered;
- interaction with taxes, rents, rates, utility bills, local jobs, procurement, housing, emergency services, schools, clinics, roads, water, broadband, and environmental monitoring.

Legally binding community benefits agreements are one possible mechanism, but the deeper rule is broader: benefits should be specific enough that people can inspect whether the bargain was kept [S337].

### 4. Benefits cannot buy permission for unacceptable harm

Benefit-sharing does not launder rights violations, ecological damage, unsafe design, corruption, displacement without remedy, unpaid labour risk, sacred-site damage, or repeated burden on already-sacrificed communities.

The order is:

1. respect rights;
2. avoid harm;
3. minimise unavoidable harm;
4. repair and compensate residual harm;
5. share value where the community hosts or enables public benefit;
6. refuse projects that require unacceptable harm to proceed.

### 5. Host communities include the whole chain

The host community is not only the town next to the solar farm or transmission line.
It can also be:

- a mining community upstream of batteries, conductors, solar inputs, or magnets;
- a port community handling heavy equipment or fuels;
- a corridor community under transmission, rail, road, pipeline, or cable routes;
- a waste and recycling community downstream of decommissioned assets;
- a public-housing community hosting retrofits;
- a rural community hosting renewable exports;
- an urban heat-vulnerable community hosting resilience hubs or district systems.

Transition-mineral, renewable-energy, and infrastructure value chains need the same rights, benefit, grievance, and proof logic as final deployment sites [S319][S339][S340].

### 6. Grievance routes are operating infrastructure

A grievance system is not a suggestion box.
It is an operating control that should be accessible before construction, during construction, during operation, during ownership change, and at decommissioning.

It should include:

- multiple entry points;
- language and disability access;
- protection against retaliation;
- independent escalation;
- time limits;
- transparent case tracking without exposing vulnerable complainants;
- repair authority;
- stop-work or pause triggers for serious harms;
- learning loops into procurement, permitting, finance, and standards.

### 7. Benefit-sharing should favour durable public value

The best host bargains are not just one-time cheques.
Depending on context, they can include:

- lower local bills or bill credits;
- community ownership stakes;
- tax-base or royalty mechanisms;
- workforce and apprenticeship seats;
- local procurement;
- community resilience funds;
- road, water, broadband, emergency-service, school, clinic, or housing improvements;
- ecological restoration;
- monitoring data governed with the community;
- decommissioning and restoration guarantees;
- revenue set-asides for future repair.

The test is not whether benefits sound generous in a press release.
The test is whether they reduce real burdens, share value fairly, and remain enforceable when conditions change.

### 8. Good social process is a speed strategy

Rights-respecting process is often portrayed as delay.
But weak process creates hidden delay: lawsuits, moratoria, refusals, distrust, vandalism, political backlash, local capacity overload, lender concern, insurance stress, and later renegotiation.

The fastest durable route is usually early evidence, independent support, explicit trade-offs, fair benefits, accessible grievance, and correction authority before the project becomes an all-or-nothing conflict [S60][S140][S141][S335][S337].

### 9. Red flags

Pause or rewrite a packet when:

- rights holders are not identified;
- Indigenous consent duties are treated as ordinary stakeholder engagement;
- affected people see documents only after finance, permits, or procurement are effectively locked;
- benefits are voluntary, vague, non-transferable, or controlled only by the developer;
- grievance routes cannot stop or repair harm;
- the project relies on security, policing, intimidation, or strategic lawsuits against public participation;
- local government lacks capacity to negotiate;
- communities hosting extraction, waste, or corridors receive fewer protections than communities hosting final clean assets;
- ownership change can dissolve promises;
- promised jobs, tax revenue, bill relief, ecological restoration, or public-service improvements are not auditable.

### 10. Completion condition

A packet has social licence only when the affected people can inspect the bargain, the rights process is fit for the rights at stake, benefits and duties are enforceable, grievance routes can repair harm, and the project can survive ownership change without resetting promises to zero.

## Preferred use

Use this canon when the live question is about:

- local opposition;
- Indigenous rights or FPIC;
- community benefit agreements;
- host-community revenue or burden sharing;
- mineral, transmission, renewable, waste, hydrogen, CCS, port, or industrial siting;
- complaints and grievance design;
- whether “streamlining” is legitimate;
- how to make fast deployment politically durable.

## Compression rule

**Treat host-community rights, Indigenous consent where applicable, benefit-sharing, and grievance repair as throughput infrastructure. A project that has to outrun the people and places it affects is not ready.**

## Rev0254 addendum — host communities include the people left with dirty afterlives

This file says host-community legitimacy is throughput infrastructure. Rev0254 extends that rule to dirty retirement and legacy reuse.

Communities near closed mines, orphan wells, refineries, terminals, ash ponds, tailings, landfills, and contaminated corridors are not former hosts whose claims expired when production stopped. They remain affected communities until hazards are controlled, cleanup is funded, monitoring is visible, benefits and reuse are governed, and grievance routes can repair late harms. A clean project on a legacy site therefore needs both a host-community ledger and a remediation-and-reuse passport [S347][S348][S350].

## Rev0255 addendum — receiving communities are host communities too

The host-community rule now applies to climate mobility. A destination city, town, rural district, Indigenous territory, or neighbourhood is not empty capacity. It is a community with existing residents, service constraints, land claims, affordability pressures, institutions, and rights.

A climate-mobility packet should therefore treat receiving places the way this file treats infrastructure host communities: name affected people, fund shared services, prevent displacement and resentment, make benefits visible, create grievance routes, and keep promises durable across administrations and funding cycles [S351][S352][S356][S357][S358].
