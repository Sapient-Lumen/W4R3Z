# 257 — Ideal Solutions: Make Climate Claims Auditable Before Trust Collapses — Claims-Integrity Compact Canon

## Claim

Rev0246 says climate risk and transition cost have to be put on visible balance sheets, with affordability firebreaks before burden pile-ups break consent.
The next operating layer is claims integrity.

A climate programme will now be surrounded by claims: net-zero claims, transition-plan claims, carbon-credit claims, clean-power claims, low-carbon-material claims, resilience claims, green-finance labels, public-procurement labels, large-load clean-supply claims, removal claims, and recovery claims.

Rev0248 adds the consequence rule: a claim should not only be specific and auditable; it should also say which correction, withdrawal, public restatement, loss of preference, repayment, redress, or penalty follows if the claim proves false, overstated, misused, or no longer supported.

Some claims are useful because they move capital, procurement, regulation, and ordinary decisions toward lower danger.
But weak claims are not harmless decoration.
They can mislead buyers, investors, regulators, households, communities, and public agencies; let dirty defaults keep social license; shift cost into weaker balance sheets; waste scarce capacity; and make honest actors look foolish.

So the doctrine is:

**a climate claim should be allowed to carry trust, subsidy, procurement preference, financing advantage, regulatory credit, or public legitimacy only when it is specific, bounded, comparable, auditable, corrected when wrong, and linked to a physical outcome rather than a mood.**

## Fast rule

**Do not let a climate claim outrank the proof behind it. Name the claim type, boundary, baseline, metric, data source, data-spine link, proof carrier, verification route, uncertainty, decision use, gross-cut path, residual / credit role, correction consequence, remedy owner, and forum before the claim is used to sell, finance, procure, permit, offset, regulate, or reassure.**

## Why this note is not redundant

- `133` gives the joined truth / measurement / enforcement layer.
- `189` says real-world physical outcomes outrank paper claims.
- `196` says paper claims do not substitute for measured outcomes.
- `244` turns work into owner-led delivery packets.
- `255` and `256` say risk, cost, and affordability have to be visible.

This note adds the claims-control rule:

**the claim itself is now a control surface.**

Rev0249 adds the record corollary: high-decision-weight claims should not float separately from the data spine or proof passport that carries their boundary, method, evidence, update cadence, expiry, correction trigger, and remedy route [S293][S294][S295][S296][S297][S298][S299][S300].

If it changes what investors buy, what public agencies procure, what users believe, what regulators permit, what polluters can advertise, or what communities are asked to accept, then it needs a claims-integrity packet.

## The compact canon

### 1. First classify the claim

Different claims fail in different ways.
The archive should not treat them as one generic “green” category.

At minimum, classify whether the claim is about:
- gross emissions reduction
- avoided emissions
- methane or other fast-warming-gas reduction
- carbon removal
- storage durability
- carbon-credit use
- net zero or carbon neutrality
- transition-plan credibility
- low-carbon product, material, fuel, electricity, or service
- resilience, protection, recovery, or adaptation outcome
- green finance, sustainability-linked finance, or fund label
- public procurement or public-spending alignment
- biodiversity, forest, water, circularity, or community benefit

House rule: **a claim is not credible until its species is known.**

### 2. Net-zero and transition claims need implementation, not only ambition

The UN High-Level Expert Group’s integrity work drew a red line around greenwashing and called for robust, accountable transition plans [S277].
IFRS’s 2025 transition-plan guidance says transition-plan disclosures are fragmented and costly, and aims to help preparers disclose relevant information about strategy and goals under IFRS S2 [S276].
SBTi’s corporate net-zero standard revision likewise reflects the continued need for a science-based, consistent understanding of corporate net zero [S279].

The archive’s rule is stricter than branding:
- show near-term gross reductions
- show the capital, procurement, product, asset, and operating changes that produce them
- show fossil expansion, fossil dependence, or dirty-default retirement assumptions
- show Scope 3 boundaries where relevant
- show what residual emissions remain and why they are hard residuals
- show what removals or credits do and do not claim
- show governance, finance, accountability, and update clocks

House rule: **a net-zero claim without a live implementation path is a future-tense reputation asset, not climate proof.**

### 3. Consumer and product claims should be clear enough to act on

OECD’s 2025 consumer-policy work says concerns about greenwashing — false or misleading environmental claims — have increased as environmental claims on products and services have grown, and that misleading claims can harm consumers and the green transition [S275].

The archive’s practical rule is that ordinary users should not need forensic expertise to understand the claim.
A consumer-facing or product-facing claim should state:
- what is being compared
- compared with what baseline
- over what life-cycle boundary
- over what time period
- using what evidence
- whether the claim is about the product, the company, the use phase, the packaging, the electricity, the offset, or the whole service
- whether material trade-offs are excluded

House rule: **“green,” “clean,” “sustainable,” “climate friendly,” or “carbon neutral” is too vague unless the sentence says exactly what changed.**

### 4. Carbon-credit integrity has two sides

ICVCM’s Core Carbon Principles are intended to identify high-quality carbon credits that create real, verifiable climate impact, and its Assessment Framework assesses programmes and categories of credits against detailed criteria [S280].
VCMI’s 2025 Claims Code addresses the demand side by guiding companies and other non-state actors on credible voluntary use of carbon credits and how they communicate that use [S281].

The archive’s rule is therefore two-sided:
- supply-side credit quality matters: additionality, robust quantification, permanence or reversal handling, leakage, safeguards, governance, tracking, validation, verification, and transparent methodology
- demand-side claim quality matters: the buyer’s gross-reduction path, what the credit is being used for, whether it is above and beyond required cuts, what claim wording is allowed, and whether retired credits are traceable

House rule: **a high-quality credit can still be used in a weak claim, and a strong claim cannot rescue a low-quality credit.**

### 5. Credits, certificates, and book claims do not erase physical hierarchy

The archive’s hierarchy remains:
1. cut gross emissions and dirty throughput first
2. cut fast-warming gases quickly
3. protect people and systems directly
4. reserve scarce residual tools for true hard cases
5. use credits, certificates, and removals with boundaries, not as a license to continue easy pollution

A carbon credit, renewable-energy certificate, book-and-claim fuel attribute, avoided-emissions estimate, or portfolio label may have a role.
But it should not be allowed to obscure whether the buyer’s actual assets, products, procurement, operations, or supply chain are changing.

House rule: **counterbalancing claims belong below direct physical change unless the claim explicitly proves why direct change is unavailable and what residual role remains.**

### 6. Green finance labels should show the theory of change

A fund, bond, loan, guarantee, or sustainability-linked instrument can be useful.
But a financial label is not a climate outcome.

The claim should say whether the instrument:
- finances a specific physical asset or activity
- lowers cost of capital for a needed transition packet
- supports a credible transition plan
- pays for adaptation, resilience, recovery, or exposure reduction
- changes an issuer’s behaviour through covenants, pricing, stewardship, or exit rules
- merely classifies an existing exposure as aligned

House rule: **do not confuse a labelled portfolio with a changed physical system.**

### 7. Resilience and adaptation claims require exposed people to be safer

A resilience claim should not be accepted because a plan says “resilience” or a project survived normal commissioning.
It should say which hazard, population, service, asset, threshold, and bad-day scenario changed.

For adaptation and protection claims, the proof should include at least one lived outcome:
- fewer people exposed to dangerous heat, flood, fire, smoke, drought, outage, or water stress
- shorter service interruption
- safer indoor temperatures
- faster recovery
- lower uninsured loss
- lower repeat loss
- more reliable critical service during compound shocks

House rule: **resilience claims are real only when vulnerability or service failure is measurably lower.**

### 8. Claim use determines claim burden

The same weak sentence should not be allowed to do heavy work.
A casual internal note, a consumer advertisement, a public tender preference, a securities disclosure, a credit claim, a ratepayer-funded programme, and a legal compliance claim should not face identical proof burdens.

The heavier the decision weight, the stronger the claim packet should be.
Use heavier proof when the claim affects:
- public subsidy
- procurement preference
- consumer price premium
- investor allocation
- regulatory compliance
- permit or queue priority
- carbon-credit retirement
- public backstop or guarantee
- household affordability promise
- community consent

House rule: **claims that move money, permissions, or trust need audit-grade support.**

### 9. Correction is part of the claim

A climate claim should state what happens if it is wrong, stale, overstated, underdelivered, or used outside its boundary.
Possible consequences include:
- corrected public statement
- relabelled product or fund
- clawback of subsidy or performance payment
- loss of procurement eligibility
- buyer or household remedy
- stronger verification next cycle
- penalty for repeated misstatement
- demotion in the work queue
- quarantine from the archive’s promoted solution set

House rule: **a claim with no correction consequence is a press release wearing a measurement costume.**

### 10. Integrity should not become greenhushing or incumbent protection

The answer to weak claims is not silence.
If only large incumbents can afford claims compliance, claims integrity can become an anti-competition device.
If honest actors are too afraid to say anything, useful learning disappears.

So the archive prefers:
- standard claim templates
- safe-harbour wording for modest, accurate claims
- public methods and default factors where direct measurement is disproportionate
- risk-based verification
- low-cost SME routes
- public open data for common baselines
- stronger burdens for large, high-value, high-harm, or residual claims

House rule: **claims integrity should make honest claims easier and dishonest claims harder.**

## Minimum claims-integrity packet

A formed claims-integrity packet should include:
- the exact claim sentence
- claim type
- owner of the claim
- intended audience
- decision use: marketing, procurement, finance, regulation, public consent, or internal management
- physical boundary: asset, product, service, portfolio, jurisdiction, supply chain, or organisation
- emissions / exposure / service boundary
- baseline and counterfactual
- time period and update clock
- metric and unit
- data source and method
- uncertainty and exclusions
- independent verification or audit route where needed
- gross-reduction path if the claim involves net zero, neutrality, credits, or residuals
- role of credits, certificates, offsets, avoided emissions, or removals, if any
- link to public ledger or disclosure
- correction trigger
- remedy, penalty, clawback, or demotion if the claim fails

## What this note demotes

This note demotes ten mistakes:
- vague green adjectives with no physical boundary
- net-zero claims with no near-term gross-cut path
- transition plans that disclose ambition but not implementation
- carbon-credit claims that ignore either credit quality or buyer conduct
- renewable-energy or clean-power claims that ignore location, time, deliverability, or additionality when those features matter
- resilience claims that do not show exposed people safer or services more reliable
- green-finance labels that do not explain how capital changes physical outcomes
- procurement claims with no comparable product definitions or audit trail
- claims that count removals, credits, and reductions as interchangeable
- claims that cannot be corrected, penalized, or withdrawn when evidence fails

### 13. Give claims a record path and an expiry path
A claim that remains in circulation after its evidence is stale becomes a trust hazard. A serious claims packet should therefore state which record stores the claim, which evidence updates it, when it expires, which passport or ledger carries it across handoff, who can challenge it, and what public correction follows when the record changes.

House rule: **a climate claim should be easier to update or withdraw than to leave misleading in the market.**

## Preferred use

Open this note first when the live question is any of the following:
- is this climate claim credible?
- how should a net-zero, transition-plan, carbon-neutral, clean-power, low-carbon-product, or resilience claim be judged?
- what should a public agency require before giving a procurement preference, subsidy, permit priority, or guarantee to a claimed climate solution?
- how should carbon credits be used or communicated without becoming offset theatre?
- how to prevent greenwashing without making accurate climate communication impossible?

Then reopen:
- `133` for the broader truth / measurement / enforcement layer
- `189` for physical-first outcomes over paper claims
- `244` for owner-led delivery packets
- `255` for balance-sheet visibility
- `256` for affordability claims and burden firebreaks
- `258` when the surviving problem is capture, fraud, collusion, or rent extraction rather than claim wording alone

## Compression rule

**A climate claim should be allowed to move trust, money, permissions, or priority only when it is specific, bounded, comparable, auditable, physically linked, and correctable. The stronger the decision it influences, the stronger the proof burden should be.**
