# 348 — Ideal Solutions: Make property records, tenure, heirs property, and occupancy proof a recovery service floor

## Claim

Recovery money often fails at the doorway. The doorway is not only a website or an application deadline; it is the ability to prove that a person lived somewhere, owned something, rented something, inherited something, had a right to return, or lost a record when the disaster destroyed the ordinary proof.

FEMA Individual Assistance requires ownership or occupancy verification for some housing assistance, and FEMA describes acceptable documentation paths for survivors [S619]. HUD's CDBG-DR FAQ is more flexible: HUD says CDBG-DR itself has no blanket proof-of-ownership requirement, and if grantees require proof they should consider multiple methods beyond deeds or title [S620]. UN-Habitat's land-and-disasters guidance makes the deeper point: land and property rights become recovery infrastructure after a disaster [S621].

House rule: **no housing recovery program is ready until it can serve a survivor who lost documents, lacks formal title, rents informally, lives on heirs property, uses paper not portals, or needs legal help before the deadline.**

## Fast rule

Treat property proof as a service floor: a household should be able to prove occupancy, ownership, tenancy, use, loss, and eligibility through several paths, with a denial reason, appeal, language access, and privacy boundary.

## The compact canon

### 1. Title is not the same as home

Deeds matter, but disaster recovery must also recognize occupancy, tenancy, family land, mobile homes, informal arrangements, life estates, tax payments, utility bills, repair receipts, affidavits, community attestation, tribal records, and other proof. The alternative is predictable: recovery selects for clean paperwork, not actual loss.

### 2. Proof substitution must be designed before the event

An improvised exception desk after the flood or fire becomes inconsistent, politicized, and slow. The archive should expect a preapproved proof-substitution menu, translated forms, offline intake, legal-aid referral, fraud controls, and public criteria for when temporary eligibility can be granted pending verification.

### 3. Heirs property and informal tenure are climate vulnerability

A household with insecure or tangled title can be hit twice: first by the hazard, then by the aid system. Inherited land, customary rights, informal settlement, shared family property, and missing probate records need specific recovery paths. Otherwise climate shocks become quiet land-loss engines.

### 4. Renter and occupancy proof need their own lane

Landlord records, leases, mail, school records, benefits records, employer records, utility payments, neighbor attestation, shelter records, and field verification may all matter. Renters should not be made invisible because repair programs are owner-centered.

### 5. Denials are data

Every denial for proof failure should produce a code: no deed, lost records, informal tenancy, heirs property, portal failure, language, disability, landlord refusal, suspected fraud, duplicate benefits, or missed deadline. If one code clusters by race, age, disability, tenure, language, rurality, or income, the program is failing.

## Minimum packet

A property-proof packet includes: document-substitution rules; offline and assisted intake; land-record backup and restoration; legal-aid referral; heirs-property pathway; renter pathway; fraud-control and privacy rule; denial-code ledger; appeal route; deadline waiver rule for records loss; and public audit of who was excluded.

## Bottom line

Recovery that cannot recognize a home without a perfect title will reproduce historical exclusion. The cube should treat property proof as a service floor because service floors depend on people being allowed back into the records that define their losses, rights, and aid.
