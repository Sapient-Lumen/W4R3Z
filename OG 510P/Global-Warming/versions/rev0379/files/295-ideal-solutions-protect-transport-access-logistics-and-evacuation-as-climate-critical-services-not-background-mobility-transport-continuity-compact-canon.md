# 295 — Ideal Solutions: Protect Transport Access, Logistics, and Evacuation as Climate-Critical Services, Not Background Mobility — Transport Continuity Compact Canon

## Claim

Files `293` and `294` made the **message-to-action spine** visible: warnings, connectivity, public information, trusted communication, feedback, accessibility, language access, telecom fallback, and rumor correction.
This file adds the next physical layer: **transport access, evacuation, logistics, freight, public transit, paratransit, fuel, charging, crews, depots, ports, and last-mile delivery are climate-critical services, not background mobility.**

A warning is incomplete if it tells people to evacuate but the bus plan, paratransit roster, bridge status, road clearance, fuel allocation, accessible pickup, pet / medicine / device transport, shelter intake, and return route are improvised under floodwater, wildfire, smoke, or grid outage.

A food-security plan is incomplete if trucks cannot reach warehouses, ports, cold chains, markets, school kitchens, shelters, and last-mile distribution points.

A health-continuity plan is incomplete if staff, patients, vaccines, oxygen, dialysis consumables, ambulances, mobile clinics, blood, generators, medicines, fuel, and maintenance crews cannot move.

A housing / legal / finance recovery plan is incomplete if contractors, inspectors, records, cash-out points, mobile teams, debris haulers, building materials, public defenders, paralegals, and benefit navigators cannot reach the people and places where the loss occurred.

The World Bank's 2025 Disaster and Climate-Resilient Transport Guidance Note says climate change and extreme weather damage critical infrastructure, isolate communities, disrupt supply chains, and require resilience across planning, financing, design, operations, maintenance, contingency planning, and institutions [S487]. ITF's transport-resilience work treats transport system resilience as the capacity to deal with, adapt to, and recover from disruptions, including climate, geopolitics, and energy security [S488]. World Bank's _Lifelines_ frames infrastructure resilience as the ability of systems to function and meet users' needs during and after natural shocks [S490].

The archive's conclusion is operational: **a serious climate packet needs a transport-access and logistics-continuity ledger.**

## Fast rule

**Any climate packet that depends on people, goods, crews, records, cash, medicines, fuel, water, food, equipment, students, patients, workers, inspectors, or responders moving before, during, or after a hazard should prove that the needed movement can occur under degraded conditions, with an accessible no-car floor, a logistics floor, a fuel / charging floor, and a public correction loop.**

That proof should name:

1. **the access function** — evacuate, shelter-in-place resupply, reach cooling / clean-air / water / food / care sites, staff critical services, move patients, move children, move workers, repair utilities, deliver cash / legal / documents, clear debris, restock markets, restore schools, or bring materials for safer rebuilding;
2. **the movement groups** — people without cars, older adults, disabled people, medically dependent people, children, caregivers, migrants, homeless people, rural households, informal settlement residents, workers, responders, utility crews, health workers, teachers, transport workers, freight drivers, port workers, informal operators, and host communities;
3. **the mode stack** — walking / rolling, bicycles / cargo bikes, buses, coaches, school buses, paratransit, demand-responsive transport, taxis, community vans, rail, ferries, boats, ambulances, freight trucks, rail freight, ports, airports, drones where appropriate, and door-to-door outreach;
4. **the network-criticality map** — bridges, tunnels, culverts, arterial roads, evacuation corridors, rural links, port gates, railheads, depots, fuel terminals, charging hubs, warehouses, cold-chain nodes, medical-supply routes, bus garages, maintenance yards, and single points of failure;
5. **the degradation scenario** — heat, flood, wildfire, smoke, landslide, snow / ice, storm surge, bridge scour, road washout, power outage, fuel shortage, charger outage, cyberattack, telecom outage, port closure, staff shortage, school closure, curfew, conflict, or price spike;
6. **the access floor** — free or affordable rides when needed, accessible vehicles, pickup support, transport for assistive devices, oxygen, medicines, pets, documents, caregivers, children, and people who cannot stand in queues or walk far;
7. **the logistics floor** — warehouses, staging areas, route clearance, driver / crew safety, fuel / charging priority, customs / permits / passes, cold chain, last-mile distribution, mutual aid, and supplier fallback;
8. **the information floor** — route status, transit status, pickup status, bridge and road closures, shelter capacity, service hours, freight windows, fuel / charging availability, and two-way reports from communities and operators;
9. **the governance floor** — who owns transport continuity, who pre-authorizes emergency service changes, who allocates scarce capacity, who protects workers, who pays, who audits equity, and who fixes the missed-access cases;
10. **the correction floor** — every missed evacuation, inaccessible pickup, closed route, failed delivery, fuel / charging shortage, unaffordable fare, driver hazard, port / warehouse choke point, and return failure becomes a public after-action correction item.

## Why this belongs in the climate archive

Climate policy often treats transport as a mitigation sector: cars, buses, trucks, aviation, shipping, fuels, batteries, public transport, compact cities, and logistics efficiency.
That is necessary but not sufficient.
A hotter and more disrupted world also turns transport into a protection sector.

Transport failure converts many other failures into harm:

- warnings become blame if people cannot leave, reach help, or receive supplies;
- heat and smoke become health emergencies if people cannot reach cooling, clean air, clinics, pharmacies, or safer indoor spaces;
- floods become isolation if roads, culverts, bridges, ports, ferries, and rural access routes fail;
- food systems fail when freight, warehouses, fuel, cold chains, markets, and last-mile delivery fail;
- water and sanitation fail when tanker routes, treatment chemicals, repair crews, backup generators, and waste-hauling routes fail;
- schools and childcare fail when safe routes, buses, staff transport, meals, and temporary learning sites fail;
- care continuity fails when home-care workers, paratransit, device replacement, oxygen, dialysis, medicines, and caregivers cannot move;
- labour protections fail if workers cannot reach safer jobs, paid cooling sites, clinics, or wage-replacement offices;
- legal and financial rights fail if claims help, cash-out points, documentation offices, courts, regulators, and community navigators cannot reach displaced or isolated people.

The transport test is therefore not only "is the vehicle low-carbon?"
It is also: **does the system preserve essential access while it decarbonizes?**

UN's sustainable-transport report emphasizes that transport connects people and communities to jobs, schools, health care, goods, and services, and is central to leaving no one behind [S501]. SLOCAT's global transport-status work similarly frames transport and mobility as climate, sustainability, and policy systems, not merely vehicle markets [S500]. The World Bank / transport policy guide for climate-resilient transport emphasizes safe and reliable networks for future generations, particularly in low-income contexts [S492].

## The compact canon

### 1. Count protected access, not route miles or vehicles

A road, bus fleet, rail line, port, charger, or bridge is not resilient because it exists.
It is resilient if essential access is preserved when hazards degrade it.

House rule: **measure whether people, crews, and goods reached the protected service on time.**

### 2. Put no-car households at the centre of evacuation planning

Evacuation plans that assume private vehicles convert car ownership into a survival credential.
Public transit, school buses, coaches, paratransit, taxis, community vans, walking / rolling routes, pickup points, and assisted transport should be planned before the alert.

House rule: **no evacuation order should be issued without a no-car pathway.**

### 3. Treat accessible transport as core capacity, not special accommodation

Accessible evacuation and continuity means vehicles, operators, pickup procedures, registries with consent and privacy, boarding assistance, companion / caregiver transport, devices, oxygen, medicines, animals, communication, and return trips.
The Partnership for Inclusive Disaster Strategies' 2025 transit guidance says evacuation transportation planning details for disabled people are often the weakest link and commonly missing from plans, annexes, procedures, policies, and training [S494].

House rule: **if the access plan does not work for disabled people and medically dependent people, it is not a public access plan.**

### 4. Keep public transit alive as emergency infrastructure

Transit is often treated as optional during disaster: shut it down, protect assets, wait for recovery.
That can be wrong.
Buses, rail, ferries, paratransit, and stations can move evacuees, staff shelters, carry essential workers, distribute information, serve cooling / clean-air trips, and support return.

House rule: **transport agencies should have emergency service patterns, not only asset-protection plans.**

### 5. Maintain the roads that hold the system together

The most important road segment during a shock may be a small bridge, culvert, low-water crossing, rural link, port gate, warehouse approach road, bus depot access road, dialysis route, or route to a water-treatment plant.
PIARC's 2023 adaptation framework is explicit that climate risk affects road and transport design, construction, operations, maintenance, and use [S489].

House rule: **rank road resilience by service criticality and detour poverty, not by traffic volume alone.**

### 6. Design logistics as a protected public function

Food, water, medicines, medical oxygen, vaccines, fuel, generators, repair parts, shelter supplies, sanitation supplies, cash, documents, and building materials need staging, storage, route clearance, loading, drivers, cold chain, permits, and local distribution.
The WFP-led Logistics Cluster exists to provide logistics coordination, operational information, and access to common logistics services in emergencies [S495].

House rule: **a service floor is only real if its supply chain has a last-mile plan.**

### 7. Keep transport workers safe and paid

Drivers, dispatchers, rail workers, ferry operators, port workers, warehouse staff, mechanics, road crews, traffic controllers, towing crews, debris haulers, delivery workers, and informal operators become protection workers during climate shocks.
They need heat, smoke, flood, violence, fatigue, PPE, rest, wage, insurance, and family-care protections.

House rule: **do not build public safety on unsafe transport labour.**

### 8. Pre-arrange fuel and charging for essential mobility

Transport continuity depends on energy continuity.
Diesel, gasoline, electricity, hydrogen, propane, batteries, generators, grid connections, microgrids, and chargers all need priority rules.
The U.S. Department of Energy's emergency-preparedness guidance tells local leaders to ensure emergency fuel supplies, maintain multiple storage locations, keep fuels fresh through turnover, arrange supplier relationships, and develop priority end-user allocation procedures [S499].
IEA's 2026 EV outlook says public charging infrastructure reached more than 7 million points globally at the end of 2025, while charging access varies sharply by housing and location [S498].

House rule: **decarbonized mobility must be outage-aware, and emergency mobility must be fuel- and charge-aware.**

### 9. Keep ports, railheads, ferries, and freight corridors in the adaptation map

Climate shocks do not respect local-service boundaries.
A port closure, rail washout, bridge failure, canal disruption, ferry outage, warehouse flood, or customs bottleneck can become a food, medicine, repair, and price shock far away.
UNCTAD notes that more than 80% of world merchandise trade is carried by sea and that ports are key nodes in supply chains, trade, and development [S496].

House rule: **critical transport nodes are climate-risk nodes.**

### 10. Make route status public and machine-usable

People, drivers, responders, schools, shelters, utilities, and logistics operators need consistent route, transit, fuel, charging, bridge, port, ferry, and shelter-status information.
That information should be versioned, interoperable, and backed by local verification.

House rule: **do not make people discover closures by driving into them.**

### 11. Use demand management before congestion becomes danger

Evacuations and resupply can fail through overload even if all assets are intact.
Staged evacuation, contraflow where appropriate, freight windows, emergency-lane rules, transit priority, pickup scheduling, free fares, destination balancing, and shelter-capacity routing should be set before the queue forms.

House rule: **manage demand as well as supply.**

### 12. Protect informal and community transport

In many places, minibuses, motorcycle taxis, shared taxis, boats, carts, community vans, volunteer drivers, and informal freight do much of the real access work.
Ignoring them creates blind spots; over-controlling them can destroy trusted capacity.

House rule: **map, support, and regulate community transport for safety without pretending it does not exist.**

### 13. Design for rural, remote, island, mountain, and settlement-edge isolation

Single-road communities, islands, mountain valleys, informal settlements, peri-urban fringes, and floodplain / coastal neighborhoods can lose access early and regain it late.
They need pre-positioned supplies, shelter-in-place logistics, boat / air / high-clearance contingencies, redundant communications, and local repair capacity.

House rule: **where evacuation is fragile, shelter-in-place resupply becomes a transport duty.**

### 14. Use transport recovery to reduce repeated loss

Restoring roads, bridges, culverts, depots, ports, rail, ferries, and transit exactly as they were can rebuild the next isolation.
UNDRR's transport-sector recovery guidance treats recovery as an opportunity to restore networks and invest in preparedness and physical asset resilience [S491].

House rule: **transport recovery should shorten the next outage, not recreate it.**

### 15. Link low-carbon transport to continuity and access

Electrified buses, e-bikes, cargo bikes, EV fleets, rail, compact development, and cleaner freight can reduce emissions and oil dependence.
But they must also be financed, maintained, charged, dispatched, and made available to people who need them most.

House rule: **clean mobility is a public-protection asset only when it is reliable, affordable, accessible, and outage-resilient.**

### 16. Audit every missed movement

After action, the transport system should publish: who could not leave, who could not return, who missed care, what routes failed, which communities were isolated, which deliveries failed, which workers were endangered, which pickups were inaccessible, which information was wrong, and what will change.

House rule: **transport continuity earns trust by fixing the journeys it failed.**

## Minimum transport-continuity packet

A formed packet should include:

- essential-access map: services, people, goods, crews, records, and time windows that must move;
- no-car and accessible evacuation plan, including pickup, paratransit, school buses / coaches, community vans, companions, assistive devices, medicines, oxygen, pets, and return trips;
- route-criticality and detour-poverty map for roads, bridges, culverts, tunnels, ferries, rail, ports, depots, warehouses, medical routes, water / power repair routes, and rural links;
- emergency transit service plan: modified routes, free-fare trigger, staff transport, shelter / cooling / clinic / school links, communication, and safe shutdown / restart rules;
- logistics and last-mile plan: staging areas, warehouses, cold chain, route clearance, permits, driver safety, fleet access, local distribution, and common-services coordination;
- fuel / charging continuity plan: priority users, storage, turnover, supplier contracts, generator / microgrid / battery support, public charger status, depot charging, and allocation rules;
- transport-worker safety and wage floor;
- public route-status and transport-status information feed linked to warning and public-information cells;
- affordability guardrails: emergency free fares, anti-price-gouging, cash / voucher support, and no exclusion for lack of documents;
- rural / remote / island / settlement-edge isolation plan with shelter-in-place resupply and alternative access;
- recovery standard that upgrades critical weak points instead of simply restoring old fragility;
- after-action dashboard for missed access, inequity, route failure, worker harm, supply-chain failure, and correction.

## What this rules out

This file rejects:

- evacuation plans that assume private cars and call the result public safety;
- transport resilience based on asset protection without service-access proof;
- road rankings based only on average daily traffic, not critical service routes and detour poverty;
- public-transit shutdown plans that ignore emergency access, staff movement, and no-car households;
- accessible evacuation treated as an annex rather than a condition of plan validity;
- logistics plans that stop at warehouses and ignore last-mile distribution, cold chain, drivers, fuel, permits, and local receiving capacity;
- decarbonized mobility plans that ignore outage, charging, affordability, accessibility, and emergency priority;
- ports, ferries, railheads, depots, warehouses, and rural links left outside climate-risk governance;
- after-action reports that count roads reopened but not people, goods, workers, and services reconnected.

## Compression rule

**A transport-continuity compact protects the physical ability to act: it pre-plans no-car and accessible evacuation, keeps emergency transit and logistics alive, ranks roads and nodes by critical service access, protects transport workers, pre-arranges fuel and charging, publishes route and service status, supports rural and last-mile access, and turns every missed movement, isolated community, failed delivery, inaccessible pickup, unsafe crew task, or broken return trip into a visible correction before the next climate shock.**
