# 172 — Ideal Solutions: Dependency-Light and Few-Missing-Prerequisites Compact Canon

## Claim

The archive already has strong notes for:
- dependency-light systems as a deeper design rule (`51`)
- robustness / no-regrets (`166`)
- turnover-aware timing (`169`)
- copyability / fast replication (`168`)
- delivery bottlenecks and bottleneck release (`132`)
- and the physical portfolio / objective-function notes (`130`, `157`)

What it still lacked was one joined first-stop note for a front-door question that keeps deciding whether a technically serious climate package can move before a whole new ecosystem matures:

**what should ideal climate solutions look like when many missing complements would otherwise all need to arrive together — new fuels, new pipelines, new standards, new offtakers, new storage, new contracts, new user conversion, and new public confidence — before the pathway starts working at real scale?**

This note is not a new branch.
It is a compression note.
Its job is to gather the archive’s dependency-light rule into one place so the programme does not keep choosing elegant climate pathways that quietly depend on too many simultaneous successes before they become real.

## Fast rule

**Among physically serious options, prefer climate measures that solve large shares of the problem with few missing prerequisites, clear near-term operating logic, and low synchronisation burden — using what is already ready or near-ready where possible — while reserving longer, dependency-heavier chains for genuinely hard or no-substitute uses** [S112][S165][S166][S167].

## The compact canon

### 1. Missing prerequisites are a real form of climate delay
A pathway can be physically plausible and still be weak if it only works once many other things arrive first.
The OECD’s 2025 sequencing work is unusually explicit on this point: climate-policy packages often depend on enabling conditions, and governments may need to develop clear infrastructure plans and implementation measures before broader policy packages can work as intended [S112].

House rule: **count missing complements as real delay risk, not as harmless detail outside the main design.**

### 2. Fewer missing complements usually means earlier real movement
Dependency-light options often move sooner because they ask less of coordination, finance, standards, and public belief all at once.
The IEA’s energy-efficiency synthesis still says efficiency is the “first fuel,” provides some of the quickest and most cost-effective mitigation options, lowers bills, and strengthens energy security [S166].
That is the pattern the archive wants to preserve: when direct, mature, demand-cutting or direct-electrification options exist, they often deserve earlier weight than more elaborate chains whose infrastructure and market ecology remain incomplete [S165][S166].

House rule: **where serious direct options exist, do not make them wait behind a longer chain unless the longer chain has a clear hard-case reason to dominate.**

### 3. Ambiguous future-system stories can slow cleaner present action
Dependency-heaviness is not only about hardware.
It is also about mixed signals.
The IEA’s Germany 2025 review says electricity will be the most efficient and scalable option for decarbonising heating in decentralised systems and warns that technology-neutral signalling around hydrogen in buildings can delay the investment case for switching to heat pumps today [S165].
That is a general archive lesson.
If people must guess which future ecosystem will actually materialise, many will defer.

House rule: **prefer pathways whose primary direction is clear enough that households, firms, and local institutions can act now without betting on a whole speculative later ecosystem.**

### 4. Dependency-heavy lanes deserve a higher burden of proof
Some climate lanes really do require new fuels, new infrastructure, new storage, or new coordination across borders and industries.
But that is exactly why they should face a stricter ranking test in easier sectors.
The IEA’s 2025 hydrogen infrastructure review shows how fragile longer chains can remain in practice: around 37 000 km of hydrogen pipelines have been announced to 2035, but less than 6% have reached final investment decision, and only 5% of announced underground hydrogen storage has reached FID or is under construction [S167].

House rule: **reserve dependency-heavier chains for genuinely hard or residual uses, and demand stronger proof before letting them displace mature direct paths in easier uses.**

### 5. This rule is about synchronisation burden, not techno-hostility
The archive does not say “only use whatever is easiest today.”
It says a pathway weakens as its synchronisation burden rises.
A measure that needs a new fuel, new storage, new transport, new standards, new contracts, and new user conversion at roughly the same time is riskier than one that works with existing electricity, proven equipment, standard finance, and known operating routines [S112][S165][S166][S167].
Hard lanes still belong where they solve hard problems.
They should not become excuses to postpone easier gains.

House rule: **build the hard chains where no serious simpler substitute exists, but do not let them delay easier lanes that can already move.**

### 6. Dependency-light design improves portfolio resilience and global travelability
A dependency-light measure usually travels better across countries, regions, and institutions because it asks less of capital depth, administrative coordination, market sophistication, and ecosystem maturity.
That makes it easier to copy, easier to finance, and less likely to stall if one complement arrives late.
The OECD’s sequencing paper and the IEA’s current efficiency and infrastructure analyses all point in the same direction: climate packages work better when the supporting conditions are deliberately prepared, and near-term gains are stronger when the pathway does not rely on too many missing pieces arriving together [S112][S166][S167].

House rule: **prefer climate forms that still produce worthwhile gains even if one or two complements lag.**

## The archive’s dependency-light screen

A future proposal should usually score higher when it passes most of these tests:

1. **Few-missing-prerequisites test** — the proposal works with a short list of complements that already exist, are near-ready, or are clearly financed, rather than with a long speculative chain [S112][S165][S167].  
2. **Clear-primary-path test** — ordinary actors can tell what the main route is, instead of being asked to wait for ambiguous future-system resolution [S165].  
3. **Low-synchronisation-burden test** — the proposal does not require many infrastructures, contracts, standards, and user shifts to arrive at roughly the same time before it begins helping [S112][S167].  
4. **Mature-direct-option test** — where a mature direct option exists, the proposal explains clearly why a more dependency-heavy path should still dominate [S165][S166].  
5. **Hard-case-reservation test** — scarce clean fuels and other long-chain infrastructures are being reserved for genuinely hard or residual uses rather than spread casually into easier ones [S165][S167].  
6. **Fail-soft test** — if one key complement is late, the proposal still yields meaningful progress rather than largely stalling [S112][S166][S167].  

## Preferred use

Open this note first when the live question is any of the following:
- what ideal solutions should look like when many missing complements would all need to mature together
- how to rank direct electrification, efficiency, and other near-ready paths against longer-chain fuel or infrastructure stories
- why mixed signalling about future systems can delay viable cleaner action now
- whether a climate package is weak because it quietly depends on too many simultaneous successes
- how to think about few-missing-prerequisites and low synchronisation burden without reopening the deeper dependency-light doctrine

Then reopen the deeper note that matches the surviving disagreement.

## Compression rule

**Ideal climate solutions should usually be dependency-light. They should solve as much as possible with few missing prerequisites, low synchronisation burden, and clear near-term operating logic, using what is already ready or near-ready where that is physically serious. Longer, dependency-heavier chains still belong for genuinely hard cases, but they should carry a higher burden of proof and should not delay easier, more direct pathways that can move now.**
