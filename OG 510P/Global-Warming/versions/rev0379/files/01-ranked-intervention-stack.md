# 01 — Ranked Intervention Stack

This ranking is about **global leverage over the next decade**, not moral purity or rhetorical popularity.

It should now be read together with `27-ideal-solution-architecture.md`, `29-ideal-solutions-allocation-under-scarcity.md`, `41-ideal-solutions-as-short-latency-systems-fast-physical-effects-and-early-benefit-capture.md`, and `57-ideal-solutions-reference-portfolio-2026-2035.md`: the archive does not rank interventions only by tonnes, but also by whether they reduce overshoot, reduce later dependence on removals, improve human welfare, can actually be delivered at speed, how quickly their benefits begin arriving after deployment, and whether they deserve scarce capital and administrative capacity ahead of other valid options.

## Tier 1 — Must dominate the decade

### 1. Clean electricity, grids, storage, and flexibility
This is the highest-leverage lane because electricity is becoming the organizing energy carrier for transport, buildings, and parts of industry. The bottleneck is increasingly grids, interconnection, permitting, queue management, and balancing rather than basic generation technology [S4][S5][S14][S55]. More than **2 500 GW** of projects are now stalled in connection queues worldwide, and variable renewables are rising fast enough that storage, demand flexibility, and other integration measures increasingly decide how much clean generation actually lands [S55][S57].

House view: every serious climate program should look first like a **power-system construction and integration program**.

### 2. End-use efficiency and electrification
Heat pumps, efficient buildings, industrial efficiency, electric vehicles, public transit, passive cooling, efficient ACs and fans, better building envelopes, efficient new construction, and demand flexibility reduce fuel demand while making clean power more valuable [S1][S4][S16][S17][S28][S51][S52][S53][S54][S56][S58].  
Efficiency is underrated because it is politically quiet and visually unimpressive. But the ideal solution includes a lot of “boring” reduction in wasted energy. The archive now makes buildings more explicit inside this lane: advanced economies need mass retrofit and clean-heat programs because most of the stock already exists, while fast-growing economies need strong codes, passive design, efficient cooling, and clean cooking access so they do not lock in new waste [S51][S52][S53][S54]. Cooling belongs here because bad cooling policy drives both emissions and dangerous peak loads.

The archive now also sharpens the ordering rule around this lane: **avoid / shift / improve** often beats “improve” alone. OECD’s 2025 demand-side review says demand-side policy remains underused despite its large mitigation potential, and IPCC says demand-side strategies can cut end-use emissions by **40–70%** globally by 2050 [S97][S98].

### 3. Methane emergency action
Oil and gas leaks, venting and flaring, coal methane, landfill gas, wastewater, rice cultivation, and livestock measures belong in a dedicated fast lane. CCAC/UNEP assessments indicate human-caused methane can be reduced substantially this decade with major climate benefit [S8].

The archive now adds a sharper implementation claim: methane strategy must prioritize **measurement, enforcement, and rapid repair**, because IEA estimates energy-sector methane is about 80% higher than the total reported by countries to the UNFCCC, while UNEP’s MARS system shows that very large emissions events can now be publicly detected but still often go unanswered [S11][S66][S67]. The new short-latency rule strengthens this lane further: methane action is not only large by leverage, but unusually valuable because it begins reducing near-term warming faster than most climate measures [S133].

## Tier 2 — Essential for a full solution

### 4. Agrifood, ecosystems, and nitrogen reform
Forests, peatlands, mangroves, wetlands, and other ecosystems are not optional “nature extras,” but neither is food-systems reform. Agrifood systems account for about one-third of anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions, deforestation still runs at about 10.9 million hectares per year, and degraded peatlands and wetlands can flip from protection assets into emissions sources [S18][S19][S20][S21][S22][S36][S37][S38][S39].

### 5. Heavy-industry decarbonization
Steel, cement, chemicals, and high-temperature heat need a mix of efficiency, material substitution, circularity, electrification where possible, hydrogen in selected cases, and carbon capture where residual process emissions remain [S1][S29].  
The archive now sharpens this lane: industry is not only a supply-side technology problem. It is also a **materials-demand and market-formation problem**. IEA says steel and cement alone account for 14% of global energy- and process-related emissions on a direct basis, while announced near-zero steel and cement capacity by 2030 is only about 10% of what is needed on a net-zero-compatible path [S26]. That makes procurement, common definitions, embodied-carbon rules, reuse, and refurbishment part of climate strategy rather than side issues [S27][S28].

### 6. Clean mobility and transport hierarchy
Passenger road transport can electrify relatively quickly and is already moving at scale: IEA says electric car sales topped **17 million** in 2024 and are expected to exceed **20 million** in 2025 [S30]. But the archive sharpens the doctrine beyond passenger cars. The correct hierarchy is **avoid / shift / electrify** for road modes, with logistics redesign and modal shift for freight, while scarce low-emissions fuels are reserved for aviation, shipping, and only the genuinely hardest road-freight applications [S1][S30][S31][S32][S33][S34][S35].

## Tier 3 — Necessary enablers

### 7. Managed fossil decline
The archive treats fossil decline as an explicit task, not an automatic consequence. This means:
- no new unabated coal except narrow security exceptions
- aggressive retirement schedules for existing coal
- no lock-in of new oil and gas demand on the assumption of future cleanup
- end to inefficient fossil subsidies [S9]
- closure finance, cleanup liability, and worker/regional transition as part of the solution rather than afterthoughts [S23][S24][S25]

### 8. Adaptation, resilience, cooling, and early warning
Mitigation without protection fails politically and morally. WMO and UNEP show climate impacts are already severe, only about half of countries report adequate multi-hazard early warning systems, and adaptation finance remains insufficient [S3][S12][S15]. UNDRR’s GAR 2025 sharpens the stakes by arguing that disaster costs now exceed USD 2.3 trillion annually when cascading and ecosystem costs are counted [S40]. UNEP’s Global Cooling Watch 2025 adds that cooling demand could more than triple by 2050 under business as usual, so cooling must be treated as both adaptation and power-system policy [S16][S17]. The same logic applies to food systems: climate politics become more durable when mitigation is tied to food security, farm resilience, and lower waste rather than framed as sacrifice alone [S19][S21][S22].  

The archive now adds a clearer adaptation doctrine: adaptation is not merely humanitarian response after harm. It is **civil protection, resilient infrastructure, land-use discipline, water security, and financial preparedness** [S12][S15][S40][S41][S72][S73][S74][S75][S76].
The ideal solution protects people while decarbonizing.

### 9. Carbon dioxide removal for residuals, with hard boundaries
CDR belongs in the stack, but low. IPCC is clear that some deployment is unavoidable to counterbalance hard-to-abate residuals [S1]. But the archive now sharpens the constraint: current novel CDR is only about **1.3 million tonnes per year**, less than **0.1%** of total current CDR, while the State of CDR project identifies a much larger **7–9 GtCO2/yr** mid-century requirement in more-sustainable Paris-consistent scenarios [S42]. Meanwhile IEA says DAC, BECCS, and broader CO2 transport-and-storage infrastructure are all still **not on track** [S43][S44][S45][S46].  
That makes CDR necessary. It does **not** make it central. The archive rejects using future removals to excuse slow fossil decline now.

### 10. Buildings, retrofits, and clean heat
The archive now makes explicit what had been implicit: buildings are not just where energy is consumed, but where climate policy becomes daily lived infrastructure. UNEP says buildings and construction consume **32%** of global energy and contribute **34%** of global CO2 emissions [S28]. IEA adds that buildings account for around **30%** of global energy demand and about **20%** of energy-demand growth since 2019 [S51]. The right doctrine is different by context: retrofit and heat-pump deployment where most stock already exists; efficient new construction, passive cooling, and clean cooking where floor area and service access are still growing [S51][S52][S53][S54].

### 11. Critical minerals, batteries, and secure supply chains
The archive now makes explicit what had been implicit: the clean-energy transition is also a mineral and manufacturing transition. IEA’s 2025 outlook says lithium demand rose by nearly **30%** in 2024, the top three refining countries raised their average market share to **86%**, and copper remains a major exception to the otherwise improving supply picture [S47]. Batteries are now a “master key” technology, but their supply chains remain highly concentrated [S48]. Recycling therefore belongs in the core solution stack as a security and sustainability tool, not as a late waste-management add-on [S50].

## Meta-enabler across all tiers

### 12. Water security and hydrological stability
The archive now makes explicit what had been scattered: hydrological stability is a climate-system, food-system, ecosystem, and electricity-system issue at once. WMO says only about one-third of river basins had normal conditions in 2024, while the World Bank says global freshwater reserves have been declining by about 324 billion cubic metres per year over the past two decades [S72][S75]. This pushes drought planning, watershed protection, groundwater governance, floodplain discipline, and selective reuse into the core climate stack rather than leaving them as background adaptation context [S72][S73][S74][S75][S76].

### 13. Cities, urban form, and municipal delivery
The archive now makes explicit what had mostly been scattered: cities are a convergence arena where housing, mobility, cooling, water, and public legitimacy meet. UNDP’s 2024 review says cities account for about **67%** of global energy consumption and **70%** of greenhouse-gas emissions, yet only **26** of **194** NDCs analysed included specific urban-level finance requests [S77]. UN-Habitat adds that urban areas host about **57%** of the global population while occupying only about **1.7%** of the world’s surface, and that in many contexts urban emissions per capita are lower than national averages [S78]. This pushes compact growth, transit-supportive land use, flood-aware siting, social-housing-first retrofit delivery, urban heat reduction, and stronger municipal procurement into the core climate stack rather than leaving them as local garnish [S77][S78][S79][S80].

### 14. Public finance, industrial policy, and state capacity
The climate problem is not simply “price carbon and wait.” The major live bottlenecks are capital cost, grid build speed, permitting quality, local administrative capacity, workforce shortages, manufacturing scale, planning capacity, and political coordination [S4][S6][S13][S59][S60][S61][S62].  
The ideal solution therefore uses public procurement, concessional finance, guaranteed demand, standards, public infrastructure institutions, workforce pipelines, and faster but more disciplined permitting systems.

### 15. EMDE finance architecture: cost of capital, currency risk, and debt space
The archive now makes explicit what had been scattered: global climate success depends on whether mature clean technologies can actually finance in EMDE. IEA says utility-scale solar cost of capital in EMDE is well over **twice** advanced-economy levels, and that EMDE clean-energy investment needs to rise from about **USD 270 billion** today to **USD 870 billion** by the early 2030s for current pledges, and to **USD 1.6 trillion** in a 1.5°C pathway [S91]. OECD and IMF sharpen the macro constraint: EMDE other than China face a cumulative **USD 10 trillion** transition-finance shortfall by 2050 at current trends, while many EMDE already face high financing costs, large refinancing needs, and declining external flows [S92][S93]. This pushes guarantees, local-currency finance, catalytic concessional capital, and debt-space realism into the core climate stack [S94][S95][S96].

### 16. Truth infrastructure, standards, and enforcement
The archive now makes explicit what had been scattered: measured methane, interoperable low-emissions product definitions, public reporting, and hard MRV / liability rules are themselves enabling infrastructure. UNFCCC’s first BTR synthesis says over 100 first BTR submissions already cover about 75% of global GHG emissions in 2020, while UNEP and IEA show that methane undercounting and non-response to large observed emissions events remain major gaps [S11][S65][S66][S67][S68].

### 17. Delivery capacity, permitting, workforce, and social license
The archive now makes explicit what had been implicit: climate transition strategy also needs a **delivery state**. OECD’s 2025 work says local authorities and spatial planning strongly shape renewable timelines, while fragmented rules, poor coordination, inadequate consultation, and staffing shortages in permit-granting authorities or grid operators can all create delay and backlash [S60][S61]. IEA says around **60%** of energy companies report labour shortages already affecting timelines, reliability, and cost control [S62]. IRENA and ILO estimate at least **16.6 million** renewable-energy jobs worldwide in 2024, showing that workforce strategy is already a central transition variable [S63].

### 18. Access, affordability, and social protection
The archive now adds one more cross-cutting constraint: the transition must become materially easier to live with. IEA’s 2026 affordability work says more than **120 million** households in advanced economies spend over **10%** of their income on residential heating, cooling, and appliances, and that just one-third of tracked affordability policies in 2025 were targeted to the households that need support most [S69]. Meanwhile, the World Bank-led SDG7 tracking report says **666 million** people still lacked electricity access in 2023, and ILO says **3.8 billion** people remain entirely unprotected by social protection systems [S70][S71]. That makes access, targeted support, and social protection part of climate implementation rather than charitable add-ons.

### 19. Exposure discipline, insurance realism, and retreat from the unprotectable
The archive now makes explicit what had previously been scattered: not every place can rationally be preserved in place forever. OECD’s 2026 catastrophe-finance work says increasing wildfire losses are already affecting the availability of affordable insurance in some high-risk countries and could have implications for housing markets because mortgage lenders often require insurance [S81]. Swiss Re says 2024 catastrophe losses reached **USD 318 billion**, with **57% uninsured** and a **USD 181 billion** protection gap [S82]. UNDRR and the World Bank sharpen the design implication: risk information should be open and decision-usable, and high-risk land should not keep being settled as if future relocation were someone else’s problem [S83][S84]. This pushes no-build rules, repeat-loss limits, risk disclosure, and public buyout / relocation capacity into the core climate stack rather than leaving retreat to silent insurer exit or household collapse.

### 20. Health, clean air, and resilient care systems

The archive now makes explicit what had mostly been scattered: climate strategy should be judged partly as health policy. WHO says combined ambient and household air pollution is associated with about **7 million premature deaths** annually, while heat stress is the leading cause of weather-related deaths [S85][S86]. ILO says at least **2.41 billion** workers are exposed to excessive heat each year, and the Lancet Countdown estimates **USD 1.09 trillion** in potential income losses from labour-capacity reduction due to extreme heat in 2024 [S87][S88]. The archive therefore treats combustion-pollution reduction, heat-health protection, occupational heat rules, and climate-resilient low-carbon health systems as part of the climate stack itself rather than a soft co-benefits appendix [S89][S90].

### 21. Demand shaping, sufficiency, and high-wellbeing design
The archive now makes explicit what had been scattered: climate success depends partly on meeting needs with less wasteful energy and material throughput. OECD’s 2025 review says demand-side policy remains underused; IPCC says demand-side strategies can reduce end-use-sector emissions by **40–70%** globally by 2050 and that low-demand scenarios can cut the need for upstream energy by **45%** relative to 2020; UNEP’s Global Resources Outlook says extraction and processing account for over **60%** of climate impacts and that high-income countries use **six times** more materials and generate **ten times** the climate impacts of low-income countries [S97][S98][S99]. This pushes compact form, repairability, reuse, food-waste reduction, smaller/ shared mobility where appropriate, and better service design into the core stack rather than leaving them as lifestyle garnish.

### 22. Critical-service floors, public safety, thermal survivability, and coastal / ocean continuity

Rev0269 adds a cube rule to the ranked stack: once a measure has a plausible emissions or protection case, ask whether it preserves critical service floors under repeated shocks. The minimum floor now includes not only power, water, food, health, shelter, transport, information, finance, legal access, energy, waste, and care, but also public-safety response, search and rescue, mass-casualty / missing-persons / family-reunification capacity, heat-safe refuge, and coastal / ocean systems that hold livelihoods, ports, fisheries, and protective ecosystems together [S536][S537][S538][S539][S540][S541][S543][S545].

This tier is not a rival to emissions reduction. It is the operational test that keeps clean-transition doctrine from ignoring the places where a hotter world is already degrading ordinary life. A packet that cuts emissions while leaving people unable to cool, evacuate, receive emergency care, dispose of hazardous debris, reconnect with family, or return safely after a coastal flood is under-specified as climate policy.

## Compression

If forced to reduce the whole archive to one ranked sentence:

**Power systems first; electrification, buildings, and demand shaping second; methane third; agrifood-and-ecosystems-and-water fourth; industrial materials and hard sectors fifth; with urban form, EMDE finance architecture, delivery capacity, truth infrastructure, mineral security, access-and-affordability design, exposure discipline, health protection, managed fossil decline, public protection, public-safety continuity, thermal survivability, coastal / ocean continuity, and inspectable service floors running across everything.**

## Rev0270 rank patch — dependency graph, long-tail constraints, SLCPs, clean air, plastics, gender, custody, and local capacity

The ranked stack now has a second-read rule: after ranking mitigation and adaptation measures by physical effect, speed, feasibility, justice, and durability, run them through the rev0270 dependency screens.

1. **Interdependency screen:** does the packet name upstream dependencies, downstream consequences, degraded modes, restoration conflicts, and correction ledgers? Route to `308`.
2. **Long-tail physical-risk screen:** does the packet avoid sea-level, cryosphere, permafrost, glacier, ocean, and tipping-risk assumptions that make long-lived assets falsely safe? Route to `309`.
3. **SLCP / clean-air screen:** does the packet cut methane, black carbon, ozone precursors, and HFCs where relevant, while preserving breathable air during smoke and pollution episodes? Route to `310` and `311`.
4. **Materials / petrochemical screen:** does the packet reduce avoidable plastic and petrochemical demand rather than shifting fossil lock-in into feedstocks, waste, and toxic exposure? Route to `312`.
5. **Rights and agency screen:** does the packet pass gendered-safety, SRHR, custodial, and local administrative-capacity tests? Route to `313`, `314`, and `315`.

A measure that scores well on emissions but fails these screens may still be useful, but it is not yet a mature climate solution.


## rev0271 hidden-rails corollary

rev0271 adds the hidden rails beneath service floors: medical products and oxygen, digital infrastructure and cyber recovery, payment rails and cash access, humanitarian logistics, displacement / receiving capacity, climate-conflict integrity, and readiness assurance. The archive should not treat these as support functions. They are the difference between a service floor that exists on paper and a service floor that works under outage, heat, smoke, flood, conflict, cyber disruption, aid scarcity, or displacement [S564][S565][S567][S568][S570][S571][S573][S574][S575].

New rule: **every continuity packet must identify hidden rails, degraded modes, and assurance tests before it can be marked ready.**

## Rev0272 ranking clarification — recovery rails are throughput multipliers

The intervention stack should now treat recovery rails as throughput multipliers. Clean power, electrification, methane cuts, adaptation, finance, and fossil decline are weakened if recovery bottlenecks turn every shock into a stalled repair market, claims backlog, fuel queue, floodwater failure, local-business collapse, or proof dispute.

This does not make repair, claims, stormwater, community organizations, or labs more important than decarbonization. It means they protect the political and human continuity that lets decarbonization persist through repeated shocks. Route recovery-rail questions to `323`–`330`.

## rev0273 stack patch — priority, mutual aid, spares, maintenance, mental health, civic continuity, reentry, and loadcases

rev0273 adds a scarcity-and-legitimacy patch to the service-floor tier. Critical services should now be ranked not only by their existence but by whether they can allocate scarce restoration, obtain mutual aid, access critical spares, maintain asset condition, support mental and psychosocial recovery, preserve elections and public participation, govern reentry and credentials, and pass compound loadcase drills [S587][S590][S592][S593][S595][S598][S600][S602].

Updated stack shorthand: **clean buildout and fossil decline remain the mitigation core; service-floor continuity now requires scarcity priority, shared capacity, inventory, maintenance, behavioral health, civic legitimacy, access-control discipline, and tested bad-day scenarios.**

## Rev0274 routing correction — protect the interface, not only the infrastructure

The ranked stack should now read service continuity through an interface lens: after mitigation, fossil decline, adaptation, finance, delivery capacity, truth, and recovery rails, the programme must protect the **points where people touch the system**. The highest-risk interfaces are shelters, registries, aid queues, payment points, contractor markets, checkpoints, animal evacuation, fatality management, cultural anchors, and isolated last-mile paths [S603][S607][S608][S610][S614][S616].


## Rev0275 stack insertion — proof-to-rebuild controls

Add a new delivery-control layer beneath recovery rails: **proof-to-rebuild controls**. These are property proof, risk-exit governance, procurement integrity, reimbursement cash flow, code / permit / inspection throughput, essential-worker sustainment, shared-housing governance, and unmet-needs closure. They do not outrank mitigation; they prevent adaptation and recovery money from becoming title exclusion, unsafe rebuild, audit paralysis, worker burnout, contractor capture, or false closeout. Route to `348`–`355`.

## Rev0276 ranking addendum — readiness proof outranks prose coverage

When two climate-service claims compete for attention, prefer the one that can show owner, score, loadcase, degraded mode, fiscal path, and current evidence. Unscored service floors remain doctrine; scored, owned, tested, and financeable floors become operating infrastructure. Files `356`–`363` add this ranking rule to the cube.

## Rev0277 enabling layer: observability before assurance

The ranked stack should now treat public observability and contestability as a delivery enabler. A service floor cannot be fairly prioritized, repaired, or financed if the cube cannot show which floor is down, which population is excluded, which data is stale, which owner is responsible, and which correction remains unfunded.

The observability layer includes query views, service-floor dashboards, sensor / remote-sensing integrity, rumor-control loops, OT cyber-physical safety, Natech overlays, disease surveillance, and contestable public ledgers. Route to `364`–`371` [S653][S654][S655][S656][S661][S663].

## Rev0278 addition — anticipatory action as a service-floor accelerator

The ranked stack should now read early warning as an accelerator only when it is connected to trigger-funded action. Good forecasts, public dashboards, and alert feeds do not by themselves protect people. They become climate infrastructure when they pre-authorize transport, cash, clean air, cooling, work modification, school and care rules, pre-positioning, and after-action calibration. Route to `372`–`380`.

## Rev0279 ranking patch — add impact-to-intake operating capacity

The resilience tier now requires an impact-to-intake layer. Plans should be ranked lower if they lack: EOC / common-operating-picture discipline (`381`), rapid damage and needs assessment (`382`), public lifeline stabilization clocks (`383`), building-safety / habitability placards (`384`), survivor intake and grievance routing (`385`), commodity distribution (`386`), volunteer / donations management (`387`), and emergency-powers guardrails (`388`).

This is not a separate humanitarian appendix. It is the conversion mechanism between forecast-to-action, live incident management, household recovery, public finance, and legitimacy.

## Rev0280 addition — stabilization-to-recovery pathways

rev0280 adds the rule that impact-to-intake is not recovery. A person can be registered, sheltered, assessed, or referred and still remain stranded. Climate service floors therefore need stabilization pathways: interim-to-durable housing, displaced-student stability, chronic-care continuity, nutrition-benefit continuity, utility-arrears protection, deadline tolling, and no-wrong-door benefit sequencing [S698][S699][S712].

The cube should now ask not only whether a service exists, but whether a household can move from first contact to a durable outcome without losing school, medicine, food, utilities, legal rights, or case status along the way.

### rev0281 ranking rule — count functional recovery above administrative throughput

Recovery throughput is not the same as recovery. Assistance awards, case closures, route reopenings, and repair counts should rank below functional household outcomes when the two diverge. The next priority layer is therefore household function: balance-sheet repair, tenancy stability, livelihood return, recovery mobility, essential property and device replacement, document / account recovery, and nonrecurrence closeout. These do not replace mitigation or resilient infrastructure; they prevent climate shocks from becoming permanent private loss [S715][S717][S719][S721][S723].

## Rev0282 ranking patch — risk reduction outranks restored exposure

The stack should now demote any recovery or adaptation claim that repairs damage while leaving future exposure unmanaged. After household-function recovery, the next ranked test is whether the programme converts lessons into a mitigation pipeline, exposes repetitive-loss areas, updates hazard maps and future-condition design values, stacks finance, applies equity-aware benefit-cost selection, blocks new risk through land use, governs nature-based solutions as lifecycle assets, and monitors performance after completion.

Updated shorthand: **recovery that recreates risk is not resilience; mitigation that cannot show pipeline, finance, standards, maintenance, and performance is not yet risk reduction.** Route to `405`–`412` [S622][S726][S733][S736][S739].

## Rev0283 addendum — adaptation must be path-governed

The canon now treats adaptation and resilience as pathway-governed rather than project-governed. A mature programme must expose signposts, thresholds, model assumptions, option value, lock-in risks, portfolio stress tests, service-level contracts, unsafe-asset exits, and professional duties. Otherwise recovery can rebuild, mitigation can fund projects, and dashboards can look current while the system still commits to a brittle future [S741][S743][S745].

## Rev0284 addendum — assurance is a cross-cutting enabler

Add an assurance tier beneath every intervention lane: source provenance, control ownership, corrective-action closure, open contracting, civil-rights access, workforce role depth, and independent redress. A fast climate programme that cannot prove delivery will be slower in reality because failures repeat, money leaks, excluded users contest, and public trust collapses [S761][S767][S768].

## Rev0289 intervention-stack refactor: nuclear moves up

The intervention stack now gives nuclear a favored clean-firm role. Existing-fleet life extension, uprates, safe restarts, repeat-build new nuclear, and credible advanced-reactor deployments should be treated as priority clean-firm interventions when their assurance gates are met. They should not be buried beneath variable-only generation portfolios where the service floor requires firm capacity, high-load reliability, industrial heat, or long-duration resilience [S771][S773][S775][S776][S779].

This is a rank change and a control change: nuclear-positive ranking is binding only through the nuclear assurance gates.


## Rev0294 nuclear integrated-energy propagation note

Rev0294 extends the nuclear-positive preference into cogeneration, district heating, process heat, desalination, hydrogen and clean molecules, data-center/AI infrastructure, critical loads, and co-product public-value scorecards. These additions are explicitly bounded by new gates NG_53–NG_66 and by sector-coupling exception rules; preference is not treated as maturity without local evidence.

## Rev0297 nuclear geopolitical-resilience overlay

The nuclear-positive orientation now includes an explicit geopolitical-resilience burden of proof. Clean-firm nuclear preference is strengthened when it reduces fossil/import/coercion exposure, but maturity is capped by unresolved fuel-cycle concentration, vendor lock-in, sanctions exposure, export-finance/debt risk, safeguards capacity, export-control boundaries, and sensitive-publication controls.


## Rev0298 nuclear human-capital refactor note

The cube's pro-nuclear orientation is now explicitly conditioned by workforce depth, operator licensing, regulator capacity, safety/quality culture, knowledge retention, craft labor, and public training pathways. Nuclear remains favored for clean firm power and system benefit, but human-capital and institutional-capacity gaps are binding maturity caps rather than implementation footnotes. ## Rev0300 upstream uranium accountability note

This revision keeps the cube explicitly nuclear-positive while adding a responsible uranium supply constraint: pro-nuclear planning must not erase mining, milling, tailings, radon, groundwater, host-community, Indigenous-rights, legacy-cleanup, transport, material-provenance, or cumulative public-health burdens. Nuclear remains preferred in relevant clean-firm contexts, but upstream uranium evidence now caps maturity when missing.

## Rev0303 physical-security nuclearization note

Rev0303 keeps nuclear energy explicitly favored while adding a physical-security maturity cap: design-basis-threat protection, force-on-force or equivalent performance testing, insider mitigation, drone/perimeter posture, transport security, law-enforcement coordination, and security-safe publication must be traceable before nuclear security claims can mature.
