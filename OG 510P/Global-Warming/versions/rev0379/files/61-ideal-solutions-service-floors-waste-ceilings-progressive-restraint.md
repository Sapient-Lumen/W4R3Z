# 61 — Ideal Solutions: Service Floors, Waste Ceilings, and Progressive Restraint

## Claim

The archive now needs one justice rule stated more plainly.

**Ideal solutions do not ask everyone for the same kind of restraint.**
They should expand essential energy and climate-protective services where people still lack them, while pushing much harder against wasteful, high-throughput demand in affluent settings.

That means the archive should stop treating fairness as a vague side principle and use a more operational distinction:

- a **service floor** below which people should be brought upward as fast as possible, and
- a **waste ceiling** above which systems should stop subsidizing, normalizing, or locking in avoidable excess [S69][S70][S71][S97][S98][S99][S104].

This note does not replace `20-access-affordability-and-the-climate-social-contract.md` or `26-demand-shaping-sufficiency-and-high-wellbeing.md`.
It gives the shortest combined answer to a recurring archive problem:

**how should an ideal climate portfolio distinguish between justified growth in basic service and unjustified growth in wasteful throughput?**

## The rule

The archive should generally promote measures that do both of the following at once:

1. **raise the floor** for people lacking electricity, clean cooking, cooling safety, durable housing quality, resilient water and health services, or affordable mobility, and
2. **lower the ceiling** on wasteful energy, material, land, and premium-fuel use where needs could be met with much less throughput [S16][S17][S69][S70][S71][S97][S98][S99][S104][S106].

This is not a contradiction.
It is the basic design rule for solving a global problem under unequal starting conditions.
IPCC is explicit that some regions and socioeconomic groups still require additional energy and resources for human well-being, even while demand-side mitigation can cut end-use emissions deeply overall [S98].
UNEP’s resource outlook is equally explicit that high-income countries consume far more materials and generate far more climate impact than low-income countries [S99].

So the archive should reject two symmetrical errors:

- **austerity universalism**, which asks underserved populations to remain underserved, and
- **abundance without discipline**, which treats affluent excess as untouchable and tries to clean it all at any cost later [S69][S70][S97][S98][S99].

## What belongs below the floor

In many places, climate progress still requires **more** of some things, not less.
The archive should treat the following as floor-raising priorities:

- reliable electricity access [S69][S70]
- clean cooking access [S69][S70]
- cooling access that is safe, efficient, and grid-manageable [S16][S17][S69][S104][S106]
- better building envelopes, safer homes, and lower household energy burden [S51][S52][S53][S69][S106]
- resilient water, health, and public-service infrastructure in high-exposure places [S71][S72][S85][S88][S90]
- affordable public and shared mobility where exclusion or dangerous car dependence is the real problem [S31][S77][S78][S97][S98]
- social protection strong enough to cushion climate shocks and transition shocks [S71]

The archive should be explicit here:
**it is not an ideal climate solution to deny basic modern energy and protection services to poor or highly exposed populations in order to preserve a theory of equal sacrifice** [S69][S70][S71][S98][S104].

## What belongs above the ceiling

The archive should also be clearer that a meaningful share of demand growth in affluent settings is not a human-needs floor problem.
It is a **waste, lock-in, and bad-design problem**.

Ceiling-lowering priorities therefore include:

- inefficient buildings and unmanaged cooling demand where better envelopes, passive design, and efficient equipment would deliver the same comfort with far less load [S16][S17][S53][S104][S106]
- car-dependent urban form and other settlement choices that harden avoidable transport demand [S77][S78][S79][S97][S98]
- short product lifetimes, blocked repair, and replacement-heavy consumption where maintenance, repair, reuse, and second life would preserve service with less material throughput [S97][S99][S184][S185][S186][S187][S188][S189]
- food waste and other high-volume discard systems that consume land, energy, water, and money for no welfare gain [S21][S97][S99]
- use of scarce clean fuels, biomass, or carbon-management capacity for easy uses that could already be electrified, avoided, or materially reduced more directly [S30][S32][S33][S42][S112][S165][S166][S167]

The point is not to moralize every instance of high consumption.
The point is to stop structuring whole systems around levels of throughput that make decarbonization slower, more material-intensive, and less globally fair than it needs to be [S97][S98][S99][S106].

## Policy consequences

This rule changes how the archive should rank policies.

### 1. Public money should raise floors before protecting waste ceilings
Targeted access, retrofit, appliance, cooling, housing, and social-protection programs should generally outrank blanket support for high consumption [S58][S69][S70][S71].

### 2. Rich-country demand shaping is not anti-development
In affluent contexts, lower-throughput design is often what creates fiscal and political room for global fairness rather than what threatens it [S97][S98][S99].

### 3. Efficiency and sufficiency are distributional tools, not only engineering tools
A better envelope, efficient AC, repairable product, compact neighbourhood, or strong bus network does not only cut emissions.
It can also narrow the gap between acceptable living standards and household cost [S16][S17][S58][S69][S97][S106].

### 4. “Universal electrification” is too blunt by itself
The archive should ask **electrify what, for whom, and with what demand shape?**
Floor-raising electrification and ceiling-lowering demand reform belong together [S58][S69][S70][S97][S98][S106].

### 5. Fairness should be judged in services, not only tonnes
A proposal deserves extra weight when it lowers tonnes while also moving more people above a threshold of comfort, health, safety, affordability, and resilience [S69][S71][S85][S90][S104].

## What this note helps the archive reject

The archive should now demote proposals that:

- describe all demand growth as equally legitimate even where much of it is driven by wasteful design and affluent overconsumption [S97][S98][S99]
- describe all demand restraint as anti-poor even when the actual target is luxury throughput, waste, or lock-in in high-consuming settings [S69][S70][S98][S99]
- demand the same household sacrifice from people below the service floor and people far above it [S69][S70][S71][S99]
- promise universal access and resilience later while protecting high-throughput incumbency now [S69][S70][S91][S94][S96]
- treat justice as equal pain-sharing rather than unequal obligation under unequal starting points [S69][S70][S71][S98][S99]

## Compression

**Ideal solutions should raise service floors and lower waste ceilings at the same time: expand reliable electricity, clean cooking, cooling safety, resilient housing, health, water, and mobility where people still lack them; and push much harder against affluent waste, inefficient buildings, car-dependent form, short product lifetimes, food waste, and easy-use consumption of scarce clean fuels. That is how the archive should cash out climate justice without drifting into either austerity for the poor or indulgence for the rich [S69][S70][S71][S97][S98][S99][S104][S106].**
