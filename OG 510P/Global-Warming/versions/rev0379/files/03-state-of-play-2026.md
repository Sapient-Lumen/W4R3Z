# 03 — State of Play in 2026

## The blunt picture

The world is no longer short on candidate solutions. It is short on **speed, coordination, finance, fossil retirement discipline, and enough willingness to reduce wasteful demand instead of only trying to green it**.

Three facts define the moment:

1. **Climate indicators are worsening.** WMO reports that 2024 was likely the first calendar year with global mean temperature more than 1.5°C above the 1850–1900 average, and that 2025 was still ranked either second or third warmest on record across eight major datasets, with ocean heat remaining among the highest on record [S3][S105].
2. **Clean-energy momentum is real.** IEA sees clean energy meeting virtually all aggregate growth in demand between 2023 and 2035 in its stated-policies case, with all three fossil fuels peaking before 2030 in that case [S4].
3. **Emissions are still not falling globally.** Energy-related CO2 rose again in 2024 [S7], and the Global Carbon Budget 2025 says fossil CO2 is hitting another record high while the remaining 1.5°C carbon budget is virtually exhausted [S10].

This means the transition has escaped the “toy phase,” but not yet the **physics phase**.

This revision adds the post-March 2026 official update, and it sharpens the same verdict rather than overturning it. IEA’s 2026 global energy review says the clean buildout is now large enough to move global aggregates — solar PV met more than a quarter of higher energy demand in 2025, low-emissions sources supplied nearly 60% of demand growth, renewable capacity additions reached about 800 GW, battery-storage additions reached almost 110 GW, and clean technologies deployed since 2019 now avoid about 3 Gt CO2 annually. But the same review says energy-related CO2 emissions still rose by around 0.4%, all three fossil fuels still grew, and electricity demand rose nearly 3%, well over twice total energy-demand growth [S247]. IEA’s 2026 methane tracker adds the near-term-forcing version of the same diagnosis: there is still no sign that fossil-fuel methane emissions fell in 2025, even though around 70% of fossil-fuel methane can be abated with existing technology and more than 35 Mt could be avoided at no net cost [S248].

Rev0240 adds the overshoot-state update. Copernicus reports that 2025 was the third warmest year on record and that 2023–2025 was the first three-year period averaging more than 1.5°C above the pre-industrial level, even though current long-term warming is estimated around 1.4°C [S249]. WMO’s 2025–2029 update says there is an 86% chance at least one of the next five years exceeds 1.5°C and a 70% chance that the five-year average exceeds 1.5°C, while long-term warming averaged over decades remains below 1.5°C [S251]. UNEP’s 2025 Emissions Gap Report says current policies point to about 2.8°C this century and current NDCs to about 2.3–2.5°C, with 2035 emissions cuts of 35% and 55% below 2019 needed for 2°C and 1.5°C pathways respectively [S250]. The governing implication is not fatalism. It is overshoot discipline: every tenth still matters, gross cuts remain the main throttle, protection has to be built now, and removals must stay bounded to true residuals and repair.

Rev0252 adds the circularity and end-of-life update. A clean buildout that scales hardware faster than repair, reuse, reverse logistics, and recycling can create a second-order bottleneck: stranded assets, e-waste, battery and refrigerant hazards, missing spare parts, orphaned products, illegal waste exports, and lost secondary materials. UNEP says municipal solid waste could rise from 2.1 billion tonnes in 2023 to 3.8 billion tonnes by 2050 without stronger waste prevention and circular systems [S320]. UNITAR / ITU say 62 billion kg of e-waste was generated in 2022, with only 22.3% documented as formally collected and recycled [S321]. IRENA and IEA-PVPS projected 60–78 million tonnes of cumulative PV panel waste by 2050, while IEA says battery recycling and reuse are needed for supply-chain sustainability and security but announced capacity and retirement geography do not automatically align [S322][S324]. The governing implication is that clean deployment should buy durable, repairable, proof-carrying, recoverable assets and build the reverse side of the clean economy before the retirement wave arrives.

## The main bottlenecks

### 1. Grids and interconnection
Generation has scaled faster than wires, substations, system planning, and queue management [S5]. IEA’s 2025 grid-congestion commentary strengthens the point: congestion is now directly delaying clean-energy connections, electrification, housing, and industrial projects in some systems [S14]. Heat is worsening the problem by pushing up cooling-driven peak demand, which means grid adequacy cannot be planned from average-load assumptions alone [S16][S17].  

Current official evidence now makes the cooling side more front-rank than the archive previously said at the smallest front door. UNEP’s 2025 cooling work says business-as-usual cooling demand could more than triple by **2050** and push cooling emissions to about **7.2 billion tonnes CO2e**, while the IEA’s 2025 World Energy Outlook says income-driven air-conditioning use adds around **330 GW** to global peak demand by **2035** and higher temperatures add another **170 GW** in the STEPS [S16][S169][S219]. The same UNEP work says nearly **two-thirds** of the emissions cuts available in sustainable cooling come from passive and low-energy solutions, and that only **54 countries** yet have comprehensive policy coverage across passive cooling, minimum energy performance standards, and refrigerant transition even though cooling is now recognized much more widely in climate strategies [S169]. The live implication is that sustainable cooling is not just a buildings subtopic. It is increasingly a joined heat-protection, electricity-peak, affordability, and refrigerant-governance lane.
The archive treats this as the highest-probability point of failure in an otherwise workable clean-energy transition.

A closely related bottleneck is still under-mobilised flexibility. IEA’s 2025–2026 work now makes plain that rapidly electrifying systems do not only need more supply and more wires; they also need much more controllable demand, better pricing, automation, and storage. IEA says only around **100 GW** of demand response is currently utilised globally even though residential air conditioning alone contributes around **600 GW** of peak demand and aluminium around **160 GW** [S221]. Its 2025 demand-flexibility brief adds that flexibility can raise system efficiency by up to **30%**, typically cut household electricity bills by **5–15%** under dynamic tariffs, and provide grid capacity at up to **three times lower** cost than building new capacity [S220]. Meanwhile, IEA’s renewables work says curtailment and negative prices are rising across multiple countries and should be read as flexibility shortages rather than simple overbuild [S222]. The live implication is that flexibility is no longer a specialist balancing tool; it is now part of whether electrification stays affordable and whether rising clean generation becomes usable service rather than stranded potential.

### 2. Clean finance in emerging markets
IEA notes that the share of global clean-energy investment going to EMDE outside China remains stuck around 15% despite their huge population and development needs [S4][S13]. IEA’s cost-of-capital work sharpens the problem: utility-scale solar cost of capital in EMDE is well over **twice** advanced-economy levels, annual clean-energy investment in EMDE needs to rise from about **USD 270 billion** today to **USD 870 billion** by the early 2030s for current pledges and to **USD 1.6 trillion** in a 1.5°C pathway, and a **tripling of concessional funding** would be required to get EMDE on track [S91]. OECD and IMF add the macro-financial constraint: EMDE other than China face a cumulative **USD 10 trillion** transition-finance shortfall by 2050 at current growth trends, while many EMDE already face high financing costs, large external refinancing needs, and declining net external flows [S92][S93]. Without a finance breakthrough in guarantees, local-currency finance, and debt-space-compatible support, global decarbonization will remain geographically lopsided and too slow [S94][S95][S96]. IEA and OECD now make the bottleneck even plainer: most missing EMDE buildout is in mature technologies rather than speculative ones, around **60%** of the needed finance outside China must ultimately come from private capital, and deeper local-currency bond markets are particularly critical for unlocking private finance for low-emissions infrastructure [S91][S195][S196]. The archive therefore treats world-scale climate speed increasingly as a finance-architecture question as well as a technology-and-delivery question.

### 3. Demand-side mitigation remains underused
The transition is still biased toward supply-side substitution even where better system design could reduce the amount of energy, material throughput, and infrastructure expansion required. OECD’s 2025 demand-side review says supply-side action has been prioritised while demand-side policies remain underutilised despite their significant potential [S97]. IPCC says demand-side strategies could cut emissions in buildings, land transport, and food end use by **40–70%** globally by 2050, and that low-demand scenarios can reduce upstream energy needs by **45%** relative to 2020 while improving services [S98]. UNEP’s resource outlook adds that extraction and processing account for over **60%** of climate impacts, which means endless “cleaner supply” without demand shaping still leaves a large material-pressure problem [S99].

### 3A. Circularity and end-of-life capacity are becoming buildout constraints
Clean hardware is no longer a niche stock. Batteries, PV, power electronics, sensors, chargers, controls, heat pumps, refrigerant equipment, and grid hardware are entering ordinary infrastructure at large scale. That makes repairability, spare parts, firmware support, take-back, recycling, and waste-shipment rules climate-speed variables rather than downstream waste policy. The EU repair directive, EU battery recycling-efficiency rules, Global Battery Alliance passport work, and Basel Convention e-waste amendments all point in the same direction: clean assets need proof, owners, rights, and safe end-of-life routes before scale makes failures expensive [S325][S326][S327][S328]. The live implication is that a serious climate programme should measure repair, reuse, refurbishment, take-back, recovered material, and asset-closure proof alongside installation counts.

### 4. Methane underperformance and undercounting
Methane remains an outsized near-term opportunity that is still underused relative to its climatic value [S8]. WMO’s 2025 greenhouse-gas bulletin says atmospheric methane reached an unprecedented high of **1 942 ± 2 ppb in 2024**, around **166% above pre-industrial levels** [S215]. IEA’s 2025 tracker adds a harsher implementation reading: the fossil-fuel sector is responsible for nearly one-third of methane emissions from human activity today, energy-sector methane emissions remain above **120 Mt** annually, around **70%** of those emissions could be avoided with existing technologies, yet only around **5%** of global oil and gas output demonstrably meets a near-zero methane standard and total energy-related methane is estimated to be about **80% higher** than the total reported by countries to the UNFCCC [S212][S213]. UNEP’s 2025 methane status report summary sharpens the policy gap again: current national plans submitted by mid-2025 could translate into only about an **8% cut by 2030** below 2020 levels if fully implemented, while reaching the Global Methane Pledge target of **30%** requires full implementation of technically feasible measures globally; over **80%** of 2030 reduction potential is low cost, with mitigation potential split roughly **72% energy**, **18% waste**, and **10% agriculture** [S214]. The archive therefore now treats methane not as a side-lane clean-up item but as one of the fastest levers on near-term warming, crop loss, and health damage in the 2020s and 2030s.

The newest IEA tracker makes this a delivery failure rather than an information gap. It estimates fossil-fuel operations still emit about 124 Mt of methane per year — oil around 45 Mt, coal around 43 Mt, and natural gas around 36 Mt — and that more than 85 Mt of emissions tied to fossil-fuel operations came from the ten biggest emitting countries in 2025 [S248]. The front-door implication is now stronger: methane needs named repair crews, satellite-response protocols, venting-and-flaring rules, performance standards, and importing-market discipline, not another decade as a familiar but under-executed opportunity.

### 5. Fossil incumbency
Incumbents continue to convert political power into delay, subsidy, new extraction, and lock-in. The problem is not just emissions; it is the institutional ability of incumbent systems to preserve themselves.

### 6. Hard-sector uncertainty and weak market formation
Industry, shipping, aviation, and some freight pathways are still more heterogeneous and expensive than power-sector solutions. This does not mean “wait.” It means use targeted industrial policy instead of pretending one global tool will solve every sector. In industry specifically, the bottleneck is not only technology readiness but also common definitions, procurement, cost-sharing, and demand formation for low-emissions materials [S26][S27]. UNEP’s latest buildings-and-construction review sharpens the point by noting that cement and steel alone are responsible for about 18% of global emissions, so industrial decarbonization is partly a construction-demand problem [S28]. In transport, the analogous mistake is to treat every mode as if it needed the same solution. Road electrification is scaling quickly, but aviation fuel remains almost entirely fossil and shipping still depends on stronger fuel and pricing rules, so clean-fuel allocation and sector-specific governance are now central transport bottlenecks [S30][S33][S34][S35].

### 7. Adaptation underfunding and under-governance
WMO’s 2026 release of the State of the Global Climate 2025 says **2015–2025** were the hottest **11 years** on record, that **2025** was the **second or third warmest year** on record at about **1.43 °C** above the 1850–1900 average, and that extreme events caused disruption, devastation, and **billions in economic losses** [S203]. UNEP’s 2025 adaptation review says visible planning and implementation progress exists, but finance remains far from enough: developing-country adaptation needs are estimated at **USD 310 billion per year** in **2035** on modelled terms, or **USD 365 billion per year** using extrapolated national-plan needs, while international public adaptation finance flows were only **USD 26 billion** in **2023** — a gap of roughly **12–14 times** current flows [S205]. WMO’s 2025 multi-hazard early-warning report adds that **119 countries** now report having such systems, up **113%** over ten years, but this is still only **60%** of all countries, and only **43%** of small island developing States reported having systems in place [S204]. OECD sharpens the infrastructure-governance side of the same diagnosis: losses from climate-related extreme events rose **sevenfold**, from **USD 198 billion** in the **1970s** to **USD 1.6 trillion** in the **2010s**, yet only **23%** of OECD countries with relevant data report resilience progress annually or biennially, only **28%** compare project alternatives on resilience grounds, and only **32%** use monetised resilience analysis to inform project prioritisation [S206].
A mitigation-only politics therefore no longer fails merely on moral grounds. It also fails on governability grounds when people face heat, flood, fire, crop, care, infrastructure, and insurance shocks now.

Health stress is part of the same picture, but still too often treated as rhetoric rather than system design. WHO says combined ambient and household air pollution is associated with about **7 million premature deaths** annually and that climate change is expected to cause about **250 000 additional deaths per year** between 2030 and 2050 from malnutrition, malaria, diarrhoea, and heat stress alone [S85][S90]. ILO says at least **2.41 billion** workers are exposed to excessive heat each year, while the Lancet Countdown estimates **USD 1.09 trillion** in potential income losses from labour-capacity reduction due to extreme heat in 2024 [S87][S88]. The live governance failure is that health is still often framed as a co-benefit instead of as a central reason to cut combustion fast and harden care systems.

Insurance stress is also starting to reveal a settlement problem rather than merely a finance problem. OECD’s 2026 catastrophe-finance work says increasing wildfire losses are already affecting the availability of affordable insurance in some high-risk countries, and that reduced access can spill into housing markets because mortgage lenders often require coverage [S81]. Swiss Re’s 2025 catastrophe review adds the larger scale signal: 2024 losses reached **USD 318 billion**, with **57% uninsured** [S82]. The live issue is therefore not just “how to insure more,” but whether governments will use risk information, land-use rules, buyouts, and resilient redevelopment to stop exposure from outrunning both insurers and households [S81][S82][S83][S84].

### 8. Water stress and hydrological instability are becoming cross-sector bottlenecks
WMO’s 2025 *State of Global Water Resources 2024* says only **one third** of river basins had normal conditions in 2024, all glacier regions worldwide reported losses for a **third straight year**, and across rivers, reservoirs, lakes, groundwater, and glaciers there were significant departures from normal [S224]. The World Bank’s 2025 *Continental Drying* work sharpens the structural side of the diagnosis: global freshwater reserves have declined by about **324 billion m3 per year** over the past two decades — enough to meet the annual water needs of **280 million people** — with median basin-level losses of about **3%** of renewable freshwater supply across all basins and **10%** in arid basins already experiencing drying [S225]. FAO’s latest land-and-water flagship adds the agrifood consequence: agriculture still accounts for **more than 70%** of global freshwater withdrawal, which means hydrological instability is not only an environmental issue but a food-system operating constraint [S227]. And IEA’s 2025 electricity-reliability analysis shows the energy consequence too: droughts reduced hydropower output worldwide in 2024, affecting Ecuador and Colombia significantly, while Mexico faced supply shortages during peak demand amid heatwaves and low hydropower generation [S226]. Climate strategy that treats water as background adaptation context is now under-specified; water security, groundwater discipline, watershed protection, reuse, floodplain rules, and basin governance increasingly belong inside the main climate programme.

### 9. Agrifood emissions remain under-governed
FAO now estimates agrifood systems at **16.5 Gt CO2eq in 2023**, or about **32%** of anthropogenic emissions [S18]. This is a strategic warning: even a fast power transition leaves a large climate problem if livestock and rice methane, fertilizer and manure nitrous oxide, deforestation, peatland drainage, wetland degradation, and food waste remain weakly governed [S19][S36][S37][S38]. UNEP’s nitrous-oxide assessment sharpens the point by arguing that N2O is rising faster than expected and that over-40% cuts are possible with stronger action [S19].
Current official evidence now makes the nitrogen side more front-rank than the archive previously said at the front door. UNEP’s 2024 nitrous-oxide launch says N2O is responsible for about **10%** of net global warming since the industrial revolution, is the **top ozone-depleting substance** still being emitted, and that practical action could cut emissions by **more than 40%** from current levels [S216]. FAO’s 2025 nitrogen work adds that humans now add about **150 Tg** of reactive nitrogen to land each year — more than **double** the pre-industrial rate — and explicitly calls for sustainable nitrogen management to be integrated into nationally appropriate mitigation actions and NDCs [S217]. UNEP’s 2025 nitrogen-governance tools then remove another excuse for delay by offering methods for national nitrogen budgets, nitrogen-impact assessment, and a menu of sustainable nitrogen-management measures [S218]. The live implication is that agrifood climate strategy is not only about methane, forests, and diets; it is also increasingly about whether nitrogen becomes a governed programme rather than a diffuse loss field.

### 10. Natural sinks are valuable but not safely bankable
The Global Carbon Budget 2025 says land-use change still emits about **4.1 GtCO2** in 2025 and that climate change is already weakening land and ocean sinks; on its estimate, **8%** of the rise in atmospheric CO2 since 1960 is due to this weakening [S39]. The archive treats this as a doctrine shift: protect forests, peatlands, wetlands, and other carbon-rich ecosystems urgently, but do not build climate strategy on the assumption that biological sinks will remain stable regardless of further warming.

### 11. Removal ambition outruns real deployment
The archive already treated CDR as subordinate, but the implementation gap is worth stating plainly. State of CDR says around **2 GtCO2/yr** of removal is already occurring, almost all from conventional land-based methods, while **novel CDR contributes only about 1.3 MtCO2/yr** [S42]. IEA says DAC, BECCS, and broader CCUS infrastructure are all still off track for net-zero-consistent 2030 levels [S43][S44][S45][S46]. This does not mean removals are optional; it means climate plans that lean on large future removals without explicit near-term deployment, storage buildout, and sustainability rules are not yet operational plans.

### 12. Clean-energy supply chains are concentrating faster than many transition stories admit
The transition now faces a second-generation bottleneck: not whether clean technologies exist, but whether their materials and manufacturing chains are secure enough to scale without new chokepoints. Current IEA work says concentration in key mineral supply chains has been getting worse, not better: across major energy-transition minerals, the average market share of the top three producers rose to **86%** in 2024 from around **82%** in 2020, with almost all recent supply growth coming from the single top supplier for several major minerals [S244]. The same analysis says the current copper mine project pipeline points to a roughly **30%** supply deficit by **2035**, while **55%** of the strategic minerals it tracks are now subject to some form of export control and China is the leading refiner for **19 of 20** such minerals with an average market share of around **70%** [S244]. Recycling helps, but current systems remain too weak to carry the full burden: IEA says scaled-up recycling could cut new mining needs by **25–40% by 2050** and that without stronger recycling the mining investment needed through 2040 would be about **30%** higher; yet only **3** of **22** surveyed jurisdictions had a broad recycling framework [S245]. IEA and OECD also now make the governance corollary clearer: overlooking social and environmental risks can itself disrupt supply, so traceability and due diligence belong in the resilience packet rather than outside it [S246].

Electricity itself is now a bottlenecked success story. IEA’s 2026 power outlook expects global electricity demand to grow by **3.6%** annually over **2026-2030**, with renewables and nuclear together reaching around **half of global generation** by 2030 [S55]. The demand story also needs to be ranked correctly: IEA’s `Energy and AI` work says data centres account for around **one-tenth** of global electricity-demand growth to 2030, less than air conditioning, industrial motors, or EVs globally, even though their local grid effects can be much sharper [S194]. But the same report says more than **2 500 GW** of projects are stalled in connection queues worldwide, while grid investment would need to rise by roughly **50%** from today’s **USD 400 billion** annual level to meet forecast demand through 2030 [S55]. Integration is therefore becoming as decisive as generation. IEA’s solar-and-wind integration work warns that delaying flexibility, storage, grid, and demand-response measures could put up to **15%** of global solar and wind generation at risk in 2030 — around **2 000 TWh** — and shrink power-sector CO2 reductions by up to **20%** [S57]. Demand response remains especially underused: only around **100 GW** is currently utilised globally, even though residential air conditioning alone contributes around **600 GW** of peak demand [S56].

Another front-rank constraint now deserves explicit status: the grid-hardware layer itself. IEA’s 2025 transmission work says procurement now takes **2–3 years** for cables, up to **4 years** for large power transformers, and more than **5 years** for some direct-current cables, with cable prices nearly doubled since 2019 and transformer prices up around **75%** [S207]. World Energy Investment 2025 adds that grid materials have nearly doubled in price over the last five years because of rising demand for cables and transformers [S208]. And the same pressure is visible closer to end use: DOE says distribution transformers — building blocks of the grid — face long lead times and component shortages, while NREL estimates U.S. distribution-transformer capacity may need to rise by **160%–260%** by 2050 as electrification and aging-asset turnover compound [S209][S210][S211]. The live implication is that grid speed is no longer only about permits and interconnection rules; it is increasingly about whether hardware throughput is planned, standardised, and expanded in time.

### 13. Buildings are still under-governed relative to their emissions and lock-in power
UNEP’s latest buildings review says the sector consumes **32%** of global energy and contributes **34%** of global CO2 emissions [S28]. IEA’s 2025 efficiency work says buildings account for around **30%** of global energy demand and about **20%** of energy-demand growth since 2019 [S51]. Yet buildings still often appear in climate politics as a grab bag of appliance rebates rather than a coherent program of codes, retrofits, clean heat, passive design, and clean cooking where needed.

This is a structural mistake. In advanced economies, IEA says **80%** of the 2050 building stock is already built, so climate progress depends heavily on retrofit and heating-system turnover [S54]. In developing economies, **80%** of future floor-area growth to 2050 is expected, so bad codes and bad urban form can still lock in decades of waste [S54]. Heat pumps remain central but under-deployed: they still meet only around **10%** of global heating needs in buildings, and the stock would need to **almost triple by 2030** on a net-zero-consistent pathway [S52].

### 14. Cities remain under-specified relative to their climate weight
UNDP’s 2024 urban-NDC review says cities are responsible for about **67%** of global energy consumption and **70%** of greenhouse-gas emissions [S77]. Yet the same review says only **66%** of analysed NDCs contain moderate or strong urban content, and only **26** include specific urban-level finance requests [S77]. UN-Habitat’s World Cities Report sharpens the design point: urban areas host about **57%** of the global population on roughly **1.7%** of the world’s surface, and in many contexts urban emissions per capita are lower than national averages [S78].

The live bottleneck is therefore not “cities are important” rhetoric. It is whether countries will actually align housing, transport, cooling, water, municipal finance, and land use strongly enough that urban growth stops creating new emissions and exposure faster than other policies can reduce them [S77][S78][S79][S80].

Current OECD and IEA work now makes the spatial-form side sharper at the front door. OECD’s 2025 intermediary-cities work says compact urban forms, strong public transport, and safe non-motorised mobility reduce emissions and improve quality of life, while uncoordinated expansion often produces car dependence, congestion, and higher emissions [S241]. IEA’s 2025 transport and city-policy work adds that managing travel demand, distances travelled, and car dependency belongs inside the main decarbonisation package, while integrated, cross-sectoral city planning and targeted national-to-local funding can accelerate inclusive clean-energy transitions [S80][S243]. OECD’s 2025 zero-carbon-buildings report then widens the same point: embodied emissions could account for around half of the carbon footprint of new buildings by 2050 if left unaddressed [S242]. The live implication is that urban climate strategy is not only about cleaner vehicles and efficient buildings; it is also about whether settlement growth itself is shaped early enough to avoid locking in long trips, diffuse infrastructure, and unnecessary material overhead.

### 15. Delivery capacity is now a first-order bottleneck
The archive had already implied this, but the evidence now makes it too central to leave scattered. OECD’s 2025 green-transition framework argues countries need a broader transformation of institutional, policy, and regulatory arrangements if governments are to design, deliver, and execute climate policy at the required scale [S59]. In concrete energy-system terms, OECD’s 2025 permitting toolkit says local and regional authorities can decisively affect timelines, that fragmented spatial planning and inadequate consultation can generate vetoes or moratoria, and that staffing shortages in permit-granting authorities or grid operators are part of the current bottleneck [S60]. OECD’s broader regulatory work makes the same point in another register: the problem is not whether risk review should exist, but how to avoid turning legitimate review into permanent drag [S61].

Workforce constraints sharpen the same diagnosis. IEA says around **60%** of energy-sector companies reported labour shortages, already affecting timelines, reliability, and cost control [S62]. Yet the transition is also already a major jobs reality: IRENA and ILO estimate at least **16.6 million** renewable-energy jobs globally in 2024 [S63]. The live question, then, is not whether the workforce matters. It is whether countries will build the training, permitting, planning, and local-state capacity to convert climate ambition into installed infrastructure before backlash and backlog become the new dominant failure mode. IEA’s 2025 renewables outlook hints at the divergence already underway: solar is being accelerated by low costs, faster permitting, and broad social acceptance, while wind still faces supply-chain issues, rising costs, and permitting delays [S64]. OECD’s 2025 public-governance overview sharpens a related point: many governments now have some green-procurement frameworks, but very few yet measure their environmental impact rigorously, which suggests climate delivery tools are spreading faster than disciplined throughput systems [S198]. The archive therefore now treats institutional throughput — planning, permitting, procurement, staffing, and digital process quality — as a distinct tempo-setting layer rather than as generic implementation background [S197][S198].

Rev0243 sharpens the workforce version of this bottleneck. IEA's 2025 employment work says applied technical roles now account for over half of the energy workforce, that two out of every three new hires through 2035 will be needed just to replace retiring workers, and that new qualified entrants into energy would need to rise by around **40%** globally by 2030 to prevent the skills gap from widening [S176]. OECD's 2025 employment-and-skills review shows that serious workforce policy has to connect climate goals to vocational education, modular training, apprenticeships, firm support, regional targeting, social-partner consultation, and monitoring rather than relying on generic green-jobs optimism [S259]. ILO's 2025 diagnostic guidance adds that the same problem is sharper in places where informal work, sectoral vulnerability, climate risk, and region-specific labour markets shape what a just transition can actually employ [S260]. So the state-of-play conclusion is now stronger: climate speed depends not only on capital and technology, but on whether crews, public delivery offices, inspectors, operators, maintainers, and user-support channels are built before the queue calls for them.

### 16. Weak measurement, standards, and enforcement fragment the transition
The archive had already implied this, but the current evidence now makes it a distinct bottleneck. UNFCCC’s first synthesis of Biennial Transparency Reports says the first wave of BTRs already covers about **75%** of global GHG emissions in 2020, underscoring transparency as part of implementation rather than diplomacy alone [S65]. Yet IEA still estimates energy-related methane emissions at about **80% above** the total reported by countries to the UNFCCC [S11], and UNEP says its MARS system has issued over **3 500** alerts across **33** countries while nearly **90%** remain unanswered [S67]. In industry, IEA’s definitions work adds another form of fragmentation: without interoperable definitions for near-zero and low-emissions steel and cement, procurement, market formation, and investment signals remain noisy and easier to game [S68].

In other words: the transition is no longer failing only on whether countries have goals or even technologies. It is also failing on whether major climate claims can be measured, compared, and enforced with enough confidence to guide capital, procurement, and regulation.

### 17. Affordability, access, and social protection remain underbuilt relative to their political importance
The archive had already implied this, but the current evidence now makes it a distinct bottleneck. IEA’s 2026 affordability work says more than **120 million** households in advanced economies spend over **10%** of their income on residential heating, cooling, and appliances, and that many lower-income households remain stuck in inefficient homes they cannot afford to improve [S69]. In emerging and developing economies, access itself remains unfinished: the 2025 SDG7 progress report says **666 million** people still lacked electricity in 2023, while decentralized renewable-energy systems benefited over **561 million** people and supplied **55%** of new connections in Sub-Saharan Africa between 2020 and 2022 [S70]. The same political-economy problem appears in welfare systems: ILO says **3.8 billion** people remain entirely unprotected by social protection, even though stronger protection can support adaptation, cushion mitigation costs, and widen public support for climate policy [S71].

A transition that is technically correct but arrives as higher household exposure, higher hassle, or badly targeted bills will stall. The archive therefore now treats access, affordability, and social protection as implementation infrastructure rather than decorative fairness language.

One more front-door conclusion now deserves explicit status: at mass uptake scale, **affordability of clean alternatives, bill certainty, and reliability are not downstream politics but a tempo-setting layer of the program itself**. IEA’s current affordability work says the poorest 10% of households in advanced economies spend close to a quarter of disposable income on residential energy and transport fuels, that many current policies still miss the households most in need, and that by 2035 electricity rises to around half of consumer energy spending in rapid transitions while oil falls to around one-quarter [S199][S200]. OECD’s 2025 Paris-at-ten survey sharpens the political consequence: experts now rank public acceptance of climate policy and affordability of clean alternatives among the four main domestic implementation barriers, while its just-transition work says public support is itself essential to accelerating climate action [S201][S202].

A second front-door conclusion now deserves explicit status too: at system-replacement scale, **dirty-asset retirement, non-reinvestment, and fossil-support phase-down are not downstream cleanup but a tempo-setting layer of the program itself**. IEA’s 2025 investment and 2024 energy-review work together show the reason: clean-energy investment now clearly exceeds fossil investment, but world energy demand still rose in 2024, demand increased for all major fuels, and energy-sector CO2 emissions still increased even though rapid clean-tech deployment avoided around 2.6 Gt of additional annual CO2 emissions [S233][S234]. OECD adds that fossil-fuel support still amounted to about **USD 916.3 billion** in 2024 across more than **1 700** documented measures, and that this support often undermines transition incentives while disproportionately benefiting higher-income households [S235]. IEA’s net-zero pathway work sharpens the replacement implication: a 1.5°C-consistent trajectory leaves no room for routine approval of new long lead-time conventional oil and gas projects and no room for new unabated coal plants beyond those already under construction at the start of 2023 [S236]. The archive therefore now treats managed fossil decline not only as a deeper doctrine but also as a front-door speed question.

A third front-door conclusion now deserves explicit status too: at rising-load scale, **efficiency, retrofits, and passive demand reduction are not downstream housekeeping after supply planning but a tempo-setting layer of the program itself**. IEA’s 2025 efficiency work says global efficiency progress is improving but still off track for the COP28 goal, that without efficiency gains since 2010 greenhouse gas emissions today would be **20% higher**, and that if every air conditioner bought since 2019 had been the most efficient available, the world could have avoided electricity-demand growth equivalent to the demand growth from data centres over the same period [S237]. UNEP’s current buildings update adds that the sector still consumes **32%** of global energy and contributes **34%** of global CO2 emissions even after energy intensity fell by almost **10%** since 2015, while more than half of new floor space in emerging and developing economies remains outside building-energy-code coverage [S238]. IEA’s global energy review sharpens the present-tense system implication: electricity use in buildings rose by more than **600 TWh** in 2024, accounting for nearly **60%** of total electricity-demand growth, driven especially by cooling and data centres [S239]. The archive therefore now treats same-service demand reduction as part of grid speed, affordability, and capital realism rather than as a side virtue [S240].

### 18. Exposure discipline and retreat remain under-specified relative to compounding losses
The archive had already implied this, but the current evidence now makes it a distinct bottleneck. OECD’s 2026 catastrophe-finance work says that in **24 of 38** OECD countries, less than half of natural-hazard losses since 2000 were insured, that average annual wildfire losses in OECD countries were up **360%** in 2015–2024 relative to 2000–2014, and that only **32%** of economic flood losses in OECD countries were insured between 2000 and 2024 [S228]. Its assessment chapter adds that rising wildfire losses are already affecting the availability of affordable insurance in Australia, Canada, and the United States, with possible implications for housing markets because mortgage lenders often require coverage, and that long-run affordability is most sustainably improved by **risk reduction and adaptation** rather than by artificially suppressing premiums [S229]. UNDRR’s GAR 2025 sharpens the public-finance and planning implication: many countries are stuck in a response-repeat spiral, and recovery should build back better and prevent future losses instead of merely restoring the status quo; it also says human choices, including land-use planning, play a crucial role in shaping future vulnerability and exposure [S230]. OECD’s climate-resilient infrastructure work states the spatial corollary plainly: managed retreat will be necessary in some places because of the great risks and likely high costs of continuing to inhabit highly exposed locations, and it should be designed with affected communities rather than imposed after serial losses [S232]. The archive therefore now treats exposure discipline, repeat-loss rules, and dignified retreat from the unprotectable as a distinct climate-governance lane rather than as a narrow adaptation appendix.

### 19. Electricity-era scarcity makes allocation discipline a front-door issue
The archive already treated grids, flexibility, hardware, efficiency, cooling, and demand relief as tempo-setting layers. Rev0241 makes the allocation implication explicit: when scarce grid capacity, transformers, staff, finance, clean power, land, water, and political attention are all claimed at once, the order of allocation becomes climate policy.

IEA's 2026 electricity outlook says global electricity demand grew **3%** in 2025 and is forecast to grow at **3.6%** per year from 2026 to 2030. Annual demand growth over the next five years is expected to be **50% higher** than during the previous decade, and electricity use is projected to grow at least **2.5 times** as fast as overall energy demand through 2030 [S252]. The same report says more than **2 500 GW** of renewable, large-load, and storage projects are stalled in grid queues worldwide; annual grid investment must rise by around **50%** from about **USD 400 billion** today; and new grids often require **5–15 years** to plan, permit, and complete, while renewables, data centres, and EV-charging infrastructure can arrive much faster [S252].

Large new loads therefore need their own governance lane. IEA's 2026 data-centre analysis says data-centre electricity demand rose **17%** in 2025, data-centre consumption is set to **double by 2030**, and AI-focused data-centre power use is poised to **triple**; it also says the pipeline is already straining grid connections, approvals, transformers, gas turbines, chips, and other supply chains [S253].

The state-of-play conclusion is sharper than “build more grids.” It is: **govern scarce capacity.** New large loads, interconnection claims, public-finance claims, hardware claims, and permitting claims should be ranked by physical public value: dirty-default displacement, protection, flexibility, affordability, readiness, integrity, and replicability. Otherwise, scarce transition headroom can be consumed by the first, richest, loudest, or most speculative claimants while essential replacement and protection remain blocked.

### 20. The next execution bottleneck is the ready shelf, not only the headline queue
Rev0239–rev0241 sharpened the front door from doctrine to work queue, delivery packet, and scarce-capacity allocation. Rev0242 adds the next operational implication: high-value climate work has to be prepared before finance, grid capacity, recovery money, or political attention appears. Otherwise the easiest projects, not the most valuable ones, can absorb the window.

Current investment and finance evidence supports this shift. IEA says global energy investment is set to reach about **USD 3.3 trillion** in 2025, with about **USD 2.2 trillion** going to clean energy, grids, storage, efficiency, electrification, low-emissions fuels, and nuclear [S254]. But IEA also says clean-energy investment remains geographically uneven: Africa accounts for only about **2%** of clean-energy investment despite about **20%** of world population, and international public finance provides only around **7%** of EMDE clean-energy investment outside China [S254]. CPI says global climate finance reached about **USD 1.9 trillion** in 2023 and likely exceeded **USD 2 trillion** in 2024, while EMDEs need more catalytic capital such as guarantees, grants, and catalytic equity [S255]. CPI's EMDE spotlight says EMDE climate finance exceeded **USD 1 trillion** in 2023, with about **80%** mobilized domestically [S256].

The operating conclusion is that climate finance should be judged not only by volume, but by absorptive readiness: prepared pipelines, project-preparation capacity, standard procurement, local-currency and guarantee routes, offtaker and payment discipline, social safeguards, and measurement. World Bank Group's 2025 climate reporting now points toward more granular project and sub-project disclosure, while the WBG Guarantee Platform and MIGA's FY2025 guarantee activity show the usefulness of one-stop, standardized de-risking channels [S257][S258].

So the state-of-play conclusion is sharper: **a serious climate programme needs a ready shelf of prepared packets and procurement platforms, not only a larger promise of money.**

Rev0243 adds the completion implication: a ready packet is still incomplete if the crews, public offices, operating teams, maintenance budgets, spare parts, commissioning tests, warranty paths, and aftercare channels are missing. The archive now treats stable clean service — not first installation or first contract — as the real delivery gate for mass deployment.

### 21. Normal-service completion is not enough; stressed-service continuity is now a delivery test
Rev0243 made stable clean service the completion gate. Rev0244 adds the bad-day corollary: a clean or protective service that works only during normal conditions is not yet ready for a hotter, more electrified, more interdependent operating environment.

WMO's 2026 State of the Global Climate 2025 reports that 2015-2025 were the hottest 11 years on record, that Earth's energy imbalance reached the highest level in the 65-year record, and that extreme weather impacts millions and costs billions [S263]. UNDRR's GAR 2025 says direct disaster costs are already about USD 202 billion annually, while the wider bill is closer to USD 2.3 trillion per year when cascading and ecosystem costs are included [S40]. IEA's 2026 energy-system resilience work frames the operating requirement clearly: short-term resilience is the ability to cope with events exceeding standard planning conditions, including cyberattacks, physical attacks, extreme and severe weather, and unexpected infrastructure failures [S261]. WHO's 2025 Lancet Countdown summary adds the human-service consequence: heat-related mortality has risen 23% since the 1990s, and heat exposure caused about 640 billion potential labour hours lost in 2024 [S262].

The state-of-play conclusion is therefore sharper: **a serious climate programme needs stress-tested packets and critical-service continuity packages.** Hospitals, schools, water systems, shelters, telecoms, public housing, lifeline mobility, food and medicine cold chains, grid-control functions, and high-exposure communities should not depend on private improvisation or routine diesel backup. Their resilience packages should combine efficiency, passive survivability, clean distributed power where useful, storage, islanding or restoration priority, drills, staff, O&M, communications, public access, and narrow fossil-fallback rules. Otherwise the transition can look delivered on a normal day and fail exactly when people most need it.

### 22. Recovery and protection gaps now decide whether shocks become correction or repetition
Rev0244 made normal-day completion insufficient. Rev0245 adds the post-shock corollary: even a system that keeps some service alive during the event can fail if recovery money restores the same exposure, uninsured losses are pushed invisibly onto households and public budgets, or uninsurability becomes retreat by market abandonment.

Swiss Re's 2026 natural-catastrophe review says 2025 produced about **USD 220 billion** in total economic losses and **USD 107 billion** in insured losses across **190** events, with a record **49%** insured share but still a large residual protection gap [S264]. IAIS says natural-catastrophe protection gaps can have financial-stability implications and can disrupt agriculture, housing, infrastructure, employment, income, public finances, displacement, poverty, and inequality, especially where significant losses remain uninsured [S265]. Centre for Disaster Protection's 2025 pre-arranged-finance work adds the timing problem: international pre-arranged finance reached **USD 9.4 billion** in 2024 and payouts more than doubled from 2023, but low-income countries and fragile and conflict-affected situations still receive less than **7%** of total international pre-arranged finance [S266].

The state-of-play conclusion is therefore sharper: **recovery finance, residual-loss ledgers, public backstops, safer-rebuild gates, and rights-respecting relocation options now belong inside the main climate operating system.** Otherwise disasters become a recurring mechanism for rebuilding yesterday's vulnerability and shifting tomorrow's losses to the least able balance sheets.

### 23. Climate risk and transition cost now need balance-sheet and affordability firebreak discipline
Rev0245 named recovery and protection gaps as central climate-governance problems. Rev0246 adds the financial-transmission corollary: if physical risk, transition cost, insurance stress, grid investment, disaster loss, and recovery cost are not placed on explicit balance sheets, they do not vanish. They migrate into household debt, rent, insurance premiums, utility bills, municipal budgets, public backstops, bank collateral, and treasury emergencies.

OECD's real-estate risk work says local climate risks can become credit, market, underwriting, liquidity, and operational risks for real-estate and associated markets, affecting borrowers, lenders, owners, firms, insurers, and investors [S269]. FSB's 2025 roadmap makes the systemic version explicit: a physical shock to real estate can affect financial stability if insurance becomes less available and risk shifts to households, businesses, or governments [S271]. NGFS's short-term scenarios and the Basel Committee's voluntary disclosure framework both point to the need for forward-looking financial-risk analysis and more complete qualitative and quantitative climate-risk information [S270][S274]. The Coalition of Finance Ministers for Climate Action adds the public-finance lens: physical climate risks are macro-critical, can require escalating public expenditure, and can put development strategies and green-transition investments at risk [S272].

The affordability side is equally direct. IEA's electricity-price work says household electricity prices in many countries have risen faster than incomes and inflation since 2019, that network charges, taxes, and fees remain a large share of bills, and that electricity is often taxed more heavily than gas, weakening household electrification incentives [S273]. This means the climate programme needs not only affordability messaging, but explicit firebreaks across bills, premiums, rents, rates, taxes, fees, deductibles, debt, and time.

The state-of-play conclusion is therefore sharper: **risk transparency, balance-sheet ledgers, cost-recovery rules, and affordability firebreaks are now delivery infrastructure.** A programme that builds clean assets but hides who carries risk, who pays for resilience, who absorbs losses, and who is protected from cumulative burden will be brittle even if its engineering is right.

### 24. Claims integrity and anti-capture discipline now decide whether trust survives scale
Rev0246 made risk and cost visible. Rev0247 adds the trust corollary: a climate programme that moves trillions through claims, procurement, guarantees, credits, tariffs, permits, recovery funds, and public backstops has to prove not only that the engineering works, but that its claims and value channels are honest enough to survive scale.

OECD's 2025 consumer-policy work says concerns about greenwashing have increased as environmental claims on goods and services have grown, and addresses false, misleading, or unsubstantiated claims and enforcement responses [S275]. IFRS's 2025 transition-plan guidance says disclosure about transition plans is fragmented and costly, while ICVCM and VCMI show the same split in carbon markets: supply-side credit quality and demand-side claims quality both have to be governed [S276][S280][S281].

The delivery-integrity side is equally material. OECD's 2026 public-procurement integrity work says procurement is a core government function, about **13% of GDP** in OECD countries, and that corruption, fraud, conflict of interest, mismanagement, and collusion create direct and indirect costs including low-quality outputs, higher prices, and delays [S282]. OECD's 2025 green-procurement evidence then shows the climate-specific measurement gap: all responding OECD countries incorporate environmental objectives into procurement-specific policy documents, but only **3** of **29** countries with procurement KPIs report measuring environmental impact such as CO2 savings [S283].

The state-of-play conclusion is therefore sharper: **claims integrity and anti-capture controls are now delivery infrastructure.** A programme that cannot tell a real transition claim from greenwash, a real credit claim from offset theatre, a real green procurement outcome from a labelled tender, or a real delivery platform from a rent channel will lose trust, waste capacity, and make honest actors pay for dishonest ones.

### 25. Enforcement and remedy design now decide whether duties are real
Rev0247 made claims and value channels auditable. Rev0248 adds the missing consequence layer: if climate duties can be missed without cure, escalation, remedy, or redress, then measurement becomes paperwork and integrity becomes aspiration. The archive now treats remedy ladders as part of climate implementation. Grants, guarantees, procurement awards, permits, grid-access approvals, recovery funds, rate recovery, public backstops, clean-service contracts, transition plans, and climate claims should say what happens when performance fails or harm is caused.

The evidence base now points in this direction. The ICJ's 2025 climate advisory opinion strengthens the international-law frame around state obligations, cooperation, prevention of significant harm, and possible reparation where the required legal conditions are met [S285][S286]. UNEP and LSE show that climate litigation is no longer marginal: UNEP counted **3,099** climate-related cases across **55** national jurisdictions and **24** international or regional bodies by **30 June 2025**, while LSE counted at least **226** new climate cases in **2024**, with over **80%** strategic and **60** classified as not aligned with climate goals [S287][S288]. UNFCCC transparency and compliance channels also matter: BTRs are now a recurring evidence base, and the Paris Agreement Implementation and Compliance Committee has begun reminding Parties of NDC and BTR obligations while keeping systemic issues under review [S290][S291].

The state-of-play conclusion is therefore sharper: **accountability is now operating infrastructure.** A programme that has targets, dashboards, claims rules, and procurement controls but no remedy ladder will discover failure, describe failure, and then often continue failure. Serious climate governance makes correction ordinary, evasion costly, harm redressable, and repeated non-performance grounds for redesign, reprocurement, quarantine, or closure.

### 26. Data-spine and proof-passport design now decide whether scale remains governable
Rev0248 made duties enforceable. Rev0249 adds the record-architecture corollary: duties, claims, procurement rules, grid approvals, recovery payments, public backstops, affordability protections, and aftercare obligations cannot be corrected reliably if their facts live in unlinked systems.

The current evidence points in the same direction. UNFCCC's first-BTR public status page now publishes BTR1, NIR, CRT, CTF, technical expert review, and FMCP records as the Enhanced Transparency Framework moves from design into operating cycles [S293]. IFRS is also moving sustainability reporting into structured taxonomy form, with a proposed update expected in the second half of 2026 after the 2025 GHG-emissions disclosure amendments [S294]. At the asset and supply-chain layer, the European Commission describes the Digital Product Passport as a way to store and share product sustainability, durability, and environmental data with consumers, businesses, and public authorities, while UNECE / ISO frame DPP work as a digital language and trust architecture for traceability [S295][S296]. Open Contracting and OC4IDS show the same logic for public value: procurement and project data should be linked across the contracting and infrastructure life cycle, not published as isolated fragments [S297][S298]. WMO's Global Greenhouse Gas Watch adds the atmospheric side: satellites, ground stations, and models are being combined to provide transparent, up-to-date GHG information and reduce data gaps [S299]. OECD's digital-government work supplies the institutional side: whole-of-government data-sharing systems, open data, data-driven public sectors, and robust monitoring mechanisms are now governance capabilities, not optional IT upgrades [S300].

The state-of-play conclusion is therefore sharper: **climate truth now needs a usable data spine and proof-carrying assets.** A programme that can spend money, approve projects, make claims, inspect assets, and issue remedies faster than it can link the underlying records will fragment under scale. The answer is not to collect everything; it is to identify the climate-significant objects, keep minimum operating records, use common interfaces, protect people from misuse, and make evidence trigger payment, repair, correction, handoff, reuse, retirement, or remedy.

### 27. Cyber-resilient fallback and climate-AI accountability now decide whether the data spine is safe to scale
Rev0249 made climate records interoperable. Rev0250 adds the safety corollary: an interoperable climate operating system is also a larger cyber-physical and algorithmic dependency surface.

The evidence points in that direction. NIST's Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 organizes cybersecurity around **Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover**, and treats governance, roles, responsibilities, supply-chain risk, response, and recovery as concurrent risk-management functions [S301]. IEA makes the electricity-specific point plainly: full prevention of cyberattacks is not possible, so power systems need to be designed to withstand, adapt to, and rapidly recover from cyber incidents while preserving critical operations [S303]. DOE's electricity-subsector RMP similarly treats cyber risk as a component of enterprise risk for generation, transmission, distribution, marketing, and supporting vendors [S304]. NREL's 2025 DER material sharpens the transition-specific issue: the future grid faces rapidly growing numbers and diversity of connected devices, more customer-owned autonomous grid assets, less exclusive utility ownership of OT / IT systems, and uncertain hardware / software supply chains [S305].

The same logic applies to models and automation. NIST's AI RMF organizes AI risk management around **Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage**, while its generative-AI profile treats LLMs, cloud services, acquisition, and other cross-sector uses as activities needing lifecycle risk management [S306][S307]. OECD's AI Principles were updated in 2024 and continue to frame trustworthy AI around human rights, democratic values, risk management, and interoperability across jurisdictions [S308]. The EU AI Act and ISO/IEC 42001 show the same governance direction from different angles: risk-based obligations for high-risk AI uses and management-system discipline for responsible AI development and use [S309][S310].

The state-of-play conclusion is therefore sharper: **climate digitalization is not neutral plumbing.** Connected meters, DER controls, flexibility platforms, public support portals, proof passports, product records, risk maps, digital twins, AI triage tools, and automated eligibility systems can accelerate delivery only if they have owners, boundaries, fallback modes, cyber controls, privacy protections, contest routes, and retirement triggers. A programme that digitizes faster than it can secure, explain, recover, and override will convert implementation speed into a new source of fragility.

Rev0251 adds the cross-border supply-chain and market-access update. IEA's clean-technology trade analysis says around half of maritime clean-technology trade passes through the Strait of Malacca and that trade measures can materially change clean-technology costs; its critical-minerals work still shows high concentration in refining, processing, export controls, and copper risk [S244][S311]. OECD's 2026 export-restrictions update adds that restrictions on critical raw materials have reached an all-time high, with roughly 70% of global cobalt and manganese exports subject to at least one export restriction between 2022 and 2024 [S312]. UNCTAD's January 2026 trade update says nearly two-thirds of global trade takes place inside value chains being reshaped by geopolitics, industrial policy, and technology, while technical regulations affect roughly two-thirds of global trade [S313]. WCO's HS 2028 update and the World Bank / UNIDO standards evidence sharpen the same point from the operating side: customs classification, standards, conformity assessment, quality infrastructure, and market surveillance are now climate-deployment infrastructure, not bureaucratic scenery [S314][S315][S316][S317].

The state-of-play conclusion is therefore sharper again: **a climate programme that can finance, permit, digitize, secure, and automate packets can still stall if the traded inputs, standards, customs codes, certificates, test labs, ports, spares, and mutual-recognition routes needed for deployment are left outside the work queue.**

## 28. Fossil retirement now needs closure proof, not paper exit

Rev0253 made place visible on the clean-buildout side. Rev0254 makes place visible on the dirty-retirement side. A fossil asset is not retired just because it has lower output, loses dispatch, is sold, enters bankruptcy, or leaves a company's emissions boundary. Its wells, mines, ash ponds, contaminated soils, tailings, terminals, pipelines, methane pathways, workers, communities, and local public finances can remain active liabilities.

The evidence is concrete. IEA's 2026 methane tracker estimates nearly **150 Mt** of energy-sector methane in 2025, with inactive mines and abandoned wells contributing material emissions alongside active coal, oil, and gas operations [S341]. IEA also says roughly **70%** of fossil methane emissions can be abated with existing technology and that more than **35 Mt** could be avoided at no net cost under 2025 average energy prices [S342]. The World Bank's 2025 mine-closure standards add the post-closure point: many coal mining operations continue to bear public-health, safety, and environmental risks beyond their closure date, and simple physical closure is not the same as sustainable closure and post-closure risk management [S344].

The state-of-play conclusion is therefore sharper: **managed fossil decline is now a liability-governance problem as much as an emissions problem.** Serious retirement packets need asset-by-asset closure proof, financial assurance, anti-orphaning rules, methane measurement, remediation, worker and community terms, and post-closure monitoring.

## 29. Legacy fossil and contaminated sites can release siting pressure only if reuse repairs the place

Rev0254 also adds the reuse corollary. Former coal lands, well pads, landfills, refineries, terminals, ash sites, rights-of-way, and brownfields can reduce pressure to open new places for clean infrastructure. EPA's RE-Powering initiative, for example, encourages renewable-energy development on current and formerly contaminated lands, landfills, and mine sites when aligned with community vision [S348]. World Bank's Just Transition for All approach similarly treats governance, people and communities, and repurposing of former mining land and assets as joined transition pillars [S346].

But reuse is not absolution. A contaminated site should not be relabelled clean because a solar array, battery, factory, greenway, or warehouse is placed on top. A mature reuse packet needs a remediation-and-reuse passport: contamination and methane assessment, hydrology and climate-exposure checks, cleanup standard matched to future use, residual-risk disclosure, monitoring and maintenance funding, host-community benefit governance, anti-displacement safeguards, and a remedy owner if hidden harm appears.

The state-of-play conclusion is therefore sharper again: **brownfield-first is a useful siting strategy only when paired with repair-first governance.** Legacy places should be reused where that reduces total danger and conflict, not where it hides old harm under new climate branding.

## 30. Climate mobility is now a receiving-capacity and portability problem, not only a displacement statistic

Rev0255 adds the human-mobility corollary to exposure discipline. If some places cannot be defended indefinitely, the climate programme has to form the other side of retreat: where people can safely go, how receiving places expand services, and how records, benefits, claims, schools, health care, work pathways, and remedies travel with people.

The evidence is already large enough to justify front-door status. IDMC's 2026 GRID reports more than **82.2 million** people living in internal displacement at the end of 2025, including nearly **13.6 million** displaced by disasters, and **29.9 million** disaster displacement movements during 2025 [S351]. World Bank's Groundswell work projects that climate change could force about **216 million** people across six world regions to move within their own countries by 2050 without concrete climate and development action, but that early emissions cuts plus inclusive resilient development could reduce the scale by as much as **80%** [S352]. PDD's COP30 messages and the WIM Task Force on Displacement now place human mobility — migration, displacement, and planned relocation — inside climate loss-and-damage architecture rather than outside it [S354].

The state-of-play conclusion is therefore sharper: **do not call retreat dignified, relocation planned, migration adaptive, or recovery complete unless receiving capacity and portability are formed.** Climate mobility policy has to include stay-in-place support where viable, movement before collapse where necessary, destination housing / water / power / cooling / health / school / work capacity, host-community benefit rules, adaptive social protection, and portable records that prevent people from restarting their whole civic life after a climate move [S353][S356][S357][S358][S359][S360].

## 31. Food security and nutrition are now climate-critical service layers

Rev0256 adds the food-security corollary to mobility, recovery, and adaptation. Climate stress does not become social failure only when a house floods or a grid fails. It also becomes failure when heat, drought, flood, disaster loss, fertilizer shock, conflict access, food-price inflation, or broken logistics push households below a food and nutrition floor.

The evidence is large enough to require front-door treatment. The 2026 Global Report on Food Crises records very high acute food insecurity across food-crisis contexts, with conflict, weather extremes, economic shocks, malnutrition, displacement, and funding shortfalls interacting [S361]. SOFI 2025 ties high food-price inflation and unaffordable healthy diets to hunger and child malnutrition risk, and calls for coherent macroeconomic, trade, social-protection, data, and investment responses [S362]. WFP's 2026 hunger-crisis materials and FAO-WFP hunger-hotspot warnings show the operating problem: shocks are often visible before peak hunger, but warnings only help if finance, delivery systems, market tools, and protection channels can act before households deplete [S363][S364][S368][S369].

The state-of-play conclusion is therefore sharper: **food security is not a humanitarian afterthought to climate policy; it is a critical-service continuity test.** A mature adaptation, recovery, mobility, social-protection, agrifood, water, health, city-receiving, or climate-finance packet should protect food access and nutrition through the shock, and should govern food-price spikes, reserves, trade, fertilizer, logistics, and anticipatory action before hunger cascades [S365][S366][S367][S370].

Rev0257 sharpens water into a service-and-allocation rule. Current water evidence points in the same direction from different angles: unsafe or unavailable WASH still affects billions, the water cycle is becoming more erratic, water stress and groundwater depletion are no longer marginal risks, and water-safety planning now needs climate resilience and equity built into the operating method [S371][S372][S373][S374][S375][S379][S381].

## 32. Water continuity is now a WASH, allocation, and shock-absorber problem

Rev0257 splits the water front door into two operating packets. The first is household and institutional service continuity: safe drinking water, sanitation, hygiene, water quality, clinics, schools, shelters, care facilities, food preparation, emergency reception, and minimum service floors through heat, drought, flood, outage, contamination, displacement, and recovery.

The second is system shock absorption: drought allocation, groundwater discipline, basin / aquifer accounts, water-quality constraints, leakage and demand management, reuse and recharge, priority-use rules, transboundary dependencies, and after-action correction. This prevents scarcity from being discovered only when wells fail, utilities ration, food systems weaken, hydropower drops, wastewater overflows, or receiving places cannot support people.

The practical implication is that water plans should not stop at infrastructure lists or average supply. They should name exposed users, protected service floors, source and quality risk, allocation triggers, owners, finance, and recovery proof. See `277` and `278` [S371][S372][S373][S374][S375][S376][S378][S379][S380][S381].

## 33. Climate health is now a surveillance, care-continuity, and disease-ecology problem

Rev0258 adds a health operating layer. Climate-health risk is no longer adequately described by saying that mitigation has health co-benefits or that heat plans need advisories. The state of play now requires health systems that can integrate climate and disease surveillance, trigger early action, keep care floors running, protect health workers and exposed groups, and manage climate-sensitive disease ecology before outbreaks cascade.

Current evidence supports the upgrade. WHO's operational framework for climate-resilient and low-carbon health systems frames health systems as needing to anticipate, prevent, prepare for, and manage climate-related health risks while reducing emissions where feasible [S382]. The 2025 Lancet Countdown reports worsening climate-health impacts across most monitored impact indicators [S383]. WHO's climate-informed surveillance work explicitly links disease and weather surveillance to early warning for dengue, chikungunya, Zika, malaria, and cholera [S384]. Meanwhile WHO reporting on dengue, malaria, and cholera shows that vector-borne and waterborne risks are not marginal: dengue is spreading into new areas, malaria still caused hundreds of thousands of deaths in 2024, and cholera deaths rose sharply in the 2024 global reporting year [S387][S388][S389].

The state-of-play conclusion is therefore sharper: **a serious climate programme needs climate-health continuity and disease-ecology shock absorbers**. Water, food, mobility, recovery, housing, labour, schools, shelters, and health facilities should be stress-tested for whether surveillance, prevention, vaccines where relevant, vector control, WASH, labs, medicines, staffing, trusted communication, and after-action correction are ready before disease signals become crisis statistics. See `279` and `280`.

## Bottom line

The world is **not** mainly blocked by lack of technology.  
It is blocked by a mismatch:

- fast-moving climate physics
- medium-moving infrastructure systems
- slow-moving institutions
- and incumbents actively trying to slow them further

The archive’s answer is therefore institutional and infrastructural, not merely aspirational.

## Rev0253 addendum — place, rights, benefits, and grievance repair

Rev0253 adds a missing control to the delivery stack: **place is climate infrastructure**.
A packet can be financed, permitted, procured, staffed, commissioned, cyber-secured, standards-compliant, supply-chain mapped, circular, and recyclable, yet still fail if its land, water, seabed, corridor, biodiversity, cultural, cumulative-impact, host-community, Indigenous-rights, benefit-sharing, and grievance conditions are unformed.

The new minimum is two ledgers for footprint-bearing or place-intensive packets:

1. a **place-capacity ledger** covering alternatives, low-conflict siting, no-go screens, biodiversity and water filters, cumulative impacts, grid / access dependencies, climate exposure, restoration, decommissioning, and correction triggers; and
2. a **host-community and rights ledger** covering affected people, Indigenous Peoples and FPIC where applicable, independent support, enforceable benefits, grievance routes, remedy triggers, and promise durability across ownership changes.

The practical rule is not “slow everything down for process.”
It is “move siting, rights, benefits, and grievance design upstream so fast clean deployment is legitimate enough to survive.”
See `269` and `270` [S60][S64][S329][S330][S331][S332][S333][S334][S335][S337][S338][S339][S340].

## Rev0256 addendum — food security and nutrition continuity

Rev0256 adds a missing protection control: **food is climate-critical service infrastructure**.
A packet can reduce emissions, harden water systems, plan recovery, and build receiving capacity, yet still fail if households lose food access, nutrition care, school meals, safe water for food use, cold-chain continuity, producer livelihoods, or market access during the shock.

The new minimum is two ledgers:

1. a **food-security and nutrition-continuity ledger** covering exposed groups, food access, nutrition floor, production and market shocks, public-service dependencies, protection instruments, anticipatory triggers, owners, finance, and recovery proof; and
2. a **food-system shock-absorber ledger** covering price monitoring, reserves, trade, fertilizer and input exposure, logistics, cash / voucher / in-kind switching rules, local procurement, anti-hoarding controls, and after-action correction.

The practical rule is not “feed people later if the climate plan fails.”
It is “design the climate plan so heat, drought, flood, displacement, recovery, and price shocks do not become hunger, malnutrition, forced movement, or irreversible livelihood loss.”

## 34. Children, schools, childcare, and learning continuity are now climate-critical service layers

Rev0259 adds the child-development and education-continuity corollary to the food, water, health, disease, mobility, recovery, and critical-service layers. Climate shocks do not only damage infrastructure. They interrupt schooling, childcare, meals, WASH, safe routes, records, teacher availability, psychosocial support, and child protection.

The evidence is large enough for front-door treatment. UNICEF's 2024 school-disruption snapshot reports at least 242 million students in 85 countries or territories affected by climate-related hazards during 2024, and UNICEF's child climate-risk work places approximately one billion children in countries at extremely high climate and environmental risk [S394][S395]. World Bank's climate-and-education work argues that education systems need adaptation because lost learning from climate-related events can reduce future earnings and deepen inequality [S396]. School safety, education-in-emergencies, and physical-learning-environment frameworks show the operating response: safe facilities, climate-aware siting, WASH, cooling, continuity modes, temporary learning, inclusive access, teacher support, and accountable recovery [S397][S398][S399].

The state-of-play conclusion is sharper: **a serious climate programme must protect children's daily service spine.** A packet that protects food, water, health, mobility, recovery, shelters, and receiving places is still incomplete if children lose safe learning, school meals, childcare, documentation, psychosocial support, protection referrals, or youth voice. See `281` and `282` [S400][S401][S402][S403][S404][S405].

## 35. Ageing, disability, medical dependency, and care continuity are now climate-critical operating layers

Rev0260 adds the age, disability, long-term-care, home-care, and medical-dependency corollary to the children, health, WASH, food, mobility, recovery, and critical-service layers. Climate shocks do not only damage infrastructure or interrupt public services. They break functional dependencies: oxygen, dialysis, medication refrigeration, elevators, ramps, home care, personal assistance, assistive devices, interpreters, accessible transport, accessible shelters, cooling, clean air, and trusted communication.

The exposure is too large for a side paragraph. WHO projects the global population aged 60+ reaching 1.4 billion by 2030 and 2.1 billion by 2050, while WHO estimates that 1.3 billion people experience significant disability [S406][S407]. WHO and UNICEF report that more than 2.5 billion people need one or more assistive products, and UNDRR's disability-and-disasters survey shows that accessible preparedness, warnings, evacuation, and participation remain underformed [S408][S409].

The archive now treats care continuity as protection infrastructure. A heat, flood, smoke, outage, water, disease, shelter, mobility, or recovery packet is not formed if it cannot say how older adults, disabled people, medically dependent people, care-home residents, home-care users, personal assistants, unpaid caregivers, assistive devices, medicines, accessible communication, and adult-safeguarding routes survive the shock and recovery. See `283` and `284`.

## Rev0261 addendum — labour is a climate exposure pathway and continuity layer

Rev0261 makes explicit that climate risk travels through work as well as households, infrastructure, and ecosystems. Heat-exposed farmworkers, construction crews, warehouse workers, delivery workers, waste and sanitation workers, school staff, health and care workers, utility crews, port workers, informal workers, migrant workers, and disaster-recovery workers are not secondary to the climate programme. They are the people through whom food, care, water, schools, transport, recovery, and clean buildout either continue safely or fail.

The state of play therefore now includes a labour shock question: can workers stop, slow, reschedule, rest, get paid, report hazards, access care, and stay represented when heat, smoke, flood, disease, and outages change the work? If not, the programme is using labour as the hidden shock absorber rather than protecting it. See `285` and `286` [S418][S419][S420][S421][S424][S425][S427][S430].

## Rev0262 addendum — housing is now a climate-critical service platform

Rev0262 makes explicit that climate risk travels through the home as well as through infrastructure, ecosystems, services, and work. Heat, smoke, flood, mold, sewage, cold, outage, rent shock, insurance stress, repair delay, and homelessness are not private background conditions. They are routes by which climate hazards become illness, school loss, care failure, unsafe return, debt, eviction, and displacement.

The state of play therefore now includes a housing shock question: can households remain in or reach habitable, affordable, accessible, serviced, and legally secure housing or shelter when hazards arrive? If not, the programme is using households, tenants, informal residents, people without housing, and receiving communities as the hidden shock absorber. See `287` and `288` [S431][S432][S433][S434][S435][S436][S441][S443].

## Rev0263 addendum — household financial continuity is now a climate operating layer

Rev0263 makes explicit that climate risk travels through household balance sheets as well as through homes, jobs, services, and infrastructure. Heat, smoke, flood, drought, disease, outage, evacuation, and displacement become materially worse when wages stop, benefits fail, remittances are interrupted, accounts lock, IDs are lost, cash-out points close, utility arrears grow, debt collectors accelerate, insurance claims stall, repair finance becomes predatory, or scams capture emergency payments.

The state of play now includes a household-finance shock question: can people receive, hold, spend, transfer, defer, document, and contest money-related claims when hazards arrive? If not, the programme is using household depletion as the hidden shock absorber. Global Findex 2025 shows both the progress and the gap: account ownership and digital payments have expanded, but emergency-money access remains incomplete, and disaster exposure is widespread [S444]. ID4D shows that ID and digital-ID gaps remain large enough to make document flexibility a climate concern [S449]. Social-protection and climate-finance sources show that adaptive payment systems, registries, grievance routes, and financing are not optional add-ons but core social infrastructure under climate stress [S425][S456]. See `289` and `290`.

## Rev0264 addendum — civil legal capability is now a climate operating layer

Rev0264 makes explicit that climate risk travels through legal systems as well as through infrastructure, markets, households, and services. Heat, flood, wildfire, smoke, drought, storm, outage, disease, displacement, and recovery can become legal cascades when people miss notices, lose documents, fail proof tests, cannot appeal benefit denials, cannot stop eviction or utility shutoff, cannot contest debt collection, cannot enforce wage and safety rights, cannot resolve insurance or repair disputes, or cannot carry custody, school, health, immigration, land, or protective records across movement.

The state of play now includes a civil-justice-continuity question: can affected people understand, claim, contest, appeal, enforce, and carry rights during climate shocks? If not, the programme is using legal default as a hidden shock absorber. WJP estimates 5.1 billion people face at least one unmet justice need, while WJP's 2025 Rule of Law Index reports broad weakening in civil justice [S458][S459]. OECD's people-centred justice work makes legal empowerment, accessible pathways, administrative justice, data, and prevention part of usable rule of law [S460][S461][S462][S463]. See `291` and `292`.

## Rev0265 addendum — the information layer is now a binding adaptation layer

The 2026 state of play now treats warning reach, telecom continuity, public information, trusted communication, rumor management, local media, accessibility, and language access as climate operating conditions. A forecast does not protect anyone until it becomes a trusted, understandable, actionable message through channels that survive outage and reach the people least connected to official systems.

State-of-play implication: **warning systems should be judged by protected action and corrected misses, not by messages sent.** Use `293` and `294` [S108][S472][S473][S485].

## Rev0266 state-of-play addendum — transport access and logistics are the physical action layer
