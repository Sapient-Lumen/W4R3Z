# 228 — Ideal Solutions: At Mass Scale, Planning, Permitting, Procurement, and Workforce Throughput Set a Large Part of Climate Speed Compact Canon

## Claim

The archive already has:
- a delivery-capacity note (`18`)
- a capability-abundance note (`54`)
- a routinization note (`55`)
- a bottleneck-release note (`132`)
- an electricity-tempo note (`226`)
- and a finance-tempo note (`227`)

That is already enough to say that delivery institutions matter.
What it still lacked was one smallest front-door note for a now-central question:

**once technology exists and money is at least partly available, what often sets the next speed limit on real climate buildout?**

This note is not a new doctrinal branch.
It is a compression note.
Its job is to make one practical truth harder to miss at the front door:

**at mass scale, institutional throughput is climate speed too.**

## Fast rule

**A serious climate programme should treat planning, permitting, procurement, inspections, queue handling, staffing, digital process quality, and workforce pipelines as first-order climate infrastructure. Once cleaner technologies are mature and finance is at least partly mobilised, programme speed is set less by abstract ambition and more by whether public and quasi-public institutions can process, approve, contract, connect, inspect, train, and repeat at the required tempo** [S59][S60][S61][S176][S197][S198].

## The compact canon

### 1. Mature technology does not self-deliver
OECD’s 2025 green-transition framework says governments will be critical to achieving durable emissions reductions and resilience, and that success depends on public institutions with credible commitment, real capabilities, and broad consensus rather than policy declarations alone [S59].

House rule: **once the solution is known, administrative machinery becomes part of the solution.**

### 2. Permitting quality is part of climate speed
The serious question is not whether review should exist.
It is whether review is organised to surface real risks early and resolve them on a workable timetable.
OECD’s regulatory work says the challenge is to avoid turning legitimate review into permanent drag [S61].
IEA’s 2025 ARPE work says the live barriers include lengthy and complex procedures, lack of coordination among agencies, insufficient administrative capacity and digitalisation, lack of maximum processing deadlines, weak coordination between grid and renewable-energy spatial planning, and insufficient engagement with local communities [S197].

House rule: **prefer faster, earlier, more predictable review over slower project-by-project chaos or crude review-free shortcuts.**

### 3. The delivery state is a physical asset, not overhead
The archive already says to build grids, storage, efficient buildings, and cleaner transport.
This note adds the operating corollary: the offices, authorities, utilities, inspectors, and public-facing systems that permit, contract, connect, and verify those assets are part of the buildout itself.
OECD’s 2025 governance work says enhanced institutional capabilities are a core requirement of the green transition [S59][S198].

House rule: **treat administrative throughput the way the archive treats grid throughput: as enabling infrastructure, not mere overhead.**

### 4. Workforce shortages are not a side constraint
IEA’s 2025 employment work says more than half of surveyed firms reported critical hiring bottlenecks, around **60%** reported labour shortages affecting timelines, reliability, and cost control, and acute shortages remain concentrated in applied technical roles [S176].
The same report says current AI uses mostly improve administrative efficiency, safety, or training but do little to remove demand for applied technical workers in construction, operations, and maintenance [S176].

House rule: **do not assume software, rhetoric, or capital commitments will substitute for electricians, line workers, installers, inspectors, operators, and maintenance staff.**

### 5. Procurement and measurement are throughput levers too
OECD’s 2025 public-governance overview says **35 of 38** OECD countries have green public procurement frameworks, but only **3** have developed methodologies to measure procurement’s environmental impact, while governments are urged to simplify regulation, invest in skills, and optimise procurement systems to improve efficiency and productivity [S198].
That means many states now possess some green tools, but still underbuild the disciplined measurement and contracting routines that let climate delivery spread without reopening design from zero every time.

House rule: **do not treat public procurement as a passive wallet; treat it as a repeatable delivery engine that needs measurement, templates, and speed discipline.**

### 6. What this note demotes
This note demotes six recurring mistakes:
- treating climate delay as mainly a technology-cost story after the technology is already mature [S59][S197]
- treating faster delivery as synonymous with weaker review [S61][S197]
- assuming agencies can absorb a mass buildout without staffing, digital process reform, or clearer deadlines [S59][S197][S198]
- assuming workforce shortages will solve themselves once projects are announced [S176]
- assuming AI will soon dissolve the skilled-trades bottleneck [S176]
- treating procurement and permitting as clerical afterthoughts instead of tempo-setting institutions [S59][S197][S198]

## Preferred use

Open this note first when the live question is any of the following:
- what now sets climate speed after the technology debate is mostly over
- why planning, permitting, procurement, and staffing keep appearing as climate bottlenecks rather than back-office details
- whether faster delivery means weaker review
- why announced capital and mature technology still fail to become installed clean infrastructure quickly enough
- what the archive means by a “delivery state” in the climate context

Then reopen `18`, `54`, `55`, `132`, `226`, or `227` if the surviving question is mainly about full delivery-capacity doctrine, labour design, routine cadence, broader bottleneck diagnosis, electricity tempo, or world-scale finance tempo.

## Compression rule

**At mass scale, institutional throughput is climate speed too. Treat planning, permitting, procurement, inspections, queue handling, and workforce pipelines as first-order climate infrastructure; demote stories that count technology maturity or capital availability as delivery by themselves.**
