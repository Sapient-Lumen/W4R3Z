# 10 — Industrial Materials and Circularity

## Claim

Heavy-industry decarbonization is not only a **factory-technology problem**. It is also a **materials-demand, design, procurement, and reuse problem**.

That matters because the sectors most often treated as “hard to abate” are also the sectors whose output we can sometimes avoid, use more efficiently, substitute, reuse, or specify differently. Steel and cement alone account for **14% of global energy- and process-related emissions on a direct basis** [S26]. Industry more broadly emits about **9 Gt CO2**, roughly **one quarter** of global energy-system CO2 emissions [S29]. And the buildings-and-construction system remains deeply dependent on these materials; UNEP says cement and steel are responsible for about **18% of global emissions** [S28].

So the archive’s house view is:

**The ideal industrial solution is a four-lane stack: use less material where possible, use material better where needed, create early markets for near-zero materials, and reserve CCS / hydrogen / other costly supply-side tools for the residual mass that cannot be designed away.**

## Why this lane is strategically important

### 1. It cuts demand for the hardest tonnes

Some industrial emissions are hard because they arise from high-temperature heat, process chemistry, long-lived capital stock, and thin-margin globally traded commodity markets. But some are “hard” only because demand is treated as fixed.

Material efficiency, reuse, refurbishment, higher utilization, lightweighting, better design, and more intensive recycling all reduce the volume of primary material that has to be produced in the first place [S29]. This is one reason the archive should treat circularity as climate strategy, not as a separate waste conversation.

### 2. It converts buildings policy into industrial climate policy

A large share of steel and cement demand is pulled by construction. That means procurement rules, building codes, structural-design norms, refurbishment policy, adaptive reuse, and embodied-carbon disclosure can move industrial emissions even when no “industry policy” is announced [S28].

The archive therefore treats **construction demand formation** as part of industrial decarbonization.

### 3. It solves a real commercialization problem

IEA’s 2025 Climate Club work says announced near-zero emissions capacity for steel and cement by 2030 is only about **10%** of what is needed on a net-zero-compatible path, while early production can cost **10% to 125%** more than conventional material depending on technology and region [S26].

So the problem is not just invention. It is market formation under cost and risk.

## Doctrine

### A. Avoid unnecessary material demand

Use less new concrete, steel, and virgin material per unit of service.

Examples:
- refurbish before demolishing
- reuse structures and components where safe and practical
- design for material efficiency rather than routine over-specification
- reduce construction waste
- extend building lifetimes where resilience permits

This is often cheaper and faster than waiting for perfect near-zero primary production.

### B. Direct scarce low-emissions materials to the right uses

Early near-zero steel and cement will be limited and expensive. Use them first where demand is difficult to avoid and public value is high: strategic infrastructure, public works, dense urban construction, and demonstrator projects that can create replicable supply chains.

The archive rejects the idea that scarce early green materials should be sprayed uniformly across all uses just to maximize branding.

### C. Create demand with procurement, standards, and common definitions

Near-zero materials markets will not emerge at adequate speed through goodwill alone. They need:
- public procurement with embodied-carbon rules
- advance purchase commitments and offtake agreements
- common definitions and measurement methods
- disclosure rules and product-level accounting
- border and trade arrangements that do not punish first movers indiscriminately

IEA’s 2024 definitions work argues that common, globally interoperable definitions for near-zero and low-emissions steel and cement are a crucial enabler because they provide a common language for demand-side policies and reduce reporting chaos [S27].

### D. Use supply-side technology selectively but seriously

After demand reduction and better material use, the remaining industrial core still needs deep supply-side change:
- electrification where technically feasible
- hydrogen in selected routes, especially some primary steel pathways
- clinker reduction and alternative binders where performance and availability allow
- CCUS where process emissions remain structurally difficult to eliminate, especially in cement and some chemical routes
- industrial clusters and shared infrastructure where this lowers first-mover risk

The archive neither fetishizes nor dismisses these tools. It ranks them after demand reduction, design improvement, and market formation because those earlier moves often make the expensive residual problem smaller.

## Practical program moves

1. Put embodied-carbon limits into public procurement for buildings and infrastructure.
2. Prefer refurbishment and adaptive reuse over demolition where safety and function allow.
3. Require material disclosure and standardized definitions for low-emissions and near-zero steel/cement [S27].
4. Use public works programs to create bankable early demand for near-zero materials [S26].
5. Build recycling, scrap sorting, and component-reuse systems as industrial policy, not only waste policy.
6. Support industrial hubs, shared CO2 transport/storage, and clean-hydrogen infrastructure only where there is a credible residual need.

## Compression

**Do not try to decarbonize every tonne of steel and cement only at the smokestack. First ask which tonnes can be avoided, reduced, reused, redesigned, or procured differently; then throw the expensive tools at the remainder.**
