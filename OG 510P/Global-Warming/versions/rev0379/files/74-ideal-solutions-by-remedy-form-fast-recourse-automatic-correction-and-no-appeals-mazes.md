# 74 — Ideal Solutions by Remedy Form: Fast Recourse, Automatic Correction, and No Appeals Mazes

## Claim

The archive already says ideal climate solutions should be service-first, low-friction, honest about contracts and verification, and designed so ordinary users do not carry hidden performance, paperwork, or coordination risk alone [S118][S119][S124][S131][S137][S144].

One more compact rule belongs in the canon:

**ideal climate solutions should usually make failures easy to flag, easy to check, and easy to correct.**

In plain terms:
- put a clear help, complaint, and escalation path close to the point of service rather than hiding it behind agency boundaries or contractor chains [S118][S131][S137]
- use existing records, meters, schedules, and programme data to correct obvious mistakes automatically where possible instead of making the user rebuild the whole case from scratch [S118][S119][S124]
- keep the burden of explanation, rework, and remedy mainly with the provider, programme operator, utility, landlord, contractor, lender, or administrator that controls the system or holds the relevant data [S131][S137][S144]
- reserve long appeals, repeated document requests, and adversarial escalation for unusual contested cases rather than making them the default operating model [S118][S119][S130][S131]

So the archive’s shortest remedy-form rule is:

**make redress simple, fast, and biased toward correction; do not make households, renters, small firms, schools, clinics, or municipalities fight through appeals mazes just to receive the clean service, payment, or support they were already supposed to get.**

## Why this belongs in the canon

### 1. Good climate design still fails in ordinary operation

A climate programme can have the right technology, decent economics, strong targeting, simple eligibility, and workable verification — and still fail in lived practice.

The failure may show up as:
- the wrong tariff,
- the wrong bill,
- the missed installation visit,
- the uncorrected commissioning error,
- the landlord refusal,
- the broken complaint route,
- or the denied payment that nobody can quickly reverse [S118][S119][S130][S131][S137][S144].

That means some climate underperformance is not a technology problem or even a finance problem.
It is a **remedy-design problem**.

If the system has no clear route for fixing predictable mistakes, then the cleaner option becomes fragile in the place where users actually experience it: after the promise is made.

### 2. Light front-end design only works if the back-end can correct mistakes quickly

The archive has recently added compact rules for:
- payment form,
- contract form,
- eligibility form,
- and verification form [S118][S119][S124][S137][S143][S144].

Those rules all push in the same direction: fewer forms, less bespoke diligence, more standardisation, more routine delivery, and fewer avoidable burdens on the user.

That is correct.
But it only stays fair if the system is also built to correct the inevitable misses.

A good climate system should not front-load every possible dispute into the entry process.
It should simplify the front door **and** make the correction path strong.

So the archive needs a compact rule against a familiar failure mode:
**using ex ante paperwork to compensate for weak ex post remedy.**

### 3. Remedy should follow control and data

The actor that controls the meter, installation file, scheduling system, asset register, tariff code, contractor network, or payment ledger is usually the actor that can resolve the problem fastest [S118][S124][S131][S144].

So the archive should say plainly:
- if a provider claims performance, it should help prove and fix underperformance [S144]
- if a programme operator already has the records, it should not force the user to resubmit them [S118][S119]
- if a public or regulated service uses procurement and delegated delivery, post-contract transparency and complaint routes should remain real rather than nominal [S130][S131]
- if standards and assurance systems detect non-conforming products or delivery failures, the burden should shift toward correction by the responsible actor, not endless user chase-work [S124]

This is just the archive’s broader actor-allocation doctrine applied to failure handling.

For the archive’s companion note on **how the system should usually avoid losing the clean or safe state at ordinary handoffs in the first place**, see `75-ideal-solutions-by-continuity-form-no-reset-to-dirty-defaults-at-handoffs-moves-and-rebuilds.md`.

### 4. Strong remedy improves legitimacy and political durability

People are more willing to trust large transitions when they believe that errors will be corrected and ordinary users will not be stranded inside opaque systems.

A programme that says “the support exists” but makes redress impossible invites distrust even if the top-line policy is sound.
A provider that promises savings but makes every dispute slow and burdensome undermines confidence in the whole delivery model.
A public procurement system that has no practical complaint path or weak post-contract visibility can lose legitimacy even when the initial tender looked proper [S130][S131].

So remedy is not a small customer-service issue.
It is part of the archive’s doctrine of legitimacy, durability, and real-world uptake [S118][S130][S131][S137][S144].

## What this rule promotes

Promote:
- climate programmes with one obvious route for help, correction, and escalation [S118][S131]
- automatic correction of routine errors when the system already has the needed data [S118][S119][S124]
- provider-side obligations to fix non-performance, billing mistakes, missed service, or failed delivery rather than merely acknowledge them [S131][S144]
- procurement and delegated-delivery systems with real complaint channels, post-contract visibility, and trackable corrective action [S130][S131]
- quality-assurance and market-surveillance systems that close the loop from defect detection to remedy rather than stopping at diagnosis [S124]

## What this rule demotes

Demote:
- climate programmes that treat delay, uncertainty, or repeated form requests as an unofficial rationing device [S118][S137]
- contract structures that mention support, savings, or service but make redress practically unreachable when those promises fail [S131][S144]
- eligibility or payment systems that can deny or stall ordinary users without any fast correction path [S118][S119][S137]
- public or regulated delivery systems with weak post-contract transparency, nominal complaint channels, or unclear responsibility for fixing visible failures [S130][S131]
- climate products or services that push diagnosis, proof assembly, and chase-work back onto the user even though the provider or administrator holds the relevant data [S118][S124][S144]

## One-paragraph compression

**Ideal climate solutions should usually make errors and underperformance easy to fix. Put clear recourse close to the service, use existing records to auto-correct obvious mistakes where possible, and keep the burden of explanation and remedy with the actor that controls the system or holds the data. Do not make households, renters, schools, clinics, small firms, or municipalities fight through appeals mazes just to receive the cleaner service, payment, or support that policy already promised them [S118][S119][S124][S130][S131][S137][S144].**
