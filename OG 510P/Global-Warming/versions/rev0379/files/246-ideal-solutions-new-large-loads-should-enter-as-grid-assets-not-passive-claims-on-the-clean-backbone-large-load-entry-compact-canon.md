# 246 — Ideal Solutions: New Large Loads Should Enter as Grid Assets, Not Passive Claims on the Clean Backbone — Large-Load Entry Compact Canon

## Claim

The age of electricity is necessary for climate progress.
But not every new electric load is automatically climate progress.
A heat pump replacing gas heat, a bus depot replacing diesel, a clean-cooking programme replacing biomass, a port electrifying dirty equipment, a factory cutting fuel combustion, a water system becoming resilient, and a hospital protecting patients during heat all make strong claims on the clean backbone.
A speculative, inflexible, poorly located, high-water, high-peak, certificate-only, or fossil-backed large load makes a weaker claim, even if it is electric.

The distinction now matters because electricity demand is rising faster than many grid systems can honestly absorb. IEA's 2026 electricity outlook says global electricity demand is forecast to grow at an average annual rate of 3.6% from 2026 to 2030, with electricity use growing at least 2.5 times as fast as total energy demand; it also says over 2 500 GW of renewable, large-load, and storage projects are stalled in grid queues worldwide and that grid buildout often takes far longer than large new loads can appear [S252]. IEA's 2026 data-centre analysis says data-centre electricity demand rose 17% in 2025, data-centre consumption is set to double by 2030, and AI-focused data-centre power use is poised to triple, while the project pipeline is already straining grid connections, approvals, transformers, gas turbines, chips, and other supply chains [S253].

So large loads need entry discipline.
The question is not whether a data centre, electrolyser, industrial plant, cooling district, EV depot, port, mine, factory, or other large load is allowed to exist.
The question is whether it enters in a way that strengthens the clean system rather than consuming the transition headroom needed to replace combustion and protect people.

## Fast rule

**A new large electric load should not receive scarce clean-backbone capacity as a passive entitlement. It should pass a load-entry test: efficient by design, transparent about demand, located where the grid can serve it cleanly, paired with additional clean supply and storage where needed, flexible when technically possible, paying its fair grid costs, disclosing water and heat impacts, avoiding routine fossil backup lock-in, and proving public value or displacement when it claims priority** [S226][S231][S235][S239][S240][S252][S253].

## Why this note is not anti-electrification

Electrification is one of the main routes to decarbonization.
But the programme must distinguish four different load types:

1. **Replacement load:** new electricity that directly displaces fossil combustion or dirty service.
2. **Protection load:** new electricity that keeps people safe under heat, outage, water, health, or disaster stress.
3. **Enabling load:** new electricity that builds or operates the clean system itself.
4. **Additive load:** new electricity that mainly adds demand without clear displacement, protection, or enabling value.

The first three can be high priority.
The fourth needs stricter tests when the system is constrained.

House rule: **do not confuse “electric” with “climate-priority.”**

## The large-load entry test

### 1. State the service and public value
A large load should say what social or economic service it provides and why that service deserves scarce capacity now.
Priority rises when the load replaces combustion, protects essential service, supports public infrastructure, or enables clean supply chains.
Priority falls when the load is speculative, duplicative, luxury-heavy, avoidably wasteful, or unwilling to adapt to grid conditions.

House rule: **the first question is not megawatts; it is purpose.**

### 2. Prove efficiency before asking for capacity
A large load should meet strong performance standards before it consumes scarce grid headroom.
For data centres this means server utilization, cooling efficiency, heat management, right-sized redundancy, and avoidance of idle or zombie compute.
For electrolysers it means siting and operating where clean electricity is genuinely additional or otherwise curtailed.
For industrial electrification it means process efficiency and heat recovery before simply buying more power.
For cooling districts it means passive reduction, high-efficiency equipment, thermal storage, and refrigerant discipline.

House rule: **do not build grid capacity to serve avoidable waste.**

### 3. Use locational discipline
Where a load connects matters.
A large load near congestion, water stress, weak distribution infrastructure, exposed communities, or fossil-heavy marginal supply has different climate meaning than one located near clean surplus, storage, industrial reuse, resilient water, and available grid capacity [S236][S252][S253].

Locational rules should consider:
- available clean generation and transmission
- distribution constraints
- curtailment patterns
- heat and water stress
- existing local pollution and vulnerability
- disaster resilience
- proximity to useful waste-heat demand
- ability to support, not weaken, local reliability

House rule: **do not make the whole system solve a siting choice that the claimant could improve.**

### 4. Require additional clean supply where the load is materially additive
A large load that materially increases electricity demand should not hide behind annual certificates while causing fossil generation or delayed retirement at the margin.
Where feasible, it should support additional clean capacity, storage, demand response, firm clean resources, or grid upgrades that match its operational footprint closely enough to reduce real system emissions and constraints.

House rule: **annual accounting cannot substitute for physical system contribution when the grid is constrained.**

### 5. Make flexibility the default expectation
Many large loads can shift, pause, store, pre-cool, pre-heat, batch, schedule, or curtail some portion of demand.
A serious load-entry rule should ask which portion is truly inflexible and which portion can serve the grid during peaks, congestion, low-renewable periods, or emergency events.

Flexibility can come from:
- battery storage
- thermal storage
- workload shifting
- interruptible tariffs
- demand-response contracts
- non-critical process scheduling
- smart charging
- storage-backed peak shaving

House rule: **a large load that can flex but refuses to flex should not be treated like a hospital.**

### 6. Pay for the grid stress it creates
A large load can trigger upstream network upgrades, local distribution reinforcement, balancing costs, reliability needs, and administrative work.
Those costs should not be silently socialized onto ordinary users, especially lower-income households, unless there is a clear public reason [S212][S217][S229][S252][S253].

The fair package may include:
- connection cost contribution
- network upgrade contribution
- interruptible rates
- congestion charges
- local resilience contribution
- affordability guardrails
- community-benefit terms where impacts are concentrated

House rule: **do not let private load growth become public bill shock.**

### 7. Disclose water, heat, land, and local resilience impacts
Large electric loads can also be water loads, heat loads, land loads, and emergency-service loads.
Data centres, cooling systems, hydrogen production, mining, industrial electrification, and new manufacturing clusters should disclose and manage these impacts, especially in water-stressed or heat-vulnerable regions [S236][S253].

House rule: **a load is not clean if it exports its stress to water, heat, housing, or local resilience systems.**

### 8. Keep fossil backup narrow, temporary, and declining
Behind-the-meter gas, diesel backup, or new gas capacity may be proposed as a way to connect faster.
This should not become a backdoor fossil buildout.
Any fossil fallback should be emergency-bounded, measured, time-limited, subject to air-quality and methane rules, paired with storage and clean firming plans, and retired or demoted as cleaner reliability becomes available [S238][S243][S252][S253].

House rule: **large-load urgency is not a license for permanent dirty reliability.**

### 9. Publish enough information for public trust
Where large loads use scarce public capacity, public authorities should publish enough to inspect the claim:
- requested capacity
- forecast load shape
- connection timing
- clean-supply contribution
- flexibility commitment
- grid-upgrade contribution
- water and heat implications
- fossil-backup limits
- local benefits and burdens
- effects on bills, reliability, and emissions

House rule: **a hidden large-load deal is usually bad climate governance.**

### 10. Make approval conditional and correctable
Large-load approvals should include operating conditions, reporting, correction triggers, and consequences for underperformance.
If a load misses flexibility commitments, causes fossil dispatch beyond permitted limits, worsens local reliability, withholds data, or fails to fund promised upgrades, the approval should be corrected, repriced, curtailed, or demoted in the queue.

House rule: **connection is not the end of governance; it is the beginning of performance duty.**

## Large-load entry ledger in one view

| Test | Question | Good answer |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | What service justifies scarce capacity? | replacement, protection, enabling, or strong public value |
| Efficiency | Is avoidable load removed first? | best-practice design, audited performance, no zombie demand |
| Location | Does siting reduce system stress? | near clean capacity, storage, reuse, and resilient water |
| Additionality | Does it add clean system capability? | new clean supply, storage, flexibility, or grid upgrade contribution |
| Flexibility | Can demand move or respond? | contracted flex, storage, interruptibility, peak shaving |
| Cost | Who pays for grid stress? | fair connection and upgrade terms, affordability guardrails |
| Local impact | What happens to water, heat, land, and resilience? | disclosed, bounded, mitigated, and community-aware |
| Fossil fallback | Is dirty backup temporary and narrow? | emergency-only, measured, declining, and replaced by cleaner firming |
| Transparency | Can the public inspect the deal? | enough data to verify emissions, bills, reliability, and local burden |
| Correction | What if promises fail? | repricing, curtailment, redesign, demotion, or enforcement |

## What this note demotes

This note demotes:
- “electric equals clean” shortcuts
- annual clean-power certificates as a substitute for operational contribution
- speculative queue hoarding by large loads
- behind-the-meter fossil buildout disguised as speed
- socializing private grid costs onto ordinary users
- water-blind or heat-blind siting
- inflexible operation where flexibility is technically available
- opaque data-centre, hydrogen, industrial, mining, cooling, or charging deals
- large-load approval with no correction trigger

## Compression rule

**A large new load should enter the clean backbone only as a formed grid citizen: efficient, located well, transparent, flexible where possible, paired with real clean-system contribution, paying fair grid costs, bounded on water and fossil fallback, and correctable if it underperforms.**
