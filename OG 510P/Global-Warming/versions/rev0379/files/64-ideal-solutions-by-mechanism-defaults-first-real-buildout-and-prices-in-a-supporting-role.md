# 64 — Ideal Solutions by Mechanism: Defaults First, Real Buildout, and Prices in a Supporting Role

## Claim

The archive already says that ideal climate solutions should be physical-first, low-friction, package-based, actor-aware, and fiscally durable.
It says they should move through concentrated control points, work under real delivery constraints, and avoid offloading system repair onto heroic consumers [S112][S118][S119][S137][S143].

One more compact rule belongs in the canon:

**ideal climate solutions should usually be delivered by changing defaults and building substitutes first, then using prices, disclosure, and incentives to reinforce the shift rather than asking them to carry the whole transition alone.**

That means the archive should usually prefer a mechanism mix in this rough order:
- **build and enable** the cleaner system,
- **change defaults** through standards, codes, and procurement,
- **remove adoption barriers** with targeted finance and simple delivery,
- then **use prices, subsidy reform, and disclosure** to align and tighten the system [S53][S59][S68][S80][S91][S96][S112][S118][S119][S137][S138][S139][S143][S146].

So the archive’s shortest mechanism rule is:

**substitute first, default second, support third, price fourth — with all four designed as a package, not a sequence of excuses.**

## Why this belongs in the canon

### 1. The climate problem is not a single-price problem

Many climate barriers are not solved by a price signal alone.
People face split incentives, poor information, contractor scarcity, high upfront cost, weak grid capacity, bad product availability, administrative complexity, and the simple fact that many high-emissions systems are inherited capital stock rather than day-to-day choices [S53][S59][S112][S118][S119][S137].

OECD’s 2025 sequencing work says policy instruments should be bundled because they address different failures, and that public investment in infrastructure and innovation can make regulatory and economic instruments more effective by ensuring low-carbon options are available, affordable, and accessible [S112].

That is enough to reject a thin doctrine in which the state mainly sets a price and then waits.
Ideal solutions need a **mechanism portfolio**, not one elegant instrument expected to overcome every barrier at once.

### 2. Many of the required substitutes are physical systems that must be built before they can be chosen

A heat pump cannot be widely adopted where electricity tariffs punish switching and installer capacity is missing.
A flexible power system cannot emerge where grids are congested and flexibility markets do not pay for useful response.
Low-emissions materials will not scale simply because someone prefers them morally if procurement language, common definitions, and market-making institutions are missing [S14][S53][S56][S68][S91][S96].

That is why ideal solutions should often begin with **asset-building and system-enabling mechanisms**:
- transmission and distribution upgrades,
- interconnection and queue reform,
- workforce pipelines,
- guarantee platforms and concessional finance,
- data, standards, and market rules,
- one-stop delivery systems and standard contracts [S14][S59][S91][S96][S112][S118][S119].

Prices can help after that.
But they cannot conjure the substitute into existence on their own.

### 3. Standards and procurement often move faster and more fairly than persuasion or price alone

Minimum standards, product rules, building codes, public procurement, fleet procurement, and utility programmes can shift whole markets at once.
They do not require every household, tenant, school, hospital, or small firm to rediscover the good choice independently [S53][S68][S80][S118][S119][S143][S146].

The archive already relies on this logic in practice:
- appliance standards can eliminate the worst products from the market while incentives accelerate the best ones [S146]
- heat-pump policy works best as a package of standards, code changes, targeted rebates, connected controls, installer training, supportive procurement, and electricity-price reform [S53]
- city-level energy policy works through regulation, procurement, planning, and targeted funding rather than information campaigns alone [S80]
- industrial decarbonization needs common definitions and procurement language so buyers and regulators can create a real market for lower-emissions materials [S68]

So the relevant question is not only “what behaviour do we want?”
It is also:

**which mechanism changes the market default for the largest number of users with the least repeated friction?**

### 4. Prices matter most when they reinforce a prepared pathway

The archive is not anti-price.
Carbon pricing, tariff reform, dynamic pricing, and subsidy reform all matter.
But they work best when the cleaner pathway is already technically available and socially legible [S58][S69][S112][S138][S139][S143].

OECD’s 2025 climate-investment synthesis says carbon-pricing revenues can be recycled into low-carbon infrastructure and household relief to improve acceptability [S138].
IEA’s 2026 affordability work says many household affordability policies remain badly targeted [S69].
IEA’s fossil-subsidy work says broad fossil support remains poorly targeted and actively distorts the transition [S139].

So the archive’s price rule should be:
- use pricing and subsidy reform to **clean up the signal**,
- recycle or target support so the vulnerable are protected,
- and do not ask prices to do the work of missing infrastructure, missing products, missing installers, or missing public capacity [S69][S112][S138][S139][S143].

### 5. A good mechanism mix is politically and fiscally more durable than a lone instrument

A lone instrument is easy to caricature and easy to reverse.
A package that delivers visible assets, lower bills, cleaner air, simpler user experience, and stronger local capability is harder to uproot because it creates beneficiaries, routines, and operating institutions [S59][S112][S137][S143][S147].

This matters because climate policy has to survive electoral turnover, fiscal stress, and implementation shocks.
A policy instrument should not only work in theory.
It should also leave behind infrastructure, trained people, standard operating procedures, and visible gains that make the next round easier [S59][S147].

## The archive’s mechanism hierarchy

A future proposal should usually be stronger when it fits most of this hierarchy.

### 1. Build-and-enable layer
Use public investment, utility reform, concessional finance, guarantees, permitting reform, workforce development, and open standards where the substitute system does not yet function at scale [S14][S59][S91][S96][S112][S119].

### 2. Default-shift layer
Use standards, codes, procurement rules, retirement schedules, product definitions, and planning rules to make the cleaner option normal rather than exceptional [S53][S68][S80][S143][S146].

### 3. Adoption-support layer
Use targeted rebates, top-up finance, on-bill or embedded support where available, one-stop delivery, simplified forms, standard contracts, and local implementation help so liquidity and hassle do not block uptake [S53][S118][S119][S137][S143].

### 4. Alignment layer
Use carbon prices, tariff reform, dynamic pricing, fossil-subsidy reform, disclosure, and performance reporting to reinforce the cleaner pathway and punish backsliding once credible alternatives exist [S58][S69][S112][S138][S139].

### 5. Truth-and-correction layer
Use interoperable definitions, MRV, public reporting, and recurring review so policy can be tightened, corrected, and defended honestly rather than drifting into slogans and exemptions [S68][S123][S124][S181].

A further archive corollary now makes the exemption point more explicit: when hardship, reliability, safety, or transition carve-outs are necessary, they should stay narrow, visible, reviewable, and sunset-bound rather than silently becoming the real policy. See `85-ideal-solutions-by-exception-form-narrow-carve-outs-visible-sunsets-and-no-permanent-loophole-path.md`.

## The archive’s mechanism tests

### 1. Substitute-before-penalty test
Does the proposal help build the cleaner substitute before or alongside any tougher penalty on the dirty one, rather than assuming people can switch into an option that barely exists? [S112][S143]

### 2. Default-shift test
Does it change market or administrative defaults for many users at once through standards, procurement, codes, or utility rules instead of depending mainly on repeated discretionary uptake? [S53][S68][S80][S146]

### 3. Barrier-removal test
Does it deal with upfront cost, split incentives, contractor scarcity, administrative hassle, or financing risk rather than pretending the right price signal dissolves all of them? [S53][S91][S96][S118][S119][S137]

### 4. Fair-pricing test
If the measure relies on pricing or subsidy reform, does it also protect vulnerable users and recycle gains into better alternatives instead of imposing naked cost pressure? [S69][S138][S139][S143]

### 5. Administrative-realism test
Can ordinary institutions actually administer it at scale, or does it demand unusually capable agencies, unusually informed consumers, and unusually frictionless markets all at once? [S59][S118][S119][S181]

### 6. Package-coherence test
Does it combine enabling, default-changing, supporting, and truth-telling mechanisms in a way that compounds, or is it only one instrument trying to solve everything alone? [S112][S151]

## One-paragraph compression

**Ideal climate solutions should usually arrive through mechanism mixes that build the clean substitute, shift defaults through standards and procurement, remove upfront and administrative barriers with targeted support, and then use prices, subsidy reform, and disclosure to reinforce the change. They should not rely on a lone elegant instrument — especially not a price-only or virtue-only strategy — to do the work of missing infrastructure, missing finance, missing state capacity, or missing market formation [S53][S59][S68][S91][S96][S112][S118][S119][S137][S138][S139][S143][S146].**
