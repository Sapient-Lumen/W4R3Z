# 255 — Ideal Solutions: Put Climate Risk on Balance Sheets Before Disorderly Repricing — Climate Balance-Sheet Compact Canon

## Claim

Rev0245 says recovery and protection gaps have to be governed before losses are repeated or abandoned.
The next operating layer is even blunter:

**climate risk that is not put on a balance sheet does not disappear; it migrates until a weaker balance sheet absorbs it or a market corrects suddenly.**

That balance sheet may be a household forced into repair debt, a tenant facing rent after an insurance shock, a public insurer carrying hidden contingent liabilities, a municipality losing tax base, a bank whose collateral is overstated, a utility raising rates after underbuilt resilience, an insurer leaving a market, or a national treasury improvising disaster aid.

So the doctrine is:

**make climate risk financially visible early enough to reduce it, price it honestly, support people fairly, and prevent disorderly repricing from becoming the real adaptation policy.**

## Fast rule

**Do not call climate risk managed because it is uninsured, underpriced, off budget, outside the utility rate case, outside the mortgage file, outside the landlord's repair plan, or outside the project pro forma. Put the risk on a public, household, financial, utility, housing, and fiscal ledger; then attach risk reduction, affordability protection, disclosure, stress testing, and correction triggers.**

## Why this note is not redundant

- `253` turns disaster recovery into risk reduction rather than loss repetition.
- `254` governs protection gaps, public backstops, uninsurability, and retreat.
- `184` governs fiscal durability and public-finance realism.
- `133` governs truth, measurement, and enforcement.
- `229` governs mass-uptake affordability, bill certainty, and reliability.

This note adds the missing cross-balance-sheet rule:

**every major climate packet should say where risk and cost sit before the event, during the transition, after the event, and after repricing.**

Without that ledger, the programme may reduce paper emissions while creating hidden fiscal exposure, unaffordable bills, brittle housing markets, uninsurable neighbourhoods, or a credit shock that is misdiagnosed as a natural market outcome.

## The compact canon

### 1. Physical risk becomes financial risk through named channels

OECD’s real-estate risk work says local climate risks can become financial risks in real-estate and associated markets, affecting borrowers, lenders, owners, firms, insurers, and investors through credit, market, underwriting, liquidity, and operational risk [S269].
It also warns that unaffordable insurance and increased household losses can amplify these channels [S269].
FSB’s 2025 climate-risk roadmap describes a severe-but-plausible physical shock to real estate where insurance becomes less available and risks shift to households, businesses, or governments [S271].

House rule: **treat “who holds the risk?” as a physical climate question, not a finance appendix.**

### 2. A loss is not managed until the absorbing balance sheet is named

If insurance is absent, a loss may appear later as mortgage delinquency, credit-card debt, rent pressure, deferred repair, illness, business failure, public-housing backlog, municipal borrowing, emergency tax, state insurer assessment, or federal disaster spending.
If grid resilience is underfunded, a loss may appear later as spoiled medicine, lost wages, diesel fallback, utility debt, or public rescue.
If heat protection is left private, a loss may appear as medical cost, labour loss, unsafe indoor temperature, or school closure.

House rule: **before declaring a risk acceptable, name the household, firm, utility, insurer, bank, landlord, municipality, or treasury that would absorb it.**

### 3. Ministries of finance belong at the table before the shock

The Coalition of Finance Ministers for Climate Action says physical climate risks already pose macro-critical risks, can force escalating public expenditures for extreme shocks and chronic events, and can put economic development strategies and green-transition investments at risk [S272].
That is not a post-disaster bookkeeping problem.
It is a pre-disaster fiscal-governance problem.

House rule: **physical risk should enter budget baselines, contingent-liability ledgers, public-investment planning, debt strategy, disaster-risk finance, and adaptation project selection before emergency spending makes the decision.**

### 4. Disclosure helps only if it leads to a decision

The Basel Committee’s voluntary climate-risk disclosure framework includes qualitative and quantitative information, recognises that climate data quality is still evolving, and says multiple quantitative metrics and qualitative information may be needed to form a comprehensive picture of banks’ climate-risk exposure [S274].
NGFS short-term scenarios give central banks and supervisors a framework for analysing near-term impacts of climate policies and climate change on financial stability and economic resilience [S270].
FSB’s roadmap similarly stresses reliable, granular, consistent, and comparable information and vulnerabilities analysis [S271].

But disclosure is not the finish line.
A disclosed risk that does not affect lending, underwriting, reserve policy, capital planning, public investment, land-use rules, utility planning, or social protection remains a labelled exposure.

House rule: **risk information should trigger risk reduction, capital allocation, backstop design, affordability protection, or managed exit — not merely better annual reports.**

### 5. Underpricing and abrupt repricing are both dangerous

There are two symmetric failures.

First, climate risk can be underpriced through hidden subsidy, stale maps, political premium suppression, optimistic infrastructure standards, or public promises that nobody funds.
That keeps exposure growing.

Second, risk can reprice abruptly through insurance withdrawal, mortgage tightening, property-value correction, credit downgrade, utility bill shock, or public backstop exhaustion.
That turns delayed adaptation into sudden private loss.

House rule: **the aim is neither denial pricing nor abandonment pricing; the aim is orderly repricing paired with risk reduction and protection.**

### 6. Public backstops must show their own balance sheet

Public insurance pools, catastrophe funds, contingent credit lines, premium assistance, resilience grants, relocation funds, and public reinsurance can be necessary.
But if their fiscal exposure is hidden, they can become a political machine for continuing risk without reducing it.

A backstop should state:
- the hazard and population it covers
- the maximum exposure and who replenishes it
- the premium, tax, or budget channel
- the risk-reduction conditions attached
- the affordability protection it provides
- the timeline for review
- the trigger for safer rebuild, buyout, land swap, or relocation

House rule: **a public backstop without a balance-sheet view is a promise to discover the cost later.**

### 7. Utility and ratepayer balance sheets are part of the climate ledger

Grid hardening, undergrounding, distribution upgrades, storage, demand flexibility, resilience hubs, critical-service microgrids, and wildfire mitigation often move through utility plans and rate cases.
If the programme does not name the ratepayer effect, the equity effect, the avoided-outage value, and the avoided-diesel or avoided-loss value, it can either underbuild resilience or load costs regressively onto essential users.

House rule: **utility climate investment should show both the cost-recovery ledger and the avoided-loss ledger.**

### 8. Household balance sheets are not residual landfill

Households are often asked to absorb the mismatch between climate reality and institutional planning: higher premiums, higher deductibles, hotter rooms, higher bills, repair debt, missed work, medical cost, retrofit co-pays, vehicle replacement, landlord pass-throughs, or relocation cost.
That can turn a physically sensible transition into a politically brittle one.

House rule: **ordinary households should not become the default shock absorber for unmanaged climate risk or badly staged transition cost.**

Use `256` for the companion affordability-firebreak rule.

Rev0247 adds the claims-integrity corollary: weak climate claims can also create balance-sheet risk. A misleading net-zero, carbon-credit, resilience, clean-power, green-finance, or procurement claim can misprice assets, misdirect capital, create legal exposure, shift costs to consumers, or hide future public liabilities. Use `257` when the balance-sheet problem is being created by the claim itself, and `258` when the problem is value-channel capture [S275][S276][S280][S281][S282][S283][S284].

### 9. Balance-sheet visibility should change priority

When two packets compete for scarce public finance, grid capacity, staff time, or political attention, the balance-sheet ledger should alter the ranking.
A project that cuts a hidden recurring liability, avoids a future public bailout, reduces household debt risk, preserves tax base, protects critical service, or prevents disorderly market exit may have higher public value than its narrow emissions score suggests.

House rule: **rank not only by tonnes, but also by avoided loss migration and avoided disorderly repricing.**

### 10. The ledger has to age and correct

Climate-risk ledgers will be wrong at first.
Hazards shift, maps update, construction costs change, premiums move, outages reveal new dependencies, and social vulnerability changes.
So the ledger needs update clocks and correction triggers:
- after each disaster
- after premium or coverage changes
- after repeated uninsured loss
- after property-value or credit signals move
- after grid outages or heat emergencies
- after new climate data, risk maps, or stress tests
- before large public backstops are renewed
- before public investment plans are finalized

House rule: **a stale balance-sheet ledger is another form of hidden risk.**

## Minimum climate balance-sheet packet

A formed balance-sheet packet should include:
- the physical hazard, transition pressure, or recovery risk
- the affected assets, services, people, and geographies
- current risk holder: household, landlord, insurer, lender, utility, municipality, treasury, firm, or public authority
- expected loss pathway under routine, stress, and recovery states
- insurance, credit, rent, utility-rate, tax, and public-budget channels
- who benefits from risk reduction
- who pays for risk reduction
- who pays if risk reduction is not done
- what disclosure or scenario analysis supports the claim
- what public backstop exists and what conditions attach to it
- what affordability protection prevents weaker parties from being overloaded
- what trigger moves the packet from defend-in-place to rebuild-safer, restrict-new-exposure, buyout, land-swap, or relocation
- how the ledger is updated after shocks and repricing events

## What this note demotes

This note demotes nine mistakes:
- treating uninsured losses as if they are outside the climate programme
- treating insurance premium stress as separate from housing, credit, and public finance
- suppressing prices while exposure keeps growing
- letting sudden market exit become the real relocation plan
- writing grid, water, transport, and housing climate costs into bills without equity, avoided-loss, or service-floor accounting
- using disclosure without decision triggers
- treating public backstops as infinite capacity rather than contingent liabilities
- ranking climate projects without counting avoided fiscal, credit, housing, and household loss migration
- letting stale maps and stale assumptions govern new debt, new housing, new infrastructure, or new public promises

## Compression rule

**Climate risk is not governed until its absorbing balance sheet is named. Put physical risk, transition cost, recovery loss, insurance stress, utility cost, housing exposure, credit exposure, and public contingent liability on a live ledger, then use that ledger to reduce risk, protect weaker parties, and prevent disorderly repricing from becoming the adaptation plan.**
