# 69 — Ideal Solutions by Upside Allocation: Public Value Capture, Lower Bills, and Shared Gains

## Claim

The archive now has short rules for context, justice, timing, actor allocation, mechanism choice, scoreboard discipline, institutional form, delivery form, risk-bearing, and the social legitimacy of visible benefit sharing.

One more rule belongs in the canon:

**ideal climate solutions should not only allocate downside properly. They should also allocate upside properly.**

When public action creates the value — through permits, regulated monopoly status, rights-of-way, public procurement, concessional finance, guarantees, tax support, subsidy reform, standards, public land access, or other state-created market structure — the resulting upside should not flow out as a one-way private windfall by default.

It should usually come back in some combination of:
- **lower bills or lower service cost**
- **public revenue or recycled savings**
- **host-community or regional benefit**
- **open-access, transparency, or replication conditions**
- **and clearer public leverage over future performance, pricing, or reinvestment** [S129][S130][S131][S138][S141][S143][S145][S153]

This is not an anti-private rule.
Private firms, utilities, developers, lenders, and manufacturers need investable returns.
The rule is narrower:

**if climate value is being created partly by public risk-taking, public authority, public subsidy, or public coordination, then some material share of the upside should usually remain public-facing rather than disappearing into opaque rents.**

This note pairs with `68-ideal-solutions-by-risk-bearing-downside-with-managers-liability-and-public-backstops.md`.

If `68` says **publics should not absorb downside by default**, this note says **publics should not surrender upside by default either**.

## Why upside allocation belongs inside the ideal solution

### 1. Public action creates a large share of climate-transition value

Many high-value climate investments do not become profitable only because a clever private actor appeared first.
They become profitable because public institutions create the investable environment:

- planning and siting systems designate viable areas [S60]
- grids and roads create network access [S14][S55][S57]
- procurement creates demand and price discovery [S129][S130][S131]
- standards, market definitions, and MRV create bankable product categories [S68][S123][S132]
- guarantees and concessional tools reduce financing cost or absorb political and currency risk [S91][S96][S153]
- subsidy reform and targeted household support change the political and spending baseline [S138][S139][S143][S145]

So the archive needs a compact rule against pretending that all upside is purely private creation.
Very often the public has already built, de-risked, or legitimised the revenue stream.

### 2. Public downside without public upside is usually a warning sign

The archive’s risk-bearing note already says public guarantees and backstops should be used selectively for socially valuable clean buildout or genuine system tail risks, not as a casual subsidy to ordinary private failure [S91][S96][S153].

This note adds the matching symmetry rule:

**the more public balance sheets, public authority, or public patience are used to make a project bankable, the stronger the case that the resulting gains should show up in lower prices, public revenue, local benefit, or other clearly socialised value.**

Otherwise the package often drifts toward a bad political economy:
- publics absorb permitting conflict, fiscal risk, or system integration cost
- users still face high prices
- host places carry landscape or land-use burdens
- and private actors keep most upside with weak conditions on affordability, transparency, or reinvestment [S129][S130][S131][S141][S153]

That pattern is not only unfair.
It also weakens the legitimacy, fiscal durability, and copyability of the transition [S59][S129][S130][S138][S141][S153].

### 3. Shared gains make transition politics more durable

OECD’s current climate-growth work says revenues generated by climate policy can be invested in low-carbon infrastructure or used to limit distributive harm and increase policy acceptability [S138].
IEA’s current affordability work says better-targeted support and standards can reduce cost for the households that need help rather than preserving broad regressive subsidy patterns [S143].
OECD and IEA current work on fossil-fuel support both say inefficient broad support is a large fiscal drain that should be replaced with better-targeted and more productive uses [S139][S145].

That implies a broader archive rule:

**when climate policy creates savings, revenues, rents, or privileged market positions, ideal designs should turn a material share of that value into visible public gain.**

That can mean:
- lower tariffs or bill credits
- household relief funded from subsidy reform or carbon revenues
- cheaper public transport or public-housing energy upgrades
- host-community amenities or local revenue sharing
- or reinvestment into grid upgrades, resilience, workforce capacity, and access expansion [S69][S138][S139][S141][S143][S145]

The archive already says fairness needs to be visible.
This note adds that **visible public gain is often the cleanest proof that public enabling has not merely created private extraction.**

### 4. Upside discipline is part of anti-capture doctrine

OECD’s current bid-rigging guidance, public-procurement review, and World Bank procurement framework all stress value for money, integrity, transparency, fit-for-purpose design, competition, and complaint channels [S129][S130][S131].

Those sources are usually read as anti-corruption or procurement process notes.
The archive should read them more broadly.

If a climate programme:
- relies on repeated opaque exceptions
- grants monopoly-like positions without strong service obligations
- uses de-risking without affordability or performance conditions
- or lets public procurement become a transfer mechanism with weak post-contract discipline

then it is not just procedurally messy.
It is usually **misallocating upside**.

Ideal solutions should therefore not ask only whether corruption is absent.
They should also ask whether public action is being translated into:
- lower prices
- better service
- better coverage
- cleaner procurement benchmarks
- or learning that reduces future cost for others [S129][S130][S131][S153]

### 5. Some of the best forms of public upside are indirect but still real

The archive should not reduce upside allocation to royalties or explicit state profit shares alone.

Often the strongest public-return channels are more ordinary:
- a regulated asset model that lowers finance cost and user prices [S91][S96]
- procurement rules that make later projects cheaper and more standardised [S129][S130][S131][S153]
- standards that move the whole market floor upward rather than only rewarding a few adopters [S143][S146]
- subsidy reform that frees fiscal space for social protection and access [S138][S139][S145]
- open interfaces, common definitions, and interoperability that stop one firm from enclosing a whole market [S68][S123][S154]
- local-benefit and participation arrangements that turn host acceptance into durable shared gain rather than a one-off extraction deal [S60][S141][S142]

So the archive’s rule is deliberately broad:

**public upside may arrive as cheaper service, better access, fiscal room, local benefit, open standards, or repeatable lower-cost delivery — not only as direct cash transfers.**

## The archive’s upside-allocation hierarchy

A future proposal is usually stronger when it fits most of this order.

### 1. Lower-bills-first layer
When public action is making a clean service possible, the first question should often be whether users get lower or more stable bills, lower operating cost, or a clearer affordability gain rather than merely a new branded asset stack [S69][S91][S143].

### 2. Recycle-savings-and-revenues layer
If a reform creates fiscal savings or revenue — especially through subsidy reform, pricing, or lower public operating cost — a material share should usually be recycled into household protection, public goods, access expansion, or further transition investment [S138][S139][S143][S145].

### 3. Host-benefit layer
Where infrastructure imposes visible local burdens or land-use change, the package should usually include durable host-community benefit, local amenities, participation rights, or regional development value rather than assuming national decarbonization alone is enough compensation [S60][S141][S142].

### 4. Open-and-replicable-market layer
Where public procurement, blended finance, or standard setting is helping create a market, the resulting model should usually be transparent, comparable, and replicable so later actors and places benefit rather than facing a permanently bespoke insider advantage [S68][S129][S130][S131][S153].

### 5. Conditioned-private-upside layer
Private return is appropriate, but it should usually come with clear conditions on performance, pricing, integrity, affordability, or reinvestment when public authority or public risk is doing a meaningful share of the value creation [S91][S96][S129][S130][S131][S153].

## The archive’s upside-allocation tests

### 1. Public-enabling test
How much of the project’s value depends on public permitting, public networks, regulated monopoly privileges, procurement, subsidy, guarantees, standard setting, or other public coordination?

### 2. Symmetry test
If the public is taking real risk, delay, or expenditure, what concrete upside does the public get back besides an abstract claim that private investment was “mobilised”? [S91][S96][S153]

### 3. Lower-bills test
Will users see cheaper, more stable, or more predictable service costs, or is the public merely helping produce a profitable project while affordability remains weak? [S69][S143]

### 4. Windfall test
Could the same climate outcome be delivered with tighter pricing, stronger procurement, clearer clawbacks, or better conditions on support, or is the current design likely to create avoidable windfall rents? [S129][S130][S131]

### 5. Host-place test
If local places carry visible burdens, what durable upside is reserved for them beyond temporary construction activity or symbolic consultation? [S60][S141][S142]

### 6. Replication test
Does the design create knowledge, standards, and templates others can use, or does it privatise the learning while the public pays for first-mover risk? [S153]

## What this rules in

This note rules **in**:
- support packages that exchange subsidy reform or carbon revenues for targeted household relief, access expansion, or public investment [S138][S139][S143][S145]
- regulated or public-interest financing models that turn lower cost of capital into lower user cost rather than only higher private spread capture [S91][S96]
- procurement structures that lock in transparency, competition, lifecycle discipline, and shareable learning rather than one-off opaque deals [S129][S130][S131][S153]
- clean-infrastructure programmes that reserve visible host-community or regional benefits where local burdens are concentrated [S60][S141][S142]
- standard-setting and interoperability choices that make the market easier for future entrants and users rather than enclosing it behind proprietary advantage [S68][S123][S154]

## What this demotes

This note demotes:
- climate deals where the public absorbs political, permitting, or financing burden while users still face weak affordability and the upside is mostly retained privately [S69][S91][S96][S143]
- broad public support with no clear conditions on service quality, affordability, transparency, or future cost reduction [S129][S130][S131][S153]
- subsidy regimes that preserve private rents or affluent overconsumption more than they improve access, lower bills, or build public goods [S138][S139][S143][S145]
- local siting packages that ask host communities to absorb land-use or visual burdens without durable upside [S60][S141][S142]
- highly bespoke first-of-a-kind structures where the public pays for learning but the resulting template remains closed and hard to copy [S129][S130][S131][S153]

## One-paragraph compression

**Ideal climate solutions should not only keep controllable downside with those who choose or manage it; they should also keep a meaningful share of upside public-facing when public action creates the value. If permits, regulated privileges, public procurement, concessional finance, guarantees, subsidy reform, or standard setting are making the project possible, the gains should usually show up in lower bills, public revenue, host-community benefit, open and replicable market design, or stronger leverage over future performance and pricing rather than flowing out as one-way private windfalls [S60][S69][S91][S96][S129][S130][S131][S138][S139][S141][S142][S143][S145][S153].**
