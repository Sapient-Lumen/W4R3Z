# 205 — Ideal Solutions: Five Front-Door Verdicts After the Proposal-Intake Interview Compact Canon

## Claim

The archive now has a shortest whole answer (`195`), a seriousness threshold (`197`), a transition-mechanism note (`198`), a replacement-proof note (`199`), a basic unit of progress (`200`), a basic unit of scale (`201`), a completion condition (`202`), a minimum winning loop (`203`), and a smallest intake interview (`204`).
That is already a strong front door.
But one small practical gap remained:

**after a claimed solution has answered the intake interview, what verdict should the archive usually give?**

That gap matters because many proposals are neither core admits nor obvious refusals.
Some are promising but unfinished.
Some belong only as enabling or conditional support.
Some need a clear future trigger before the archive spends more attention on them.
So this note adds the smallest front-door verdict rule after `204`.
It is not a new doctrinal branch.
Its job is to compress the archive’s screen, intake, routing, and proportionality rules into one tiny disposition card that can be used before reopening the deeper screening stack in `59`, `92`, or `127` [S97][S100][S101][S103][S106][S127][S128][S145][S147][S148][S180].

## Fast rule

**After the proposal-intake interview, give one of five verdicts: admit, rewrite, demote, quarantine, or refuse. Admit only when the proposal is formed enough for the winning loop now. Rewrite when the proposal may be good but is still missing package, proof, archetype, or retirement logic. Demote when it belongs mainly as an enabling or conditional support. Quarantine when a clear future trigger could change the verdict. Refuse when the claim mainly preserves delay, substitution error, or dirty continuation.**

## The five verdicts

### 1. Admit
Use this when the proposal can already answer the front-door interview well enough to enter the winning loop now.
It names a real control point, a believable replacement package, a proof rule, a replication story, and a dirty path that should actually lose ground.
“Admit” does **not** mean “add a new file.”
It means the proposal is formed enough to be treated as serious programme material and routed into the design, delivery, proof, and scaling notes that already exist [S97][S100][S101][S103][S106][S127][S128][S145][S147][S148].

### 2. Rewrite
Use this when the proposal may still matter, but is not yet shaped well enough for admission.
Typical rewrite causes:
- the technology is named but the control point is not
- the package is partial
- the proof rule is vague
- the replication story is missing
- the dirtier path that should shrink has not been named

“Rewrite” is the archive’s default verdict for promising but under-specified proposals.
It keeps the archive from mistaking raw intuition, pilots, or enthusiasm for finished design [S97][S100][S103][S106][S127][S128].

### 3. Demote
Use this when the proposal has a role, but mainly as an enabling, supporting, niche, or residual layer rather than as a first-order climate pathway.
A demoted proposal may still help the serious package.
But it should not carry central explanatory weight, and it should not displace cleaner, faster, or more scalable primary lanes.
This verdict is often right for tools that matter only under narrow boundary conditions, after prior bottlenecks are released, or inside the hard-residual lane [S100][S101][S103][S106][S145].

### 4. Quarantine
Use this when the proposal is not formed enough now, yet a specific future trigger could justify reopening it.
A quarantine verdict should always name that trigger:
better evidence,
lower scarcity cost,
clearer proof,
a stronger delivery path,
or a narrower boundary.
Without a named trigger, quarantine easily becomes a polite holding pen for archive clutter.
The archive should therefore treat quarantine as conditional waiting, not half-admission [S97][S100][S103][S127][S145].

### 5. Refuse
Use this when the proposal mainly works by delay, paper substitution, speculative future cleanup, scarcity waste, or preservation of dirty defaults behind clean rhetoric.
Refusal is not harshness for its own sake.
It is how the archive protects attention from attractive but structurally unserious claims.
When refusal is right, the archive should usually not expand around the proposal.
At most it should clarify the boundary once inside an existing note [S100][S101][S103][S106][S145][S148].

## Why this compression helps

### It closes the gap between interview and action
`204` asks what a proposal must answer.
This note says what verdict those answers should produce before the archive spends more design attention.

### It gives “unfinished but promising” a real home
Many proposals are not wrong.
They are merely not yet shaped enough.
“Rewrite” keeps the archive from either over-promoting or prematurely discarding them.

### It keeps archive actions proportional
A front-door verdict is smaller than a deep doctrinal treatment.
That helps the archive stay sharp and avoid procedural sprawl.

## Preferred use

Open this note first when the live question is any of the following:
- what verdict the archive should give after the five front-door intake questions
- how to distinguish an admit from a rewrite
- what to do with a proposal that sounds promising but is still under-specified
- when a proposal belongs as a supporting or residual lane rather than as a central pathway
- how to keep the archive from expanding around weak or premature claims

Then reopen:
- `59` if the question becomes a fuller promote/demote/quarantine screen
- `92` if the question becomes what archive action should follow the verdict
- `127` if the question becomes how intake and admission fit into the archive’s broader operating sequence
- `198`, `199`, `200`, `201`, or `203` if the surviving issue is mainly about transition mechanism, replacement proof, the atomic unit, the scaling unit, or the winning loop itself

## Compression rule

**After the front-door intake interview, do not jump straight to archive growth. First assign one of five verdicts: admit, rewrite, demote, quarantine, or refuse.**
