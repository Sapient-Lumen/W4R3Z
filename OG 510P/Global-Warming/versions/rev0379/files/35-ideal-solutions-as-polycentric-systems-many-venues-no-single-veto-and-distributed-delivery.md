# 35 — Ideal Solutions as Polycentric Systems: Many Venues, No Single Veto, and Distributed Delivery

## Claim

The archive now has strong answers to what ideal climate solutions should build, what they should exclude, how they should allocate scarcity, how they should stay fail-soft, how they should avoid one-way loss, how they should arrive as packages, how they should compound, and how they should correct themselves.

One more governance rule belongs inside the canon:

**ideal solutions should be polycentric.**

That means they should be able to advance through **many centres of action at once**: national governments, states and provinces, cities, regulators, public-development banks, utilities, grid operators, procurement bodies, housing agencies, firms, insurers, community institutions, and households.

They should not rely on one perfect treaty, one heroic national government, one ministry, one financing channel, or one election cycle to carry the whole transition.

OECD’s 2025 mission-governance work says the Paris framework left implementation choices largely to national governments, effectively creating a **multitude of sandboxes** for different governance approaches, and explicitly notes that the Agreement welcomes experimentation among national governments while encouraging action by non-state and subnational actors alike [S115]. UNFCCC’s 2025 Yearbook of Global Climate Action adds that non-Party stakeholders are now a **key driver** of implementation of the first Global Stocktake outcome; **95%** of Parties reported engagement of non-Party stakeholders in NDC implementation, and actions registered through NAZCA rose from about **18 000** in 2020 to over **43 000** in 2025 [S116].

This note therefore adds a polycentric rule to the canon:

**prefer measures and institutions that can be advanced, defended, financed, and improved through multiple venues at once, so no single blockage becomes a veto over climate progress.**

## Why polycentricity belongs inside the ideal solution

### 1. The problem is too cross-cutting for one centre to own all the levers

Climate mitigation and adaptation do not sit inside one administrative box.

Power systems involve utilities, regulators, grid operators, manufacturers, land-use authorities, and national finance. Buildings involve codes, landlords, social housing, appliance standards, installers, local inspection, and electricity pricing. Mobility involves ministries, city governments, road agencies, ports, vehicle rules, procurement, and logistics firms. Exposure discipline involves insurers, lenders, planners, mayors, emergency managers, and courts.

A solution that assumes one level of government or one instrument can coherently command all these levers is usually not ideal. It is administratively under-imagined.

The archive already had parts of this view scattered across other notes. UNDP’s urban NDC review says cities account for about **67%** of global energy consumption and **70%** of greenhouse-gas emissions, yet only **26** of **194** NDCs analysed included specific urban-level finance requests [S77]. IEA’s 2025 city toolkit says national and city-level alignment is a key dimension of clean-energy transitions and highlights local regulation, data systems, targeted city funding, and procurement as practical levers [S80]. The design implication is straightforward: ideal solutions must be governable at more than one level at once [S77][S80].

### 2. Waiting for one perfect centre of action wastes time the problem does not offer

A climate strategy that depends on a single decisive forum — a treaty round, one parliament, one presidency, one carbon price, one flagship industrial package — leaves too much of the real transition idle while waiting.

Polycentric design is not an excuse to abandon coordination. It is the recognition that climate progress can move in parallel:
- building codes can tighten while grids are upgraded
- cities can change procurement and land use while national tax reform stalls
- methane measurement can improve while industrial-product standards are negotiated
- concessional finance platforms can de-risk projects while domestic institutions mature
- insurers and lenders can price exposure more honestly while retreat policy catches up

The ideal solution therefore treats venue plurality as a speed advantage, not as messy second-best governance [S59][S77][S80][S115][S116].

### 3. Many-venue delivery is also more resilient to backlash and blockage

The archive’s robustness note already argues that ideal solutions should reduce dependence on single points of failure [S103][S107].

This note sharpens the governance version of that argument.

A portfolio is stronger when progress can continue even if one lane stalls:
- if national politics freezes, city procurement, utility planning, corporate supply-chain standards, and local retrofit programs may still move [S77][S80][S115]
- if transmission lags, efficiency, flexible demand, distributed storage, better building envelopes, and local peak management can still buy time [S51][S53][S56][S103][S111]
- if concessional finance is tight, domestic procurement, standards, and lower-cost distributed systems may still advance portions of the transition [S69][S70][S91][S95][S96]

Ideal solutions should therefore be designed so they are not institutionally hostage to one blockage.

## Polycentricity is not the same as fragmentation

A weak climate system can also contain many actors. Mere multiplicity is not enough.

Polycentric climate solutions are **plural in action but coherent in direction**.

They still need shared goals, common accounting, interoperable standards, visible progress metrics, and some central functions that lower levels cannot do alone:
- long-range grid and market rules [S55][S56]
- national or supranational product standards [S27][S68]
- large-scale fiscal transfers, guarantees, and concessional finance [S91][S94][S95][S96]
- open risk data, early-warning systems, and climate information infrastructure [S15][S83][S108]
- legal frameworks that set floor protections and prevent high-risk free-riding [S61][S81][S84]

So the polycentric rule is **not** “let every actor improvise.”
It is:

**create many implementation venues, but hold them together with common direction, truth infrastructure, and enabling support.**

## What polycentric ideal solutions look like

### 1. Multiple venues per important objective

An ideal solution does not ask one institution to do everything.

For example, building decarbonization can advance through:
- national appliance and equipment standards [S16][S17][S54]
- utility efficiency and demand-flexibility programs [S56][S106][S111]
- local code enforcement and permitting [S60][S80]
- public and social-housing retrofit programs [S53][S69]
- procurement and lending standards [S27][S68][S96]

Each venue is partial. Together they become harder to veto.

### 2. Higher levels resource lower levels instead of dumping responsibility downward

Polycentricity does **not** mean unfunded localism.

OECD’s 2025 place-based work says multi-level governance is critical to climate resilience and that national governments have a key role in providing technical support, downscaled risk information, finance, and local inclusion in planning processes; it also stresses regular consultation and vertical coordination between levels of government [S117]. IEA’s city toolkit similarly points to targeted city funding, national-city alignment, data systems, and procurement support as practical transition levers [S80].

The archive therefore favors:
- national frameworks with local delivery room
- fiscal transfers tied to real capacity needs
- model rules, procurement templates, and technical assistance
- shared data systems and open climate-risk information
- city-capable and region-capable finance, not only city-level obligations [S77][S80][S117]

### 3. Distributed experimentation with transferable learning

The archive’s flywheel and corrigibility notes already value learning and adjustment [S59][S113][S114].

Polycentricity adds the governance mechanism for how such learning often happens in practice:
**many jurisdictions and institutions try variations, but their results are made comparable enough that useful lessons travel.**

OECD’s 2025 mission-governance report says the Paris framework created governance sandboxes, allowing states to account for specific circumstances while experimenting with laws and organisational structures [S115]. That makes climate governance less like one master blueprint and more like coordinated comparative learning.

Ideal solutions therefore prefer:
- interoperable definitions and reporting [S65][S68][S113]
- open implementation metrics [S66][S83][S114]
- common evaluation frameworks across programs [S59][S113][S117]
- policy designs that can be copied, improved, and adapted rather than reinvented from zero in every place [S59][S80][S115]

### 4. Non-state actors matter, but should not be allowed to hide in voluntarism

UNFCCC’s 2025 Yearbook makes clear that climate action by cities, regions, businesses, investors, and civil society is now an important implementation lane, not only a diplomatic side show [S116].

But the archive’s house rule remains hard-edged:
- non-state action is valuable when it accelerates real delivery
- it is less valuable when it substitutes for binding standards, physical deployment, or accountable finance [S65][S68][S116]

So the polycentric rule upgrades:
- real-economy procurement coalitions
- utility and grid-operator reforms
- city and regional transition platforms
- public-development-bank market creation
- insurer and lender exposure discipline
- community institutions that make retrofit, cooling, or resilience delivery more reachable [S69][S77][S80][S81][S83][S116]

It downgrades:
- voluntary pledge theater without implementation architecture
- branding-heavy coalitions with weak measurement
- private commitments that depend on public bailout when conditions change [S65][S68][S113][S116]

## How this differs from neighbouring ideal-solution rules

This note is adjacent to several recent notes, but it is not redundant with them.

- `32-ideal-solutions-as-packages-complementarity-sequencing-and-bottleneck-release.md` asks which measures belong together and in what order.
- `33-ideal-solutions-as-flywheels-compounding-learning-curves-and-self-reinforcing-delivery.md` asks whether action today makes later action cheaper and easier.
- `34-ideal-solutions-as-corrigible-systems-feedback-measurement-and-ratcheting.md` asks whether the system can notice underperformance and improve.

This note asks something different:

**through how many real centres of action can this solution move, and can one blockage veto the whole thing?**

## The archive’s polycentric screen

A future proposal should usually be promoted only if it passes most of the following tests.

### 1. No-single-veto test
Would progress still be possible if one national ministry, one election result, one permitting bottleneck, one finance channel, or one treaty process stalls?

### 2. Multi-venue test
Can at least several different actor classes act on this — for example national government, subnational government, regulated firms, public finance, and households or communities?

### 3. Support-not-dump test
Does the design give lower-capacity actors money, tools, data, and legal room, or does it merely assign them responsibilities they cannot carry?

### 4. Transferable-learning test
Will different implementations generate knowledge that can travel because accounting, reporting, and evaluation are sufficiently comparable?

### 5. Coherence test
Even with many venues, are the shared goals, standards, and truth systems strong enough that action adds up rather than cancels out?

Measures that pass four or five of these tests deserve promotion. Measures that depend on one decisive centre should be treated more cautiously unless their upside is extraordinary.

## What polycentric portfolios overweight

The archive’s polycentric rule pushes the ideal portfolio toward:
- appliance, equipment, and building standards that can be enforced nationally but implemented locally [S16][S17][S51][S54][S80]
- city and regional land-use, transport, heat, drainage, and procurement reforms tied to national support [S77][S78][S79][S80][S117]
- utility regulation, queue reform, flexibility programs, and distributed-energy rules that can move even when grand bargains lag [S55][S56][S103][S111]
- concessional, guarantee, and local-currency finance platforms that unlock many projects rather than only flagship deals [S91][S94][S95][S96]
- open methane, risk, and performance data that many institutions can act on simultaneously [S11][S65][S66][S67][S83][S113]
- decentralized and distributed-access solutions where they solve real constraints faster than grid-only or ministry-only pathways [S69][S70]

## What polycentric portfolios distrust

The archive therefore distrusts:
- climate strategies that wait for one perfect treaty, one once-and-for-all national package, or one carbon price to solve everything [S1][S59][S115]
- national target-setting that is not translated into city, regional, utility, procurement, finance, and regulatory lanes [S77][S80][S115]
- local responsibility without money, standards, data, or technical support [S77][S80][S117]
- corporate and city pledges that are not tied to real assets, standards, measurement, or finance [S65][S68][S113][S116]
- governance designs that leave whole sectors or territories waiting for a single institution to become competent before any progress can occur [S59][S61][S117]

## Bottom line

The ideal solution to global warming is not only low-carbon, high-welfare, robust, corrigible, and compounding.

It is also **polycentric**.

It moves through many venues at once, protects itself against single-point political failure, uses shared standards so learning can travel, and gives lower levels of government and other delivery actors the resources needed to act.

That is messier than the fantasy of one lever.
It is also much closer to how real decarbonization, adaptation, and exposure reduction actually happen.
