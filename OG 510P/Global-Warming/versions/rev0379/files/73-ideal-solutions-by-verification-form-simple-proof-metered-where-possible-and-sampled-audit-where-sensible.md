# 73 — Ideal Solutions by Verification Form: Simple Proof, Metered Where Possible, and Sampled Audit Where Sensible

## Claim

The archive already says ideal climate solutions should be physical, measurable, low-friction, fiscally durable, service-first, and honest about risk, contracts, payment, and eligibility [S42][S58][S65][S67][S70][S71][S72][S118][S119][S123][S124][S137][S143][S144].

One more compact rule belongs in the canon:

**ideal climate solutions should usually prove ordinary climate claims through the lightest verification form that is still credible.**

In practice that usually means:
- use direct metering, operational data, or obvious physical counts where those already exist [S123][S124][S144]
- use standard methods, deemed savings, approved product classes, or reference performance bands for routine and repetitive actions where bespoke proof would cost more than the extra precision is worth [S119][S124][S137]
- use sampling, spot checks, and risk-based audit where the task is large-scale and mostly standardised [S118][S119][S124]
- reserve full bespoke MRV, engineering diligence, or project-by-project proof for large, novel, high-risk, high-value, or easily gameable claims [S123][S124][S129][S130][S131]

So the archive’s shortest verification-form rule is:

**make proof proportionate to stakes and repeatability; do not make every household, school, clinic, municipality, or small firm carry bespoke evidentiary burdens just to deliver routine climate gains.**

For the archive’s matching remedy-form rule on how ordinary users should get fast correction when routine proof, billing, or provider claims go wrong, see `74-ideal-solutions-by-remedy-form-fast-recourse-automatic-correction-and-no-appeals-mazes.md`.

## Why this belongs in the canon

### 1. Verification can quietly become the bottleneck even when the underlying action is good

A climate programme can have a sound technology, good economics, eligible users, decent payment form, and clear contracts — and still stall because the proof burden is too bespoke, too slow, or too expensive.
That happens when every small project needs custom modelling, repeated document assembly, consultant-heavy diligence, or post-hoc evidence chains that overwhelm the value of the action itself [S118][S119][S124][S137][S144].

This is especially damaging for measures that are:
- routine,
- high-volume,
- relatively standard,
- and socially useful even when each individual case is not very large.

So the archive needs a compact rule against confusing **maximum theoretical precision** with **good system design**.

### 2. The strongest verification form often reuses proof that already exists

OECD’s 2025 work on regulatory quality and quality infrastructure says interoperability, standards, measurement systems, and assurance arrangements can reduce navigation burden and close the loop between design and delivery [S118][S124].
OECD’s MRV interoperability work says practical systems should build on existing MRV rules and data structures where possible rather than rebuilding comparability from scratch each time [S123].

That implies a broad operational lesson for climate delivery:
when a claim can be checked through existing meters, bills, product standards, installation records, procurement records, or other routine operational data, the design should usually start there rather than demand a bespoke proof ritual [S119][S123][S124][S144].

### 3. High-friction proof is often worst exactly where scale matters most

IEA’s efficiency and affordability work says administrative complexity, fragmented pathways, and high transaction costs are major barriers to uptake, especially in buildings and household-facing programmes [S119][S137][S143].
The same logic applies one step later in the chain.
A climate programme may survive a difficult proof burden in a few flagship projects, but not across millions of homes, thousands of public buildings, or dispersed fleets of small and medium-size actions.

So this note adds a narrower rule:

**if the action is meant to become ordinary, the proof pathway usually has to become ordinary too.**

### 4. Verification should usually burden providers and systems more than end users

The archive’s recent notes already say burdens should sit with concentrated control points, contracts should be legible, eligibility should avoid proof mazes, and service providers should keep meaningful performance responsibility [S63][S67][S71][S72].
Verification form should follow the same logic.

That means the archive should usually prefer designs where:
- providers submit standard data,
- systems reuse metered or administrative records,
- regulators and programme managers run sampling and audit,
- and users are not turned into unpaid evidence clerks for the privilege of receiving a cleaner service [S118][S119][S124][S137][S144].

### 5. Proportional proof improves integrity when it targets the right claims

This note is not a plea for weak evidence.
It is a plea for evidence effort that matches where gaming risk and value concentration actually sit.

Large industrial claims, high-value procurement promises, carbon-intensity labels, carbon-credit issuance, methane accounting, and savings-backed performance contracts can justify tighter verification, common metrics, stronger assurance, and more explicit audit trails [S11][S27][S65][S68][S123][S124][S129][S130][S131][S144].
But forcing the same proof intensity onto every small, routine, standardisable action can reduce delivery while adding little integrity.

So the archive needs a plain hierarchy:

**tight proof where claims are large, novel, or gameable; light proof where actions are routine, standard, and easy to check indirectly.**

### 6. This note complements, rather than duplicates, neighbouring notes

This note is not the same question as:
- `65-ideal-solutions-by-scoreboard-physical-outcomes-service-gains-and-bottleneck-release.md`, which asks **what should count as real progress**,
- `67-ideal-solutions-by-delivery-form-clean-service-performance-and-less-asset-shopping.md`, which asks **what the user should receive**,
- `71-ideal-solutions-by-contract-form-standard-offers-prequalified-delivery-and-performance-backed-terms.md`, which asks **how the promise should usually be written and compared**,
- or `72-ideal-solutions-by-eligibility-form-presumptive-inclusion-existing-data-and-no-proof-mazes.md`, which asks **how intended users should qualify without repeated bureaucratic exclusion**.

This note asks something narrower and later:

**once the action is offered and accepted, how should the system usually prove that the claim is real without drowning ordinary delivery in bespoke audit burdens?**

## The archive’s verification-form hierarchy

A future proposal is usually stronger when it fits most of this hierarchy.

### 1. Direct-data layer
Use direct metering, bills, operating data, installation records, retirement records, or other routine physical / administrative traces where they already exist and are good enough for the claim [S119][S123][S124][S144].

### 2. Standard-method layer
For repetitive measures, use standard methods, approved baselines, deemed savings, standard product classes, or common reporting templates where bespoke engineering proof would add more friction than value [S119][S124][S137].

### 3. Provider-facing layer
Put more of the reporting and verification burden on providers, programme operators, regulators, and procurement systems than on households, renters, small firms, or weak-capacity local institutions [S118][S119][S124][S137][S144].

### 4. Sampled-audit layer
For large-scale routine programmes, use sampling, spot checks, exception handling, and risk-based audit to preserve integrity without requiring every case to carry full bespoke proof [S118][S119][S124].

### 5. High-scrutiny layer
Reserve deeper custom verification, stronger assurance, and more intensive audit for high-value, novel, systemic, or highly gameable claims [S123][S124][S129][S130][S131].

## The archive’s verification-form tests

### 1. Proportionality test
Is the proof burden matched to the value, novelty, and gaming risk of the claim, or has every case been given a flagship-project evidentiary burden? [S123][S124][S129][S130]

### 2. Existing-data test
Could the design rely more on meters, bills, operating records, approved product classes, or other data already generated in normal operation? [S119][S123][S124][S144]

### 3. User-burden test
Who is being asked to assemble the proof — the provider and programme manager, or the constrained end user who was supposed to benefit? [S118][S119][S137][S144]

### 4. Replication test
Can the verification pathway survive scale across many similar actions, or does it collapse into consultant-heavy bespoke work when volumes rise? [S119][S124][S137]

### 5. Integrity-focus test
Is the tightest scrutiny aimed at the claims that are largest, easiest to game, or most commercially sensitive, rather than sprayed uniformly across everything? [S123][S124][S129][S130][S131]

### 6. Learning test
Does the verification design produce standardised information that can improve future procurement, contracts, and programme design, or does each project disappear into one-off paperwork? [S119][S123][S124][S130][S144]

## What this rules in

This note rules **in**:
- meter-based, bill-based, or operational-data verification where those traces already exist and are reasonably reliable [S119][S123][S144]
- standard methods, approved product categories, and common reporting templates for routine, high-volume actions [S119][S124][S137]
- sampled audit and exception handling for large portfolios where checking every small case at flagship-project intensity would destroy throughput [S118][S119][S124]
- provider-facing verification duties in performance contracting, procurement, utility programmes, and other settings where the seller or operator is better placed to carry proof obligations [S129][S130][S131][S144]
- tighter MRV and stronger assurance for high-value industrial claims, product-intensity claims, methane claims, carbon-market claims, and other areas where comparability and gaming risk are central [S11][S27][S65][S68][S123][S124]

## What this demotes

This note demotes:
- routine household or small-institution programmes that require bespoke modelling, consultant-heavy diligence, or repeated custom proof for every case [S118][S119][S137]
- verification systems that ask users to submit evidence the provider, utility, regulator, or programme already has or could gather more cheaply [S118][S119][S124]
- one-size-fits-all proof burdens that treat every small, standard action like a novel capital-market instrument or contested industrial benchmark [S123][S124][S129][S130]
- integrity systems that spray scarce audit effort across low-risk routine cases while under-scrutinising high-value or highly gameable claims [S123][S124][S129][S130][S131]
- climate delivery models that become so evidentiary-heavy that the administrative burden erases much of the social value of the underlying action [S118][S119][S137][S143]

## One-paragraph compression

**Ideal climate solutions should usually prove ordinary claims through ordinary proof paths: meters, operating data, bills, standard methods, approved product classes, and sampled audit where sensible. Put heavy verification where claims are large, novel, or easy to game, not on every small routine action. In the archive’s terms: use the lightest proof form that is still credible, and keep most of the evidentiary burden with providers and systems rather than with end users [S118][S119][S123][S124][S129][S130][S131][S137][S143][S144].**
