# 240 — Ideal Solutions: At Urbanizing Scale, Spatial Form, Proximity, and Transit-Supportive Growth Set a Material Part of Transport Demand, Infrastructure Cost, and Urban Lock-In Compact Canon

## Claim

The archive already has:
- a deeper cities note (`22`)
- a deeper mobility note (`11`)
- a deeper buildings note (`16`)
- a deeper delivery-capacity note (`18`)
- a copyability / standardisation note (`168`)
- a lower-overhead note (`173`)
- and, more recently, front-door tempo notes for grids, finance, institutions, uptake, protection, hardware, methane, nitrogen, cooling, flexibility, water, managed retreat, managed fossil decline, and efficiency (`226`–`239`)

That is already enough to imply that urban form matters.
What it still lacked was one smallest front-door note for a now-practical question:

**once hundreds of millions of people are still being urbanised, housed, and connected to jobs and services, what determines whether those new patterns lock in high travel demand, high infrastructure cost, and high exposure — or lower-overhead access instead?**

This note is not a new subtree.
It is a compression note.
Its job is to make one practical truth harder to miss at the front door:

**at urbanizing scale, spatial form, proximity, and transit-supportive growth set a material part of transport demand, infrastructure cost, and urban lock-in.**

## Fast rule

**Do not treat urban form as a background planning aesthetic after the “real” climate programme is designed. Treat land use, proximity, infill, reuse, transit-supportive density, safer walking and cycling access, and anti-sprawl discipline as climate-speed variables that decide how much mobility energy, road space, grid, water, and building overhead the programme must carry for decades** [S77][S80][S241][S242][S243].

## The compact canon

### 1. Bad spatial form enlarges almost every other climate problem
When homes, jobs, schools, commerce, and care are spread too far apart, the programme must serve longer trips, more paved surface, more vehicle dependence, and more network length just to deliver ordinary access.
That means more transport energy, more congestion, more infrastructure cost, and usually more exposure when growth spills into hotter, riskier, or weakly serviced places.

House rule: **treat access patterns as first-order climate design, not as neutral background geography.**

### 2. Compact, connected growth is a real emissions and service lever
OECD’s 2025 work on intermediary cities says compact urban forms, strong public transport, and safe non-motorised mobility reduce emissions and enhance quality of life [S241].
It adds that uncoordinated expansion often produces car dependence, congestion, and emissions, and argues that the goal is not to stop urbanisation but to shape it intentionally [S241].
IEA’s transport and city-policy work points the same way: planning compact urban development with improved public transport and infrastructure for micromobility and active travel is one of its core transport recommendations, and managing travel demand, distances travelled, and car dependency belongs inside the main transport decarbonisation package rather than outside it [S80][S243].

House rule: **rank compact access-forming growth ahead of sprawl that later has to be compensated for with bigger energy and transport systems.**

### 3. The real variable is not motion but access
OECD’s intermediary-cities work explicitly argues for moving from systems intense in mobility to systems that bring people, jobs, and services closer together [S241].
That is a useful archive compression. A city does not become better merely because more vehicles move through it faster. It becomes better when ordinary people can reach what matters with less distance, less friction, and less overhead.

House rule: **optimise for access and proximity first; demote transport strategies that only move the same bad geography faster.**

### 4. Spatial form also sets building and materials lock-in
OECD’s 2025 zero-carbon-buildings report says embodied emissions could account for around **half** of the total carbon footprint of new buildings by **2050** if left unaddressed, and only **21%** of countries responding to its survey currently have policies specifically targeting whole-life carbon [S242].
That matters here because urban form is not only a transport issue. It also decides how much new floor area, road surface, utility extension, and material throughput the programme must build in the first place, and whether new districts arrive in forms that are compact and serviceable or diffuse and infrastructure-hungry.

House rule: **treat urban expansion as a whole-life-carbon decision, not only as a zoning decision.**

### 5. City-level climate success depends on national-city alignment and usable local channels
IEA’s 2025 city-policy toolkit says national and city-level alignment is a key dimension of clean-energy transitions, that planning works best when integrated and cross-sectoral, and that targeted funding flows to cities can accelerate inclusive clean-energy transitions [S80].
It also says local regulations, smart data and metering, and green public procurement can unlock system-wide efficiency gains [S80].
So the archive should not romanticise “heroic localism.” The practical question is whether national rules, city powers, utility coordination, procurement, and finance actually let urban growth be shaped before bad patterns harden.

House rule: **no serious urban climate lane without national-city alignment, usable city finance, and joined local delivery channels.**

### 6. Transport efficiency is not just vehicle efficiency
IEA’s 2025 transport-efficiency work says transport still accounts for around **30%** of global energy demand, with road transport making up nearly **90%** of domestic transport energy demand [S243].
It adds that buses move people far more efficiently than private cars and that governments can accelerate progress not only through electrification and technical vehicle efficiency, but also by making efficient modes attractive and enabling modal shift [S243].
This sharpens the archive’s rule: a city that keeps lengthening trips and deepening car dependency cannot fully decarbonise just by swapping drivetrains.

House rule: **vehicle electrification is necessary, but demote transport strategies that ignore trip length, mode share, and spatial form.**

### 7. Every serious climate programme now needs a spatial packet
The archive already had deeper city and mobility doctrine.
This note adds the operating corollary: every serious climate programme should now carry an explicit **spatial packet** — anti-sprawl and infill rules where context supports them, transit-supportive housing and job growth, mixed-use zoning, safe walking and cycling access, bus priority and frequent transit, road-space reallocation where viable, flood- and heat-aware siting, and public-investment tests that rank proximity and infrastructure efficiency higher than peripheral lock-in [S77][S80][S241][S242][S243].

House rule: **no serious urban climate strategy without a spatial packet specific enough to inspect and ordinary enough to repeat.**

### 8. What this note demotes
This note demotes eight recurring mistakes:
- treating urban form as a cultural or aesthetic question rather than a climate-speed variable [S241][S243]
- allowing urban growth to lengthen trips and deepen car dependence while calling the result clean transport policy [S241][S243]
- assuming EV adoption can indefinitely compensate for sprawl and poor access geometry [S243]
- treating roads only as car-capacity assets rather than as public space that can be allocated across walking, cycling, buses, commerce, and shade [S241]
- treating housing, transport, and land use as separate files even when their lock-ins compound [S80][S241][S242]
- ignoring embodied carbon and materials overhead in new urban expansion [S242]
- leaving cities with targets but without usable finance, procurement power, or integrated data and planning tools [S80]
- waiting until car dependence is entrenched before acting, thereby making later reversals more expensive and politically harder [S241]
