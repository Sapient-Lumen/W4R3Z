# 180 — Ideal Solutions: Incentive Compatibility, Aligned Prices, Revenue Models, and Compensation Compact Canon

## Claim

The archive already has strong notes for:
- incentive-compatible systems as a deeper design rule (`43`)
- political economy / social contract (`131`)
- funding and burden allocation (`156`)
- control-point duties (`163`)
- lived experience / everyday fit (`160`)
- and multiple dividends / public gains (`167`)

What it still lacked was one joined first-stop note for a front-door question that keeps deciding whether the cleaner pathway can become ordinary behaviour instead of permanent moral exertion against hostile defaults:

**what should ideal climate solutions look like when the cleaner, more flexible, less wasteful, and more protective action must become the financially normal action for each decisive actor?**

This note is not a new doctrinal branch.
It is a compression note.
Its job is to gather the archive’s incentive-compatibility rule into one first-stop answer so the programme does not keep reopening the deeper doctrine, the funding canon, the social-contract canon, the lived-experience canon, and the control-point-duties canon every time a proposal is physically serious but still asks actors to do the right thing while the payoff structure keeps rewarding the wrong thing.

## Fast rule

**Prefer climate measures that make the cleaner, more flexible, less wasteful, and more protective action financially normal through aligned prices, targeted support, payment for useful system services, and explicit compensation where concentrated losses are real. Demote pathways that keep broad dirty subsidies in place, preserve split incentives, dump upfront cost and performance risk on constrained actors, or ask for repeated virtue against hostile payoffs** [S137][S138][S139][S58][S144].

## The compact canon

### 1. Incentive compatibility is delivery quality, not economist garnish
IEA’s affordability work says good climate design must account for split incentives, high upfront costs, and administrative complexity instead of treating uptake failure as a mystery or a character flaw [S137].

House rule: **rank climate pathways higher when the financially easier action for the decisive actor is also the cleaner and more protective one.**

### 2. Stop subsidising the wrong behaviour while claiming to correct it
IEA’s fossil-fuel-subsidy work says many subsidies remain elevated, poorly targeted, and discouraging to cleaner alternatives, while OECD’s 2025 growth-and-development synthesis says carbon revenues and subsidy reform can be recycled into low-carbon investment or household relief to increase acceptability [S139][S138].

House rule: **do not try to build strong clean uptake on top of broad dirty-price support that keeps telling households, firms, or utilities that the fossil baseline is still the protected option.**

### 3. Shift upfront cost and performance risk away from the weakest actor
IEA’s affordability work highlights high upfront costs and administrative complexity as major barriers, while the IEA’s 2026 ESCO commentary says verified-savings and performance-guarantee models can reduce upfront investment barriers and shift technical and performance risk away from consumers [S137][S144].

House rule: **prefer pathways that let households, small firms, or public institutions buy the clean result as a reliable service, managed package, or repay-over-time upgrade instead of demanding large cash exposure and technical confidence at the point of decision.**

### 4. Pay for useful system behaviour, not only for commodity throughput
IEA’s 2025 demand-flexibility work says flexibility contracts can pay households and businesses through direct payments, rebates, or bill credits for shifting or shedding load, and that flexibility can deliver capacity at much lower cost than building new capacity [S58].

House rule: **when the system needs flexibility, demand shifting, or peak relief, pay for those services explicitly rather than pretending actors will provide them for free while the tariff still rewards volume and timing that worsen system stress.**

### 5. Solve split incentives directly where they are structurally baked in
Landlord-tenant misalignment, builder-buyer separation, utility sales incentives, and fleet-procurement splits are not edge cases. They are recurring reasons the technically superior option loses even when lifecycle costs are better [S137].

House rule: **where the chooser is not the beneficiary, climate design should change standards, leases, tariffs, contract terms, procurement rules, or repayment form so the split is narrowed rather than moralised.**

### 6. Compensation belongs where losses are concentrated, but it should not become permanent dirty protection
OECD’s 2025 climate-growth work says revenue recycling and partial redistribution can limit distributive harm and strengthen policy acceptability [S138]. The archive’s own burden-allocation rule then sharpens the point: concentrated losses may justify explicit transition support, but not indefinite shielding of high-emissions defaults.

House rule: **compensate concentrated legitimate losses openly and with discipline, but do not mistake blanket fossil-price suppression or permanent dirty carve-outs for fair climate policy.**

### 7. Good incentives should reinforce lived experience, not fight it
If the cleaner pathway lowers bills only on paper while the user faces reimbursement mazes, unstable tariffs, or opaque performance risk, the incentive design is still weak. Incentive compatibility should show up in ordinary life as easier uptake and lower recurring burden, not only in policy diagrams [S137][S138][S144].

House rule: **prefer pathways whose incentive design is legible enough that ordinary users can feel the better deal without needing expert interpretation.**

## The archive’s incentive-compatibility screen

A future proposal should usually score higher when it passes most of these tests:

1. **Default-payoff test** — the financially easier or safer action for the decisive actor is also the cleaner one [S137][S139].  
2. **Wrong-price-cleanup test** — broad dirty subsidies or tariff structures that reward the wrong behaviour are reduced, narrowed, or offset rather than left untouched [S138][S139].  
3. **Split-incentive test** — the package directly addresses landlord-tenant, builder-buyer, utility-customer, or similar structural misalignments [S137].  
4. **Upfront-risk-shift test** — high upfront cost and technical-performance risk are moved away from actors least able to carry them [S137][S144].  
5. **System-service-payment test** — useful actions like demand shifting, flexibility, or verified savings are actually paid for where the system needs them [S58][S144].  
6. **Compensation-discipline test** — concentrated losses get explicit transitional support, but support is targeted, visible, and does not harden into permanent dirty protection [S138][S139].  
7. **Lived-deal test** — the better financial deal is visible enough in ordinary use that uptake does not depend on heroic literacy or trust [S137][S138][S144].  

## Preferred use

Open this note first when the live question is any of the following:
- what ideal climate solutions should look like when the cleaner option still loses because prices, tariffs, contracts, or business models reward the old pathway
- whether a proposal is physically right but still financially misaligned for households, landlords, firms, utilities, or fleet buyers
- how to think about split incentives, dirty subsidies, demand-flexibility payments, or targeted compensation in one joined rule
- whether a pathway is asking for repeated virtue against hostile payoffs instead of making the right action financially normal
- how to decide when compensation is legitimate and when it has turned into permanent protection for inferior defaults

Then reopen the deeper note that matches the surviving sub-question.

## Compression rule

**Ideal solutions should not depend on permanent heroism: they should make the cleaner action the better deal, pay for useful system behaviour, remove or narrow dirty price distortions, shift risk away from constrained actors, and compensate concentrated losses without freezing the old regime in place.**
