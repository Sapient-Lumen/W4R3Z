# 99 — Ideal Solutions: Seven Action Verbs for a Serious Climate Program

## Claim

The archive already has:
- a one-page answer (`58`)
- a compact package note (`57`)
- an acceptance-tests note (`97`)
- and a positive design-grammar note (`98`)

What it still lacked was the shortest **action grammar** for the question:

**What do ideal climate solutions mostly do, in concrete verbs simple enough to remember and reuse?**

That matters because many readers do not first need more sectors, more tests, or more meta-rules.
They need the shortest set of actions that a serious climate programme should usually contain.

This note is a **compression note**, not a new doctrinal branch.
Its job is to reduce the archive to seven reusable action verbs.

## Fast rule

**Ideal solutions to global warming should usually build clean backbones, cut waste, electrify routine demand, slash methane, retire fossil dependence, protect people and ecosystems, and reserve scarce tools for hard residuals.**

## Seven action verbs

### 1. Build
Build the clean backbone at the center of the system.

What this usually means:
- abundant clean electricity
- transmission, distribution, storage, interconnection, and flexibility
- public, regulated, and institutional capacity that lets the cleaner system actually connect, spread, and stay reliable [S100][S101][S102][S103][S117]

Why it matters:
- the transition fails without real substitute infrastructure

### 2. Cut
Cut waste before asking scarce clean supply to carry wasteful demand.

What this usually means:
- insulation, passive performance, efficient cooling, efficient motors, and load reduction
- compact urban form and other demand-shaping choices that lower the energy and material burden of delivering the same service
- removal of obviously wasteful high-footprint uses where they are easiest to shrink [S104][S106][S111][S117]

Why it matters:
- ideal solutions solve the service with less throughput first

### 3. Electrify
Electrify the large share of ordinary end uses that already have strong direct clean substitutes.

What this usually means:
- heat pumps, EVs, electric motors, electric cooking, and other direct-use substitutions
- pairing electrification with smarter operation, flexible demand, and building upgrades rather than treating equipment swap alone as the whole answer
- keeping scarce clean fuels out of routine uses that can already run on electricity [S100][S103][S104][S112][S165][S166][S167]

Why it matters:
- direct electrification is usually the cleanest, simplest, and least wasteful replacement lane for ordinary services

### 4. Slash
Slash methane and other fast-forcing leaks on an emergency timescale.

What this usually means:
- measured leak detection and repair in oil and gas
- strong control of venting and flaring
- landfill, wastewater, and agrifood methane action with real enforcement and measurable follow-through [S101][S105][S133]

Why it matters:
- ideal solutions should take the fast pressure-relief lane where it exists

### 5. Retire
Retire, constrain, or phase down dirty systems deliberately rather than waiting for them to fade politely.

What this usually means:
- coal retirement and no-new-lock-in rules where feasible
- subsidy reform, cleanup liability, and clear exit paths for temporary dirty bridges
- transition arrangements for workers, regions, and exposed users so retirement is politically and socially workable [S100][S102][S108][S109][S110]

Why it matters:
- clean addition without dirty subtraction is too slow and too vulnerable to lock-in

### 6. Protect
Protect people, ecosystems, and exposed places directly, not only through future emissions benefit.

What this usually means:
- adaptation as civil protection: cooling safety, water security, flood and fire resilience, health-system readiness, and exposure reduction
- protection and restoration of forests, peatlands, wetlands, and other ecosystems that buffer risk and avoid avoidable emissions
- treating food, water, land, and care systems as core climate infrastructure rather than side issues [S104][S105][S106][S107][S117]

Why it matters:
- ideal solutions must reduce harm during the transition, not only promise a cleaner end state later

### 7. Reserve
Reserve scarce tools for the hard residual uses that truly need them.

What this usually means:
- keep scarce clean molecules, biomass, carbon capture, and removals for difficult industrial processes, long-distance transport niches, and genuine residual emissions
- refuse to spend scarce options on easy uses that already have cheaper and cleaner substitutes
- keep removals in a residual lane rather than letting them excuse weak near-term abatement [S100][S103][S112][S165][S166][S167]

Why it matters:
- an ideal solution is not just effective; it is resource-disciplined

## Screening shortcut

A proposal is usually weak when it tries to:
- skip **build** and talk mainly in targets or credits
- skip **cut** and feed wasteful demand with heroic new supply
- skip **electrify** and jump straight to scarce fuels
- skip **slash** and leave methane to later
- skip **retire** and assume incumbents will disappear on their own
- skip **protect** and treat present harm as secondary
- skip **reserve** and spend scarce tools on easy cases
