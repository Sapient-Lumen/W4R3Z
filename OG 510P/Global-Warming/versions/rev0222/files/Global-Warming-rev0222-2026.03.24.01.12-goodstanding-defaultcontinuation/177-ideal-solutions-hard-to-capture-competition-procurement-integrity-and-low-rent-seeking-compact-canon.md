What it still lacked was one joined first-stop note for a front-door question that keeps deciding whether a climate programme stays publicly defensible once money, contracts, permits, queues, and regulated privileges get large:

**what should ideal climate solutions look like when they must be hard to loot, cartelise, quietly steer, or freeze through capture rather than merely technically sound on paper?**

This note is not a new doctrinal branch.
It is a compression note.
Its job is to gather the archive’s anti-capture rule into one first-stop answer so the programme does not keep reopening the deeper doctrine, the political-economy canon, and the control-point canon every time a proposal looks physically sensible but institutionally easy to rig.

## Fast rule

**Prefer climate measures that are contestable, schedulable, transparent, auditable, and difficult to monopolise or collude around. Demote pathways that depend on bespoke discretionary deals, opaque subsidy allocation, unstable stop-go rules, hidden contract drift, or narrow insider gatekeeping over essential transition lanes** [S129][S130][S131][S132].

## The compact canon

### 1. Contestability is part of climate design, not administrative garnish
A climate portfolio is less ideal when only a tiny club can realistically bid, build, supply, finance, aggregate, or interconnect the work.
The 2025 OECD bid-rigging guidelines stress that procurement design should make collusion harder to establish and maintain, while the 2025 OECD public-procurement implementation report still highlights persistent challenges around competition, transparency, and risk management [S129][S130].

House rule: **prefer pathways with genuine room for competing entrants, open standards, and repeatable access routes over pathways that quietly manufacture a gatekeeper class.**

### 2. Stable public calendars beat improvised one-off deals
Climate buildout gets easier to capture when delivery arrives through sporadic bespoke announcements, emergency exceptions, or retroactive rule changes.
IEA’s auction guidance says governments should provide long-term schedules of regular auctions tailored to ambition because predictable competitive procurement improves investment certainty and helps supply chains and skills scale [S132].

House rule: **when procurement or auctions are part of the lane, prefer regular calendars, known award logic, and durable eligibility rules over improvisation.**

### 3. Transparency has to survive beyond announcement and award
A programme can look clean at launch and become dirty during implementation.
The OECD’s 2025 procurement report says pre-tender and post-contract transparency remain uneven, and the World Bank’s procurement framework points to value for money with integrity, supported by complaint routes, conflict-of-interest guidance, and beneficial-ownership disclosure tools [S130][S131].

House rule: **treat integrity as a lifecycle property — visible rules before tender, intelligible award logic, auditable delivery, and scrutiny of amendments, not just ribbon-cutting transparency.**

### 4. Standardization is not only about scale; it is also about anti-favouritism
The archive already likes standard offers, common documents, one-stop processes, and repeatable procedures because they reduce friction.
This note adds the anti-capture side: they also reduce room for hidden favouritism, tailor-made exemptions, and insider-only interpretation [S130][S131].

House rule: **prefer climate pathways that can use standard documents, common procurement forms, open criteria, and repeatable governance instead of hand-built special deals.**

### 5. Publicly enabled gain should come with public-facing obligation
When public power creates the value — through permits, regulated privilege, de-risking, procurement, subsidies, land-use decisions, or queue access — ideal solutions should not let the resulting upside disappear into opaque rents.
That obligation is strengthened, not weakened, when the state is helping create the market [S130][S131][S132].

House rule: **if public coordination helps create the gain, some material share should show up as lower cost, wider access, better terms, host benefit, open standards, or reusable public learning.**

### 6. Anti-capture design protects speed and legitimacy together
A climate programme that looks like a racket becomes easier to stall from many directions at once.
Higher prices, lower quality, thin competition, insider exceptions, and unexplained contract drift do not only waste money; they make the programme publicly easier to discredit [S129][S130][S131].

House rule: **anti-capture design is not a side ethics layer; it is part of throughput, cost control, and coalition durability.**

## The archive’s anti-capture screen

A future proposal should usually score higher when it passes most of these tests:

1. **Contestability test** — more than a tiny incumbent club can realistically enter, compete, or replicate the lane [S129][S130].  
2. **Calendar test** — the lane can run through visible recurring auctions, procurements, or programme windows instead of improvised discretionary deals [S130][S132].  
3. **Transparency test** — award logic, contract terms, change orders, complaint paths, and ownership interests are legible enough to audit [S130][S131].  
4. **Integrity-controls test** — conflict-of-interest rules, beneficial-ownership disclosure, complaint routes, and anti-collusion discipline are present where money or permits are being allocated [S129][S131].  
5. **Lifecycle-oversight test** — scrutiny survives through contract management, amendment, delivery, and operation rather than stopping at announcement or award [S130][S131].  
6. **Public-defensibility test** — a reasonable outsider could understand why this supplier, project, or support flow won and why the public should believe the process was fair [S129][S130].

## Compression rule

**Ideal solutions should be built to stay hard to capture: contestable where possible, regular rather than improvised, transparent through the full contract lifecycle, protected by anti-collusion and conflict-of-interest controls, and structured so public authority creates public-facing value instead of a permanent insider tollbooth.**
