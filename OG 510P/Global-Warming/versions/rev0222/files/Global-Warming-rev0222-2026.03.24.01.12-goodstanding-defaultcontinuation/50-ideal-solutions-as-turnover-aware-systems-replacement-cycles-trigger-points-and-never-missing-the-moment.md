# 50 — Ideal Solutions as Turnover-Aware Systems: Replacement Cycles, Trigger Points, and Never Missing the Moment

## Claim

Ideal climate solutions should be **turnover-aware**.

They should attach themselves to the moments when assets are already being bought, sold, rented, financed, repaired, renovated, rebuilt, or retired.
A solution is not ideal if it requires every household, landlord, firm, fleet manager, or ministry to act in a policy vacuum divorced from the moments when capital stock actually changes.

This is not the same as the archive’s low-friction rule.
`36-ideal-solutions-as-low-friction-systems-defaults-standardization-and-administrative-simplicity.md` asks whether the cleaner option is the easier option.
This note asks whether the intervention arrives at the **moment when a real decision is already happening**.

It is also not the same as the short-latency rule.
`41-ideal-solutions-as-short-latency-systems-fast-physical-effects-and-early-benefit-capture.md` asks whether benefits arrive quickly.
This note asks whether policy is timed to the **natural clocks of stock turnover, transaction cycles, and recovery windows**.

And it is not a blanket argument for waiting.
Sometimes delay itself is the lock-in.
If an old boiler, inefficient appliance, diesel truck, exposed building, or vulnerable settlement would impose too much harm by remaining in place, ideal policy should accelerate replacement or retirement.
The narrower claim is:

**where assets are going to change anyway, ideal portfolios should be engineered so the climate-positive option wins at that trigger point instead of missing the moment and locking in another dirty or fragile cycle.**

## Why this belongs in the canon

The archive already prefers low-friction delivery, short latency, fiscal durability, copyability, and legitimacy.
Turnover-awareness strengthens all five.

OECD’s 2025 real-estate work makes the trigger-point logic explicit: Minimum Energy Performance Standards can use **property sales or rentals** as trigger points, embedding compliance within routine transaction cycles and requiring disclosure at the moment financial decisions are made [S161].
IEA’s 2025 buildings toolkit says replacing fossil-fuel boilers with high-efficiency heat pumps can cut energy use by up to **75%**, and ties this to doubling retrofit rates and expanding code coverage rather than waiting for abstract long-run market drift [S162].
IEA’s 2025 EV Outlook adds the fleet-turnover version of the same lesson: in China, continued incentives for **replacing older vehicles** plus falling EV prices are expected to push electric cars to around **60% of total car sales** in 2025 [S163].
UNDRR’s 2025 recovery-readiness guidance makes the disruption-window version explicit: governments that prepare in advance can seize the **narrow post-disaster window of opportunity** to build back better rather than simply rebuilding previous vulnerability [S164].

So the archive needs a tighter rule:

**prefer climate measures that ride normal replacement, transaction, and recovery cycles — and distrust strategies that repeatedly miss those windows and then ask for more disruptive, more expensive correction later.**

## Why ideal portfolios need turnover-awareness

### 1. Most of the world changes at decision moments, not continuously

Buildings do not retrofit themselves continuously.
Cars do not electrify continuously.
Appliances do not improve continuously.
Leases, mortgages, public procurement rounds, boiler failures, roof replacements, fleet renewals, permit renewals, tenant turnover, and disaster recovery all create concentrated moments when choices become live.

A serious climate archive should care about those moments because they are when default options, finance, standards, and disclosure rules can bite hardest with the least friction.
OECD’s 2025 property work is useful precisely because it shows how climate requirements can be attached to routine transactions instead of treated as a separate exceptional event [S161].

### 2. Trigger-point policy often lowers cost and political pain

If policy intervenes when an asset is already due for replacement or a building is already being sold, rented, re-roofed, refinanced, or renovated, it can avoid some of the waste and backlash associated with premature scrappage.
That does not make the transition free.
It makes it more synchronized with how households, firms, and governments already make capital decisions.

This is one reason the archive should prefer:
- boiler and heater replacement rules that steer failed or aging equipment toward clean heat rather than letting one emergency swap lock in another fossil cycle [S162]
- appliance standards and replacement incentives that bite when households are already buying new equipment [S146][S162]
- fleet-turnover incentives, standards, and procurement rules that hit the moment when vehicles are actually being renewed [S134][S163]
- property-sale, rental, refinance, and renovation triggers that pull building performance into routine decisions rather than relying only on generic exhortation [S161]

### 3. Trigger points are also information and enforcement moments

A climate rule works better when it meets a moment of paperwork, valuation, inspection, financing, or registration.
That is when hidden defects surface, prices are renegotiated, disclosures matter, and compliance is easier to verify.

OECD’s 2025 analysis says trigger points such as sale or rental help address information asymmetries because energy performance is disclosed the moment a financial decision is being made [S161].
That is not just a buildings fact.
It generalises.
The best climate rules often attach themselves to:
- sale or rental
- permit renewal
- mortgage origination or refinance
- procurement and tender cycles
- insurance renewal
- fleet renewal
- post-disaster reconstruction

Turnover-aware policy therefore strengthens the archive’s measurement and physicality rules.
The system sees more, earlier, and at the moment action is most feasible.

### 4. Recovery windows matter because rebuilding repeats or repairs the past

Post-disaster reconstruction is one of the clearest examples of climate timing.
When homes, public buildings, feeders, clinics, roads, or water systems are already being rebuilt, governments can either restore yesterday’s vulnerability or capture the moment to improve resilience and reduce future emissions.

UNDRR’s 2025 recovery-readiness work says recovery preparedness allows governments to seize the narrow post-disaster window to build back better instead of reproducing previous vulnerabilities [S164].
That belongs in the canon because climate strategy that ignores reconstruction timing will repeatedly spend public money to reinstall heat risk, flood risk, diesel dependence, weak envelopes, and exposed infrastructure.

### 5. Turnover-awareness complements short latency, low friction, and fiscal durability

This note is best read as a bridge across several existing rules.

- It supports **short latency** because benefits often arrive fastest when policy rides decisions already about to happen.
- It supports **low friction** because people are more likely to switch when they are already buying, renovating, or rebuilding.
- It supports **fiscal durability** because public support aimed at trigger points can be more targeted than blanket indefinite subsidy.
- It supports **legitimacy** because people are more willing to accept stronger standards when they arrive through transparent transition pathways and predictable moments rather than surprise one-off shocks.

### 6. “Wait for turnover” is not always a serious answer

The archive needs one warning here.
Turnover-awareness can be abused as an excuse for delay.
Sometimes the asset life is too long, the damage too large, or the risk too immediate to wait.
The archive therefore keeps a two-part discipline:

- **ride turnover where the timing is near and the lock-in cost of waiting is acceptable**
- **accelerate retirement or replacement where the lock-in cost of waiting is not acceptable**

For example:
- a sales or rental trigger for building upgrades can be sensible [S161]
- a failed boiler should generally be replaced with clean heat, not another fossil unit [S162]
- an extremely exposed repeatedly flooded building may require buyout or retreat, not “wait until the next owner” [S81][S82][S83][S84]
- old high-use fleet vehicles may deserve accelerated scrappage or targeted incentives because their remaining emissions are large and near-term [S134][S163]

## The archive’s turnover-aware screen

A future proposal should usually be promoted only if it passes most of the following tests.

### 1. Decision-moment test
Does the proposal attach itself to a real decision point — purchase, sale, rental, refinance, renovation, procurement, renewal, failure, or rebuild — rather than waiting for detached voluntary action? [S161][S162][S163][S164]

### 2. Never-miss-the-moment test
If the relevant turnover moment passes, does the system risk locking in another long-lived dirty or fragile asset cycle? If yes, the proposal deserves higher priority. [S161][S162][S163]

### 3. Lower-disruption test
Does the proposal reduce scrappage waste, hassle, and political pain by aligning with natural replacement or renovation cycles where feasible? [S161][S162]

### 4. Targeted-public-money test
Can public finance be concentrated at the trigger point — where it changes the choice most — rather than spread thinly as permanent general support? [S162][S163][S164]

### 5. Recovery-window test
Does the proposal prepare in advance so post-disaster reconstruction, public-asset renewal, or emergency replacement becomes a better build rather than a replay of the old vulnerability? [S164]

### 6. Don’t-wait-too-long test
Has the proposal identified where natural turnover is too slow or too dangerous, so accelerated retirement, mandatory upgrade, or managed exit is justified instead of passive waiting? [S81][S82][S83][S84][S162]

Measures that pass four or five of these tests deserve promotion. Measures that fail most of them should usually be demoted unless they solve a genuinely continuous-flow problem where turnover logic is less relevant.

## What this rules in and out

This note rules **in**:
- building standards and upgrade triggers tied to sale, rental, major renovation, refinancing, or equipment failure
- heat-pump and clean-heat support aimed at boiler replacement moments
- appliance standards and replacement incentives aimed at natural purchase cycles
- EV, bus, truck, and municipal-fleet rules that focus on renewal schedules and high-use vehicles first
- resilience standards and finance that are ready before disasters so reconstruction upgrades rather than reinstalls failure
- public-procurement cycles that convert routine replacement into decarbonization and resilience gains

It rules **out** or at least demotes:
- strategies that miss repeated purchase and renovation windows and then hope for heroic mass correction later
- policies that allow one more long-lived fossil or fragile asset at the moment of replacement on the theory that it can be cleaned up in the next cycle
- climate programmes that are blind to lease cycles, refinance cycles, procurement calendars, and disaster-recovery windows
- “wait for natural turnover” arguments where the natural turnover horizon is too long to be compatible with climate safety or exposure reduction

## Compression

**Ideal climate solutions should arrive when real decisions are already being made: at sale, rental, replacement, renovation, procurement, renewal, and rebuild. Missing those moments is one of the quiet ways societies lock in another generation of emissions and vulnerability.**
