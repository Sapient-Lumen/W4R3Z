What it still lacked was the shortest **burden-of-proof note** for the question:

**Once authorities collide, who has to prove what before a dirtier, slower, or more weakly governed path is allowed?**

That matters because many climate systems fail not only by making the wrong decision, but by assigning the proof burden to the wrong side.
The clean mainline is forced to prove itself again and again, while delay, waiver, dirty fallback, or prolonged exception is treated as the low-evidence default.
A compact archive needed one short bridge from **precedence** to **admissibility under stress**.

This note is a **compression note**, not a new doctrinal branch.
Its job is to reduce a large family of recurring arguments to five proof burdens that a serious climate state should place on any actor asking for dirtier, slower, or more weakly disciplined operation [S9][S23][S24][S55][S84][S85][S103][S104][S111][S114][S145][S146][S147][S181][S182][S183].

## Fast rule

**The actor asking for dirty delay, fallback, or exception bears the burden to prove necessity, lack of a cleaner near-term alternative, tight containment, explicit exit, and full visibility of who pays, who decides, and who is exposed.**

## Five burdens of proof

### 1. Necessity burden
The actor asking for delay, dirty fallback, waiver, or looser discipline should have to show that the request is genuinely needed, not merely more convenient, more familiar, or less politically costly.

What this usually means:
- inconvenience is not necessity
- incumbent comfort is not necessity
- ordinary underdelivery is not necessity if the real problem is neglected maintenance, poor queue reform, weak staffing, or avoidable coordination failure
- the request should describe the concrete failure condition it claims to solve, not only the preferred remedy [S14][S24][S84][S103][S104][S147]

Why it matters:
- if necessity is presumed instead of demonstrated, exception pressure becomes the standing operating system
- serious climate governance cannot let dirty fallback inherit the status of common sense

### 2. Cleaner-alternative burden
The actor asking for the off-normal path should have to show that a cleaner or less harmful near-term alternative is not feasible in the relevant time window.

What this usually means:
- the claimant should show why available clean substitutes, demand shaping, flexibility, targeted repair, shielding, queue reform, reserve use, or temporary conservation cannot solve the problem fast enough
- “not ideal” is not the same as “not available enough to matter”
- the comparison set should include operational fixes and partial substitutes, not only perfect replacements [S14][S17][S24][S30][S84][S103][S104]

Why it matters:
- many bad climate decisions survive by pretending the only alternative to a dirty bridge is an unrealistically perfect clean system
- a serious state should require proof that the less harmful bridge really is the least harmful available bridge

### 3. Containment burden
If an off-normal path is admitted, the claimant should have to prove that it is tightly contained in scope, duration, geography, and emissions or harm.

What this usually means:
- keep the exception as narrow as the problem actually requires
- avoid turning local stress into system-wide relaxation
- tie the measure to a bounded window, bounded set of users, or bounded operating condition rather than a vague emergency atmosphere
- prefer the least damaging backup, used the least amount, for the shortest credible interval [S84][S85][S87][S103][S104][S107]

Why it matters:
- most sticky loopholes begin as broad or weakly bounded exceptions
- containment is what separates emergency use from policy drift

### 4. Exit burden
The actor asking for the off-normal path should have to prove how it ends and what returns the system to the cleaner mainline.

What this usually means:
- name the closure trigger, review date, and decision owner up front
- show the practical route back to the clean path rather than assuming recovery will happen by goodwill
- explain what must be repaired, built, financed, or enforced so the same request does not recur by inertia
- do not treat “temporary” as self-proving [S86][S87][S103][S104][S107][S147]

Why it matters:
- the easiest way to make dirty fallback durable is to approve it without an exit discipline
- a serious climate state should demand a return path before it approves a detour

### 5. Visibility-and-liability burden
The actor asking for delay, fallback, or exception should have to make visible who decides, who benefits, who bears the downside, who is exposed, and who pays for correction.

What this usually means:
- the request should state the accountable owner and the independent metric that will judge success or failure
- affected users or places should not absorb hidden risk while decision makers keep broad discretion and vague obligations
- public subsidy or public tolerance should not flow without public-facing disclosure and a correction plan
- if the claimant wants flexibility, the claimant should also accept auditability, review, and correction obligations [S68][S83][S103][S104][S105][S106][S107][S111][S114]

Why it matters:
- invisible exceptions are politically easy to renew and administratively hard to unwind
- when liability is blurred, the easiest path is to socialise downside and privatise convenience

## Minimal admissibility gate

The smallest serious gate for dirty delay, fallback, or exception claims is:

- **prove necessity**
- **prove no cleaner near-term option can do enough**
- **prove strict containment**
- **prove a real exit path**
- **prove visibility of owner, exposure, and correction liability**

If one of these gates fails, the request should usually be denied, narrowed, or sent back for redesign rather than treated as the default realistic option.
