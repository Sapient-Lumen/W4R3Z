# 09 — Managed Fossil Decline

## Claim

The climate solution is incomplete unless it contains an **explicit retirement architecture for fossil assets**.

Buildout alone does not guarantee fast enough emissions decline. Clean investment is now roughly **twice** fossil investment globally [S6], and COP28 established a multilateral direction to **transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems** while phasing down unabated coal and phasing out inefficient fossil-fuel subsidies [S9]. But public support for fossil fuels still remained around **USD 916.3 billion in 2024**, and much of it still distorted markets and weakened transition incentives [S23].

The archive’s house view is therefore simple: **the ideal climate program builds clean systems and retires fossil systems on purpose**.

## Why the archive upgrades this lane

The archive already treated managed decline as necessary. This note makes the doctrine more explicit because three mistakes recur:

1. **Buildout-only optimism** — assuming fossil assets will quietly disappear once clean alternatives get cheaper.
2. **Service blindness** — planning to replace annual fossil energy volumes while forgetting reliability, flexibility, industrial heat, revenues, jobs, and cleanup liabilities.
3. **Justice vagueness** — saying “just transition” without concrete wages, pensions, regional investment, land rehabilitation, or closure finance.

A practical retirement doctrine has to solve all three.

## House doctrine

### 1. Coal is the first major retirement lane
Coal power is usually the most emissions-intensive major fossil asset class and often the clearest retirement target. IEA’s coal-transition work argues that governments need to scale up replacements not only for the electricity coal plants produce, but also for the **system services** they provide [S24]. In its Announced Pledges Scenario, repurposing coal plants to run less and more flexibly delivers about **60%** of coal-power CO2 savings, while **early retirements** deliver a further **33%** [S24].

### 2. Stop creating new long-lived unabated lock-in
The archive defaults against new long-lived unabated coal and against new oil-and-gas expansion justified mainly by hypothetical future cleanup. Where energy-security exceptions exist, they should be narrow, time-limited, and paired with explicit exit plans [S9].

### 3. Replace functions, not slogans
Fossil retirement succeeds when clean systems replace the functions fossil assets currently perform:
- electricity generation
- peak capacity and balancing
- industrial heat and feedstocks where relevant
- public revenue in fossil-dependent regions
- jobs and local demand
- system reliability during transition phases

This is why managed decline must be coupled to grids, storage, demand flexibility, industrial policy, and regional development rather than framed as a moral demand alone [S4][S24].

### 4. Subsidy reform should protect people, not fossil dependence
OECD’s 2025 inventory says the fiscal cost of government support for fossil fuels fell in 2024 but still remained around **USD 916.3 billion**, elevated relative to historical norms [S23]. The archive’s rule is to replace broad fossil support with:
- targeted household protection
- income support for exposed workers
- public investment in clean substitutes
- affordability measures that do not preserve structural fossil dependence

### 5. Closure and cleanup are part of the transition
A real retirement program includes decommissioning funds, orphan-well and mine-site cleanup, environmental remediation, and legal clarity over who pays. Otherwise societies inherit stranded hazards while private actors bank prior profits.

### 6. “Just transition” must be budgeted, not merely declared
IEA reports that at the end of 2023 only about **14%** of coal workers in coal-dependent countries were covered by just-transition policies [S25]. This is strategically dangerous. Workers and regions should see wage support, pension protection, retraining, regional investment, and public employment or rehabilitation pathways before closures are forced.

## Minimum package for fossil decline policy

A serious climate program should include at least:
- plant-by-plant coal retirement schedules where alternatives can be built in time
- no new unabated coal except narrow emergency backstops with sunset clauses
- subsidy reform that swaps generalized fossil support for targeted protection [S23]
- coal transition compacts, securitization, regulated-asset write-downs, or public-interest buyouts where these genuinely accelerate closure
- decommissioning and remediation requirements for wells, mines, and fossil infrastructure
- worker and regional transition funds with real fiscal backing [S25]
- planning for replacement system services: storage, transmission, demand response, clean firm capacity where needed [S24]
- explicit treatment of methane, flaring, and abandonment liabilities in oil and gas systems [S11]

## What the archive rejects

- pretending that clean buildout alone automatically retires incumbents
- keeping broad fossil subsidies in place while calling the system “technology neutral”
- closures without worker protection or regional transition planning
- using speculative future CCS or CDR to justify routine expansion today
- forcing poor households to absorb transition costs while fossil rents remain protected

## Compression

**Buildout without retirement leaves incumbents alive; retirement without replacement breaks systems; just transition without money is rhetoric. The real solution is all three together.**
