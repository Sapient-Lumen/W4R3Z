# 120 — Ideal Solutions: Five Anti-Churn Rules So Readiness Status Does Not Flap

## Claim

The archive now has:
- a readiness-status ladder (`117`)
- a readiness-promotion note (`118`)
- and a readiness-demotion note (`119`)

What it still lacked was the shortest **anti-churn rule** for the practical governance question:

**How should a serious climate state change readiness status without flapping, gaming, or ceremonial oscillation?**

That gap matters because once an archive can classify states, allow upgrades, and force downgrades, it can still fail in a subtler way.
Institutions can upgrade on one good exercise, delay demotion until embarrassment becomes unavoidable, relabel the same unstable condition every few weeks, or treat status changes as public-relations events rather than evidence-bound operating decisions.
A compact archive needed one short note saying that readiness labels should move with **deliberate asymmetry**:
**slow to upgrade, fast to downgrade, impossible to cosmetically churn, and always tied to the next proof date.**
Without that bridge, the readiness cluster can still be captured by mood, politics, or optics even when its tests are well written.

This note is a **compression note**, not a new doctrinal branch.
Its job is to make the readiness ladder behave like a serious state machine rather than a reputational scoreboard [S34][S55][S73][S83][S102][S103][S111][S112][S113][S114][S117][S118][S119][S145][S147].

## Fast rule

**Make readiness status hard to upgrade, easy to downgrade, and impossible to churn cosmetically. Require sustained proof for promotion, immediate downgrade on material doubt or live detour, and a named next-review date for every status change.**

## The five anti-churn rules

### 1. Promotion requires sustained proof, not one clean signal
A stronger status should not be awarded on the basis of a single successful exercise, a quiet week, or one repaired indicator.

What this looks like:
- more than one proof source points the same way
- the evidence covers the whole critical path rather than one healthy segment
- proof survives at least one meaningful operating cycle instead of a cherry-picked calm interval [S55][S83][S113][S114][S118][S145]

Default consequence:
- no upgrade until the stronger claim is supported by **sustained**, not merely fresh, proof

### 2. Demotion happens faster than promotion by design
Promotion and demotion should not use identical evidence windows.
A serious climate state should demand more proof to claim strength than to admit that strength no longer governs.

What this looks like:
- stale proof, a broken critical join, a governing gap, or a tripwire can trigger immediate downgrade
- the archive does not wait for several confirming failures before protecting against overclaim
- the burden of delay falls on the actor defending the stronger status, not on the actor arguing for caution [S83][S103][S108][S114][S118][S119]

Default consequence:
- **upgrade slowly, downgrade quickly**

### 3. A live detour suppresses cosmetic relabeling
Once fallback, exception, or escalation is already open, the system should not keep toggling among stronger readiness labels as though the detour were a side note.

What this looks like:
- local successes inside the incident do not justify re-promoting the overall state while detour governance is still carrying the mission
- the status remains **live detour** until closure tests are passed and after-action hardening is underway [S103][S104][S109][S110][S112][S117][S119]

Default consequence:
- no cosmetic upgrade while the detour is still governing in fact

### 4. Every status change needs a cause, an owner, and a next proof date
A status label should never change without leaving an operating trace.

What this looks like:
- the reason for the status change is written plainly
- a named owner is responsible either for preserving the stronger state or repairing the weaker one
- the next proof date is explicit, so the label cannot drift indefinitely on inertia [S34][S55][S83][S102][S114][S117]

Default consequence:
- if the archive cannot say **why the status changed, who owns it now, and when it must be checked again**, the change is incomplete

### 5. Repeated toggling is evidence that the higher state is not governing
If a system keeps bouncing between statuses over a short interval, that instability is itself information.
The archive should treat recurrent toggling as a sign that the stronger label is not yet stable enough to govern.

What this looks like:
- repeated promotion and demotion over adjacent review cycles
- readiness claims that depend on short-lived repairs, borrowed effort, or lucky periods
- disagreement about status that persists because the underlying evidence base is not yet stable [S83][S102][S113][S114][S117][S118][S119][S145]

Default consequence:
- hold the **more conservative** state until the cause of oscillation is repaired and the stronger claim survives a credible interval

## Minimal anti-churn map

The smallest serious anti-churn map is:

- **upgrade only on sustained proof**
- **downgrade on first material evidence that the stronger state no longer governs**
- **freeze cosmetic promotion while a live detour is still open**
- **attach every status change to a cause, owner, and next proof date**
- **treat repeated toggling as evidence for holding the more conservative state** [S83][S102][S103][S112][S114][S117][S118][S119][S145][S147]

## Conservative default

If a team wants promotion and demotion to use the same burden and timing because asymmetry feels unfair, it is probably protecting reputation more than it is protecting the mission.
The archive should prefer:
- **asymmetric caution over symmetric vanity**
- **status stability over label churn**
- **evidence windows over mood swings**
- **the more conservative state until proof has truly settled** [S83][S102][S114][S116][S117][S118][S119][S147]
