# 25 — Global South Finance, Cost of Capital, and Debt Space

## Claim

A large part of the remaining climate challenge in emerging markets and developing economies (EMDE) is now a **finance-architecture problem**, not mainly a technology problem.

The archive already said this in passing. This revision makes it explicit: **if clean projects in EMDE face high financing costs, hard-currency mismatch, thin local capital markets, weak guarantee capacity, and debt stress, then even mature technologies arrive too slowly and too expensively to solve global warming at world scale.**

IEA says the cost of capital for utility-scale solar PV projects in EMDE is **well over twice** as high as in advanced economies [S13][S91]. The same IEA report says annual clean-energy investment in EMDE needs to rise from about **USD 270 billion** today to **USD 870 billion** by the early 2030s to align with national climate and energy pledges, and to **USD 1.6 trillion** in a 1.5°C pathway [S91]. OECD adds that, if current investment growth trends persist, EMDE other than China face a cumulative **USD 10 trillion** climate-transition investment shortfall by 2050 [S92]. IMF says many EMDE still face **high financing costs, large external refinancing needs, and declining net external flows** even before adding the climate investment requirement [S93].

So the archive’s conclusion is now harder-edged: **the transition is not only blocked by missing projects or missing technologies. It is blocked by expensive money, currency mismatch, debt pressure, and insufficient risk-reduction architecture.**

## Why the archive upgrades this lane

The archive already had strong notes on grids, buildings, affordability, delivery capacity, and adaptation. But one cross-cutting implementation bottleneck still remained under-specified: why clean deployment that is already technically mature often remains financially non-viable or politically thin in the places where future demand growth is largest.

That gap now matters because:
- most of the needed EMDE clean-energy buildout is in already-deployable technologies rather than speculative ones [S91]
- financing costs can make clean projects uneconomic even when their underlying technology is mature [S13][S91]
- hard-currency borrowing and FX risk can turn otherwise viable local-revenue projects into fragile financial structures [S95]
- public debt constraints and refinancing pressure mean many governments cannot simply borrow their way through the transition [S92][S93]
- concessional finance, guarantees, and blended tools remain too limited, too fragmented, or too poorly targeted relative to the scale of the problem [S91][S94][S96]

The archive therefore now treats **finance architecture** as part of the climate solution itself.

## House doctrine

### 1. Cheap clean technology is not enough if money stays expensive
In capital-intensive clean sectors, financing cost is often as important as equipment cost. IEA says financing costs for utility-scale solar PV projects in EMDE can make up around **half or more** of the levelised cost of electricity [S91].

The archive therefore treats lower cost of capital as a first-order climate variable, not a background macroeconomic detail.

### 2. Currency mismatch is a climate bottleneck
Many clean projects earn domestic-currency revenues but seek finance in hard currency. OECD’s 2025 local-currency work says critical FX risk remains a major barrier to external finance for sustainable development and clean investment [S95].

That means climate finance doctrine should include:
- local-currency lending where possible
- hedging and FX-risk facilities where needed
- domestic capital-market deepening
- MDB / DFI balance-sheet reforms that make local-currency finance easier rather than exceptional

### 3. Debt space matters, so mitigation finance cannot rely only on sovereign borrowing
OECD’s debt analysis says EMDE other than China face a **USD 10 trillion** cumulative transition-finance shortfall by 2050 at current growth rates, and that their debt-to-GDP ratio could rise from **59% in 2024** to **75% in 2040** in a public-sector-led financing scenario [S92]. IMF says many EMDE are already managing high interest costs, refinancing needs, and weaker external flows [S93].

The archive therefore rejects climate doctrine that quietly assumes governments can just add more hard-currency debt until the transition is financed.

### 4. Concessional finance should be catalytic, not vague
IEA says a **tripling of concessional funding** for EMDE energy transitions is needed to get countries on track for their energy and climate goals [S91]. But the archive sharpens the design principle: concessional finance should be used where it reduces specific risks, lowers financing costs, improves project preparation, or makes socially necessary but commercially thin projects bankable.

This means concessional finance should target problems such as:
- weak project preparation
- off-taker and payment risk
- transmission and grid-connection risk
- first-loss or risk-sharing needs for frontier projects or frontier markets
- adaptation and resilience investments with large public value but weak private returns

### 5. Guarantees are climate infrastructure when political and payment risk are decisive
World Bank Group’s guarantee platform aims to raise annual guarantee issuance to **USD 20 billion by 2030** and simplify access through a one-stop structure [S96]. The archive treats this as more than institutional housekeeping. In many EMDE contexts, guarantees are part of the physical deployment stack because they help projects clear political risk, non-payment risk, or public-counterparty risk that would otherwise keep private capital out [S96].

### 6. The NCQG matters, but headline numbers are not deployment by themselves
UNFCCC says COP29 agreed to raise the goal for finance to developing countries to **USD 300 billion annually by 2035** and to secure efforts to scale overall finance to **USD 1.3 trillion per year** by 2035 [S94].

The archive treats this as directionally important but operationally incomplete. The real question is not only how much finance is promised, but whether it is:
- concessional enough where needed
- available in the right currency
- attached to usable pipelines and institutions
- structured to crowd in more capital rather than simply displace it
- fast enough to match near-term buildout windows

### 7. Finance reform is a climate justice issue because it shapes who gets the clean system on time
If clean finance remains expensive, volatile, and dollarized, then poorer countries face a double penalty: higher exposure to climate harm and slower access to the cheaper, cleaner, safer system. The archive therefore now treats cost of capital, local-currency finance, guarantees, and debt-space realism as justice issues as well as deployment issues [S91][S92][S93][S95][S96].

## Minimum package for this lane

A serious climate program should include at least:
- lower cost of capital for clean-power, grid, storage, building-efficiency, and resilient-infrastructure projects in EMDE [S13][S91]
- targeted concessional finance and technical support where risk, project-preparation weakness, or social value justify it [S91]
- local-currency finance expansion, FX-risk facilities, and domestic-capital-market deepening where feasible [S95]
- guarantee capacity strong enough to reduce political, payment, and public-counterparty risks for viable projects [S96]
- climate finance that respects debt-space constraints and does not casually convert resilience or mitigation into future debt distress [S92][S93]
- finance pipelines and institutions capable of turning NCQG-style headline commitments into actual disbursements and actual assets [S94]

## What this note rules out

The archive rejects:
- climate doctrine that assumes cheap clean technology automatically means cheap clean deployment [S13][S91]
- hard-currency dependence as the default for domestic-revenue infrastructure [S95]
- adaptation and mitigation finance strategies that quietly rely on already-stressed sovereigns borrowing far beyond prudent debt space [S92][S93]
- vague blended-finance rhetoric without specific risk allocation, pipeline quality, or public purpose [S95][S96]
- treating COP finance headlines as equivalent to bankable project finance [S94]

## Compression

**A global climate solution fails if the clean system remains cheap in theory but expensive where future growth is concentrated. Lower cost of capital, better guarantees, more local-currency finance, and real debt-space realism are not side issues. They are part of the machinery that decides whether the transition becomes global in time.**
