# 71 — Ideal Solutions by Contract Form: Standard Offers, Prequalified Delivery, and Performance-Backed Terms

## Claim

The archive already says ideal climate solutions should be low-friction, service-first, realistic about risk, and designed so the cleaner option is payable in time rather than only attractive on paper [S118][S119][S137][S143][S144].

One more compact rule belongs in the canon:

**ideal climate solutions should usually move through simple and comparable contract forms: standard offers where possible, prequalified delivery where useful, transparent post-contract obligations, and performance-backed terms when providers are claiming savings, comfort, flexibility, or reliability.**

In plain terms, a climate programme gets much weaker when each household, school, hospital, housing provider, municipality, or small firm must decipher a bespoke contract stack before the cleaner option can happen.

OECD’s 2025 regulatory work says governments reduce friction through one-stop shops and the once-only principle rather than making users recreate information and procedure at every step [S118].
IEA’s efficiency-policy toolkit says standardised contract templates and terms, expedited procedures, and streamlined administration reduce transaction costs and simplify replication [S119].
IEA’s current ESCO work says integrating project development, finance, and performance guarantees can reduce upfront barriers and shift technical and performance risk away from consumers [S144].
OECD and World Bank procurement guidance add that clear requirements, fit-for-purpose design, integrity controls, transparency, and comparable data matter if public or regulated climate delivery is to scale without collusion, opacity, or value-for-money loss [S129][S130][S131].
OECD’s blended-finance guidance adds a parallel point for capital mobilisation: bespoke opaque structures deter replication, while standardisation improves comparability and scale [S153].

So the archive’s shortest contract-form rule is:

**where quality can be preserved, climate delivery should prefer standard offers, clear comparable terms, prequalified channels, and performance-backed obligations over bespoke contract mazes that hide risk, raise search cost, and slow replication.**

For the archive’s matching remedy-form rule on what should happen when the promised service, savings, or support fails in practice, see `74-ideal-solutions-by-remedy-form-fast-recourse-automatic-correction-and-no-appeals-mazes.md`.

## Why this belongs in the canon

### 1. Bad contract form is a hidden climate bottleneck

Many otherwise good climate measures fail not because the physics are weak or the lifetime economics are bad, but because the transactional interface is messy.
The offer is hard to compare, the exclusions are unclear, the contractor quality is uncertain, the savings claim is vague, the paperwork is fragmented, or the user cannot tell who is responsible after installation [S118][S119][S137][S143][S144].

That means some climate underperformance is really a contract-design failure.
The programme exists, but the path from interest to a trustworthy signed agreement is too bespoke, too opaque, or too risky for the actors who must actually say yes.

So the archive needs a compact rule against confusing **technical availability** with **signable delivery form**.

### 2. Standard offers increase comparability and reduce rent-seeking room

OECD’s 2025 bid-rigging update says authorities should define requirements clearly, maximise genuine bidder participation, and watch for warning signs in bidding patterns and tender design [S129].
OECD’s 2025 procurement implementation review says stable rules, stronger lifecycle transparency, and better data are still needed across procurement systems [S130].
The World Bank’s current procurement framework likewise emphasises value for money, integrity, fit for purpose, complaints channels, and beneficial-ownership / conflict-of-interest controls [S131].

The practical lesson is wider than public tenders.
When contract terms are easier to compare, requirements are clearer, and post-contract duties are visible, it becomes harder to hide weak value, transfer risk quietly downward, or win on confusion rather than performance.

So standard offers are not only an administrative convenience.
They are also an anti-capture and anti-rent device.

### 3. Prequalified delivery matters when the user cannot realistically screen the market alone

Climate programmes often rely on actors who are not expert buyers:
- households,
- renters and landlords,
- schools,
- clinics,
- small firms,
- municipalities,
- and social-housing providers.

Those actors usually cannot evaluate technical claims, financing terms, maintenance promises, complaints routes, insurance quality, and performance exclusions from scratch every time [S118][S119][S137][S143].

A stronger design often uses a narrower, better-governed channel:
- prequalified vendors,
- approved contract templates,
- price books or reference packages where appropriate,
- complaint pathways,
- standard data fields,
- and visible service standards [S118][S119][S129][S130][S131].

That does not require monopoly.
It requires a more intelligible market.

### 4. Performance-backed terms are the contract counterpart to service-first delivery

The archive’s delivery-form note says people should usually receive clean service and measurable performance rather than a pile of assets to manage alone.
The archive’s risk-bearing note says providers should keep meaningful downside when they claim the gain.
The contract layer is where those principles become real.

IEA’s ESCO work says standardised processes, project development, financing, and performance guarantees can reduce upfront barriers and move technical and performance risk away from consumers [S144].
That implies a simple archive rule:

**if a provider is selling savings, comfort, flexibility, or reliability, the contract should make those promises legible, measurable, and enforceable.**

Without that, service-first rhetoric can collapse back into asset dumping with prettier language.

### 5. Standard contract form helps replication and finance scale

IEA’s efficiency toolkit says standardised templates simplify replication [S119].
OECD’s blended-finance guidance says lack of transparency and lack of standardisation undermine comparability and deter investors who want predictable, simple, and scalable structures [S153].

So contract form matters not only to the user at the front end.
It also matters to scaling at the system level.
When each project is a bespoke legal and commercial object, learning travels slowly, diligence costs stay high, and expansion into weaker-capacity places becomes harder.

The archive therefore needs a shortest rule that says:

**good climate delivery should be easier to sign again, not only easier to admire once.**

## The archive’s contract-form hierarchy

A future proposal is usually stronger when it fits most of this hierarchy.

### 1. Common-terms layer
Use standard offers, common definitions, and visible baseline obligations where possible so users and procurers can compare like with like rather than deciphering bespoke language every time [S119][S129][S130][S131][S153].

### 2. Prequalified-channel layer
Use prequalified vendors, approved delivery partners, reference packages, and complaint / redress pathways where the buyer cannot realistically screen quality and integrity alone [S118][S119][S129][S130][S131].

### 3. Performance-and-warranty layer
Where providers promise savings, comfort, flexibility, uptime, or resilience value, use measurable service levels, warranty duties, verification steps, and meaningful recourse if those promises fail [S119][S144].

### 4. Plain-comparability layer
Keep exclusions, fees, tenors, maintenance obligations, ownership boundaries, and post-install responsibilities legible enough that a normal user or public buyer can compare offers without specialist legal labor [S118][S119][S130][S131][S137][S143].

### 5. Replicable-data layer
Use standard data fields and post-contract reporting so successful models can be learned from, financed more easily, and copied across places instead of disappearing into bespoke paperwork [S119][S130][S153].

## The archive’s contract-form tests

### 1. Compare-offers test
Can two offers be compared quickly on scope, price, term, performance, maintenance, and recourse, or is each agreement too bespoke to compare honestly? [S119][S129][S130][S131][S153]

### 2. Hidden-risk test
Does the contract quietly move performance risk, maintenance risk, or cost-overrun risk onto the weaker party despite selling a clean-service promise? [S137][S143][S144]

### 3. Prequalification-and-redress test
Is there a credible channel for vetting providers, handling complaints, and removing bad actors, or is the user largely alone if things go wrong? [S118][S129][S130][S131]

### 4. Plain-language test
Could a normal user, local official, or public institution understand the main obligations, exclusions, and payment path without specialist interpretation? [S118][S137][S143]

### 5. Replication test
Can the agreement be reused, financed, audited, and improved across many similar projects, or does each case restart commercial design from zero? [S119][S130][S144][S153]

## What this rules in

This note rules **in**:
- standard offers and comparable retrofit / clean-heat / appliance / flexibility packages where users would otherwise struggle to compare terms [S118][S119][S137][S143]
- prequalified vendor pools, approved delivery partners, and visible complaint channels where quality and integrity screening matter [S118][S129][S130][S131]
- performance-contracting and ESCO-style structures where savings, comfort, or service claims are clearly measured and providers keep meaningful accountability [S144]
- public and regulated procurement designs with clear requirements, stable rules, strong anti-collusion design, and better post-contract transparency [S129][S130][S131]
- replicable financing and blended-finance structures that are simple enough to compare and repeat rather than vanishing into bespoke legal engineering [S119][S153]

## What this demotes

This note demotes:
- climate programmes that rely on opaque, bespoke agreements when standard forms would preserve quality and cut friction [S118][S119][S153]
- offers that market future savings or comfort while leaving verification, exclusions, and recourse vague [S137][S143][S144]
- procurement designs that tolerate weak comparability, unstable rules, or low transparency in ways that invite collusion, confusion, or avoidable price inflation [S129][S130][S131]
- delivery models that force every household or small institution to evaluate contractor quality, legal risk, and post-install duties from scratch [S118][S119][S137]
- capital-mobilisation structures that are so bespoke and opaque that they cannot be learned from or copied into wider markets [S153]

## One-paragraph compression

**Ideal climate solutions should usually be easy to sign, easy to compare, and hard to game. Prefer standard offers where possible, prequalified delivery where useful, clear post-contract obligations, and performance-backed terms when providers claim savings or service. The contract should make promises legible, risk allocation visible, redress real, and successful models repeatable. Do not force households, schools, hospitals, municipalities, or small firms to solve a bespoke legal and commercial puzzle every time the cleaner option is offered [S118][S119][S129][S130][S131][S137][S143][S144][S153].**
