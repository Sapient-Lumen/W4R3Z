# 75 — Ideal Solutions by Continuity Form: No Reset to Dirty Defaults at Handoffs, Moves, and Rebuilds

## Claim

The archive already says ideal climate solutions should be turnover-aware, low-friction, service-first, simple to access, proportionate to verify, and easy to correct when ordinary failures occur.
It says the cleaner option should usually attach to real decision moments, use existing records where possible, and avoid turning households, renters, small firms, or municipalities into full-time administrators of the transition [S118][S119][S161][S162][S164].

One more compact rule belongs in the canon:

**ideal climate solutions should usually make climate gains stick across ordinary handoffs.**

In plain terms:
- do not let a sale, rental turnover, account change, provider switch, refinancing event, or post-disaster rebuild silently reset the user or asset to a dirtier or weaker baseline when the relevant facts have not materially changed [S118][S119][S161][S164]
- keep clean-service eligibility, support status, building-performance records, and other routine climate advantages continuous where that is administratively feasible and substantively fair [S118][S119][S161]
- attach durable improvements to the asset, building, meter, route, or service relationship where that is the more sensible carrier of continuity than the individual person alone [S119][S161][S162]
- make reconstruction and major replacement moments ratchet upward into safer and cleaner states rather than recreate the old exposure or fossil lock-in by default [S161][S162][S164]

So the archive’s shortest continuity-form rule is:

**once a cleaner or safer state has been reached, ordinary handoffs should usually preserve it; do not treat every move, tenancy change, provider switch, or rebuild as an excuse to restart from the dirty default.**

## Why this belongs in the canon

### 1. Many climate losses happen at handoffs, not only at first adoption

A household can get into the cleaner pathway and still lose the gain later.
The building changes hands.
The tenant moves.
The utility account changes.
The landlord re-lets the unit.
The school changes administrators.
The municipality refinances or retenders.
A storm damages the asset and reconstruction starts from yesterday’s template.

At each handoff, one of two things can happen:
- the cleaner state carries forward,
- or the system quietly resets to the old baseline and makes the next user fight to recover what already existed.

That means some climate backsliding is not mainly a technology failure or even a finance failure.
It is a **continuity-design failure**.

### 2. Turnover moments should ratchet progress, not erase it

The archive’s turnover-aware note argues that sale, rental, replacement, renovation, procurement, renewal, and rebuild moments are some of the strongest places to steer the next choice [S161][S162][S164].
OECD’s 2025 real-estate work makes this explicit for buildings: trigger points such as **sales or rentals** can embed energy-performance obligations inside routine transaction cycles [S161].
IEA’s 2025 buildings toolkit makes the equipment version explicit: boiler replacement and code coverage are leverage points because the asset is already being changed [S162].
UNDRR’s 2025 recovery-readiness work makes the disaster version explicit: governments that prepare beforehand can use the narrow reconstruction window to **build back better** rather than reproduce prior vulnerability [S164].

The continuity lesson is the next step beyond that trigger-point insight:

**if a turnover moment is strong enough to improve the baseline, it is also important enough not to let the improved baseline vanish at the next handoff.**

### 3. Dirty resets recreate friction, waste, and unfairness

When the system forgets a valid prior state, users are often forced to:
- re-prove facts the system already knows,
- reapply for support that should have continued,
- reassemble records that should have travelled with the asset or programme,
- or accept a temporary dirty fallback because continuity was not designed in [S118][S119].

That reproduces exactly the frictions the recent eligibility, verification, and remedy notes are trying to remove.
A climate system is weaker if it makes each new occupant, customer, manager, or rebuilding authority solve the same administrative puzzle again from zero.

So this note adds a simple ratchet principle:

**where the cleaner state has already been established, ordinary administrative change should usually preserve it unless there is a real reason to reopen the case.**

### 4. Continuity is part of justice because instability is not evenly distributed

The people most exposed to repeated resets are often the ones with less spare time, less bargaining power, less tenure security, and more frequent life disruption.
Renters move more.
Low-income households face more paperwork fragility.
Small institutions and weaker-capacity local authorities are more vulnerable to staff turnover and fragmented systems.
Disaster-hit places face reconstruction pressure precisely when administrative capacity is weakest [S118][S119][S164].

That means a climate programme can look fair at entry and still become regressive in operation if benefits, records, or cleaner defaults disappear at each handoff.
Continuity design is therefore not only an administrative convenience.
It is a justice rule.

### 5. Continuity matters at both person level and asset level

Some things should stay with the person or household unless their circumstances materially change.
Examples include enrolment status, targeted support, or a prior verification that does not need to be rebuilt from scratch [S118][S119].

Other things should stay with the asset, building, site, or service relationship.
Examples include:
- a building’s disclosed energy status,
- a retrofit passport,
- wiring and envelope improvements,
- a clean-heating replacement standard,
- or a resilient-rebuild standard after damage [S119][S161][S162][S164].

The archive therefore needs a compact rule for choosing the right carrier of continuity:

**keep the climate gain with whichever holder — person, asset, account, or place — can preserve it most reliably and fairly across ordinary change.**

### 6. This note complements the recent form notes rather than duplicating them

This note is not the same question as:
- `72-ideal-solutions-by-eligibility-form-presumptive-inclusion-existing-data-and-no-proof-mazes.md`, which asks **how the intended user gets through the door**,
- `73-ideal-solutions-by-verification-form-simple-proof-metered-where-possible-and-sampled-audit-where-sensible.md`, which asks **how ordinary claims should usually be proved**,
- or `74-ideal-solutions-by-remedy-form-fast-recourse-automatic-correction-and-no-appeals-mazes.md`, which asks **how ordinary failures should be corrected once something goes wrong**.

This note asks a different question:

**how should the cleaner or safer state survive ordinary change so the user does not keep falling back to the old baseline?**

## The archive’s continuity-form hierarchy

A future proposal is usually stronger when it fits most of this hierarchy.

### 1. Stick-with-the-asset layer
Where the improvement is fundamentally about the building, equipment, site, route, or system, let the improvement record and associated obligations travel with that asset rather than vanishing when occupants or providers change [S119][S161][S162].

### 2. Stick-with-the-user layer
Where support is rightly person-specific or household-specific, preserve enrolment, verified status, and routine eligibility across ordinary administrative changes unless material facts changed [S118][S119].

### 3. Change-only-what-changed layer
At handoff, ask only for the delta.
Do not rebuild the whole file when most relevant facts are already known and unchanged [S118][S119].

### 4. No-dirty-reset layer
When equipment fails, tenants rotate, ownership changes, or services are retendered, do not default back to the fossil or weaker baseline if a cleaner and already-legible path exists [S161][S162][S164].

### 5. Rebuild-ratchet layer
After disruption or damage, reconstruction should normally preserve or improve resilience and emissions performance rather than reproducing the previous vulnerability as the easiest administrative option [S164].

## The archive’s continuity-form tests

### 1. Handoff test
If the occupant, account holder, owner, manager, or provider changes, does the cleaner state survive, or does the system quietly revert to the old baseline? [S118][S119][S161]

### 2. Re-proof test
Does the new user or administrator have to re-prove mostly unchanged facts the system already holds? [S118][S119]

### 3. Carrier test
Is the relevant climate gain attached to the most sensible carrier — person, asset, account, site, or service relationship — or is it attached in a way that makes loss at handoff likely? [S119][S161][S162]

### 4. Rebuild test
If the asset is damaged or rebuilt, does the system restore yesterday’s weak condition or carry forward a cleaner and safer standard? [S164]

### 5. Burden test
Who does the work of preserving continuity — the institution that holds the records and controls the system, or the weaker actor arriving midstream? [S118][S119][S164]

### 6. Ratchet test
Does each natural decision point make climate progress more likely to persist over time, or does every transition reopen the possibility of backsliding? [S161][S162][S164]

## What this rule promotes

Promote:
- programmes that preserve routine eligibility and support status through ordinary administrative change unless material circumstances changed [S118][S119]
- building and equipment records that travel with the property, asset, or service relationship and inform the next decision cleanly [S119][S161]
- sales, rental, replacement, and reconstruction rules that ratchet standards upward rather than allow one more dirty cycle by default [S161][S162][S164]
- systems that ask for changed facts only, not whole-file resubmission, at provider switch, move, renewal, or retender [S118][S119]
- recovery systems prepared in advance so post-disaster rebuilding preserves or improves resilience and emissions performance [S164]

## What this rule demotes

Demote:
- climate programmes that make users restart from zero at every move, renewal, or account change even when the core facts are already known [S118][S119]
- rental, sales, or replacement systems that allow clean or resilient gains to disappear because records do not travel or obligations do not stick [S161][S162]
- provider-switch and retender processes that reset service quality, reporting, or support to a weaker dirty default for purely administrative reasons [S118][S119]
- reconstruction systems that restore the pre-disaster vulnerability because continuity and readiness were not designed in beforehand [S164]
- any policy that treats ordinary change as a fresh excuse for fossil lock-in, paperwork reset, or deferred correction [S118][S119][S161][S162][S164]

## One-paragraph compression

**Ideal climate solutions should usually make gains stick. Once a cleaner or safer state has been reached, ordinary handoffs — moves, tenancy changes, account switches, ownership changes, provider retenders, and post-disaster rebuilds — should preserve it by default unless material facts changed. Attach continuity to the right carrier, ask only for what changed, and do not let every transition become an excuse to restart from the dirty baseline [S118][S119][S161][S162][S164].**
