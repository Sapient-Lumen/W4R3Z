# 158 — Ideal Solutions: Division of Labor Across Levels Compact Canon

## Claim

The archive already has joined notes for:
- actor allocation (`63`),
- place-sensitive delivery (`53`),
- institutional form (`66`),
- political economy / social contract (`131`),
- delivery bottlenecks (`132`),
- and the international layer (`135`)

What it still lacked was one joined first-stop note for a practical question that appears whenever a climate programme moves from principle to statecraft:

**who should do what across national, subnational, local, utility, household, and international levels if the solution is supposed to be fast, fair, and real?**

This note is not a new branch.
It is a compression note.
Its job is to gather the archive’s level-assignment rule into one place so the programme does not keep rediscovering it by rereading several adjacent notes.

## Fast rule

**Centralize backbone, devolve fit, protect end users, and push the hardest obligations toward the level or institution that controls the system lever. National and international layers should usually carry standards, backbone finance, market rules, common metrics, and long-horizon infrastructure obligations; subnational and local layers should usually fit land, buildings, mobility, water, and protection to place; utilities and other service operators should carry continuity and integration duties; households and small firms should mostly receive well-shaped clean defaults and support rather than acting as unpaid systems integrators** [S60][S69][S80][S91][S96][S117][S137][S141][S142][S143][S172].

## The compact canon

### 1. Put common rules, backbone finance, and long-horizon obligations high enough to hold
Some climate jobs fail when they are fragmented.
National governments and, where relevant, supranational frameworks should usually hold the tasks that need broad risk pooling, legal consistency, system-wide standards, long planning horizons, and fiscal capacity.
That includes major product and building standards, electricity-market and tariff architecture, social-protection backstops, backbone grid and corridor planning, national data and MRV standards, public guarantee platforms, and broad phase-down or performance rules [S60][S69][S71][S91][S96][S117][S118][S119][S137][S143][S172].

OECD’s place-based climate work says national governments have a key role in providing technical support, downscaled risk information, finance, and local inclusion in planning, and that effective coordination between national, regional, and local governments is needed [S117].
That yields the archive’s first level-assignment rule:

House rule: **keep floors, standards, finance backstops, and backbone obligations at the highest level that can carry them credibly.**

### 2. Push local fit, spatial choice, and integrated civic delivery down toward place
Other climate jobs fail when they are over-centralized.
Land use, street design, transit service, district systems, cooling access, drainage, urban heat reduction, local adaptation, and much building enforcement depend on local geography, settlement form, exposure, politics, and administrative reality.
These should usually be adapted and integrated near the place where they are lived, not designed only from the capital city [S60][S80][S117][S172].

OECD’s permitting toolkit says local authorities are often frontline decision-makers and that clear spatial plans can reduce conflict, legal risk, and timelines, while fragmented plans and weak cross-level coordination can create delay and veto points [S60].
IEA’s cities guidance makes the same practical point for urban systems: city-level tools strongly shape buildings, mobility, public procurement, local service access, and energy use [S80].

House rule: **devolve the fit-sensitive work to the level that can see the place, but not the cost and risk alone.**

### 3. Do not dump mandates downward without money, data, or authority
A recurring failure mode is rhetorical decentralization that is really fiscal and administrative abandonment.
The archive should demote climate designs that tell cities, public-housing providers, school systems, utilities, or local agencies to deliver difficult transitions without funding, templates, data systems, legal powers, or technical support.
That is not subsidiarity.
It is offloading [S80][S91][S96][S117][S137][S143][S172].

The proper assignment rule is paired responsibility:
- if higher levels keep the rulemaking power, they must also resource implementation,
- if lower levels are expected to tailor delivery, they must receive timely data, stable finance, and enough discretion to use local knowledge,
- and if a task spans several levels, one level must still be visibly accountable for the join [S80][S91][S96][S117][S143][S172].

House rule: **never devolve obligation without devolving means.**

### 4. Give utilities and other service operators the integration burden, not households
Many climate transitions are really service-system transitions.
The key question is not whether a household can buy a device, but whether the electricity system, building owner, lender, installer chain, transit operator, district system, or municipal service can make the cleaner state work reliably.
Utilities, grid operators, housing providers, public-transit authorities, and similar service operators should usually carry interconnection, balancing, flexibility, commissioning, continuity, and quality duties because they control the live system [S56][S69][S80][S117][S137][S143].

This note therefore sharpens the archive’s earlier actor rule.
Households, tenants, and small firms should usually choose within a well-shaped clean offer, especially at natural turnover moments, but they should not be treated as the principal coordinators of tariffs, contractors, interoperability, proof, and performance risk [S69][S118][S119][S137][S143].

House rule: **the operator of the system should carry the integration burden for the system.**

### 5. Use the international layer for capital costs, common definitions, supply chains, and hard cross-border sectors
A domestic climate package can be well designed and still fail globally if poorer or higher-cost-capital countries cannot finance the same broad direction of travel, or if trade, shipping, aviation, and supply chains remain weakly governed.
The international layer should therefore usually carry the work that only cross-border coordination can do well: lower the cost of capital, expand guarantee and concessional capacity, support local-currency and MDB risk reduction where possible, align product definitions and comparability, and build stronger sector regimes for hard cross-border activities [S13][S68][S91][S92][S93][S94][S95][S96][S123][S124][S135][S141][S142][S143].

This is not foreign-policy decoration.
It is part of the level assignment of a real climate answer.
If only rich, low-capital-cost jurisdictions can build the programme cleanly, the answer is incomplete [S91][S96][S141][S142][S143].

House rule: **assign cross-border bottlenecks to cross-border instruments, not to isolated domestic improvisation.**

### 6. Leave households and small firms with bounded, supported choices at natural decision points
The archive does not erase end users.
People still decide whether to accept a retrofit, change a boiler at end of life, replace a vehicle, join a flexibility programme, or move away from repeated loss.
But those choices should usually arrive as supported decisions made at natural capital-turnover moments, within good defaults, clear standards, and low-friction offers [S69][S71][S118][S119][S137][S141][S143].

That means the household role is usually:
- to consent, choose among a small number of legible options, and receive benefit,
- not to build the market architecture,
- not to discover hidden subsidy routes,
- and not to absorb the downside of upstream design failure [S69][S118][S119][S137][S143].

House rule: **bounded supported choice for users; system obligation for institutions.**

## Tie-breaker rule

When more than one plausible governance shape is available, the archive should usually prefer the one that:
- keeps standards, backbone finance, and system comparability at a level broad enough to hold,
- gives local and subnational actors real room to fit delivery to place,
- makes one institution visibly accountable for each join,
- avoids duplicate proof mazes and unfunded mandates,
- and leaves households, tenants, and small firms with the least coordination burden consistent with real agency [S60][S69][S80][S91][S96][S117][S118][S137][S141][S143][S172].

## Preferred use

Open this note first when the live question is any of the following:
- who should do what across levels in a serious climate programme
- what belongs at national versus local versus international level
- how to divide labour between ministries, cities, utilities, and households
- how to avoid dumping climate burden downward while still preserving local fit
- how a serious climate answer should assign backbone, delivery, and user-facing responsibility

Then reopen the deeper note that matches the surviving disagreement.

## Compression rule

**A serious climate programme should centralize backbone and devolve fit. Put standards, risk pooling, backbone finance, common metrics, and long-horizon infrastructure duties high enough to hold; push place-shaped delivery and civic integration down toward subnational and local levels; keep utilities and service operators responsible for system integration; use the international layer for cross-border finance and standards problems; and leave households with supported choices inside good defaults rather than with the job of coordinating the transition themselves.**
