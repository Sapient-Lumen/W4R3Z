# 12 — Ecosystem Shield: Forests, Peatlands, Wetlands, and Blue Carbon

## Claim

Ecosystem protection is not a decorative “nature” lane beside the real climate program. It is a **core climate lane** because it does four jobs at once:
- avoids large land-use emissions
- preserves natural carbon sinks that the world is already overusing
- stabilizes water, fire, soil, and coastal-risk systems that societies depend on
- protects adaptation capacity while energy and industrial systems are still being rebuilt

The archive’s house view is therefore simple:

**Protect intact high-carbon ecosystems first; restore degraded carbon-rich ecosystems second; never treat fragile biological sinks as permission for new fossil lock-in.**

## Why the archive upgrades this lane

FAO’s **Global Forest Resources Assessment 2025** says deforestation has slowed, but is still running at about **10.9 million hectares per year** in 2015–2025, with forests still covering **4.14 billion hectares** and holding about **714 gigatonnes** of carbon [S36]. That is progress, but not safety.

The **Global Wetland Outlook 2025** makes the broader systems point. Since 1970, the world has lost an estimated **411 million hectares of wetlands**, about **22%** of the global total, and around **25%** of remaining wetlands are now in poor ecological condition [S37]. Wetlands regulate floods, support drought resilience, and provide some of the highest-value ecosystem services on Earth [S37].

UNEP’s **Global Peatlands Assessment** sharpens the climate logic further: peatlands cover only about **3–4%** of land area, yet contain up to **one-third of the world’s soil carbon**; about **12%** are drained and degraded, contributing around **4%** of annual human-induced emissions [S38].

The **Global Carbon Budget 2025** then adds a harder boundary condition: land-use change still emits about **4.1 GtCO2** in 2025, while climate change is already weakening natural sinks; the budget team says **8%** of the rise in atmospheric CO2 since 1960 is due to climate change weakening land and ocean sinks [S39].

So the correct climate doctrine is not “nature will offset us later.” It is **stop destroying major carbon stores and stop assuming the biosphere will keep bailing out a high-emissions economy**.

## House doctrine

### 1. Protect intact high-carbon ecosystems before promising restoration later
Primary forests, intact peatlands, mangroves, salt marshes, seagrasses, floodplains, and other high-carbon, high-integrity ecosystems are more valuable kept intact than converted and partially repaired later [S36][S37][S38].

### 2. Put peatlands near the front of the restoration queue
Peatlands are disproportionately important because small areas hold huge carbon stocks and can turn from sinks into major sources when drained or burned [S38]. Rewetting and protection often beat more cosmetically appealing but lower-integrity interventions.

### 3. Treat wetlands and blue-carbon systems as climate and civil-protection infrastructure
Wetlands are not only biodiversity assets. They moderate floods, support water security, buffer storm surges, and store carbon, which means they belong inside adaptation and infrastructure strategy as well as mitigation [S37].

### 4. Do not confuse ecosystem protection with offset theatre
The archive rejects the habit of using tree-planting claims, low-integrity credits, or monoculture expansion as substitutes for preventing primary loss or reducing fossil emissions. Biological sinks are valuable, but they are reversible, disturbance-prone, and now exposed to worsening climate stress [S39].

### 5. Govern land, water, fire, and agriculture together
Forest, peatland, and wetland protection fail when nearby agricultural expansion, drainage, mining, roads, or fire regimes are governed separately from ecosystem maps. The correct unit is not a fenced reserve by itself, but the wider land-and-water system [S36][S37][S38].

### 6. Use restoration strategically, not romantically
Restoration matters, but the archive ranks it after avoiding fresh destruction of intact systems. A hectare not drained is usually a better climate result than a hectare restored after years of damage.

## Minimum package for ecosystem protection

A serious climate program should include at least:
- anti-deforestation enforcement in major forest frontiers [S36][S39]
- no-drainage and no-conversion rules for peat-rich landscapes [S38]
- wetland, mangrove, and floodplain protection tied to water and disaster-risk policy [S37]
- peatland rewetting and fire-risk reduction where degradation is already severe [S38]
- climate and infrastructure planning that recognizes natural sinks are valuable but weakening under climate stress [S39]
- restoration finance directed first to carbon-rich and risk-buffering ecosystems rather than public-relations planting campaigns [S37][S38]

## What the archive rejects

- “net zero” plans that keep destroying forests and wetlands while counting future removals
- primary-forest loss justified by plantations elsewhere
- peat drainage treated as a normal development pathway
- mangrove and wetland destruction treated as a local land-use issue rather than a climate, flood, and coastal-risk issue
- the assumption that natural sinks will remain stable regardless of continued warming [S39]

## Compression

**The ideal climate strategy protects carbon-rich ecosystems the way it protects grids: as foundational infrastructure. Forests, peatlands, wetlands, and coastal blue-carbon systems are not side benefits of the transition. They are part of whether the transition works.**
