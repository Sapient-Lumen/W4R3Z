# 185 — Ideal Solutions: Low Friction, Defaults, One-Stop Delivery, and Administrative Simplicity Compact Canon

## Claim

The archive already has strong notes for:
- the deeper low-friction doctrine (`36`)
- lived experience and everyday fit (`160`)
- copyability and fast replication (`168`)
- routinization and boring repeatability (`171`)
- and incentive compatibility plus payment-form / interface-form / enrolment-form notes (`180`, `70`, `76`, `77`)

What it still lacked was one joined first-stop note for a front-door question that repeatedly decides whether a climate programme stays mass-usable rather than expert-only:

**what should ideal climate solutions look like when the clean path should usually be the easy path to understand, qualify for, finance, approve, install, and keep using?**

This note is not a new branch.
It is a compression note.
Its job is to gather the archive’s low-friction rule into one first-stop answer so the programme does not keep reopening the deeper doctrine, the lived-experience canon, the interface and enrolment notes, and the copyability / routinization notes every time a proposal seems climatically serious but still asks ordinary users or over-stretched local institutions to carry too much paperwork, search, cash, or coordination burden.

## Fast rule

**Among physically serious options, prefer climate pathways that make the cleaner and safer action the easier action through defaults, standards, one-stop delivery, interoperable data, standard contracts, passive-first design, embedded finance, and short administrative paths. Demote pathways that mainly work only if households, small firms, landlords, or local officials repeatedly overcome high hassle, fragmented approvals, reimbursement mazes, or expert-only coordination burdens** [S53][S69][S118][S119][S120].

## The compact canon

### 1. Friction is not peripheral; it is a throughput constraint
A climate pathway may be technically strong, fair in principle, and even cost-saving over time, yet still underperform if the path from interest to action is cluttered with too many forms, agencies, quotes, proof burdens, waiting periods, or reimbursement delays.
OECD’s 2025 regulatory work says governments have improved interactions through one-stop shops that gather information requirements, approvals, and payment in a single place, and that these arrangements save users time, effort, and frustration [S118].
The archive therefore treats administrative simplicity as delivery capacity, not as cosmetic service design.

House rule: **do not confuse a good long-run climate option with an actually usable climate pathway.**

### 2. The clean path should win more often by default than by repeated persuasion
The archive already prefers standards, codes, and public procurement where they can reliably shift the baseline.
This note makes the delivery implication explicit: when government already knows the desired direction, it should not express that knowledge mainly as a maze of optional programmes.
IEA’s 2025 heat-pumps toolkit recommends Minimum Energy Performance Standards, building-code changes, harmonised labels, and one-stop-shop platforms for uptake support [S53].
The broader efficiency-finance toolkit says streamlined and digitised administrative processes, including one-stop shops, reduce barriers to investment [S119].

House rule: **where codes, standards, defaults, or bundled offers can safely carry the cleaner outcome, prefer them to information-heavy voluntarism.**

### 3. One-stop delivery often matters because coordination burdens are real burdens
Many climate measures fail in practice not because the hardware is unknown, but because the user must coordinate contractors, finance, permits, eligibility proof, and aftercare across multiple institutions.
The archive already rejects “user as systems integrator” in other notes; this note states the front-door version.
When several steps are necessary, they should be pulled behind one accountable front door where possible.
OECD’s 2025 work explicitly highlights one-stop shops and once-only principles; IEA’s 2025 permitting support says implementing one-stop shops, preferably with digital tools, is a critical step for faster processing [S118][S119].

House rule: **the more agencies and steps the pathway needs, the more the programme should hide that complexity from the end user or local implementer.**

### 4. Passive-first and default-first design remove friction by subtraction
Some of the best low-friction climate design does not simplify a later choice; it removes the need for the choice or shrinks its stakes.
UNEP’s 2025 cooling work says about two-thirds of mitigation in its sustainable cooling pathway comes from passive and low-energy cooling, and describes mandatory passive cooling in codes as the strongest single lever in fast-growing cooling markets [S120].
This wider rule recurs across the archive: a better envelope reduces later heating and cooling burden, compact settlement reduces forced mobility burden, and safer land-use choices reduce later emergency adaptation burden.

House rule: **value friction removed upstream at least as much as friction managed downstream.**

### 5. Rebate mazes, proof mazes, and pay-first-recover-later design should be distrusted
A great many climate programmes quietly select for households, firms, or municipalities with spare cash, high administrative confidence, and time to chase forms.
IEA’s 2026 affordability work lists administrative complexity, split incentives, and high upfront costs among major barriers to household uptake, and points toward targeted support with simplified programme design and strong local delivery models [S69].
That pushes the archive toward point-of-sale support, on-bill or embedded finance, prefilled eligibility where possible, standard offers, and assisted pathways rather than schemes that require high liquidity first and proof later.

House rule: **demote pathways that ask the weakest actor to carry search, proof, cash, coordination, and performance-risk burdens at the same time.**

### 6. Simplicity and fairness usually reinforce each other
Low-friction design is not merely a convenience upgrade for people who would adopt anyway.
It is often the difference between a broadly usable programme and one that mainly works for owner-occupiers with spare time, secure tenure, strong credit, and high confidence navigating bureaucracy.
OECD’s 2025 regulatory work explicitly says simplification can benefit people and very small businesses, and that improving interactions through one-stop shops and user-centred design requires constant effort [S118].
The archive therefore reads simplicity as a fairness screen as well as a speed screen.

House rule: **the clean path should not require the most from the actors with the least slack.**

### 7. Low-friction systems are easier to copy, routinize, and defend
A climate pathway that already uses common forms, standard contracts, interoperable data, and short administrative paths is easier to replicate across places, easier to calendarize, easier to audit, and easier to defend politically than one that depends on bespoke negotiation and high user effort every time.
IEA’s 2025 efficiency-finance toolkit explicitly points to standardised contract templates and terms, streamlined and digitised administration, and one-stop shops as ways to reduce transaction costs and simplify replication [S119].
That means low friction is not only a consumer issue; it is a governing and scaling issue too.

House rule: **promote pathways that become administratively boring as they scale.**

## The archive’s low-friction screen

A future proposal should usually score higher when it passes most of these tests:

1. **Default-path test** — if no one behaves heroically, does the system still tend toward the cleaner or safer outcome because codes, standards, procurement, passive design, or default offers carry the direction?  
2. **Front-door test** — can the user or implementing institution reach action through one intelligible route rather than a scavenger hunt across agencies, vendors, and funding windows?  
3. **Proof-maze test** — does the programme reuse existing data, prefill where possible, and minimise repeated proof demands instead of making eligibility or compliance a bureaucratic obstacle course?  
4. **Embedded-finance test** — does support arrive near the point of decision or on the bill / contract, rather than requiring high upfront payment and uncertain later recovery?  
5. **Standardisation test** — are contracts, forms, technical packages, and reporting requirements standard enough to reduce transaction costs and simplify replication?  
6. **Passive-substitution test** — can upstream design, building form, code, or infrastructure reduce the amount of later equipment, optimisation, or behavioural vigilance required?  
7. **Weak-actor test** — would the pathway still work for renters, low-liquidity households, small firms, overstretched local authorities, and multi-actor buildings rather than only for confident, cash-rich owner-operators?  

## Preferred use

Open this note first when the live question is any of the following:
- what ideal climate solutions should look like when the clean path should usually be the easiest path to find, qualify for, finance, approve, and finish
- whether a proposal depends too heavily on rebates, forms, fragmented approvals, contractor search, or expert-only navigation
- how to think about defaults, one-stop shops, passive-first design, standard contracts, point-of-decision finance, and streamlined administration in one joined rule
- whether a programme is technically fine but still too administratively hard for mass uptake or place-by-place replication
- how low friction differs from generic affordability, generic lived experience, generic copyability, or generic routinization

Then reopen the deeper note that matches the surviving sub-question.

## Compression rule

**Ideal climate solutions should make the clean and safe path the easy path — by default, by design, and through short administrative routes rather than repeated heroic effort.**
