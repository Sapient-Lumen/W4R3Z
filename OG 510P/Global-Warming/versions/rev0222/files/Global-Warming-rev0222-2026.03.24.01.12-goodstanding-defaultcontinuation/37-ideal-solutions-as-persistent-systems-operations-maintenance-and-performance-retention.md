# 37 — Ideal Solutions as Persistent Systems: Operations, Maintenance, and Performance Retention

## Claim

The archive now has strong rules for what ideal climate solutions should build, exclude, prioritise, bundle, correct, decentralise, and simplify.

One more rule belongs in the canon:

**ideal solutions must persist.**

A measure is not ideal if its headline benefit appears at approval, installation, or ribbon-cutting and then quietly decays because nobody funded operations, retained skilled staff, maintained equipment, updated software, repaired leaks, replaced parts, or monitored whether the promised service level was still being delivered.

OECD’s 2025 infrastructure-governance work states the principle clearly: a life-cycle approach is needed to optimise assets and ensure **whole-of-life performance**, and efficient management through planning, construction, operation, maintenance, and decommissioning is key to value for money and resilience [S121]. Yet OECD also says only **36%** of countries with data systematically factor full life-cycle costs, including operation and maintenance, into all project appraisals, only about **24%** require asset-management plans under law or regulation, and only **52%** use predefined service-delivery targets in continuous monitoring [S121].

So this note adds a persistence rule to the canon:

**prefer climate measures that can retain performance over time through explicit operations, maintenance, stewardship, monitoring, and workforce support — and downgrade measures that look good at installation but are likely to underperform, drift, or fail in use.**

## Why persistence belongs inside the ideal solution

### 1. Buildout is not the same thing as durable performance

Climate archives, budgets, and politics often overvalue visible capital expenditure and undervalue the less glamorous work that keeps systems functioning.

But whole climate strategy now depends on long-lived assets and services: grids, transformers, substations, district energy systems, wetlands, flood defenses, cooling centres, public-health response, clean-cooking supply chains, heat pumps, ventilation systems, drought monitoring, and early-warning systems. These do not succeed once. They succeed repeatedly.

OECD’s 2025 governance evidence makes the problem concrete. It says infrastructure investment is under increasing pressure to perform as climate-driven crises become more frequent, and that assets need continuous monitoring through their life cycle because this increases accountability and enables resilience measures to be adopted early [S121].

This pushes the archive toward a harder judgment:

**an ideal solution is not only one that can be built fast, but one whose climate and protection value can stay real after enthusiasm fades.**

### 2. O&M debt is climate debt

A recurring failure mode in climate and resilience policy is to fund installation while silently deferring the budget and institutional machinery required for upkeep.

That creates what the archive should treat as **operations-and-maintenance debt**:
- equipment installed without trained technicians and spare-parts planning
- infrastructure approved without realistic lifecycle costing
- public systems launched without recurring operating budgets
- monitoring platforms created without staff to interpret, verify, and respond
- restoration projects announced without long-run stewardship or enforcement

OECD says efficient management across planning, construction, operation, maintenance, and decommissioning is essential to resilience, and notes that asset-management plans remain uncommon [S121]. That means capex-only climate success stories are frequently overclaimed.

The archive should therefore ask of every proposed solution:

**who keeps it working in year 5, year 10, and year 20?**

If the answer is vague, the solution is not yet ideal.

### 3. Operating workforces are climate infrastructure

The archive already treats workforce as infrastructure. This note sharpens the composition of that workforce.

Ideal solutions do not only need people who can manufacture and install clean assets. They also need people who can **operate, maintain, inspect, repair, recalibrate, and improve** them.

IEA’s 2025 transmission work says around **8 million** people are employed worldwide in constructing, maintaining, and operating grids, and that this workforce will need to rise by **1.5 million by 2030** under today’s policy settings, and faster in scenarios that fully meet emissions goals [S122]. That is not a side constraint. It is proof that long-run system performance is a labour question as much as a hardware question.

This logic reaches far beyond transmission:
- methane abatement depends on inspection and repair crews, not only methane pledges [S11]
- building efficiency depends on commissioning, controls, maintenance quality, and competent retrofit delivery, not only hardware subsidy [S53][S119]
- cooling access depends on correct installation and upkeep as well as passive design [S16][S17][S120]
- resilience depends on public operators, emergency managers, and utilities who can actually run systems during stress [S40][S41][S108]

### 4. Some of the most important climate gains are recurring practices, not one-time purchases

The persistence rule matters because many high-value climate and resilience actions are inherently ongoing.

IEA’s 2025 methane work says robust MMRV systems are essential and highlights leak detection and repair, low-emissions equipment, and restrictions on routine flaring and venting as tried-and-tested policies [S11]. None of these are “buy once and forget.” They are repeated disciplines.

UNDRR’s 2025 early-warning assessment makes the same point from the adaptation side. It says funding for multi-hazard early warning systems must be **targeted and sustained**, and that there remains a shared need for investment in automated multilingual warning systems, reliable digital infrastructure, and ongoing community engagement to ensure warnings lead to early action [S108].

So ideal solutions must be judged partly by whether their benefits depend on:
- recurring monitoring,
- recurring repair,
- recurring community contact,
- recurring ecological stewardship,
- recurring operator skill,
- and recurring public finance.

Those recurring requirements do **not** make a solution bad. But they do make hidden underfunding a central climate risk.

### 5. Persistence changes which measures deserve priority

Once persistence is taken seriously, some options improve and others fall.

Measures that often rise in the ranking:
- passive cooling, efficient envelopes, shade, urban trees, and compact form, because they reduce the operating burden of later cooling and transport systems [S16][S17][S26][S120]
- fossil retirement, because a retired high-emitting asset does not need decades of further vigilance to keep its emissions low [S9][S23][S24][S25]
- ecosystem protection before degradation, because intact systems are usually easier to preserve than degraded systems are to recreate [S36][S37][S38][S39][S109][S110]
- no-build rules in repeatedly exposed zones, because they remove the need for endless future defense and compensation [S81][S82][S83][S84]
- well-governed grids, flexible demand, and standards-based building upgrades, because they keep delivering system value across many years of use [S55][S56][S58][S121][S122]

Measures that should be downgraded:
- capex-heavy plans that omit lifecycle costing, staffing, and maintenance
- subsidy programs that install equipment but do not ensure competent operation or service support
- restoration or resilience claims without long-run stewardship and monitoring
- data dashboards or transparency systems without enforcement, follow-up, or response capacity

### 6. Persistence is a different question from robustness or low friction

This note sits near several recent doctrine notes, but it is not redundant with them.

- `30-ideal-solutions-robustness-failure-tolerance-and-no-regrets.md` asks whether a package still helps when assumptions break.
- `34-ideal-solutions-as-corrigible-systems-feedback-measurement-and-ratcheting.md` asks whether underperformance can be noticed and corrected.
- `36-ideal-solutions-as-low-friction-systems-defaults-standardization-and-administrative-simplicity.md` asks whether adoption requires too much hassle.
- `56-ideal-solutions-as-repairable-systems-upgradeability-life-extension-and-second-life.md` asks whether persistence was designed into the asset through spare parts, repair access, upgrade paths, and safe second-life use rather than being left to heroic upkeep after sale.

This note asks a different question:

**after adoption, does the solution keep performing?**

A measure can be robust in theory, low-friction to adopt, and still fail because service quality drifts, maintenance budgets vanish, or operating institutions are too thin.

## The archive’s persistence screen

A future proposal should usually be promoted only if it passes most of the following tests.

1. **Lifecycle realism:** does appraisal include operating, maintenance, replacement, and decommissioning costs rather than only upfront capex? [S121]
2. **Service-level clarity:** are there explicit performance targets for the service that the measure is supposed to keep delivering? [S121]
3. **Workforce realism:** is there a plausible pipeline for operators, maintainers, inspectors, and repair capacity? [S62][S122]
4. **Monitoring and response:** can drift, leaks, failure, or underperformance be detected and acted on in time? [S11][S108][S113]
5. **Recurring finance:** is there a believable source of operating finance, not just construction finance? [S12][S91][S92][S121]
6. **Stewardship fit:** does the measure avoid creating a long-tail burden that weak institutions are unlikely to carry well? [S96][S108][S121]
7. **Persistence by design:** where possible, does the measure reduce later upkeep needs through passive, structural, or one-way improvements? [S16][S17][S81][S120]

If a proposal fails most of these tests, the archive should treat it as less ideal than its headline abatement number suggests.

## One-paragraph compression

The ideal solution to global warming is not just a buildout plan. It is a **stewardship plan**: one that budgets for operation, maintenance, repair, monitoring, and skilled staff; one that uses lifecycle costing and service-level targets rather than ribbon-cutting metrics alone; one that recognises methane control, grids, cooling, resilience, and ecosystem protection as ongoing disciplines; and one that values passive, structural, and retired-forever gains because they keep working with less future heroism [S11][S16][S17][S108][S120][S121][S122].
