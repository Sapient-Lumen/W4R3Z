What it still lacked was one joined first-stop note for a front-door question that keeps deciding whether a climate programme is merely choosing among present options or is also protecting the future option set itself:

**what should ideal climate solutions look like when delay, mixed signals, or wrong buildout would create one-way losses, deeper fossil or exposure lock-in, stranded assets, or narrower choices for later generations?**

This note is not a new doctrinal branch.
It is a compression note.
Its job is to gather the archive’s irreversibility rule into one first-stop answer so the programme does not keep reopening the deeper doctrine whenever the live question is not only “what works now?” but also “which choices become bad ancestral choices because they normalize one-way loss or close off better later paths?”

## Fast rule

**Prefer climate measures that avoid one-way losses, keep future options open, and refuse new long-lived dirty or exposure lock-in when cleaner and lower-regret alternatives are ready. Promote protect-before-restore ecology, right-first-time buildings and networks, no-build-or-rebuild-as-before discipline in repeatedly unsafe places, and clear signals against new fossil lock-in. Demote “build dirty now, repair later” pathways whose value depends on future cleanup, future conversion, or future retreat arriving on time and at scale** [S109][S110][S191][S192][S193].

## The compact canon

### 1. Not all delays are equal
WMO says some consequences of recent warming are irreversible over hundreds to thousands of years, and IPCC says some irreversible impacts have already occurred while overshoot and maladaptation can lock in future vulnerability and risk [S109][S110].
That means the archive should not treat all delay as symmetric.
Some delay merely postpones gain; other delay destroys futures that later money cannot fully buy back.

House rule: **promote earlier action where delay creates inherited losses rather than just slower benefits.**

### 2. Protect-before-restore should outrank “damage now, fix later” stories
Ecosystems, groundwater, floodplains, coastlines, peatlands, and settlement patterns can cross into states that are slower, costlier, or only partly reversible.
That is why ideal solutions should protect key ecological and hydrological systems before assuming later restoration will compensate, and why repeated rebuild-as-before in exposed places should face much harder scrutiny [S109][S110].

House rule: **where prevention preserves options better than later restoration, prevention should rank first.**

### 3. Right-first-time investments are worth extra priority
Buildings, boilers, power plants, roads, zoning decisions, and district networks often last long enough to shape emissions, bills, and exposure for decades.
Current IEA country reviews make the lock-in risk concrete: Germany is told to avoid a lock-in effect to fossil fuel boilers through clearer electrification signals, while Czechia is warned not to rely primarily on gas plants because that would risk fossil-fuel lock-in and stranded assets [S191][S192].

House rule: **when an asset is long-lived, “mostly right later” is often not good enough; build it right first time where credible lower-lock-in alternatives exist.**

### 4. Option value belongs in climate ranking, not just in finance language
A good climate package does not only cut emissions now.
It also preserves a wider, safer decision set for later.
That usually favors grids, efficient building shells, flexible demand, open standards, ecological protection, and place disciplines that remain useful across several futures over narrow bets that require one exact fuel, technology, or political story to arrive on schedule [S109][S110][S191][S192].

House rule: **promote measures that leave later generations with durable assets and wider choices, not just today’s headline progress.**

### 5. Mixed signals can themselves create lock-in
Lock-in is not produced only by physical construction.
It can also be produced by ambiguous strategy.
IEA’s Germany review says mixed “technology-neutral” signaling around hydrogen in home heating can delay heat-pump investment today and deepen fossil-boiler lock-in, while OECD’s packaging-and-sequencing work warns that poorly phased public support can create path dependencies and high fiscal costs [S191][S193].

House rule: **where the cleaner path is supposed to become dominant, strategy should signal that clearly enough to move investment before the next replacement cycle hardens the old path.**

### 6. Do not confuse speculative conversion stories with real option preservation
Some assets are genuinely repurposable.
Others are defended with decorative “future-ready” narratives that depend on uncertain fuel availability, uncertain standards, uncertain retrofit economics, or uncertain political follow-through.
The archive should reward credible platform value and credible repurposability, but it should distrust pathways whose only defense is that a later conversion might one day rescue a bad present choice [S191][S192][S193].

House rule: **reward credible repurposing; demote speculative salvage stories.**

### 7. Irreversibility is a reason to accelerate the right kinds of action, not to freeze
This rule is not a brief for paralysis.
It is a reason to move faster on measures that prevent one-way losses, avoid dirty lock-in, or keep the later option set wider.
That includes ecosystem protection, clean replacement at trigger points, clear no-new-lock-in discipline where feasible, and exposure-aware rebuilding rules [S109][S110][S191][S192].

House rule: **treat irreversibility as an acceleration rule for protect-first, right-first-time, option-preserving action.**

## The archive’s irreversibility screen

A future proposal should usually score higher when it passes most of these tests:

1. **One-way-loss test** — delay would create losses that are only partly reversible or much more expensive to avoid later [S109][S110].  
2. **Lock-in test** — the proposal avoids creating long-lived fossil, land-use, settlement, or exposure commitments that later generations would struggle to unwind [S110][S191][S192].  
3. **Option-value test** — the move preserves several good later pathways instead of betting on one narrow future [S109][S110][S193].  
4. **Right-first-time test** — long-lived assets and rebuilding moments are used to install the cleaner or safer default now rather than normalizing another dirty or exposed cycle [S191][S192].  
5. **No-fake-repair-later test** — the pathway does not depend on vague future cleanup, future conversion, future restoration, or future retreat to justify present preventable damage [S109][S110][S193].  
6. **Inheritance test** — the choice leaves future people wider choices and lower liabilities rather than narrower choices and larger cleanup burdens [S109][S110][S191][S192].  

## Preferred use

Open this note first when the live question is any of the following:
- what ideal climate solutions should look like when some delays create one-way losses rather than merely slower gains
- whether a proposal is locking in decades of emissions, exposure, or future cleanup liabilities
- how to think about protect-before-restore, right-first-time buildout, and “bad ancestral choices” in one joined rule
- when a “future-ready” or “convertible later” story is credible and when it is just decorative optionality
- why mixed strategic signals can be part of the lock-in problem rather than just a communications flaw

Then reopen the deeper note that matches the surviving sub-question.

## Compression rule

**Ideal solutions should preserve future options. They should move earlier where delay creates one-way loss, use long-lived investment and rebuild moments to install cleaner and safer defaults right first time, protect ecological and hydrological systems before assuming later restoration, and distrust all “build dirty now, fix it later” stories that depend on speculative future cleanup or conversion.**
