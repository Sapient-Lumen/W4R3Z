# 63 — Ideal Solutions by Actor: Concentrated Control Points, Protected Households, and No Heroic Consumers

## Claim

The archive already says that ideal climate solutions should be polycentric, low-friction, incentive-compatible, and place-sensitive.
It says they should move through many venues, rely on standards and defaults where possible, and avoid making ordinary people solve system problems alone [S115][S116][S117][S118][S119].

One more compact rule belongs in the canon.
For the archive’s companion note on **which policy mechanisms** should usually be used once those actors are identified, see `64-ideal-solutions-by-mechanism-defaults-first-real-buildout-and-prices-in-a-supporting-role.md`.
For the archive’s companion note on **which institutional form** should usually hold the backbone once those actors and mechanisms are chosen, see `66-ideal-solutions-by-institutional-form-public-backbones-regulated-service-and-competitive-edges.md`.
For the archive’s companion note on **which delivery form** should usually reach end users once the actors are chosen, see `67-ideal-solutions-by-delivery-form-clean-service-performance-and-less-asset-shopping.md`.

**ideal solutions should put the heaviest obligations on actors who control large systems, and make the cleaner outcome easy for actors who do not.**

In practice that means the archive should usually prefer climate designs that work through **concentrated control points**:
- standards bodies and regulators,
- utilities and grid operators,
- public procurement bodies,
- landlords and large property owners,
- manufacturers and fleet buyers,
- lenders, insurers, and public-development finance institutions,
- national and city governments with code, tariff, land-use, and infrastructure powers [S27][S53][S56][S68][S80][S91][S96][S117].

Households, tenants, small firms, and local communities still matter.
But ideal solutions should not treat them as the primary systems integrators, financiers, and compliance managers of the transition.

So the actor-allocation rule is:

**push obligations upward toward concentrated levers; push support downward toward constrained users.**

## Why this belongs in the canon

### 1. Most climate outcomes are structured upstream before households ever choose

A great deal of climate performance is decided before a household or small firm reaches the point of choice.

Building codes shape whether a home needs less heating or cooling.
Appliance and equipment standards shape what is even sold.
Tariffs and market rules shape whether electrification is cheap or punitive.
Grid rules shape whether clean supply, storage, and flexibility can connect.
Urban form and transport procurement shape whether daily travel requires high fuel use.
Industrial definitions and procurement rules shape whether low-emissions materials have a market [S27][S53][S54][S56][S68][S80].

That is why the archive should not overstate atomised consumer choice.
The decisive climate levers are often upstream, concentrated, and institutional.

### 2. High-hassle climate design systematically excludes the people who most need help

OECD’s 2025 regulatory work says one-stop shops, interoperability, and once-only systems reduce time, effort, and frustration for users [S118].
IEA’s 2024 efficiency-policy toolkit says one-stop shops, standardised contracts, renovation passports, expedited procedures, and digitised administration reduce barriers to investment [S119].
IEA’s 2025 affordability-focused efficiency work adds that major barriers include split incentives, high upfront costs, and administrative complexity, and that good policy design uses targeted support, simplified programmes, and strong local delivery models [S137].

That is already enough to state a stronger archive rule:

**when a climate measure works only for the cash-rich, time-rich, landlord-free, digitally fluent, owner-occupying, contractor-literate user, it is usually not ideal.**

The ideal solution should work for renters, lower-income households, multi-family buildings, small firms, and places with thin administrative capacity — not only for motivated early adopters [S69][S118][S119][S137].

### 3. Concentrated levers usually move more tonnage per unit of friction

A well-designed standard, tariff reform, procurement rule, or utility programme can shift millions of devices, vehicles, buildings, or tonnes of materials at once.
A household-by-household persuasion campaign usually cannot.

The archive already leans this way:
- near-zero steel and cement definitions matter because common product language can shape whole markets [S27][S68]
- heat-pump and building-policy design matters because standards, installer pipelines, and electricity-price reform affect adoption at scale [S53][S54]
- flexibility programmes matter because utilities and system operators can reward demand response rather than merely asking for altruism [S56]
- city toolkits matter because procurement, land use, data systems, and local regulation are concentrated municipal levers [S80]

So the question should not only be “is this behaviour desirable?”
It should also be:

**where is the smallest decision point that can move the largest physical system?**

### 4. Polycentric climate action still needs responsibility discipline

The archive’s polycentric note rightly says climate progress moves through many centres of action [S115][S116][S117].
But many-venue action is not the same as evenly distributed burden.

Ideal solutions should distinguish between:
- **actors with leverage**, who can alter system defaults for many others, and
- **actors with exposure**, who mainly live inside those defaults.

That distinction matters politically and morally.
A system that asks exposed actors to correct upstream design failures with money, time, and expertise they do not have is not merely inconvenient.
It is usually unjust and fragile.

### 5. Upward obligation and downward support is the cleanest justice rule for implementation

The archive already says climate strategy should raise essential service floors and lower affluent waste ceilings [S69][S70][S71].
This note adds the implementation counterpart:

- put stricter duties on actors that set defaults for many others,
- give more support to actors with less liquidity, weaker bargaining power, or less direct control,
- and do not confuse these two jobs.

National governments should not dump climate responsibility on cities without money and data [S80][S117].
Landlords should not leave tenants inside inefficient buildings while pocketing the status quo [S137].
Utilities should not ask households for flexibility while failing to pay for it or simplify participation [S56].
Finance systems should not keep clean infrastructure expensive in EMDE and then blame local uptake [S91][S96].

## The archive’s concentrated-control rule

A future proposal should usually be promoted only if it passes most of these tests.

### 1. Control-point test
Does the proposal act on an upstream decision point that influences many later choices — standards, procurement, tariffs, planning, finance, fleet turnover, landlord obligations, or utility rules — rather than depending mainly on atomised virtue? [S27][S53][S56][S68][S80][S137]

### 2. Burden-fit test
Is the hardest work assigned to the actors with the most leverage, technical capacity, and balance-sheet room, rather than to the actors with the least? [S69][S91][S96][S117][S137]

### 3. Low-hassle pass-through test
Will end users experience the gain through defaults, embedded finance, simpler bills, better buildings, better products, better service, or one-stop delivery, instead of a maze of claims, contractor management, and ongoing performance risk? [S53][S69][S118][S119][S137][S144]

### 4. Split-incentive test
Does the design repair landlord-tenant, owner-user, lender-borrower, developer-occupant, or utility-customer misalignment rather than pretending those problems away? [S53][S56][S137]

### 5. Public-capacity test
If local governments, utilities, or public agencies are asked to deliver, are they also given the funding, templates, data, guarantees, and technical support required to do so? [S80][S91][S96][S117]

### 6. Mass-adoption-without-heroics test
Could the proposal still work if most people remain busy, liquidity-constrained, imperfectly informed, and only intermittently attentive? [S118][S119][S137]

Measures that pass most of these tests deserve promotion.
Measures that fail most of them should usually be demoted unless they are very narrow emergency interventions.

## What this rules in

This note rules **in**:
- product, appliance, equipment, and materials standards that clean up the baseline offer rather than asking every buyer to become an expert [S27][S53][S68]
- utility and regulator programmes that pay for flexibility, reduce interconnection friction, and make electrification workable in practice [S14][S56]
- public and fleet procurement that creates dependable demand for cleaner buses, trucks, materials, and buildings [S27][S30][S32][S80]
- social-housing-first, public-building-first, and landlord-accountable retrofit strategies rather than owner-occupier-only policy [S53][S69][S137]
- EMDE finance platforms, guarantees, and cost-of-capital reduction tools that change the feasibility of whole project classes rather than celebrating isolated pilot deals [S13][S91][S94][S95][S96]
- national frameworks that resource city and local delivery rather than offloading obligations downward without capacity support [S80][S117]

## What this demotes

This note demotes:
- climate strategies that rely mainly on information campaigns, moral exhortation, or app-based self-optimisation by households while leaving prices, defaults, building quality, and product standards largely unchanged [S53][S118][S119][S137]
- rebate mazes and tax-credit-heavy designs that require low-liquidity actors to pay first, navigate complexity, and wait for relief later [S69][S118][S119][S137]
- city-localism that praises local action while failing to supply finance, data, or vertical coordination [S80][S117]
- voluntary corporate or consumer pledges that are not tied to procurement, standards, physical assets, or auditable delivery [S68][S116]

## Compression

**Ideal climate solutions should do the hardest work at concentrated control points and spare ordinary people from acting as unpaid systems engineers. Push obligations upward to regulators, utilities, landlords, procurement bodies, manufacturers, lenders, and governments that set defaults for many others; push support downward so households, tenants, and small firms receive the cleaner, safer, cheaper outcome with low hassle.**
