# 21 — Water Security, Drought, and Hydrological Stability

## Claim

Water security is not background adaptation context. It is a **core climate lane** because it sits upstream of food security, ecosystem health, hydropower reliability, public health, urban resilience, and industrial continuity.

The archive’s house view is therefore simple:

**Treat watersheds, aquifers, glaciers, wetlands, reservoirs, drought planning, floodplain discipline, and selective water reuse as part of the climate solution itself — not as a downstream cleanup problem once warming has already destabilized the hydrological cycle.**

## Why the archive upgrades this lane

WMO’s **State of Global Water Resources 2024** says only about **one-third of river basins** had normal conditions in 2024, while **all glacier regions worldwide** reported losses for the **third straight year** [S72]. That is not a local water-management glitch. It is a signal that hydrological stability itself is degrading.

The **UN World Water Development Report 2025** sharpens the cryosphere point: **billions of people** depend on mountain meltwater for drinking water and sanitation, food and energy security, and environmental integrity, while glacier retreat is making the water cycle more unpredictable and extreme [S73].

The **World Bank’s drought-resilience work** adds the governance diagnosis. Droughts are becoming more frequent, severe, and widespread, and extreme drought conditions have increased sharply in some regions over the last 50 years; reactive crisis management is too late, while early warning, watershed restoration, and cross-sector planning are central to resilience [S74].

The **World Bank’s Continental Drying** report then adds a harder systems constraint: global freshwater reserves have been declining by about **324 billion cubic metres per year** over the past two decades, with median basin-level losses equivalent to about **3%** of annual renewable freshwater supply and **10%** in already-drying arid basins [S75].

And this is already an energy-system issue, not only a water-sector issue. IEA’s **Electricity 2025** says extreme weather including droughts and heatwaves caused widespread power disruptions in 2024, while reduced hydropower output strained systems in multiple countries and heatwaves plus low hydropower contributed to supply tightness in Mexico [S76].

So the correct climate doctrine is not “decarbonize first, then water will sort itself out.” It is **treat hydrological instability as a live constraint on mitigation, adaptation, agriculture, ecosystems, and electricity security at once**.

## House doctrine

### 1. Protect watersheds and aquifers as infrastructure
Forests, peatlands, wetlands, floodplains, soils, uplands, and aquifers regulate water timing and quality. The archive therefore treats watershed protection and wetland restoration as infrastructure policy, not decorative conservation [S72][S73][S74][S75].

### 2. Govern drought and flood together
A real climate strategy cannot treat drought policy, flood policy, reservoir operations, groundwater depletion, and land-use planning as separate silos. The same destabilized hydrological cycle can produce scarcity in one season and destructive excess in another [S72][S74].

### 3. Reduce demand before overbuilding supply
Leak reduction, irrigation efficiency, water-smart crop choice, industrial recirculation, appliance efficiency, and better pricing/governance often beat brute-force supply expansion. The archive therefore ranks demand management above romantic faith in endless new abstraction [S74][S75].

### 4. Use reuse, storage, and desalination selectively rather than as excuses for waste
Water reuse, smarter storage, and desalination all matter in some places, but they should support disciplined water governance rather than substitute for it. In energy terms, the archive prefers options that reduce both water stress and power-system stress where possible.

### 5. Stop assuming hydropower is automatically stable under warming
Hydropower can still be valuable, but its climate role now depends on rainfall variability, drought exposure, sedimentation, and system flexibility. A serious climate program climate-proofs hydro rather than treating it as permanently secure legacy clean power [S72][S76].

### 6. Treat water security as part of food and city strategy
Water politics becomes climate politics in farms and cities first: crop choice, irrigation regimes, leakage, wastewater treatment, heat plans, and urban design all shape whether scarcity becomes chronic instability [S73][S74][S75].

## Minimum package for water security

A serious climate program should include at least:
- watershed, wetland, peatland, and upland protection tied directly to water-security planning [S72][S73][S74]
- drought plans with triggers, early warning, and staged demand-management measures before emergency collapse [S74]
- groundwater governance, leakage reduction, and water-accounting rules strong enough to detect chronic overuse [S75]
- irrigation modernization and crop/water alignment in overdrawn basins [S74][S75]
- selective wastewater reuse, stormwater capture, and storage upgrades where they reduce real vulnerability
- floodplain discipline and no-build rules where repeated losses make rebuilding irrational [S72][S74]
- energy-water coordination so hydropower, thermal generation, cooling demand, and water stress are planned together rather than separately [S76]
- glacier- and mountain-water risk planning in downstream-dependent regions [S73]

## What the archive rejects

- treating water as a secondary issue that can wait until mitigation is “finished”
- groundwater mining treated as a normal development pathway
- replacing watershed protection with purely engineering-first supply fantasies
- assuming hydropower and river flows remain stable regardless of warming
- rebuilding the same floodplain or drought vulnerability repeatedly without changing land or water rules
- using desalination or interbasin transfer rhetoric to avoid governing waste, leakage, and crop mismatch

## Compression

**The ideal climate strategy protects water systems the way it protects grids: as foundational infrastructure. If the hydrological cycle is destabilized, food systems, ecosystems, cities, and even low-emissions power systems become harder to keep stable.**
