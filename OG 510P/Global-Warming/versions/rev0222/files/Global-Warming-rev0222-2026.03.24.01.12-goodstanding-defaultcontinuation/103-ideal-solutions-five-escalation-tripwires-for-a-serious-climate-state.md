# 103 — Ideal Solutions: Five Escalation Tripwires for a Serious Climate State

## Claim

The archive already has:
- a one-page answer (`58`)
- a compact package note (`57`)
- an execution-lanes note (`100`)
- a steering-levers note (`101`)
- and a governing-clocks note (`102`)

What it still lacked was the shortest **escalation-logic note** for the question:

**When should a serious climate state stop treating underperformance as routine drift and start forcing repair, surge capacity, redesign, or constraint?**

That matters because many programmes have goals, levers, and cadences yet still fail softly for too long.
They have calendars, but no hard enough signal for when the operating mode must change.

This note is a **compression note**, not a new doctrinal branch.
Its job is to reduce the archive to five tripwires that should trigger stronger response.

## Fast rule

**A serious climate state should escalate when five things happen: the pipeline clogs, delivered performance misses, dirty fallback grows, affordability or legitimacy breaks, or exposure outruns protection.**

## Five escalation tripwires

### 1. Backlog tripwire
Escalate when the clean substitute is not arriving at the speed required because queues, permits, procurement, grid connection, finance approval, or workforce bottlenecks are piling up.

What this usually looks like:
- connection and permitting queues growing faster than completions
- retrofit, public-housing, or municipal delivery pipelines slipping repeatedly
- standard projects becoming bespoke, slow, or locally stuck
- training and contractor capacity failing to keep up with target volumes [S55][S103][S117][S180][S181][S182]

What escalation usually means:
- queue reform, standardization, extra delivery capacity, surge staffing, template procurement, triage rules, and focused bottleneck-release action

Why it matters:
- a climate programme with a clogged substitute pipeline will quietly fall back to the dirty baseline even if strategy documents remain impressive

### 2. Performance-miss tripwire
Escalate when built or funded clean assets are underperforming in the field and the problem is not being corrected fast enough.

What this usually looks like:
- heat pumps, retrofits, demand-response schemes, methane programmes, or industrial measures producing less real benefit than promised
- weak commissioning, poor maintenance, sloppy measurement, or delivery contractors gaming easy metrics
- repeated complaints or error patterns showing that the service is not holding in ordinary use [S65][S111][S113][S114][S119][S121][S143][S147]

What escalation usually means:
- tune, recommission, fix contracts, change vendors, tighten standards, increase audit intensity, and route recurring failures into redesign rather than public relations

Why it matters:
- without a hard performance tripwire, climate policy drifts into ceremony, subsidy habit, or announcement accounting

### 3. Dirty-relapse tripwire
Escalate when temporary fossil fallback, dirty exemptions, methane leakage, or new high-carbon lock-in begins expanding instead of shrinking.

What this usually looks like:
- routine reliance on backup fossil generation rather than true short-duration stress use
- repeated extension of transition waivers, reliability carve-outs, or emergency dirty bridges
- continued methane super-emitter patterns or other fix-now operational leakage
- fresh long-lived dirty capital being added because the clean substitute was not made ready in time [S9][S23][S24][S84][S85][S86][S103][S108][S109][S110][S133][S146][S147]

What escalation usually means:
- narrow the carve-out, force closure logic, accelerate the substitute, assign cleanup liability, and treat repeated “temporary” reliance as evidence that the baseline design needs repair

Why it matters:
- the programme is no longer credible if dirty fallback is easy to start, easy to renew, and politically easier than fixing the mainline clean path

### 4. Affordability-and-legitimacy tripwire
Escalate when the clean path becomes materially hard to reach, publicly illegible, or visibly unfair for ordinary households, workers, tenants, small firms, or poorer jurisdictions.

What this usually looks like:
- bill shock, cash-heavy switching costs, or cost-of-capital barriers blocking uptake
- complaint backlogs, proof mazes, remedy failure, or visible exclusion of likely-fit users
- transition burdens landing on weak actors while gains are privatized upward
- widening political backlash because people experience the programme as exposure rather than support [S58][S68][S69][S70][S72][S73][S74][S75][S76][S80][S117][S143][S145][S146][S147]

What escalation usually means:
- rebalance tariffs and support, simplify eligibility and remedy, lower upfront burden, protect exposed users, and redirect public de-risking toward lower bills or broader access rather than one-way subsidy

Why it matters:
- climate programmes fail politically when their user pathway and burden shape are wrong even if the technical logic is sound

### 5. Exposure-and-protection tripwire
Escalate when climate harms are rising faster than direct protection, resilience, retreat, or public-health readiness.

What this usually looks like:
- repeated heat, fire, flood, smoke, drought, outage, crop, or water-security stress outrunning response systems
- losses concentrating in the same places without stronger adaptation, civil protection, or managed retreat
- insurance stress, rebuilding into repeat loss, or public-health systems absorbing predictable climate damage without structural repair [S104][S105][S106][S107][S143][S146][S147]

What escalation usually means:
- move protection and adaptation into priority status, tighten exposure discipline, change rebuilding rules, fund cooling and resilience measures, and expand retreat or relocation capacity where staying safely is no longer realistic

Why it matters:
- emissions strategy without visible protection loses legitimacy, and protection strategy without escalation locks societies into repeated avoidable loss

## What this note changes in practice

When a proposal claims to be an ideal climate solution, ask not only:
- what lane it belongs to (`100`)
- which steering lever moves it (`101`)
- and which clock keeps it live (`102`)

Also ask:

**What measurable condition tells us routine governance has failed and stronger correction must begin?**

If there is no tripwire, drift will usually win.
If the tripwire exists but has no owner, escalation will be delayed.
If the owner exists but no prepared response is attached, the tripwire is theatre.

The best programmes therefore attach to each major lane:
- a small number of visible tripwires
- a named owner
- a prepared escalation move
- and a return condition for stepping back down once repair has occurred

This note therefore sits in a distinct place:
- `100` says how the programme is split into **execution lanes**
- `101` says what public authority uses as **steering levers**
- `102` says what recurring **clocks** keep the programme live
- `103` says what **failure signals** should force escalation before drift hardens into lock-in or loss
