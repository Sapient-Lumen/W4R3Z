# 11 — Mobility, Freight, Aviation, and Shipping

## Claim

Transport strategy should follow a **mode hierarchy**, not a fuel free-for-all.

The house view is:
- **avoid** unnecessary travel and freight movement where design can do that without sacrificing welfare
- **shift** passengers and freight toward more efficient modes where geography and density permit
- **electrify** the large road modes that are actually electrifiable
- **reserve scarce low-emissions fuels** for the hardest modes: aviation, shipping, and selected slices of long-haul freight

This matters because transport is not one sector with one solution. Road passenger transport, trucks, aviation, and shipping sit at different points on the electrification–fuel continuum.

## Why the archive upgrades transport

Road transport is still a large climate problem. The IEA’s 2025 Breakthrough Agenda road-transport review says road emissions were **just over 6 Gt CO2 in 2024**, with **over 60%** coming from passenger cars and vans and **about one-third** from trucks [S31].

At the same time, electrification is no longer hypothetical. IEA’s **Global EV Outlook 2025** says electric car sales topped **17 million in 2024**, or about **22%** of all car sales, and are expected to exceed **20 million in 2025**, representing **more than one-quarter** of cars sold worldwide [S30]. The same source says electric truck sales grew by nearly **80%** in 2024 to reach close to **2%** of total truck sales [S30]. A related IEA analysis adds that battery-electric heavy-duty truck purchase prices could fall by around **15–35%** over the next five years, though they remain above diesel at the point of purchase [S32].

The harder modes remain genuinely hard. IEA’s aviation tracking page says jet kerosene still dominates aviation fuel demand and **SAF accounts for less than 0.1%** of aviation fuels consumed today [S33]. Shipping has moved somewhat further on international rulemaking: in April 2025 the IMO approved a draft net-zero framework combining a **marine-fuel standard** with **GHG pricing**, with mandatory application to large ocean-going ships over 5,000 gross tonnage that account for **85%** of international-shipping CO2 emissions [S34]. ICAO, for its part, has adopted a **long-term aspirational goal** of net-zero carbon emissions for international aviation by 2050, but implementation remains more uneven and fuel-constrained [S35].

## House doctrine

### 1. Passenger mobility is not just a drivetrain problem

Cars matter, but the archive rejects the idea that climate-safe transport is simply “replace every car with an EV.” Urban form, transit quality, walkability, cycling, trip length, and vehicle size all affect how much energy the system needs in the first place [S1].

So the right road-passenger doctrine is:
- compact and transit-viable urban growth where possible
- fewer unnecessary vehicle-kilometres
- smaller and more efficient vehicles
- rapid electrification of the kilometres that remain

### 2. Electrify the easy and medium-hard road modes quickly

The archive treats light-duty vehicles, buses, municipal fleets, urban delivery, depot-based vans, and a growing share of medium- and heavy-duty trucking as the **high-confidence electrification zone** [S30][S32].

The point is not that every truck route electrifies first. The point is that policy should not waste a decade pretending all road freight is equally hard. Many road applications can move now with charging, depot planning, fleet procurement, and corridor buildout.

### 3. Treat freight as a logistics-and-infrastructure problem, not only a vehicle problem

Freight emissions depend on load factors, routing, warehousing, mode choice, last-mile design, and the balance between truck, rail, and waterborne movement. The archive therefore treats freight decarbonization as partly a **systems-efficiency** problem and not merely a fuel-substitution problem.

### 4. Reserve scarce low-emissions fuels for the hardest modes

Aviation and shipping need dense energy carriers. The archive therefore rejects the habit of spraying limited sustainable fuels across all transport modes for branding symmetry.

Use the scarce clean-fuel pool first where direct electrification is structurally hardest:
- long-haul aviation
- international shipping
- selected long-haul or off-grid freight segments where batteries are not yet practical

Road cars, city buses, and many urban-commercial vehicles should not compete first for scarce SAF, e-fuels, or other expensive low-emissions fuels when electricity can often do the job more directly [S30][S33][S34].

### 5. Aviation and shipping need sector-specific governance

These modes cross borders and run on globally traded fuels. They therefore need stronger international rule systems than ordinary domestic road transport.

The archive’s house preference is:
- efficiency and operational improvements first
- binding or quasi-binding fuel standards
- real price signals for lifecycle emissions
- port and airport infrastructure aligned with future fuels and electrified ground operations
- no reliance on low-integrity offsets as a substitute for physical decarbonization

### 6. Demand management belongs in the hard-mode conversation

A serious climate program should not be afraid of demand-shaping tools where politically feasible: better rail substitutes for short-haul travel, fewer empty freight movements, better digital substitution for some business travel, and pricing or procurement rules that discourage the most emissions-intensive low-value trips. The archive does not treat all travel demand as sacred and exogenous.

## Minimum package for transport

A serious climate program should include at least:
- EV and charging buildout for cars, vans, buses, and fleet vehicles [S30]
- depot charging and targeted freight-corridor charging for trucks [S32]
- rail, bus, walking, and cycling investment where mode shift is physically plausible [S1]
- freight-efficiency standards, route optimization, load-factor improvement, and modal-shift policy
- clean port and airport infrastructure, including shore power and electrified ground operations where applicable
- scarce low-emissions fuel strategy focused on aviation, shipping, and the hardest freight slices [S33][S34][S35]
- aviation and shipping rules that rely less on aspirational targets and more on concrete fuel and emissions standards [S34][S35]

## What the archive rejects

- treating passenger-car EV adoption as the whole transport solution
- using scarce biofuels or e-fuels where direct electrification is already practical
- waiting for perfect hydrogen or perfect batteries before acting on freight efficiency
- offset-heavy aviation strategy as a substitute for real fuel and demand reform
- transport planning that keeps increasing car dependence while calling the result climate policy

## Compression

**The ideal transport solution is not one fuel for every mode. It is a hierarchy: redesign demand where possible, electrify what is electrifiable, and reserve scarce clean fuels for planes, ships, and the genuinely hardest freight uses.**
