## Fast rule

**A serious climate programme is not only domestic. It must also travel across borders. That means cheaper capital where money is the blocker, common measurement and product language where trade and procurement need comparability, coordinated decline where fossil dependence is sticky, stronger rules for methane and other large spillovers, and sector-specific international regimes for shipping and aviation where no single state can solve the whole governance problem alone.**

## The compact canon

### 1. Treat the cost of capital as climate infrastructure
In many EMDE, the missing ingredient is not the basic technology.
It is the price of money, debt stress, currency risk, and thin risk-reduction architecture.
So a serious international climate programme should treat concessional finance, guarantees, public-balance-sheet de-risking, and lower-spread deployment structures as part of the climate solution itself rather than as optional charity [S4][S6][S13].

House rule: **if capital is mispriced, the right physical portfolio will still under-arrive.**

### 2. Use common product language and comparable measurement
A world-scale programme cannot rely on every country, buyer, and regulator inventing different definitions for “clean,” “near-zero,” or “credible.”
So the international layer should push toward:
- common emissions-accounting methods where possible
- interoperable product definitions for traded clean materials and fuels
- procurement and disclosure language that many jurisdictions can reuse
- and verification simple enough to travel across borders without bespoke translation every time [S11][S27].

House rule: **cross-border scale needs common language, not thousands of incompatible labels.**

### 3. Coordinate scarce supply chains without reproducing old extraction politics
Batteries, grids, transformers, critical minerals, and industrial inputs are now strategic climate bottlenecks.
The international layer is stronger when it diversifies supply, expands recycling and second life, reduces wasteful material demand, and ties upstream extraction to stronger local benefit, labour, and environmental protections rather than treating speed as a licence for abuse [S6][S27].

House rule: **secure the supply chain, but do not solve one transition problem by deepening another.**

### 4. Put hard-to-govern international sectors inside real sector regimes
International shipping and aviation cannot be left to generic national climate policy alone.
They need corridor logic, fuel rules, standards, and sector-specific international regimes that align investment with actual decarbonisation pathways rather than symbolic targets only [S34][S35].

House rule: **where the system is international by design, the governance must be international enough to match it.**

### 5. Make methane, fossil decline, and subsidy reform part of international coordination
Large methane leaks, open-ended fossil support, and unmanaged decline create spillovers that weaken everyone else’s effort.
So the international layer should coordinate around:
- measured methane reduction and repair
- reform of fossil support with protection for those who actually need help
- explicit managed-decline pathways where lock-in is otherwise likely
- and worker / regional transition support where decline is politically and socially hardest [S9][S11][S23].

House rule: **international climate cooperation is not only about adding clean supply; it is also about shrinking the dirty default together.**

### 6. Prefer copyable arrangements over bespoke hero transactions
If each project, standard, corridor, or de-risking structure must be reinvented from scratch, global buildout stays too slow.
So the international layer should favour reference contracts, reusable guarantees, standard procurement language, template corridors, common data fields, and other copyable forms that lower transaction cost for the next case [S13][S27][S34].

House rule: **global scale arrives through repeatable templates more often than through one-off masterpieces.**

## Compression rule

**Treat the international layer as operating infrastructure for world-scale delivery: lower the cost of capital where money blocks buildout, use common measurement and product language where trade needs comparability, coordinate supply chains without reproducing extractive abuse, govern shipping and aviation through real sector regimes, and make methane control plus fossil decline part of cross-border cooperation rather than waiting for each country to solve the spillovers alone.**
