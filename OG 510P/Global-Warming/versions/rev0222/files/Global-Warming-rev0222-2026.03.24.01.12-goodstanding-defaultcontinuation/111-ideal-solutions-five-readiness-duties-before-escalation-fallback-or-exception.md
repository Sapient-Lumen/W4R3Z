## Fast rule

**Do not wait for the bad day to decide the bad-day system: pre-map triggers and fallback ladders, pre-identify who must be protected first, pre-authorize surge tools, keep live operational truth, and drill the playbook until escalation is a switch, not a brainstorm.**

## Five readiness duties

### 1. Pre-map triggers, fallback ladders, and closure conditions
A serious climate state should decide in advance what kinds of stress trigger escalation, what the least-damaging fallback ladder is, and what conditions close the detour.

What this usually means:
- keep a standing map from common stress conditions to the first, second, and last-resort responses
- define which options preserve the clean mainline and which options count as temporary dirty bridges requiring higher proof
- name the closure trigger and review owner before the detour is ever used [S55][S103][S104][S147]

Why it matters:
- if the fallback ladder is designed during the incident, the dirtiest familiar option often wins by speed
- preparedness starts by deciding the order of substitutes before fear, lobbying, or confusion can reorder them

### 2. Pre-identify who and what must be protected first
The state should know in advance which people, services, places, and loads must be shielded first when affordability, reliability, heat, smoke, flood, drought, or outage stress arrives.

What this usually means:
- maintain a standing priority map for critical services, exposed households, care systems, water systems, cooling needs, and other mission-critical uses
- know which users need shielding, manual support, bill relief, reserve access, safer service levels, or temporary procedural simplification first
- tie protection rules to visible criteria rather than ad hoc discretion in the middle of the event [S83][S84][S87][S104]

Why it matters:
- a system that has not pre-decided who is protected first will protect the loudest, richest, or easiest-to-serve users first
- legitimacy under stress depends on visible protection order, not only technical response speed

### 3. Pre-authorize surge tools, contracts, and queue privileges
A serious climate state should keep the surge instruments ready that let it repair, reconfigure, shelter, or substitute quickly without reinventing authority every time.

What this usually means:
- standing procurement vehicles, emergency maintenance and retrofit contracts, reserve mobilisation rules, and pre-cleared financing windows
- known queue-priority rules for urgent repair, critical clean replacements, interconnection fixes, and protection measures
- short authority chains for switching into higher-tempo delivery when a tripwire fires [S55][S103][S111][S147]

Why it matters:
- when the legal, procurement, staffing, or finance tools are assembled from scratch during the incident, escalation arrives late and costs more
- preparedness is not only stockpiles; it is pre-positioned authority

### 4. Keep live operational truth and contact chains
The state should keep an always-usable picture of system conditions, exposures, backlogs, asset status, and decision contacts so that escalation is driven by reality rather than rumor.

What this usually means:
- live dashboards or regularly refreshed ledgers for key assets, queues, outages, heat risk, service failures, and exposed populations
- named owners, contact trees, decision pathways, and data-sharing routes across agencies, utilities, operators, and local governments
- measurement good enough to tell whether the problem is local, systemic, temporary, or becoming structural [S55][S83][S103][S111][S147]

Why it matters:
- weak truth systems create slow escalation, misallocated protection, and excuses for broad dirty fallback
- you cannot run a disciplined bad-day system if you do not know what is failing, where, for whom, and at what rate

### 5. Drill the playbook and update it before the incident updates you
A serious climate state should rehearse its escalation, fallback, and protection playbooks often enough that the first real use is not the first serious test.

What this usually means:
- regular tabletop exercises, live drills, after-action reviews from near-misses, and revision of the playbook even when the event was small
- testing whether tripwires fire soon enough, whether contacts respond, whether surge tools are really usable, and whether protection reaches the intended users
- public-facing communication templates that can explain what is happening, what the fallback is, who is protected first, and how normal service will be restored [S83][S87][S103][S104][S147]

Why it matters:
- systems that never rehearse usually discover their blind spots in public and under pressure
- drills convert doctrine into muscle memory and expose readiness theater before a real shock does

## Minimal readiness gate

The smallest serious readiness gate before a climate system claims it can handle bad-day operation is:

- **the trigger map exists**
- **the fallback ladder is ordered**
- **the protection priority list is explicit**
- **the surge tools are actually pre-authorized**
- **the data, contacts, and drills are live**

If these are missing, the programme is not truly prepared for escalation or exception.
It is depending on improvisation.
