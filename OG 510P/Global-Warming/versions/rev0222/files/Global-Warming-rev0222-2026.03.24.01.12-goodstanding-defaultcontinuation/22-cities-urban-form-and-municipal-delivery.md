# 22 — Cities, Urban Form, and Municipal Delivery

## Claim

Cities are not just where climate impacts show up. They are where **buildings, transport, cooling, water, waste, land use, and public legitimacy** meet.

UNDP’s 2024 urban-NDC review says cities are responsible for about **67% of global energy consumption** and **70% of greenhouse-gas emissions** [S77]. UN-Habitat’s World Cities Report 2024 adds two clarifications that matter for doctrine: urban areas currently host about **57% of the global population** while occupying only about **1.7% of the world’s surface**, and in many contexts **urban emissions per capita are lower than national averages** [S78].

So the archive’s conclusion is not “urbanization is bad.” It is **the shape of urbanization is now a core climate variable**.

## Why the archive upgrades cities

The archive already had strong notes on buildings, transport, cooling, water, delivery capacity, and affordability. But these lanes still mostly met only implicitly.

That is now too weak. In practice, they meet in cities first:
- whether homes are close enough to jobs and services to avoid long fossil-dependent trips
- whether growth locks in bad envelopes and dangerous heat islands or efficient new construction and passive cooling
- whether floodplains are still being urbanized as if hydrological risk were static
- whether distribution grids, EV charging, cooling demand, storage, and buildings are managed as one system or as disconnected silos
- whether low-income households experience the transition as lower exposure and lower bills, or as new burdens

UNDP’s urban-NDC review sharpens the governance gap: **66%** of the 194 NDCs analysed contain moderate or strong urban content, but while **160** NDCs request finance at national level, only **26** include specific requests for finance at urban level [S77]. The archive therefore now treats cities not as a soft implementation layer, but as a place where climate strategy often succeeds or fails.

## House doctrine

### 1. Urban form is emissions policy
Compact, mixed-use, transit-supportive growth usually beats sprawl because it shortens trips, lowers per-capita infrastructure demand, and makes walking, cycling, and frequent public transport more viable.

That does not mean every city must look the same. It means climate strategy should bias growth toward **infill, reuse, connected density where appropriate, and better access to daily needs**, rather than locking in long-trip, car-dependent expansion.

### 2. Municipal systems should be planned together
IEA’s 2024 urban-transitions work says cities have the right level of density and granularity for demand-side assets to be optimised and aggregated at the building, neighbourhood, and community levels [S79]. It also says digitally enabled technologies could reduce variable-renewables curtailment by more than **25% by 2030**, increase system efficiency by **30%**, and reduce customer costs by up to **30%** [S79].

The archive therefore favors integrated urban planning that treats:
- buildings
- cooling
- EV charging
- flexible demand
- storage
- district infrastructure
- water and wastewater systems
- transit and freight access

as one urban climate system rather than separate departments with separate clocks.

### 3. National-city alignment matters more than “city leadership” rhetoric
Cities can deliver a great deal, but they rarely control the full rulebook. IEA’s 2025 city-policy toolkit says national and city-level alignment is a key dimension of clean-energy transitions, and that planning works best when it is **integrated, cross-sectoral, and long-term** [S80]. The same toolkit stresses local regulations, data systems, national-to-local funding, and green public procurement as practical levers [S80].

So the archive’s doctrine is not “leave climate to heroic mayors.” It is **build multilevel climate governance that gives cities authority, capacity, and money to act on housing, mobility, cooling, and infrastructure together**.

### 4. Urban climate finance should reach the level that actually builds things
More than **60% of public investment** occurs at the subnational level, and nearly a third of that is channelled into transport systems [S79]. But the urban-NDC evidence suggests climate-finance architecture still often remains too national and too abstract [S77].

The archive therefore favors:
- city-capable climate-finance windows
- municipal project-preparation support
- stronger local borrowing and guarantee frameworks where fiscally appropriate
- national transfer formulas that reward low-emissions and resilience-enhancing urban buildout
- procurement rules that turn municipal demand into market formation for buses, retrofits, efficient public buildings, cooling, and resilient water systems [S79][S80]

### 5. Heat, flood, housing, and access should be solved together
The urban climate problem is not one silo. A city that adds AC without shade, paving without drainage, housing without transit, or retrofits without affordability design is still building instability.

The archive therefore treats the following as one urban package:
- heat plans and public cooling access
- trees, shade, reflective surfaces, and green-blue infrastructure where appropriate
- flood-aware and no-build land-use rules in exposed areas
- social-housing-first retrofits and efficient affordable new housing
- zero-emission buses and protected access for walking and cycling where viable
- wastewater, reuse, and stormwater upgrades where hydrological stress is rising

### 6. Cities are not a side arena; they are a convergence arena
UN-Habitat’s 2024 reporting argues urbanization remains a major source of emissions, yet in many contexts urban emissions per capita are lower than national averages [S78]. That is exactly why cities matter so much: dense settlements can either become **high-efficiency, lower-carbon, lower-exposure systems**, or they can become **heat-trapping, flood-prone, car-dependent vulnerability machines**.

## Minimum package for cities

A serious climate program should include at least:
- anti-sprawl and infill / reuse / transit-oriented land-use reform where context supports it
- frequent public transport, zero-emission buses, and safer walking / cycling access where density supports them
- social-housing-first retrofits plus strong standards for efficient affordable new housing
- city heat plans: shade, cooling access, passive cooling, and urban-heat reduction
- municipal procurement rules for low-emissions materials, efficient equipment, buses, and resilient public buildings
- integrated city data platforms and utility coordination for buildings, mobility, flexibility, and power demand
- explicit national-to-local finance and delivery support rather than unfunded local mandates [S77][S79][S80]

## What this note rules out

The archive rejects:
- treating EV adoption alone as an urban climate strategy while land use keeps expanding trip lengths
- treating municipal climate action as optional branding rather than core implementation
- separating housing, transport, cooling, water, and flood risk into unrelated policy silos
- forcing local governments to deliver national targets without fiscal tools, staffing, or authority
- assuming that “more urbanization” automatically worsens emissions regardless of urban form

## Compression

**Climate strategy is partly city-making. Shape urban growth well and the world gets lower transport demand, better building performance, lower heat exposure, stronger public legitimacy, and cleaner infrastructure that reinforces itself. Shape it badly and climate policy spends decades compensating for avoidable urban lock-in.**
