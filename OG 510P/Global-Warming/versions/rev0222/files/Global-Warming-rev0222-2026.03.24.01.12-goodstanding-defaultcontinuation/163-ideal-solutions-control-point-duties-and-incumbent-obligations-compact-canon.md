# 163 — Ideal Solutions: Control-Point Duties and Incumbent Obligations Compact Canon

## Claim

The archive already has strong notes for:
- actor allocation and no-heroic-consumer design (`63`)
- downside discipline and liability (`68`)
- upside allocation and public value capture (`69`)
- one accountable lead rather than user-side systems integration (`81`)
- managed fossil decline (`09`)
- truth, measurement, and enforcement (`133`)
- the political-economy / social-contract canon (`131`)
- and the funding / burden-allocation canon (`156`)

What it still lacked was one joined first-stop note for a recurring front-door question:

**what should ideal solutions require first from the actors that already control buildings, grids, fleets, procurement, finance, standards, permits, and other system chokepoints?**

This note is not a new doctrinal branch.
It is a compression note.
Its job is to turn the archive’s scattered answer into one clear rule so the programme does not keep rediscovering, file by file, that identifying concentrated control points is only half the work; the other half is assigning them the right duties.

## Fast rule

**Ideal solutions should place the heaviest positive duties on powerful control-point actors and incumbents: change defaults in the clean direction, stop new dirty lock-in, tell the truth about real performance, keep cleanup and downside with managers and owners, pass publicly enabled gains through into lower bills, wider access, or shared public value, and do the coordination work upstream so ordinary users are not forced to act as unpaid systems integrators** [S53][S56][S59][S60][S68][S69][S80][S91][S96][S117][S118][S119][S137][S143][S145].

- `69-ideal-solutions-by-upside-allocation-public-value-capture-lower-bills-and-shared-gains.md` answers **what should happen to gains created partly by public authority, de-risking, or regulated privilege**

## The compact canon

### 1. Use control to change defaults, not to wait for retail virtue
The archive already rejects climate designs that depend mainly on atomised willpower.
This note makes the obligation side plainer.
If an actor controls product shelves, fleet replacement cycles, procurement frameworks, interconnection rules, landlord obligations, building standards, finance terms, network planning, or market access, the relevant duty is not merely to “offer options.”
The duty is to shift the default conditions under which many later choices are made [S53][S56][S68][S80][S118][S119][S137].

House rule: **control-point actors should be judged first by how they change the baseline, not by how many voluntary premium options they place beside it.**

### 2. Stop new dirty lock-in and carry retirement duties honestly
A serious programme should not let incumbents keep building or refinancing long-lived dirty dependence while speaking the language of future transition.
Where actors control fossil production, fossil generation, inefficient building stock, dirty fleets, or other lock-in machines, ideal solutions should require retirement schedules, conversion plans, connection reform, replacement buildout, and no-excuse scrutiny for claims that a new dirty asset is “temporary” or “necessary” [S4][S9][S11][S23][S25][S53][S59][S143][S145].

House rule: **incumbent control does not create a right to prolong lock-in; it creates a higher duty to unwind it without breaking continuity.**

### 3. Tell the truth in physical, comparable terms
Powerful actors should not be allowed to satisfy climate duty with soft narratives, portfolio averages, or paper claims that users and regulators cannot compare.
The archive already prefers measured methane control, common product definitions, interoperable accounting boundaries, and public truth infrastructure.
This note gathers the duty-holder side of that rule: incumbents, utilities, manufacturers, lenders, and project sponsors should have to disclose and evidence real performance in forms that support comparison, enforcement, and correction rather than marketing theatre [S11][S27][S65][S66][S67][S68][S133].

House rule: **the more leverage an actor has, the less permissive the archive should be about unverifiable claims.**

### 4. Keep downside, cleanup, and tail risk with managers and owners
The archive already says controllable downside belongs with those who choose, design, manage, or profit from it.
This note makes that duty legible at the front door.
If a project, asset, or business model creates long-tail system risk, cleanup burden, methane leakage, decommissioning cost, underperformance risk, or local damage, ideal solutions should resist moving those burdens downstream to households, host communities, thin municipalities, or the public purse except where a true public backstop is needed for system continuity [S11][S25][S68][S91][S96][S145].

House rule: **public rescue may sometimes protect continuity, but it should not become the standard exit path for privately created transition liabilities.**

### 5. Pass public enabling through into public-facing gain
Many control-point actors operate with public permits, regulated monopoly status, procurement support, concessional finance, guarantees, subsidy reform, public land-use decisions, or grid priority.
When public action helps create the value, ideal solutions should require that a material share of the gain show up in lower bills, wider access, host-community benefit, open standards, stronger service obligations, or reusable lower-cost templates rather than flowing out mainly as private windfall [S59][S60][S69][S80][S91][S96][S143][S145][S153].

House rule: **if public coordination makes the value, the result should look recognisably public-facing somewhere on the bill, the service, the local place, or the next project.**

### 6. Repair split incentives and do the coordination work upstream
Landlords, utilities, developers, fleet managers, public buyers, and financiers often sit exactly where the archive’s hardest frictions live: split incentives, high upfront burden, fragmented paperwork, poor sequencing, and unresolved interface risk.
Ideal solutions should therefore require these actors to do more of the coordination work before the burden reaches end users.
That means standards, embedded finance, standard offers, one-stop routing, prequalified delivery, and accountable orchestration rather than pushing tenants, households, or small firms into search, proof, cash, and contractor mazes [S53][S56][S69][S118][S119][S137][S143].

House rule: **the programme should remove complexity closest to the chokepoint that created it, not export it to the least powerful user.**

### 7. Accept stronger public-interest conditions when holding systemic power
The more an actor functions as a backbone, gateway, gatekeeper, dominant landlord, utility, grid operator, public contractor, or de-risked project platform, the more appropriate it is to bind that actor to transparency, non-discrimination, service obligations, complaint routes, local participation, and correction duties.
This is not anti-market ornament.
It is how a programme keeps concentrated private or quasi-private power from slowing buildout, capturing rents, or quietly selecting against the users and places least able to fight back [S59][S60][S80][S117][S118][S131][S143].

House rule: **systemic leverage should come with thicker public-interest obligations, not thinner ones.**

### 8. This is an execution rule, not only a fairness rule
If powerful control-point actors are weakly governed, the programme becomes slower, more fragile, and more expensive even when the technical package is right.
Users face more hassle, local resistance grows, public money buys less, and old systems retain covert staying power.
So this note belongs in the archive’s front door because implementation quality depends on whether the actors with the most leverage are under duties strong enough to move the system, not merely invited to participate in it [S53][S59][S60][S69][S80][S91][S117][S118][S137][S143].

House rule: **ideal solutions do not only identify leverage; they govern it.**

## Preferred use

Open this note first when the live question is any of the following:
- what powerful incumbents and control-point actors should be required to do first
- what utilities, landlords, fleet owners, public buyers, manufacturers, and financiers owe the transition when they control chokepoints
- whether a package is relying too much on ordinary users while leaving upstream actors under-obligated
- how actor allocation, liability, truth, value capture, and low-friction delivery fit together
- why identifying concentrated control points is not yet enough unless the archive also states their duties

Then reopen the deeper note that matches the surviving sub-question.

## Compression rule

**Ideal solutions should govern power where it already sits: require control-point actors and incumbents to clean the default, stop new lock-in, disclose real performance, keep cleanup and downside with managers and owners, pass publicly enabled gains through into public-facing benefit, and do upstream coordination work so ordinary people receive the cleaner path as the easier path rather than as a second unpaid job.**
