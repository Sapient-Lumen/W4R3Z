# 32 — Ideal Solutions as Packages: Complementarity, Sequencing, and Bottleneck Release

## Claim

The archive now has a strong answer to what ideal climate solutions should build, exclude, prioritize under scarcity, keep robust, and protect against one-way loss. One missing layer remained:

**ideal solutions should be designed as packages of complements, not as piles of individually respectable moves.**

Many climate measures underperform not because their core logic is wrong, but because they are implemented as **orphans**: renewable targets without grid upgrades, heat-pump pushes without envelope work or tariff reform, EV pushes without charging and distribution upgrades, phase-outs without affordable substitutes, carbon pricing without redistribution or visible alternatives, and adaptation spend without land-use discipline.

OECD’s 2025 sequencing review makes the general rule explicit: climate instruments can reinforce one another, some work better when preceded by enabling conditions, and public investment in infrastructure and innovation can make later economic and regulatory instruments more effective by making low-carbon alternatives available, affordable, and accessible [S112]. IEA’s 2025 flexibility work sharpens the system version of the same point: flexibility and efficiency reinforce each other, and better coordination between them can raise system efficiency by up to **30%** [S111].

This note therefore adds a packaging rule to the canon:

**prefer bundles whose parts unlock one another, and sequence enabling conditions before ratcheting obligations that depend on them.**

That still leaves a further question, which `33-ideal-solutions-as-flywheels-compounding-learning-curves-and-self-reinforcing-delivery.md` takes up: once the right bundle exists, does repeated deployment of that bundle create a self-reinforcing flywheel, or does it remain expensive, bespoke, and politically brittle each time? `51-ideal-solutions-as-dependency-light-systems-few-missing-prerequisites-low-synchronisation-burden-and-use-what-is-ready.md` then adds a companion screen: even a well-packaged bundle may still deserve demotion if too many of its critical complements are still missing and too much simultaneous coordination must succeed before it starts working at scale.

## Why this belongs inside the ideal solution

A climate portfolio can look ambitious on paper and still disappoint in the real world if each instrument is asked to work alone.

Examples of orphan failure include:
- clean-power additions that arrive faster than interconnection, storage, and flexible demand, leaving curtailment, queues, and local backlash to do the governing [S55][S56][S57][S103]
- building electrification that arrives without envelope improvement, connected controls, installer capacity, or tariff reform, making the clean option look expensive or unreliable [S51][S52][S53][S54][S106]
- cooling-access expansion that ignores passive design, appliance efficiency, refrigerants, and peak-load management, so adaptation success becomes a new mitigation and grid problem [S16][S17][S104][S106]
- pricing or phase-out measures introduced where practical substitutes are still scarce, making the policy feel punitive rather than developmental [S69][S97][S112]

The archive therefore treats complementarity as a design rule, not a presentational nicety.

## Four kinds of complementarity the archive cares about

### 1. Physical complements

Some interventions are technically inseparable in practice.

The clearest example is the power system:
- renewable generation needs transmission, distribution, interconnection, storage, and flexible demand if its theoretical output is to become reliable delivered service [S55][S56][S57][S103]
- IEA says demand flexibility can provide capacity to the grid at a cost up to **three times lower** than building new capacity, while also reducing emissions and peak stress [S111]
- IEA also says global grid queues now exceed **2 500 GW**, so generation-only ambition is increasingly bottlenecked ambition [S103]

The same pattern appears elsewhere:
- heat pumps work best with envelope upgrades, efficient emitters, smart controls, trained installers, and electricity pricing that does not structurally penalize efficient electric heat [S51][S52][S53][S54]
- EVs work best with charging networks, distribution upgrades, smart charging, and tariff design that rewards off-peak use [S30][S56][S58]
- sustainable cooling works best as a package of passive design, fans, efficient AC, refrigerant governance, and cleaner grids rather than as brute-force compressor growth [S16][S17][S104][S106]

### 2. Policy complements

The archive rejects the idea that one instrument should carry the entire transition.

OECD’s 2025 review says policy instruments can be bundled to address multiple failures, that some are more effective if preceded by others that create enabling conditions, and that public investment in infrastructure and innovation can pave the way for regulatory and price-based instruments [S112].

So ideal packages pair:
- public investment with standards and mandates [S4][S14][S59][S112]
- procurement with common definitions and MRV [S26][S27][S65][S68]
- carbon pricing with redistribution, alternatives, and communications that preserve legitimacy [S69][S97][S112]
- fossil phase-down with worker protection, replacement capacity, and regional transition [S23][S24][S25]

This is not policy maximalism. It is realism about how systems actually change.

### 3. Social complements

A measure is more durable when people can actually use the cleaner alternative.

That means climate policy often needs a paired social design:
- clean cooking with affordability and distribution systems [S70][S71]
- retrofit with tenant protection and financing access [S51][S52][S69]
- transit and active mobility with safe networks, frequency, and land-use support [S77][S79][S80][S97]
- carbon pricing or restrictions with visible household protection and practical substitutes [S69][S97][S112]

OECD’s sequencing work notes that infrastructure availability can also increase support for stricter climate policy; its cited survey evidence suggests that making public transport widely available increases support for banning combustion-engine cars [S112].

### 4. Time complements

Some measures are complements because of order, not only coexistence.

The archive’s default sequence is:
1. build alternatives and enabling infrastructure,
2. reduce cost and hassle of cleaner choices,
3. ratchet standards, pricing, or phase-out rules more firmly once substitutes are usable,
4. keep tightening as institutions learn and delivery improves [S29][S51][S59][S97][S112]

This is not a plea for delay. It is a rule for **credible acceleration**.

Poor sequencing can make a valid policy look socially or technically impossible. Good sequencing can make a previously contested policy normal.

## The packaging rule in practice

### Renewables are not a package by themselves
Renewables become an ideal solution when packaged with grids, storage, demand flexibility, queue reform, and planning competence [S55][S56][S57][S103][S107].

### Electrification is not a package by itself
Electrification becomes ideal when packaged with efficiency, wiring, controls, tariff reform, and reliability design so demand growth does not outrun system readiness [S51][S52][S53][S54][S56][S106][S111].

### Cooling access is not a package by itself
Cooling becomes ideal when packaged with passive-first design, efficient appliances, refrigerant management, and clean power so adaptation gains do not create a new peak-load trap [S16][S17][S104][S106].

### Pricing is not a package by itself
Pricing becomes ideal when packaged with affordability protection, communication, and available low-carbon substitutes so it pushes on an open door rather than a locked one [S69][S97][S112].

### Fossil decline is not a package by itself
Managed decline becomes ideal when packaged with replacement services, labour transition, local economic transition, and enough clean capacity that retirement does not look like imposed scarcity [S23][S24][S25][S102][S103].

## What this rule changes in research judgment

When two proposals look similarly climate-positive, the archive should usually promote the one that:
- unlocks or strengthens several other measures at once,
- removes a bottleneck that is suppressing many other gains,
- improves the effectiveness or legitimacy of later standards, prices, or phase-outs,
- reduces the chance that a valid policy will fail because its complements were missing,
- creates a platform on which future tightening becomes easier rather than harder.

This tends to upgrade grids, flexible demand, retrofit quality, public transport, tariff reform, standards infrastructure, public procurement, installer capacity, risk disclosure, and household protection. These are often undervalued if each is judged as a narrow standalone abatement measure rather than as an enabler.

## A simple orphan-policy screen

A future proposal should usually be demoted if it cannot answer most of the following questions.

### 1. Complement test
What other measures must exist for this to work well, and are they named?

### 2. Bottleneck test
Does this relieve a system bottleneck, or does it add demand to a bottlenecked system?

### 3. Sequence test
Is the enabling layer built before the tightening layer, or is the proposal trying to force behavior before alternatives are practical?

### 4. Interaction-risk test
Could this policy underperform or backfire because another needed instrument, institution, or infrastructure layer is absent?

### 5. Legitimacy test
Will households, firms, cities, and workers experience the package as workable, or only the headline instrument?

## What the archive distrusts more strongly after this note

The archive becomes more skeptical of:
- renewable targets without grid, storage, and flexibility packages [S55][S57][S103]
- EV or heat-pump pushes without charging, wiring, smart controls, installer capacity, and tariff reform [S51][S52][S53][S54][S56]
- adaptation spending that protects assets but leaves exposure growth untouched [S81][S82][S83][S84]
- carbon pricing debates that abstract away from redistribution and substitute availability [S69][S97][S112]
- industrial decarbonization mandates without definitions, procurement, infrastructure, and market formation [S26][S27][S112]
- cooling-access strategies that scale electricity demand without equal attention to passive measures and efficiency [S104][S106]

These are not always wrong. But they are frequently **under-packaged**.

## One-paragraph compression

Ideal climate solutions should be assembled as **mutually reinforcing packages**. They should pair clean supply with the grid and flexibility needed to use it, electrification with efficiency and tariff reform, cooling access with passive-first design and clean power, phase-outs with affordable substitutes and worker protection, pricing with redistribution and visible alternatives, and standards with procurement, definitions, and enforcement. They should sequence enabling conditions before ratcheting harder obligations, so climate policy works as a widening platform rather than a series of orphaned commands [S23][S25][S51][S56][S69][S97][S103][S104][S106][S111][S112].
