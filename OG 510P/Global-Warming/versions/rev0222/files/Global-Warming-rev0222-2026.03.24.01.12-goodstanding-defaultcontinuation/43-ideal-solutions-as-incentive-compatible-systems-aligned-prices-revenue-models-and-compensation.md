# 43 — Ideal Solutions as Incentive-Compatible Systems: Aligned Prices, Revenue Models, and Compensation

## Claim

The archive now has strong answers to what ideal climate solutions should build, what they should exclude, how they should allocate scarcity, how they should stay fail-soft, how they should avoid one-way loss, how they should package measures, how they should compound, how they should correct themselves, how they should move through many venues, how they should stay low-friction, persistent, interoperable, buffered, hard to capture, fast enough to matter, and grounded in physical outcomes rather than paper stories.

One more rule belongs inside the canon:

**ideal solutions should be incentive-compatible.**

That means the cleaner, more flexible, less wasteful, more protective action should usually make **financial and institutional sense for the actor taking it**.

The archive should therefore prefer climate designs that:
- stop paying for harmful behaviour,
- pay or reward useful behaviour,
- repair split incentives where the chooser does not capture the benefit,
- and compensate losers or vulnerable households openly rather than preserving destructive price signals in the background.

IEA’s 2025 affordability work says major barriers include the **landlord-tenant split incentive, high upfront costs, and administrative complexity**, while good policy design uses targeted financial support and simplified delivery [S137]. IEA’s 2026 flexibility work says demand-response programmes let households and firms shift or shed load in response to grid or market signals and often provide **direct payments, rebates, or bill credits** for doing so [S56]. OECD’s 2025 climate-growth work adds the macro-political point: **revenue recycling** from carbon pricing or subsidy reform can limit distributive harm and increase the acceptability of climate policies [S138]. And IEA’s fossil-subsidy work says many fossil subsidies remain elevated, poorly targeted, and still send the wrong price signals to users [S139].

This note therefore adds a simple rule to the archive:

**prefer climate systems in which ordinary self-interest is pointed in the right direction over systems that ask people, firms, utilities, landlords, or local governments to fight the incentive structure all by themselves.**

## Why incentive compatibility belongs inside the ideal solution

### 1. Heroism is scarce

A climate package that works only when people repeatedly choose against their immediate financial interest is not an ideal package.

It may work for a minority of highly motivated actors. It rarely works at civilizational scale.

The archive already argues that climate policy should not depend on permanent household expertise, paperwork stamina, or altruistic sacrifice [S58][S69][S118][S119]. This note adds the adjacent economic rule: it should also not depend on actors repeatedly paying more, waiting longer, or taking more risk than the dirty alternative while being told to feel virtuous about it.

Ideal solutions should reduce the amount of virtue required.

### 2. Price signals still matter, even in portfolios that use many instruments

The archive does not reduce climate policy to carbon pricing. But it also does not pretend prices are irrelevant.

If fossil fuels, peak-time consumption, waste, sprawl, leakage, and other harmful behaviours remain implicitly rewarded, then cleaner substitutes must fight uphill in every transaction.

IEA’s current fossil-subsidy work says inefficient fossil subsidies can harm energy markets, government budgets, and climate efforts; that many such subsidies are poorly targeted and disproportionately benefit higher-income groups; and that governments still spent **USD 620 billion** in 2023 subsidising fossil-fuel use, far above the roughly **USD 70 billion** spent on consumer-facing clean-energy investments such as efficiency upgrades, EV grants, or heat-pump rebates [S139].

That is not a minor accounting detail. It is a statement about which behaviours are still being paid to persist.

So the ideal solution should generally prefer:
- fossil-subsidy phaseout paired with targeted protection [S23][S69][S138][S139]
- electricity-pricing and tariff reform that stops punishing useful electrification or flexibility by design [S53][S56]
- standards and fees that make wasteful options less attractive where direct prohibition is not used [S16][S17][S58]

### 3. Split incentives are not edge cases; they are central design failures

Many climate actions are blocked not because nobody benefits, but because the **wrong actor** receives the benefit.

IEA’s 2025 affordability report names the landlord-tenant split incentive directly, alongside high upfront costs and administrative complexity [S137]. The archive’s buildings file already says retrofits stall because landlords, tenants, multi-owner buildings, fragmented contractors, and finance structures are misaligned [S53][S54].

This pattern appears far beyond rental housing:
- building owners may pay while tenants save,
- developers may optimise for lowest capex rather than lowest lifetime cost,
- households may be asked to provide flexibility without being paid for it,
- municipalities may carry adaptation costs while higher-level systems capture part of the fiscal benefit,
- firms may face transition risk while lacking credible long-run demand or price floors for cleaner output [S27][S28][S56][S91][S132].

An ideal solution therefore does not merely identify the technically efficient measure.
It repairs the actor mismatch.

### 4. Compensation is not a side concession; it is part of good design

The archive already insists that climate policy must be materially fair [S69][S70][S71]. This note adds a sharper point:

**compensation and revenue recycling are part of incentive design, not evidence of weakness.**

OECD’s 2025 climate-growth work says governments can use revenues generated by carbon pricing to invest in low-carbon infrastructure or climate priorities, or to limit distributive impacts and increase the acceptability of climate policies [S138]. It also says partial redistribution to households can help limit negative effects on consumption and labour markets [S138].

That matters because many climate policies fail not when the environmental logic is explained, but when households experience them as a one-way cost increase with no visible reciprocal benefit.

Ideal solutions should therefore prefer packages in which:
- broad harmful subsidies are replaced by narrower targeted support,
- carbon or energy-transition revenues are recycled visibly,
- vulnerable households see bill reduction, cash support, or service improvement,
- and workers or regions facing real losses are protected explicitly rather than symbolically [S23][S69][S71][S138][S139].

### 5. Flexibility, efficiency, and clean behaviour should be paid for when they provide system value

Some climate-relevant behaviour is not only morally desirable. It is operationally valuable.

IEA’s 2026 flexibility analysis says demand-response programmes help households and businesses shift or shed electricity use in response to grid or market signals, and that direct payments, rebates, or bill credits can reward participation [S56]. It also says only around **100 GW** of demand response is currently utilised worldwide, even though the addressable potential in industry and cooling loads is far larger [S56].

The archive should read this as a general design lesson:

**when households, buildings, fleets, or factories are being asked to provide a useful climate or grid service, they should usually be paid for it or see a clear bill benefit from it.**

Otherwise governments and system operators are trying to procure value while pretending it is free.

## What incentive compatibility means in practice

The archive’s incentive-compatible rule does **not** imply that every climate problem should be solved with a tax or a subsidy.

Sometimes the right answer is a ban, a standard, a code, a public buildout, or a procurement rule.

The rule is narrower:

**after the chosen policy package is in place, are actors still being rewarded to do the wrong thing, or has the system been redesigned so the right thing is normal, bankable, and self-reinforcing?**

That can be achieved in different ways:
- standards can remove bad options from sale,
- targeted support can reduce upfront cost,
- dynamic tariffs and flexibility payments can reward useful load shifting,
- revenue recycling can protect legitimacy,
- and auction / contract structures can give cleaner producers bankable demand or price visibility where markets are immature [S53][S56][S69][S132][S138].

## The archive’s incentive-compatibility screen

A future proposal should usually be promoted only if it passes most of the following tests.

### 1. Wrong-price test
Does the proposal remove or counteract a price signal that still rewards harmful behaviour, or does it leave the dirty baseline quietly subsidised? [S23][S139]

### 2. Split-incentive test
Does the actor who must pay also capture enough of the benefit, or has the design repaired landlord-tenant, owner-user, public-private, or multi-owner misalignment? [S53][S137]

### 3. Useful-service-payment test
If the system needs flexibility, demand response, ecosystem stewardship, risk reduction, or verified performance, are actors actually rewarded or contracted to provide it? [S56][S121][S122]

### 4. Compensation test
If the policy raises visible costs for some households, workers, or regions, is there a clear compensation or revenue-recycling pathway that protects legitimacy? [S69][S71][S138]

### 5. Persistence test
Will the clean behaviour remain financially sensible over time, or does the design depend on short-lived grants followed by reversion to a still-distorted baseline? [S53][S132][S138]

### 6. Administrative-fit test
Is the support aligned with how people actually pay bills and make decisions — point of sale, on-bill, in rent-setting rules, in default tariffs, in procurement rules — or only through delayed reimbursement and complex claims? [S53][S69][S137]

Measures that pass four or five of these tests deserve promotion. Measures that pass only one or two should usually be demoted unless they solve an exceptional hard constraint.

## What incentive-compatible portfolios overweight

The archive’s incentive-compatible rule pushes ideal portfolios toward:
- phasing out broad fossil-fuel subsidies while protecting vulnerable households through targeted support rather than preserving cheap dirty energy for everyone [S23][S69][S138][S139]
- point-of-sale, on-bill, or otherwise embedded support for retrofits, efficient appliances, and clean heat rather than reimbursement-heavy schemes that favour the cash-rich [S53][S69][S137]
- landlord-tenant and multi-owner-building fixes so efficiency upgrades and clean heating can move in the real building stock rather than only in ideal owner-occupied cases [S53][S137]
- direct payment, rebate, or bill-credit structures for flexible demand where households and firms are being asked to help balance clean power systems [S56]
- electricity-tariff and market reform that stops punishing electrification or undervaluing flexibility [S53][S56]
- revenue recycling that funds low-carbon infrastructure, lowers household burden, or cushions transition losses rather than vanishing into general political fog [S138]
- auction and contract structures that make clean deployment bankable when market formation is the bottleneck, especially in newer or harder sectors [S132]

## What incentive-compatible portfolios distrust

The archive therefore distrusts:
- climate packages that ask households to absorb higher visible costs while fossil support remains broad, hidden, or politically protected [S23][S69][S139]
- rebate mazes that require the actor with the least liquidity to pay first and recover later [S69][S137]
- building strategies that assume owner-occupiers are the universal delivery model [S53][S137]
- grid or tariff designs that ask for flexibility but do not pay for it [S56]
- carbon-pricing or subsidy-reform packages with no visible revenue recycling or social protection [S71][S138]
- moralised messaging standing in for actual incentive repair [S58][S69]

## Bottom line

The ideal solution to global warming should not rely on constant virtue against a hostile incentive system.

It should make the cleaner, safer, more flexible, and less wasteful action **financially and institutionally normal** more often: by removing broad dirty subsidies, fixing split incentives, paying for useful system services, and recycling revenues or support in ways that protect legitimacy.

That is not a decorative improvement.
It is part of what makes the solution scale.
