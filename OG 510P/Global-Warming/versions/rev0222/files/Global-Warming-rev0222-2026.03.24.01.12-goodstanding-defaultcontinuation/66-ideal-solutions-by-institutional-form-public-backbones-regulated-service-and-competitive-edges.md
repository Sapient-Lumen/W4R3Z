# 66 — Ideal Solutions by Institutional Form: Public Backbones, Regulated Service, and Competitive Edges

## Claim

The archive already says that ideal climate solutions should be physical-first, low-friction, fiscally durable, actor-aware, and delivered through mechanism mixes rather than one elegant instrument.
It says obligations should be pushed toward concentrated control points and that public money should often build durable capacity rather than subsidise bad baselines forever [S112][S117][S118][S119][S137][S143].

One more compact rule belongs in the canon.
For the archive’s companion note on **who** should carry the hardest duties, see `63-ideal-solutions-by-actor-concentrated-control-points-and-no-heroic-consumers.md`.
For the archive’s companion note on **which mechanisms** should usually be used, see `64-ideal-solutions-by-mechanism-defaults-first-real-buildout-and-prices-in-a-supporting-role.md`.

**when the climate task is a network, a universal-service obligation, or a high-coordination system, the archive should usually prefer public or tightly regulated backbones — and use competition mainly at modular, contestable edges.**

In plain terms:
- use **public provision, regulated utilities, municipal systems, public banks, or tightly governed service obligations** where continuity, interoperability, universal access, and low-cost capital matter most,
- and use **competition, auctions, procurement, and multiple vendors** where modules are genuinely contestable and comparison can lower cost without destroying coordination [S13][S14][S69][S80][S91][S96][S112][S129][S130][S131][S132][S143].

So the archive’s shortest institutional-form rule is:

**public or regulated backbone where coordination is the problem; competitive edge where modular supply is the opportunity.**

## Why this belongs in the canon

### 1. Many decisive climate layers are not simple retail markets

Grids, distribution systems, urban transport interfaces, social-housing retrofit pipelines, early-warning systems, water resilience, and other long-lived civic systems are not well described as a pile of isolated purchases.
They are coordination-heavy service systems with reliability duties, interoperability needs, and repeated maintenance obligations [S14][S15][S53][S56][S69][S72][S80][S104][S117].

That means the archive should not default to the idea that every climate problem is best solved by atomised market choice plus a price signal.
Some climate lanes are closer to **utility design, public works, regulated service delivery, or civic protection** than to ordinary consumer markets.

### 2. Long-lived backbone assets usually benefit from cheaper capital and durable obligations

A great deal of climate work now turns on infrastructure with long asset lives, heavy upfront cost, and large system spillovers.
IEA’s EMDE finance work makes clear that cost of capital is itself a climate variable, and that clean projects in many emerging and developing economies are punished by financing conditions far more than by underlying technology cost [S13][S91][S96].

That pushes the archive toward an institutional conclusion:
when the task is a long-lived backbone asset with large public value, **public balance sheets, regulated returns, guarantees, concessional structures, or public-development-finance platforms** often fit better than hoping fragmented private retail uptake will somehow assemble the system in time [S13][S14][S80][S91][S94][S96][S143].

### 3. Universal service and fairness often require obligations that markets alone undersupply

The archive already says ideal solutions should widen access, lower bills structurally, and avoid forcing poorer or more exposed people to buy their way into safety one household at a time [S69][S70][S71][S104][S117][S137].
That has an institutional-form implication.

Where access, affordability, continuity of service, or territorial coverage matter, the archive should usually favour forms that can carry **service obligations**:
- utilities with regulatory duties,
- municipal or public systems,
- social-housing-first programmes,
- public procurement for shared infrastructure,
- or concession / franchise models that are tightly governed against cream-skimming and neglect [S53][S69][S80][S91][S96][S117][S137][S143].

Otherwise the transition can become socially selective: the easiest customers get served, the hardest buildings and districts are deferred, and the people most in need face the highest hassle.

### 4. Competition still matters — but mostly in the right layer

This note is not a blanket argument for monopoly or for state operation of everything.
The archive already values competition, procurement integrity, standardisation, and multiple routes of delivery where those reduce cost and rent extraction [S129][S130][S131][S132].

So the right question is not “state or market?” in the abstract.
It is:

**which layer needs coordination and universal-service discipline, and which layer can safely be opened to contestable supply?**

Often the best answer is hybrid:
- a regulated or publicly coordinated backbone,
- common standards and open interfaces,
- then competitive procurement of equipment, construction, software, services, and maintenance around that backbone [S14][S27][S59][S68][S129][S130][S131][S132].

### 5. Institutional form is part of delivery realism, not a side ideology debate

The archive already says ideal solutions must be deliverable by real institutions under real constraints [S59][S91][S96][S103].
Choosing the wrong institutional form can make a technically sound solution fail in practice:
- too little coordination,
- too much customer hassle,
- no duty to serve hard places,
- expensive capital,
- weak maintenance,
- or fragmented data and standards.

So institutional form belongs in the canon for the same reason mechanism hierarchy, actor allocation, and scoreboard discipline belong in the canon: **it changes whether the package works in the world or only on paper.**

## The archive’s institutional-form hierarchy

A future proposal should usually be stronger when it fits most of this hierarchy.

### 1. Backbone layer
Use public provision, regulated utilities, public banks, municipal systems, or tightly governed concession / franchise models where the task requires:
- universal or near-universal service,
- network coordination,
- interoperability,
- long asset life,
- low-cost capital,
- or standing maintenance and reliability obligations [S13][S14][S69][S80][S91][S96][S143].

### 2. Common-rules layer
Use public standards, common definitions, tariffs, codes, planning rules, and open interfaces so participants can plug into the same system rather than rebuilding the rules project by project [S14][S27][S68][S118][S119][S131].

### 3. Competitive-delivery layer
Use auctions, procurement, framework agreements, multiple vendors, and transparent competition for the modules that are genuinely contestable: equipment supply, construction packages, software, materials, operations contracts, and other comparable services [S27][S59][S129][S130][S131][S132].

### 4. Protection layer
Where markets still leave gaps, use explicit affordability rules, social tariffs, targeted support, public-service obligations, and anti-exclusion safeguards rather than assuming the underserved will be reached eventually [S69][S70][S71][S137][S143].

## The archive’s institutional-form tests

### 1. Coordination-density test
Is the proposal mainly a coordination problem with network effects, interoperability needs, and shared reliability duties, or is it mostly a modular product market?

### 2. Universal-service test
Would failure to serve low-income, rural, rental, informal, or high-risk users materially undermine the climate and justice case? If yes, stronger public or regulated obligations usually belong in the design [S69][S70][S71][S137].

### 3. Cost-of-capital test
Is the asset long-lived and capital-heavy enough that cheaper public, regulated, guaranteed, or concessional finance could change feasibility more than marginal retail incentives could? [S13][S91][S94][S96]

### 4. Cream-skimming test
Could a lightly governed market serve the easiest customers first while leaving hard buildings, weak grids, exposed communities, or poorer districts stranded? [S69][S80][S91][S96][S137]

### 5. Accountability-and-maintenance test
Does the chosen form create a standing duty to maintain service, report performance, and fix failures over time, or does it mainly reward initial transaction volume? [S59][S65][S80][S123][S143]

### 6. Contestability test
Are there layers that can and should remain competitive without losing system coherence? If yes, open those layers deliberately instead of turning the whole system into one opaque incumbent [S129][S130][S131][S132].

## What this rules in

This note rules **in**:
- regulated-utility and public-investment approaches where grids and distribution are the bottleneck [S14][S55][S56]
- public-building-first and social-housing-first retrofit delivery where programme coordination and inclusion matter more than early-adopter retail marketing [S53][S69][S137]
- municipal or public-procurement-led delivery for buses, public buildings, cooling, drainage, and other shared urban systems [S80][S104]
- public-development-finance, guarantee, and concessional platforms where high financing cost blocks otherwise sound clean infrastructure [S13][S91][S94][S96]
- tightly governed franchise or concession models where private delivery is useful but continuity, access, and performance obligations must remain explicit [S131][S143]

## What this demotes

This note demotes:
- retail-choice-only strategies for problems that are mostly network, landlord, utility, or municipal coordination problems [S53][S56][S80][S117][S137]
- privatisation or liberalisation moves that weaken universal-service duties, planning capacity, or maintenance discipline without solving the real bottleneck [S69][S80][S143]
- subsidy-heavy market creation that pays many actors to do piecemeal what a regulated backbone or public programme could organise more reliably and more fairly [S91][S96][S137][S143]
- monopoly or incumbent protection in layers that could be openly procured or competitively supplied under common standards [S129][S130][S131][S132]

## One-paragraph compression

**Ideal climate solutions should match institutional form to system shape. When the problem is a long-lived network, a universal-service obligation, or a coordination-heavy civic system, the archive should usually prefer a public or tightly regulated backbone with explicit duties to build, maintain, and serve. When the problem is modular and contestable, it should use competition, procurement, and multiple vendors at the edge. The goal is not “state versus market” in the abstract, but the right mix of public coordination and competitive supply for fast, fair, durable delivery [S13][S14][S69][S80][S91][S96][S129][S130][S131][S132][S137][S143].**
