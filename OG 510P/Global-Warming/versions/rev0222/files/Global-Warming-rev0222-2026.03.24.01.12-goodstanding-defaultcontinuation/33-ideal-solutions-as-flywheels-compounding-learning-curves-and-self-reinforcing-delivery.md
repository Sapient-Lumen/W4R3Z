# 33 — Ideal Solutions as Flywheels: Compounding, Learning Curves, and Self-Reinforcing Delivery

## Claim

The archive now has a strong answer to what ideal climate solutions should build, exclude, prioritize under scarcity, protect against failure, protect against one-way loss, and package together.

One missing layer remained:

**ideal solutions should create flywheels.**

That means they should not only cut emissions directly. They should also make later climate action **cheaper, faster, more routine, more politically durable, and more administratively competent**.

A weak climate measure can still look respectable in a static cost-per-tonne table. But an ideal climate measure does something more valuable: after the first wave, it leaves behind lower unit costs, better delivery routines, stronger supply chains, more usable standards, better data, more confident institutions, and a larger group of households, firms, workers, and cities with a material stake in continuing the transition.

Recent evidence sharpens why this belongs inside the ideal solution. IRENA’s 2025 cost update says **91%** of new utility-scale renewable projects commissioned in 2024 were cheaper than the cheapest new fossil alternative, with solar PV on average **41%** cheaper and onshore wind **53%** cheaper [S107]. IEA says battery costs have already fallen by about **90%** since 2010 and could fall a further **40%** from 2023 to 2030 as manufacturing and chemistry improve [S48]. IEA’s Global EV Outlook 2025 says electric-car sales topped **17 million** in 2024, battery-pack prices fell by more than **25%** from 2023 to 2024, and electric-car sales are expected to exceed **20 million** in 2025 [S30]. OECD’s 2025 governance work adds the political-institutional version of the same point: durable climate delivery depends on **commitment, capabilities, and consensus**, and progress monitoring is part of evidence-based policy adjustment over time [S59].

This note therefore adds a flywheel rule to the canon:

**prefer measures whose first wave makes the second wave easier.**

## Why compounding belongs inside the ideal solution

The climate problem is too large to solve through a long sequence of bespoke heroic efforts.

If every heat pump, transmission line, industrial retrofit, cooling upgrade, electric bus fleet, or methane-repair regime requires the same level of subsidy, specialist attention, political drama, procurement improvisation, and public confusion forever, the transition remains fragile even when individual projects succeed.

Ideal solutions therefore cannot be judged only by their first-round effect. They must also be judged by their **second-round and third-round effects**:
- do they lower future unit costs?
- do they standardize delivery?
- do they widen industrial and social constituencies?
- do they increase institutional confidence and competence?
- do they reduce the need for future crisis-level intervention?

This is different from the archive’s packaging rule in `32-ideal-solutions-as-packages-complementarity-sequencing-and-bottleneck-release.md`.

- the packaging rule asks **what must arrive together** for a policy to work well
- the flywheel rule asks **whether today’s package makes tomorrow’s package easier**

An ideal solution should usually pass both tests.

## Five flywheels the archive cares about

### 1. Technology-cost flywheels

Some measures improve as scale itself does part of the work.

The clearest examples are solar, wind, batteries, and electric vehicles:
- IRENA says in 2024 solar PV was on average **41%** cheaper than the cheapest fossil alternative and onshore wind **53%** cheaper [S107]
- IEA says average battery costs have fallen about **90%** since 2010 and further manufacturing and chemistry gains could cut global average lithium-ion battery costs by another **40%** from 2023 to 2030 [S48]
- IEA says electric cars often now have a lower lifetime total cost of ownership than internal-combustion cars, and that falling battery-pack prices and economies of scale materially improved affordability in 2024 [S30]

These are not just encouraging market statistics. They reveal a selection rule.

Ideal solutions should generally prefer technologies and delivery models that become more affordable, more available, and easier to mass-deploy as adoption rises. They should be suspicious of strategies whose costs remain stubbornly artisanal even after years of effort.

This tends to upgrade:
- standardized retrofit components,
- mass-market heat pumps and efficient appliances,
- battery manufacturing and recycling,
- proven modular renewables,
- common charging hardware and software,
- open product standards,
- procurement designs that aggregate demand and reduce first-mover risk [S30][S48][S50][S51][S52][S53][S107]

### 2. Infrastructure-utilization flywheels

Ideal solutions do not only add new assets. They also learn how to use existing assets better.

IEA’s 2025 flexibility work says demand flexibility and efficiency reinforce each other, and that by improving the use of existing generation and network assets, demand flexibility can raise system efficiency by up to **30%** and provide capacity at a cost up to **three times lower** than building new capacity [S111]. IEA’s 2025 efficiency review also shows how better equipment and controls can avoid large amounts of demand growth before new supply has to be built [S106].

This means some interventions compound not mainly by making the unit cheaper, but by making the **whole system less stressed**:
- better building envelopes reduce the size and cost of later heating and cooling systems [S51][S52][S53][S54]
- smart charging and dynamic tariffs make EV growth easier for the grid to absorb [S30][S56][S111]
- passive cooling, fans, and efficient AC reduce later peak-load and grid-expansion pressure [S16][S17][S104][S106]
- digitalized flexibility and better load management let grids carry more electrification before physical bottlenecks force costlier upgrades [S56][S103][S111]

The archive therefore prefers interventions that create a **gentler future baseline** rather than repeatedly adding capacity to a badly designed demand profile.

### 3. Administrative-capability flywheels

A climate program that never gets easier to administer is not ideal.

OECD’s 2025 governance work says effective green-transition delivery depends on a reinforcing architecture of **commitment, capabilities, and consensus**, and that public institutions need progress monitoring to support evidence-based adjustments over time [S59]. The archive’s delivery-state sources also show that permitting quality, local-authority competence, procurement design, grid queue management, skills pipelines, and public participation are major practical determinants of speed [S60][S61][S62][S63][S64].

That means ideal solutions should usually prefer designs that become more **routine** with repetition:
- standardized project documents instead of bespoke negotiations every time
- repeatable procurement templates instead of one-off contracting theatre
- one-stop shops instead of fragmented household navigation [S53][S69]
- common MRV and reporting rules instead of bespoke claims markets [S65][S66][S67][S68]
- programmatic retrofit, cooling, and resilience delivery instead of scattered pilotism [S16][S17][S51][S53][S54]

When institutions repeat similar tasks at scale, they learn. Queue reform improves. Installers become more productive. Inspectors know what good looks like. Procurement officers stop reinventing eligibility rules. Public agencies build memory. Firms invest because rules look durable rather than improvised.

The ideal solution therefore values **routinization** as a climate asset in its own right.

### 4. Coalition and legitimacy flywheels

Some climate measures strengthen themselves because they create visible winners, not just abstract future beneficiaries.

OECD says around **70%** of people across OECD countries think addressing climate change should be a priority for government, but only about **42%** are confident their country will reduce greenhouse-gas emissions over the next decade; this confidence gap matters because doubts about fairness or effectiveness weaken long-term implementation [S59]. OECD’s policy-packaging work also says sequencing and enabling infrastructure can build support for tougher climate policy later [S112].

This matters because a measure that improves daily life can recruit its own defenders:
- lower bills from efficiency and better tariffs [S58][S69][S106][S111]
- cleaner air from fossil-combustion reduction [S85][S86][S89]
- safer homes from cooling, retrofits, and flood/heat preparedness [S16][S17][S41][S88]
- better mobility from transit, safer streets, and less forced car dependence [S77][S79][S80][S97]
- more reliable power from better grids and flexible demand [S55][S56][S57][S103][S111]

This is why the archive keeps preferring high-wellbeing climate design over sacrifice-first design. An ideal solution does not just survive public scrutiny. It **improves the social base for later climate action**.

### 5. Truth-and-standards flywheels

A solution that cannot be measured or compared cleanly is harder to scale cleanly.

The archive already treats methane measurement, interoperable low-emissions product definitions, public reporting, and liability rules as truth infrastructure [S11][S65][S66][S67][S68]. The flywheel point is that these do more than prevent greenwash.

They also lower the transaction costs of future action:
- investors can price risk more confidently,
- buyers can compare claims more easily,
- regulators can tighten standards with less confusion,
- suppliers know what must be delivered,
- enforcement gets more predictable,
- credible producers are less undercut by unverifiable marketing.

In other words, truth infrastructure compounds because each round of better data and clearer definitions makes the next round of deployment, procurement, and enforcement less improvisational.

## What this rule changes in research judgment

When two proposals look similarly climate-positive in a narrow static sense, the archive should usually promote the one that:
- gets cheaper or easier with repetition,
- creates a standardized delivery pathway,
- widens the constituency that materially benefits from continued action,
- builds institutional capability rather than consuming it,
- reduces future subsidy, transaction, or coordination burdens,
- generates information that improves later rounds of policy.

This tends to upgrade:
- retrofit-at-scale institutions,
- standard-based appliance and building upgrades,
- grid digitization and flexible-demand programs,
- public procurement that creates repeatable demand,
- charging and interconnection standards,
- battery manufacturing and recycling,
- methane-measurement systems,
- open, comparable industrial-product definitions,
- household-facing programs that make lower-carbon service visibly better rather than merely morally preferable [S11][S30][S48][S53][S56][S65][S66][S67][S68][S107][S111][S112]

## A simple flywheel screen

A future proposal should usually be promoted when it can answer most of these questions well.

### 1. Learning test
Will each additional unit make the next unit cheaper, faster, or easier to deliver?

### 2. Routinization test
Can this become a normal program, standard product, or standard contract rather than remaining bespoke?

### 3. Capability test
After five years of doing this, will public and private institutions be better at climate delivery generally, or merely exhausted by this specific task?

### 4. Constituency test
Who becomes a defender of this policy after the first wave because they directly benefit from it?

### 5. Fiscal-dependence test
Does the measure need permanent exceptional subsidy or political mobilization, or does it become more self-sustaining over time?

### 6. Knowledge test
Does implementation generate better data, standards, templates, and operational knowledge that improve later rounds?

Measures that pass four or more of these tests deserve extra weight even when a narrower annual-abatement ranking would underrate them.

## What the archive distrusts more strongly after this note

This note makes the archive more skeptical of:
- perpetual pilotism,
- artisanal one-off decarbonization,
- prestige projects with weak replication pathways,
- climate measures whose benefits are too hidden or delayed to build social durability,
- policy designs that consume scarce administrative capacity without leaving behind better routines,
- strategies that stay dependent on rare experts, bespoke contracts, or emergency subsidies year after year.

The archive does **not** conclude that every measure must become mass-market and self-propelling quickly. Some residual lanes will remain difficult, including parts of heavy industry, long-duration storage, hard-to-abate transport, and durable CDR [S14][S30][S42][S43][S44][S45][S46].

But even there, ideal solutions should ask whether today’s choices are building the standards, infrastructure, monitoring, procurement, and learning effects that make later deployment less fragile.

## One-paragraph compression

Ideal climate solutions should be chosen not only for what they cut today, but for whether they create **self-reinforcing transition dynamics**: lower technology costs, better asset utilization, stronger delivery institutions, wider political support, and cleaner truth infrastructure for future action. That is why the archive should keep preferring measures that become cheaper, more routine, more popular, and more governable after the first deployment wave — renewables, batteries, efficiency, flexible demand, standards, procurement, retrofit-at-scale systems, and measurable methane control — over measures that remain bespoke, subsidy-hungry, politically isolated, or administratively artisanal even after years of effort [S11][S30][S48][S53][S56][S59][S65][S66][S67][S68][S107][S111][S112].
