**Treat ideal solutions as a feedback system, not a checklist. Good design should produce a cleaner physical portfolio; real physical gains should produce lower bills, lower pollution, lower risk, and visible protection; those gains should strengthen legitimacy, lower resistance, and widen fiscal and political room; that should make delivery faster, truth easier to defend, readiness easier to keep live, and detours rarer and narrower. ## The compact canon

### 1. Design discipline should make the rest of the system easier, not harder
A serious design kernel should bias the programme toward physical substitution, low-friction delivery, scarce-resource thrift, and short-latency gains.
That creates a better starting position for every later layer.

Good loop:
design discipline -> simpler portfolio -> fewer bottlenecks -> faster visible gains

Bad loop:
weak design -> fragile portfolio -> more bottlenecks -> slower gains -> rising pressure for gimmicks or dirty shortcuts

### 2. The physical portfolio should create visible life improvements, not only abstract carbon claims
The physical portfolio should not only cut emissions.
It should also lower fuel-price exposure, improve air quality, reduce heat risk, harden buildings and networks, and make cleaner service the ordinary path.

Good loop:
better physical system -> visible service gains and lower exposure -> stronger public tolerance for continued buildout

Bad loop:
paper-heavy or user-burdened package -> thin lived benefits -> weak legitimacy -> easier backlash and slower next-round delivery

### 3. Protection and legitimacy should reinforce each other
Protection is not a side task.
When people see heat, flood, fire, water, health, and insurance risks being taken seriously, the programme looks less like symbolic sacrifice and more like competent protection.

Good loop:
real protection -> higher legitimacy -> stronger consent for routine governance and future tightening

Bad loop:
visible damage with weak protection -> falling trust -> stronger resistance to the wider programme -> harder future correction

### 4. A fair social contract should widen delivery room
If the programme keeps household burdens tolerable, shares upside visibly, reduces bill volatility, and lowers the cost of capital where it is highest, it creates more room for buildout and faster policy continuity.

Good loop:
fair burden-sharing and cheaper capital -> wider adoption and lower resistance -> faster scale -> better economics next round

Bad loop:
stacked burdens and weak compensation -> distrust -> stop-start policy -> higher delivery cost and weaker future coalitions

### 5. Cross-border coordination should strengthen domestic success, not sit beside it
The international layer is not an optional add-on.
Cheaper capital, common standards, cleaner trade rules, and cleaner supply chains can make domestic delivery easier and cheaper.

Good loop:
international coordination -> stronger supply chains and lower capital cost -> faster domestic buildout -> more global confidence and imitation

Bad loop:
fragmented standards and expensive capital -> slower buildout -> weaker proof of feasibility -> thinner international cooperation

### 6. Bottleneck release is a force multiplier across almost every layer
Permitting, queues, grid connections, workforce shortages, procurement friction, and missing public delivery capacity can block even a well-designed package.
Releasing those bottlenecks has compound effects.

Good loop:
bottleneck release -> faster project arrival -> earlier lived benefits -> stronger legitimacy -> more room for the next round

Bad loop:
queues and coordination failures -> delayed arrival -> fading trust -> stronger pressure for exceptions or retreat

### 7. Truth infrastructure keeps good loops from being stolen by paper claims
Measurement, comparability, enforcement, and corrective ratchets stop paper progress from masking physical underperformance.
This protects the rest of the system from false reassurance.

Good loop:
strong truth layer -> earlier detection of underperformance -> faster correction -> more credible claims -> stronger future consent

Bad loop:
weak measurement and enforcement -> paper success with physical drift -> later disappointment -> mistrust of the whole programme

### 8. Routine governance should shorten the path from signal to correction
The governing kernel matters because a mature programme lives or dies on how quickly it converts new information into repair, retargeting, or escalation.

Good loop:
clear roles, rights, clocks, and levers -> short latency from signal to correction -> lower drift and stronger performance retention

Bad loop:
blurred authority and weak clocks -> slow correction -> bigger failure accumulation -> harder and more conflictual intervention later

### 9. Readiness should lower the cost of shocks before they happen
Readiness is the system's hidden resilience reserve.
When readiness is live, rehearsed, honestly classified, and evidence-backed, shocks require less improvisation and less dirty fallback.

Good loop:
live readiness -> narrower shocks and faster recovery -> less need for exceptions -> stronger future readiness confidence

Bad loop:
paper readiness -> surprise at first stress -> wider detours -> weaker credibility and more future conservatism

### 10. Detours should restore the mainline, not become an alternative regime
Off-normal governance exists to protect people and restore the clean mainline quickly.
If detours become comfortable, broad, or weakly time-bounded, they poison the rest of the system.

Good loop:
narrow proof-gated detours -> protected people plus fast mainline repair -> tighter routine after the incident

Bad loop:
loose fallback or exception -> normalisation of dirtier practice -> weaker incentives to fix the mainline -> repeat detours
