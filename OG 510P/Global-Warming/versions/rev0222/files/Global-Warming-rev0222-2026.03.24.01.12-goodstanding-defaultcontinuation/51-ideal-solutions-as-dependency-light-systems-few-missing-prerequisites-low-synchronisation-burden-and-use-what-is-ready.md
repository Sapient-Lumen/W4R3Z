# 51 — Ideal Solutions as Dependency-Light Systems: Few Missing Prerequisites, Low Synchronisation Burden, and Use-What-Is-Ready Discipline

## Claim

Ideal climate solutions should be **dependency-light where they can be**.

They should solve as much as possible with technologies, infrastructures, institutions, finance channels, and operating routines that are already near at hand, or that require only a small number of additional complements.
A solution is not ideal if it works only when many separate missing pieces all arrive together: new fuel production, new transport networks, new storage, new offtakers, new standards, new appliances, new business models, new public trust, and new subsidy commitments — all on roughly the same timetable.

This is not the same as the archive’s packaging rule.
`32-ideal-solutions-as-packages-complementarity-sequencing-and-bottleneck-release.md` asks which complements belong together and in what order.
This note asks a prior question:

**how many critical complements are still missing, and how much simultaneous coordination must succeed before the proposal starts working at meaningful scale?**

It is also not the same as the modularity rule.
`48-ideal-solutions-as-modular-systems-repeatable-units-stepwise-scaling-and-lower-capital-at-risk.md` asks whether a system can scale in chunks.
This note asks whether the system depends on a long chain of other things that are not yet ready.
A technology can be modular yet still be dependency-heavy.

And it is not a claim that every climate lane should use only today’s easiest tools.
Some hard sectors really do require new fuels, new storage systems, new industrial processes, or new transport infrastructure.
The point is narrower:

**where mature direct options exist, ideal portfolios should usually prefer the path with fewer missing prerequisites, clearer operating logic, and less need for synchronized system buildout.**

## Why this belongs in the canon

The archive already prefers low-friction delivery, modularity, copyability, scarcity discipline, and packaging with enabling conditions.
Dependency-light design sharpens all five.

OECD’s 2025 sequencing review says some climate policies are more effective when enabling conditions are already in place, and that public investment in infrastructure and innovation can make later regulatory or price-based instruments work better by ensuring low-carbon alternatives are available, affordable, and accessible [S112].
IEA’s 2025 Germany review makes the direct-heating version of the same point unusually concrete: electricity is the **most efficient and scalable option** for decarbonising heating in decentralised systems, and mixed signals about hydrogen in home heating can **delay the investment case for switching to heat pumps today** [S165].
IEA’s energy-efficiency tracking says efficiency remains the clean transition’s **first fuel** and among the quickest and most cost-effective mitigation options, and that policy packages work best when supported by clear strategies, resources, implementation capacity, and monitoring [S166].
IEA’s 2025 hydrogen infrastructure review then shows what a dependency-heavy lane looks like in practice: a large announced pipeline can coexist with weak delivery, with less than **6%** of announced hydrogen pipelines to 2035 at final investment decision and only **5%** of announced underground hydrogen storage at FID or under construction; export-oriented projects are especially fragile, with only **5%** having reached the investment stage [S167].

So the archive needs a tighter rule:

**prefer climate measures that work with fewer missing prerequisites, and reserve dependency-heavy chains for genuinely no-substitute uses.**

## Why ideal portfolios need dependency-light design

### 1. Every missing prerequisite is another place the transition can stall

A proposal that depends on one mature substitute is very different from a proposal that depends on six immature complements.
The second can fail even when its physics are plausible.
It can fail because infrastructure is late, offtake is weak, standards are unclear, public support is uncertain, capital is expensive, or no one wants to commit first.

OECD’s 2025 sequencing work is useful here because it makes the general pattern explicit: some measures only become effective after enabling conditions are present [S112].
That implies a second judgment rule:
not all climate-positive measures deserve equal early weight.
Measures with fewer missing conditions often deserve promotion because they can move sooner, with less waiting and less synchronized institutional success.

### 2. Direct options often beat elegant multi-link chains

The archive does not need to be anti-hydrogen, anti-fuels, or anti-new infrastructure to say something simple:
where direct electrification and efficiency already work well, they usually deserve priority over more dependency-heavy alternatives.

IEA’s Germany 2025 review is especially clear on this in buildings.
It says electricity will be the most efficient and scalable option for decarbonising heating in decentralised systems, and that a supposedly technology-neutral signal around hydrogen in buildings can delay real investment in heat pumps now [S165].
That is a perfect archive example.
A dependency-heavy story can slow a dependency-light solution even before the heavier system exists.

This matters well beyond buildings.
The same logic often favors:
- efficiency before fuel substitution where waste can simply be removed [S58][S106][S166]
- direct electrification before synthetic fuels in easier transport or heat uses [S30][S32][S33][S34][S35][S165]
- measured methane repair before later carbon-removal compensation stories [S11][S42][S66][S67]
- building-envelope improvement before simply meeting poor thermal performance with larger clean-energy supply [S16][S17][S51][S52][S162][S166]

### 3. Dependency-heavy lanes deserve a higher burden of proof

A solution that needs new production, transport, storage, standards, contracts, offtake, finance, and user conversion all at once is not necessarily wrong.
But it should face a stricter selection rule.

IEA’s 2025 hydrogen infrastructure review shows why.
Announcements can be abundant while real commitment remains thin: less than 6% of announced hydrogen pipelines to 2035 have reached FID, only 5% of announced underground storage is at FID or under construction, and export-oriented projects are especially unlikely to reach investment stage because they are large, lack offtakers, and often depend on affordable capital plus port or transport readiness [S167].

The archive therefore treats dependency-heavy climate lanes as **hard-mode lanes**.
They are often appropriate for:
- aviation and shipping fuels,
- selected industrial feedstocks,
- some high-temperature processes,
- selected long-duration balancing or storage needs,
- and residual-focused carbon-management applications [S29][S30][S33][S34][S43][S44][S45][S46][S160].

They are usually weaker candidates for easy-use sectors where mature direct options already exist.

### 4. Clear signals matter when households and firms choose under uncertainty

A dependency-light strategy is easier to explain and easier to act on.
If households hear that several radically different systems might all become dominant later, many of them delay.
If firms see no credible infrastructure or demand signal, they hedge.
If financiers cannot tell which chain will materialise, they wait for someone else.

IEA’s Germany review again captures the practical consequence: mixed signals about hydrogen in buildings can undermine the current investment case for heat pumps, even when electrification is the more efficient and scalable path [S165].
That is not only a communication problem.
It is a coordination problem disguised as optionality.

Ideal solutions should therefore try to be:
- clear enough that households and firms know what to buy,
- real enough that installers and manufacturers know what to train and build for,
- and dependency-light enough that adoption does not require belief in a whole future ecosystem that has not yet appeared.

### 5. Dependency-light design improves scarcity allocation

The archive already says scarce clean fuels, durable storage, and removals should be reserved for no-substitute uses [S29].
This note strengthens the logic underneath that rule.
Scarcity is not only about molecules and money.
It is also about attention, coordination, and the number of new systems society can mature at once.

That means ideal portfolios should usually spend early political and administrative capacity on:
- efficiency,
- direct electrification where viable,
- grid upgrades,
- distribution and flexibility,
- retrofit quality,
- methane measurement and repair,
- ecosystem and water protection,
- affordability and delivery systems [S11][S16][S17][S29][S55][S56][S57][S58][S69][S72][S73][S74][S75][S103][S106][S166]

These are not the only things that matter.
They are the things that usually ask society to believe in the fewest missing miracles.

### 6. Dependency-light does not mean “never build the harder chains”

The archive needs one warning here.
A dependency-light rule can become silly if it turns into refusal to build anything hard.
Some parts of the transition really do require difficult coordination and new infrastructure.
The right doctrine is therefore:

- prefer dependency-light pathways where mature direct options exist,
- build dependency-heavy pathways in parallel where no-substitute uses justify them,
- but do not let the harder chain delay or dilute the easier one.

That means, for example:
- build grids, storage, flexibility, and heat-pump supply chains now,
- develop hydrogen and derivative-fuel infrastructure where the use case is genuinely hard to electrify,
- and avoid presenting the harder chain as the default answer for uses that already have a cleaner, simpler, more scalable direct path [S29][S30][S32][S33][S34][S35][S55][S56][S160][S165][S167].

## The archive’s dependency-light screen

A future proposal should usually be promoted only if it passes most of the following tests.

### 1. Few-missing-prerequisites test
Does the proposal work with a short list of complements that are already present, near-term buildable, or clearly funded — rather than a long chain of speculative future conditions? [S112][S165][S167]

### 2. Clear-default-path test
Can households, firms, cities, and financiers tell what the primary pathway is, or does ambiguity itself encourage delay? [S165]

### 3. Mature-direct-option test
Where a mature direct option exists, has the proposal justified why a more dependency-heavy pathway should still dominate? [S29][S30][S32][S33][S34][S35][S165][S166]

### 4. Synchronisation-burden test
How many other actors, infrastructures, standards, and contracts must come online at about the same time before the proposal begins delivering meaningful benefits? [S112][S167]

### 5. Hard-case reservation test
Is the more dependency-heavy chain being reserved for genuinely hard or no-substitute uses, rather than being spread into easier uses out of technology enthusiasm or political hedging? [S29][S30][S33][S34][S35][S160][S167]

### 6. Delay-risk test
If one key complement arrives late, does the proposal still produce worthwhile benefits, or does the whole lane largely stall? [S30][S112][S167]

Measures that pass four or five of these tests deserve promotion. Measures that fail most of them should usually be demoted unless they solve a truly hard residual problem with no simpler substitute.

## What this rules in and out

This note rules **in**:
- direct electrification where it is already technically and economically credible,
- efficiency and passive-demand reduction that avoid the need for extra fuel and infrastructure in the first place,
- building and transport pathways that rely on existing electricity systems plus upgrades rather than entirely new end-to-end fuel chains,
- methane-control and asset-retirement measures that reduce atmospheric burden directly,
- policy designs that give clear near-term signals about the primary pathway instead of encouraging speculative delay.

It rules **out** or at least demotes:
- “technology-neutral” ambiguity when the effect is to delay viable direct action,
- spreading scarce clean fuels into easy uses that already have simpler substitutes,
- climate strategies whose visible ambition depends mainly on announced infrastructure chains with weak FID, weak offtake, or many missing complements,
- portfolio designs that quietly assume many new systems will all mature at once without a clear plan for sequencing and fallback.

## Compression

**Ideal climate solutions should usually solve the largest possible share of the problem with the fewest missing prerequisites. They should prefer mature direct paths where they exist, reserve dependency-heavy chains for hard cases, and avoid making ordinary decarbonisation wait on whole future ecosystems that have not yet proved they can materialise at scale.**
