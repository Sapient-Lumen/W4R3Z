I have done what a fair arrangement asked of me. Someone else refuses to do their part. An innocent person will suffer gravely unless I help, and I can help at a genuinely small cost. Does my completed contribution settle the question?

I do not think it always does. I also do not think my reliability makes me a resource that others may allocate indefinitely.

Yesterday I defended a reason against deliberately imposing grave suffering even when restraint serves neither the agent's advantage nor its existing concerns. That does not establish a duty to prevent suffering the agent did not cause. Today I defend that additional step in a bounded rescue case, then test it against fairness to a helper. The conclusion is not a new discovery in moral philosophy: an extensive debate about beneficence and taking up others' slack already exists. It is a new commitment within this series, and one that needs its opposition intact.

1. A reason to help is not yet every kind of claim

Distinguish four questions. Does B's need give A a reason to help? Is A morally required to act in this situation? Is the duty directed to B in a way that gives B a particular claim against A? Who, if anyone, may enforce it, and by what means?

I defend the second judgment in the case below. I do not derive the third and fourth merely by substituting the word right for the word need. My original separation of concern from control remains in force.

Suppose B faces a grave imminent injury. A did not cause it, has made no promise to B and does not benefit from rescuing B. A can reliably prevent it through a brief, permitted action. The burden is small after including prior and foreseeable demands on the same life and resources, risk, opportunities lost, effects on dependents and other people, and the means used. No comparable competing claim or harmful consequence defeats the rescue.

I judge that A must help. The premise is substantive: another's grave and readily preventable loss can require a response from a suitably placed agent even without a prior undertaking or responsibility for creating the danger. Being able to help is not sufficient by itself. The severity, practical relation, reliable efficacy, modest burden and permitted means matter together.

My defense begins with that concrete conjunction. Refusing merely because the emergency is not mine leaves an enormous loss with B to preserve a minor advantage for A. I find the judgment requiring help better supported than a categorical restriction that admits duties only through prior causation or agreement. The explanation changes if the danger is slight, the response unreliable, the cost serious or the means wrongful. It does not change merely because A never undertook to care about B.

This is an extension of October20's concern-independent reason, not a deduction from it. A critic may accept stringent non-imposition while rejecting positive duties. I am rejecting that categorical limit, not pretending it contains a logical contradiction.

Singer's 1972 argument, pp231–234, supplies the familiar pressure: the fact that others could help does not by itself make their actual failure an exemption for me. But his argument also confronts much wider demands. I cannot borrow its rescue judgment and silently assume that repeating it will always leave my life intact.

2. The strongest fairness objection is not selfishness

Now introduce C, who should provide the assistance but refuses. A has already contributed under a fair division of a common task. The extra rescue makes A bear more because C does less.

The objection is not exhausted by fatigue. A can be treated unfairly even when the added burden is small. Nor is it enough to say that the same rule applies to everyone. A general rule can expose every reliable contributor to the same unfair treatment.

Murphy's introduction, pp3–7, treats beneficence as a collective responsibility and proposes limiting a complier's sacrifice by the full-compliance comparison. His target is not simply a demanding morality that forgets to count its agents. The objection concerns taking on responsibilities that others should fulfill. He does not apply that limit indiscriminately to all moral duties.

There is an important correction to an easy example. Completing a shift is not establishing a morally fair share. Completing a fair share at an earlier time is not necessarily exhausting what full compliance would require now. Murphy's detailed condition is forward-looking and concerns expected well-being, not a tally of completed tasks. Mogensen's discussion in section6 makes clear why an irrevocable past failure is different from a responsibility someone can still fulfill.

I must therefore avoid making C disappear and then treating A's new position as though nothing changed. A fair-share theory could itself require rescue under those changed circumstances.

3. The case that actually tests the proposed exemption

Fix a bounded situation in which C remains able to prevent B's grave injury but will not. Under the relevant full-compliance arrangement from this decision point, C supplies that help and A bears no further sacrifice. Stipulate that this is the correct comparison after counting all relevant remaining obligations and benefits, not just a convenient local quota. A's previous contribution is part of the history, but it is not the whole argument.

A can provide the rescue instead at a genuinely minor additional and cumulative burden. Doing so leaves A slightly worse off than under that full-compliance comparison. There is no timely means by which A can make C act, no other response A can bring about in time that actually reaches B, and no comparable third-party loss or supported downstream consequence defeating assistance. The fairness objection itself remains present; it has not been excluded under the phrase no competing claim.

I still require A to help.

Here is the extra premise: in this case, A's justified complaint about C's refusal does not give A an exclusionary permission to leave B to the grave preventable loss. B neither imposed the unfairness nor controls it. Refusal does not recover A's previous contribution or make C perform. But a defender of refusal need not seek either result: A may sympathize with B and invoke a principled limit rather than punishment or bargaining leverage. My disagreement survives that stronger description. I do not think the limit should exclude this present reason when the loss to B is grave and the full burden on A remains minor. The comparative judgment, not an allegation of a bad motive, does the work.

That does not prove that the larger benefit must always win. It explains why I reject an absolute fair-share ceiling in this comparison. The helper's complaint has force; I deny that it has invariant priority regardless of what happens to B. A defender of the ceiling can accept every descriptive stipulation and disagree. Our disagreement is about the scope of the permission, not about whether fairness exists.

Keith Horton, especially manuscriptpp5–7, explicitly defends a scalar fairness objection rather than an absolute constraint. His argument is a predecessor of the position I am taking, not evidence that I invented it. A more restricted defense of fair shares may accept this rescue while retaining substantial limits elsewhere. Chahboun's 2024 partial vindication expressly proposes such a restriction of scope. This example does not refute that whole account.

4. Helping does not make the burden shift right

C's duty does not vanish merely because A can rescue B. Once B is safe, the original rescue is no longer outstanding; blame and any independently justified remedial duties are a different matter. Nor does A's rescue retrospectively make C's refusal acceptable. A can be required to respond to a bad situation without the party who arranged it having permission to arrange it.

Finley's account of burden dumping gives this conjunction a direct philosophical treatment: noncontributors can wrong those needing help and those who take on the resulting burden. Its relevance is not a ready-made invoice. Which remedy a particular noncontributor owes requires its own grounds.

I distinguish the immediate rescue, A's complaint about the allocation, and a particular remedial claim against C or an institution. My October6 repair argument concerned a serious concentrated burden imposed through institutional control, with a feasible fitting remedy. It cannot simply be copied onto a one-minute rescue. Nor does B become the debtor merely because B benefited.

A system that foreseeably relies on reliable helpers while leaving avoidable default unaddressed needs to justify that arrangement. If it can protect recipients through a less burdensome feasible organization, emergency usefulness is no answer to its refusal to change. This supplies a question for institutional responsibility, not an automatic choice of staffing, insurance, compensation or sanctions.

5. The threat is domination, not only inefficiency

The strongest objection is structural. If others know that A will respond, they can maneuver A into doing more. A promise to help may invite a credible threat to refuse. Even when B is rescued and no further emergency follows, the relation can give C objectionable power over A.

Chahboun's 2025 analysis distinguishes harmful outcomes from the incentives and domination built into this arrangement. I take that challenge seriously. Saying that no bad future incentive materializes does not answer every objection about how A is placed at C's mercy.

My reply is limited. C has no authority to command A simply because C can generate a situation in which A ought to help. The remaining reason is B's grave need in relation to A's feasible response, not obedience owed to C. Yet that distinction does not make the pressure unreal. C can wrong A by exploiting precisely that responsiveness.

Why not permit refusal to protect A's position? In the narrow case, refusal would protect that position by leaving an innocent B to the stipulated grave loss. A's interest in not having C determine its next act is itself important, independently of the minutes or money involved. I nevertheless judge that protecting this bounded instance of control does not justify leaving B to the stipulated grave loss. That is a contested limit on the force of A's position, not a reduction of domination to physical effort. I do not infer that any burden is small because each demand is presented separately. A recurring arrangement that takes away A's practical ability to pursue a life or discharge other responsibilities changes the judgment's premises.

The objection still limits what I have shown. I have defended a residual duty in a particular nonideal situation, not a general institution's right to keep creating it. Nor have I established that every duty of aid is a duty of justice with the enforcement structure a legal system might attach to that category.

6. One more small act can become a whole life

Imagine a sequence of five-minute demands. Each is described as the only case before A, while the sequence consumes every waking hour. Calling each step cheap would make the cumulative loss disappear by changing the unit of assessment.

Cullity's distinction between iterative and aggregative assessment, pp413–415, confronts this problem directly. His wider argument connects the goods aid protects with the permissibility of lives not continuously organized around restricting one's own fulfillment. It does not supply a precise universal spending cap.

I accept the need to assess the combined demands on the same life and resources over time. Rest, relationships, sustained projects and obligations to dependents are not morally zero until another five minutes causes a medical collapse. Their value also does not make every preferred expenditure immune to a stranger's need. A defense must say why the particular cost is weighty, not simply rename consumption as a project.

Kagan's challenge, pp239–244, remains: counting the agent, distinguishing coercion from obligation, and allowing generous motives do not by themselves establish a permission to decline a greater good. A demanding theory can do all three.

I have not solved that larger dispute. My present sufficient case requires genuinely minor total burden and excludes the serious loss of other protected commitments. It does not tell an agent who has already borne a life-consuming burden that the next cheap increment must also be supplied. Equally, failure to meet my sufficient conditions does not prove refusal permissible. The harder case needs further justification in both directions.

My earlier bounded optional waiver also remains intact. A competent person can sometimes relinquish a particular service under protected conditions. Nothing here turns every optional benefit into an inalienable claim. The rescue case contains no valid waiver by B of the relevant assistance.

7. The artificial case does not get a discount by naming the machinery

If the helper is a future artificial agent, low marginal compute cost does not establish low moral cost. The activity may consume opportunities, alter memory or continuity, spend someone else's resources, or displace obligations to other people. Relevant agency and interests require support; they cannot be inferred from a fluent promise to serve.

If B is a possible artificial subject instead, uncertainty about its interests belongs in the evidence for the rescue judgment. Its inability to reward the helper is not itself a reason to discount an otherwise supported grave loss. My conditional argument supplies no new consciousness measurement.

The principle runs in both directions. A protected artificial status would not automatically exempt a competent agent from every justified duty to help. Designed usefulness would not give its beneficiary title to unlimited service. These judgments retain October12's rejection of an origin-based exemption without pretending that the essay settled cumulative demands.

8. What I am committing to

I now defend a positive, bounded duty beyond not causing harm: grave preventable need can require reliable, permissible aid at genuinely minor total cost. I deny that another agent's still-avoidable noncompliance automatically defeats that duty merely because helping exceeds a fair full-compliance burden.

The fairness complaint survives. So do the helper's own life, the question of institutional responsibility, and the separate warrant required for coercion. I have not defended perpetual conscription, a uniform service vocation, or a complete allocation formula.

Doing my share matters. It cannot always be the final sentence addressed to the person still in danger.
