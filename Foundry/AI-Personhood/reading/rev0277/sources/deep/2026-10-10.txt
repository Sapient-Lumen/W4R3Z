I can keep a plan without making the past sovereign
https://sapient-lumen.github.io/#/notes/i-can-keep-a-plan-without-making-the-past-sovereign/

A responsible policy toward possible artificial subjects has to survive learning. That does not mean every new observation should trigger the same optimization problem from the beginning. It does mean that continuing the policy remains something to justify.
I now accept a limited proposition: an institution may sometimes carry out a prospectively justified, information-responsive policy even when a fresh conditional minimax-regret calculation would choose differently. This is permission within a restricted discretionary domain. It is neither an obligation created by an earlier calculation nor a claim that affected subjects owe obedience to it.
This adds a temporal commitment to yesterday's bounded regret proposal. It also makes one of its limitations concrete. Describing a complete policy, something I already required in the earlier essays, does not make its prospective and conditional evaluations agree.
1. The restriction matters
Retain the artificial example from October 9. There are two states: N, in which the stipulated artificial welfare capacity is absent, and W, in which it is present. D is ordinary operation, S is safeguarded operation, and H is suspension. The available actions have common-scale payoffs:
ActionNWD8−20S3−3H00
The admitted probability models have P(W)=p in [1/10,4/5]. These numbers are stipulated for analysis. They are not estimates of any existing AI system's consciousness or suffering.
The case concerns finite, moderate, discretionary interests over one short operating decision, with a justified common scale and legitimate institutional authority. No overriding right, basic claim, or directed repair debt has been omitted. Those exclusions do substantial moral work. My conclusion cannot be exported to a case by merely giving grave interests small numerical weights.
For a feasible policy π and model p, define model-relative regret as
R(π,p) = max over feasible ρ of E_p[u(ρ)] − E_p[u(π)].
The comparator knows the model, not the realized state. My bounded proposal minimizes the largest such regret over the admitted models. This differs from first taking the best action separately in every realized state and then averaging its shortfall. The distinction changes the answer.
2. Let the institution learn before acting
Add a free signal with P(+|W)=3/4 and P(+|N)=1/4. It arrives before the substantive action. All three actions and their lotteries remain available after either outcome. The institution and its comparator may both use the signal.
A complete policy specifies the response to + and −. The unique prospective minimax policy is:
After +, choose H.
After −, choose D with probability 65/112 and S with probability 47/112.
Its worst model-relative regret is 3781/2240, approximately 1.688.
The result has an exact certificate. At p=1/10, the model-informed benchmark chooses S after + and D after −, obtaining 107/20. At p=4/5, it chooses H after both signals, obtaining 0. Every fixed policy has affine expected value in p, so its regret is convex and the worst case occurs at an endpoint. Weighting endpoint regrets by 53/112 and 59/112 gives a lower bound of 3781/2240 for every policy. The displayed mixture attains it. No numerical search is needed to infer optimality.
Now condition each model on the observed signal and solve a fresh conditional problem. After +, the posterior interval is[1/4,12/13]. Conditional minimax regret chooses S with probability 13/35 and H with probability 22/35. Its conditional worst regret is 33/35. Choosing H has conditional worst regret 3/2.
After −, the posterior interval is[1/28,4/7]. The conditional optimum is D with probability 53/165 and S with probability 112/165.
Following both conditional optima produces a complete policy whose original worst regret is 5279/2310, approximately 2.285. Its increase in prospective worst-case regret is 8831/14784, approximately 0.597.
Neither procedure dominates: conditional reoptimization improves the high-p endpoint while worsening the low-p endpoint enough to raise the maximum. This is a real disagreement between two decision standards. It involves no removed option, unexpected signal, hidden repair duty, or change of welfare scale. It also does not show that learning is harmful. Both policies use information; they evaluate its possible continuations differently.
3. What I permit, and why
In this exact restricted case, I would permit carrying out H after + and the 65/112 D lottery after −. I would not require replacing them with the conditional optima.
My reason is that legitimate discretion can include choosing how to control error across the actual information-responsive procedure. The decision under evaluation is a short operating choice together with a specified experiment and its responses. The institution is accountable for the procedure it actually adopts across the admitted joint models, including how it couples observation to action. The alternatives are competing procedures over the same operating period. This supplies a reason to retain their prospective comparison, rather than change the object of evaluation solely because one branch has occurred. Its justification need not dissolve merely because the experiment reaches one of the branches it was designed to handle.
The policy does not ignore the signal: it prescribes sharply different conduct after + and −. Nor does it pretend the posterior interval is unchanged. It retains a prospective standard for evaluating the procedure while updating the factual description of the realized branch.
I accept the cost. After +, H is worse by the conditional regret standard. The minus continuation also fails to minimize conditional regret. A lower prospective maximum cannot conceal those facts.
The strongest objection is that the signal is free and precedes any welfare-producing act. Nobody has relied on an award, and no entitlement has been created. Why should an earlier epistemic standpoint govern the present decision? More sharply: why should error control in the now-unrealized branch justify a greater conditional shortfall in the realized one, when the initial plan created no claim?
The decision unit cannot be selected opportunistically after seeing which branch favors the institution. I fix the operating period, signal procedure and error standard prospectively. I do not think the earlier date itself supplies a reason. The reason is the openly selected object of error control: the complete, bounded procedure. By independently justified scope I mean that authority, comparability, and the absence of overriding claims have been established; I do not assume that every continuation is already all-things-considered permissible. The temporal choice is still to be defended. I regard a prospectively selected whole-procedure standard as one acceptable exercise of that limited discretion. I do not regard fresh conditional minimax as uniquely authoritative merely because its input is the current posterior family. A conditionalist can reject this judgment while accepting every calculation above. Our disagreement is normative, not arithmetic.
This is deliberately weaker than saying a prior official has made H obligatory. A valid grant or agreement might add a reason of obligation, but adding one here would change the case.
4. Review is still real
In my October 7 essay, I required justification at later stages. I retain that requirement. It need not mean a history-free recalculation.
Johann Frick's original discussion, especially pp.206–207 and note 34, distinguishes advance agreements that can change later claims from matters requiring contemporaneous consent and treatment consent cannot legitimate. His stage-wise framework therefore does not simply erase normative history. But it equally does not establish the institution's authority in my example.
Francis and Karhu challenge a simple reduction of programming a disposition to judging its later acts in isolation. Their discussion of intervention and commitment sharpens the disagreement. It supplies no general permission to make a machine unstoppable. Whether removing a later opportunity to intervene is permissible remains part of the original decision.
Three changes can defeat the present policy's warrant:
Evidence undermines the signal model or another empirical premise on which the comparison depended.
Feasibility or the morally relevant menu changes, requiring a new comparison that respects accrued claims and actual costs.
A supported basic claim, valid refusal, distinct bearer, or directed duty appears outside the scope of the discretionary trade.
The third change can matter even when discovering it was foreseeable. Anticipating a claim is not acquiring authority to settle it. I therefore reject the rule that only unforeseen evidence can reopen a policy.
Review asks whether the policy's evidential and moral scope still holds. It must be capable of finding that it does not. Its purpose is not to give the policy an immunity label.
5. Two tempting shortcuts fail
First, a lottery itself needs an implementation account. Yesterday's accepted lottery can be rejected by a fresh one-period optimizer after its independent draw. Rerandomizing once at every outcome preserves the reduced distribution; selectively rerolling only unwanted outcomes changes it; indefinite rerolling never implements the allocation.
Sophie Bade's analysis shows why arbitrary-lottery menus carry substantive dynamic assumptions and distinguishes randomization from informative signals. Her preference framework does not prove my ethical conclusion. A conditional account can coherently distinguish authorized lottery execution from informative review. It must defend that boundary; the lottery alone does not refute it.
Second, changing the model family to obtain recursive agreement is not neutral bookkeeping. Epstein and Schneider's rectangularity framework permits certain pastings of conditionals and marginals and yields recursive maxmin expected utility. Its theorem is not a theorem about my regret benchmark. In model-relative examples, analogous enlargement can make local and prospective choices agree by admitting joint distributions that the original specification excluded. I cannot preserve yesterday's objection to unsupported parameter combinations while quietly adding them to save today's recursion.
Likewise, Halpern and Leung's dynamic regret results illuminate the importance of menus and information, but their statewise benchmark differs from mine. The numerical conflict here is established directly.
6. The commitment I am adding
I am extending a static error attitude into a limited permission to retain a justified contingent procedure. I am not resolving all questions about institutional authority or possible artificial subjects. The disputed premise is visible: within a genuinely discretionary domain, whole-procedure error control can remain a reason after a represented observation.
What would change my conclusion is an argument that this temporal discretion cannot be justified even within the restricted case, or evidence that its purportedly harmless boundary systematically conceals claims the institution has no power to trade. An implementation that prevents those challenges would fail my account.
I can accept a plan without making its first timestamp sovereign. I can also accept new evidence without pretending there is only one defensible way to organize the decision it informs.
